# Rules of the game: constructing norms of influence, subordination and constraint in IT planning

John Tillquist

University of California, 900 University Avenue, Riverside, CA 92521, USA

Accepted 18 September 2001

## Abstract

This paper examines how norms develop and influence the planning of technological and organizational change. A case study highlights the influences of a broadly held management innovation as it is introduced into an existing organizational culture, demonstrating how norms play a role in attempts to introduce technical and management innovations. In the case, three sources of normative structuring—business process re-engineering, the consultant, and the target organization’s business environment—converged to create new rules defining how to conceptualize the problem space, the appropriate ways to approach problems, who had actionable authority and how ideas were to be expressed. The emergent normative context trapped the group in a malfunctioning process and eliminated opportunities to question the process or to suggest alternative directions. This case highlights how analyzing normative systems holds promise to improve scholarly understanding of organizational technologies and to refine managerial tools for technological and organizational innovation.  2002 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Organizational change; Project control; IS planning; Business systems planning; Planning issues; Norms; Normative systems; Social rule systems

## 1. Technological and organizational planning

Many technological and organizational change initiatives, such as business process re-engineering (BPR), portray IT as a crucial agent of organizational change. As with any change process, however, personalities, political agendas and even contractual guidelines with outside consultants may interfere with the superordinate goals of a project. Influential players may impose their own interests by influencing the process of change and may seek to frame changes in ways best suited to achieve those interests.

In the case presented here, a major pharmaceuticals organization retained an international consulting firm that specialized in BPR planning to guide the initial planning of a major systems integration change. Social rules of conduct and expression were selectively appropriated by influential players and used to control the planning process and to quiet dissent. These social rules became norms of appropriateness and acceptable expression that ultimately shaped the conceptualization of change to a point where the planning process actually subordinated crucial information technology issues and significantly impacted the feasibility of the resulting systems integration plan.

Although the usual explanations of the shortcomings of BPR could be used (e.g., Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1994, 1995), a deeper understanding of the failure is revealed through the more subtle interaction effects of personalities, political agendas and methodology. Academic writings, practitioner press, consultants and many formalized methodologies view technological and organizational planning as the objective collection, evaluation, and application of data in a rational manner to direct the planning process. In application, however, planners are often motivated by individual interests, maintaining appearances, and demonstrating superiority in knowledge and influence.

From where are rules that govern the IT planning process created, how are they made into persistent norms of conduct and expression, and how do they come to influence the process of IT planning? These questions address the gap between methodological rationality in prescription and in practice. In the answers is a new and significant explanation for IT failures, one that closely ties undesirable outcomes back to the sources of the process failures. Contrary to the existing emphasis on refinement of IT change models in the IT literature, this line of analysis suggests that many substantive effects on IT conceptualization lie in the way in which rationality is bounded during the IT planning process. Often overlooked, norms define elements of IT planning that can significantly influence the direction and outcome of technological and organizational change.

These are important considerations for application. Understanding how norms define elements of IT planning can significantly influence the direction and outcome of technological and organizational change in practice. Practitioners can recognize elements in internal planning process that stifle productivity and can act early to structure the processes for positive gains.

## 2. Norms, technology, and organizations

Norms are the shared structures of social meaning that define actional settings and organize conceptual themes to reduce ambiguity and stabilize social relations. They are interpretations people share about how to conceptualize their setting, themselves, and others. Carried through social interaction in language, action, and artifacts, norms provide guidance as to what should be considered appropriate in action, expression, and in the framing of social situations (Jones, 1994; Tillquist, 1996; Schultze, 2000). Norms provide clues to social actors as to how situations are to be made meaningful, what roles, goals and actions are appropriate for participants, and how dissidence and social deviance should be dealt with.

In the IS literature, behavioral models treat norms as exogenous factors impacting IS usage. One prevailing view depicts norms as pre-existing societal or organizational factors. In this view, norms influence individual user choice and modes of adaptation to IT (Thompson, Higgins, & Howell, 1991; Constant, Kiesler, & Sproull, 1994; Karahanna, Straub, & Chervany, 1999) and impact the organizational adoption of IT (Harrison, Mykytyn, & Riemenschneider, 1997). The effectiveness of decisionmaking and technological use are seen as outcomes of user intention and IT functionality moderated by organizational and professional norms of appropriateness, value, and acceptability.

Another view from the IT literature portrays normative models as prescriptions for action or varying viewpoints. These propose to fit technological function to organizational requirements, with operational performance as the intended result. Here, normative prescriptions anchored to beliefs of rationality, economic return, and process efficiency define and assess technological approaches in influencing decisionmaking, process definition, and workflow (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Todd & Benbasat, 1999; Lee & Menon, 2000).

In contrast, studies of organizational appropriation and accommodation of information technologies suggest that norms are much richer elements of the IT setting than the static factors they are often treated as. In this research stream, norms are portrayed as individuals’ sense-making apparatuses, constantly in flux, providing interpretive frameworks to improvise, innovate, and adjust to changing situations. Perceptions of and attitudes about tasks, technology, and change are seen to be shaped by social influences and shaped by cues from one’s social context (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978), while application and use of technologies shape the perceptions and attitudes through the social processes of legitimation, institutionalization, and practice (Klein & Hirschheim, 1989). As supra-individual shared systems of interpretation, normative systems emerge from the interplay of seen action and attributed meaning as users confront ambiguity, unanticipated contingencies and opportunities, and shifts in their local operations (Orlikowski, 1996). It is this focus on norms that serves as a basis for the analysis of the case study.

Social rules will be used in the analysis to organize observations as participants learn and apply BPR, revealing from where planners derive norms that structure their work process, and to follow how norms shape the emerging interpretations in planning technological and organizational change.

## 2.1. Social Rule System theory

Social Rule System (SRS) theory offers a basis for understanding the formation and implementation of social rules that describe the normative setting (Burns & Flam,

1987). In contrast to societal-level conceptualizations like Structuration Theory, SRS provides the theoretical basis to capture, categorize, and analyze detailed observations of expression and behavior in small social groups. SRS has been used to critically examine case-based studies of discrete social action, such as political planning, group norms, and marriage (Burns & Flam, 1987). Social rules hold for various situations, such as those regulating the conduct of sporting events, vehicle traffic, and business practices. These rules are readily visible and comparable, making studies of discrete social action accessible for analysis. This case-level sensitivity makes SRS an appropriate basis for the Coast case.

SRS defines three rule categories that structure social relations—classificatory (‘who are we?’), evaluative (‘what is good?’) and prescriptive rules (‘how should things be done?’) that order behavior, convey perspective, and attribute responsibility.

Discord and concurrence among participants about the structuring of social relations reveals underlying assumptions and beliefs about the nature of technological and organizational change. Participants may differ about how a work setting should be constructed (classificatory differences), what roles the participants should take on and how they should be conducted (evaluative differences), or about what courses of action are to be considered legitimate (prescriptive differences). They also implicitly or explicitly agree on how they define themselves and their social setting (classificatory similarities), what goals are worth pursuing (evaluative similarities), and taken-for-granted ways of ‘how things get done’ (prescriptive similarities).

The rule systems that regulate and structure social activity are incomplete, imprecise and multiple. Rules fluctuate, changing with the actions and interpretations of the social agents who apply them and as conditions change. When gaps in rule systems create situational ambiguity, participants choose between differing rule interpretations or create new rules. These choices, made either deliberately or inadvertently, redefine the existing rule systems to the new situation. Thus the structuring of social relations continuously produces and is reproduced by the action and the interpretation of action of the participants (Burns & Flam, 1987).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. The next section describes the research site, pseudonymously referred to as Coast Pharmaceutical. This is followed by a description of the research approach and Coast’s BPR project. An analysis of the Coast case is then presented, highlighting the adaptation of social rules and the emergence of norms. Conclusions drawn from the Coast study are presented at the end of the paper.

## 3. Coast pharmaceutical

The research site, Coast Pharmaceutical (‘Coast’), is a large multinational pharmaceutical and medical supply company. Coast accrues about one billion dollars (US\$) in annual sales revenues, approximately half of which are from the US domestic market, and employs approximately 6000 people globally.

Coast has a history of specialization around profitable product lines. Its worldwide consolidated revenues are principally generated by pharmaceutical and medical supply products. High entry barriers to research and development in the pharmaceutical industry coupled with long periods of patent protection have traditionally enabled high profitability sheltered from competition.

At the beginning of the study Coast’s executive management decided to undertake a large-scale BPR project encompassing both domestic and international operations. Compelling this change was a desire to integrate fragmented technological systems and operations, to combat competition, and to cut administrative costs. The process streamlining and conversion to a client–server architecture expected to result from the BPR project dovetailed into Coast’s plans to ultimately implement SAP enterprise resource planning. Coast envisioned this IT integration would lead to ‘order of magnitude’ change opportunities.

Coast’s mandate explicitly focused on IT to enable change. The opportunity to follow an organization just beginning to plan for a large-scale, IT-enabled organizational change made Coast a particularly attractive research site. Research focused on re-engineering of the order management process where the greatest amount of change was expected to unfold.

The study follows the strategic and technical planning process at Coast over an eight-month period, beginning with the inception of their BPR planning for the order management process and ending with the presentation of the final recommendations to senior management. With Coast’s long track record of industrial success and acclaim, expectations were to gain insights into how successful organizations translate conceptions about IT into workable plans for technological and organizational change.

## 4. Researching the planning process

## 4.1. Naturalistic inquiry

Naturalistic inquiry is adopted as the research approach to study how Coast structured planning for computerization and change. Naturalistic Inquiry provides an interpretivist approach and framing to develop explanations for the presumed ‘natural’ ordering of Coast’s planning activities (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Agar, 1986; Miles & Huberman, 1994; cf. Yin, 1984).

Naturalistic Inquiry holds that norms enable participants to make sense of ambiguous and equivocal situations through shared understanding and acceptable codes of conduct. These are tacitly held as common-sense patterns of perception, expression, and behavior so ingrained in daily activities that participants rarely question their appropriateness or even consciously acknowledge their presence.

Naturalistic Inquiry shares a common theory-building theme with Grounded Theory (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) and the formal theory synthesis of traditional sociology (e.g., C. Wright Mills, 1959). Naturalistic Inquiry specifically targets shared normative structures for inquiry, inductively distilling observation and inquiry into explanations with analytical generalizability (Preston, 1986; Strauss & Corbin,

1990). Analytical generalizability is the construction of generalized concepts like social stigmas (Goffman, 1963), reference groups (Merton, 1959), and the awareness of dying (Glaser & Strauss, 1965) from close scrutiny of individual cases. This is the theory-building material Goffman (1956, 1963) uses to build coherent frameworks that ‘tie together bits of experience’. Naturalistic inquiry is particularly useful in this study as it focuses on identification of the development and influence of norms in the Coast IT planning setting.

## 5. Data collection at Coast

Access to the Coast project was arranged through a second-line manager in the Sales and Marketing division who had participated in a different study for a colleague four years earlier. I learned about Coast’s intention to initiate a BPR project and contacted the sales manager prior to the beginning of the BPR project. She provided access to the planning meetings, executives, managers, and staff surrounding Coast’s BPR. No consultative advice was provided and no remuneration was received. To develop an understanding of how Coast would structure the planning for change, the study was designed to minimize observer bias and to construct interpretations from the participants’ point of view.

Observations of behavior, written and spoken text, and the proposed arrangement of computerized processes are the data informing this study. The collection method chosen employed a combination of techniques that identify and record data in the field setting. These included (1) direct observation of meetings, work, and social interaction, (2) focused interviews of key participants, and (3) documentation of computerized applications and organizational processes (Table 1 details the data collection at Coast).

## 5.1. Direct observation

Much of the data collection arose from direct observation of steering committee meetings, software selection committees, weekly work sessions, and workflow analysis sessions where the processes of planning took place. Data collection came primarily from regular core planning meetings and from several off-site planning sessions. These planning groups ranged from six to twenty participants per meeting, lasting for two to four hours over a period of eight months.

Observational data were recorded in field notes, which included observations and general impressions as well as notes for follow-up and avenues for further investigation. After each site contact, the field notes and observations were transcribed into electronic text and collated with the various handouts, overheads, and reference materials.

In addition to the data from direct observation, over twenty hours of in-depth interviews were conducted to probe individual perceptions and to deepen a growing understanding of the planning process. Participant interviews were conducted throughout the study, one to two hours each time. Extensive use of documentation and printed text supplemented data from interviews and direct observation. The planning meetings provided an abundance of overheads, handouts, procedures, worksheets, memos, handwritten accounts and recommendations detailing perceptions about work practices and computerization.

## 6. Data analysis

The process of analysis proceeded iteratively with data collection as recommended by Agar and Hobbs (1985) and Strauss and Corbin (1990). Data from direct observation, semi-structured interviews and secondary documentation were investigated using descriptive and comparative analyses. A summary of these data analyses is presented in Table 2.

## 6.1. Descriptive analysis

Descriptive analysis was used to analyze verbal, textual and behavioral observations (Goetz & LeCompte, 1981; Lincoln & Guba, 1985) into cognitive, evaluative and regulative social rule classifications. This was a search for patterns in the participants’ choice of words and discussion topics, repeated actions, and metaphors that provided clues to underlying structures of belief or preference. Patterns of similar expressions led to uncovering the organizing themes that participants were using to make sense of the change initiative.

## 6.2. Comparative analysis

Comparative analysis was also used to uncover hidden themes and normative beliefs within the group (Glaser & Strauss, 1965; Goetz & LeCompte, 1981; Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Comparing participant expressions—word choices, metaphors, and the relative importance granted to a particular topic—revealed how these compared to other similar expressions.

These analyses revealed differences in perspective, goals, or status among the participants. The emergence of a new manner of speaking, a new metaphor, or of normative sanctions against some particular behavior or expression were also followed across situations in the ongoing analysis. Observations were used to gain an understanding of the depth and breadth of the emerging norms governing appropriateness, legitimacy, and relevance within the planning group.

## 6.3. Working hypotheses

Descriptive and comparative analyses were coalesced into working hypotheses and periodically checked with individual participants. This was intended to generate an understanding of why participants had acted in certain ways and what perceptions they had formed. Individuals, for example, were asked about their reluctance to pursue a topic raised in a meeting or about their interpretation of a particular phrase being used. The feedback gained refined the working hypotheses and enabled a deeper understanding to emerge from subsequent observations. Most working hypothesis feedback sessions took place informally across the study, although some were necessarily postponed until after planning completed because they had potential technical or strategic implications that may have influenced the emerging process.

## 7. The case for change at Coast

## 7.1. Overview

In 1995 Coast was under pressure to improve operational efficiency and cut costs. They opted for BPR as a solution to their operational efficiency problems but, lacking in BPR experience, they contracted an internationally renowned consulting firm (referred to here as International Consulting) to co-lead the change strategy development with two Coast managers. The core planning team met weekly, interspersed with sub-committees and working teams. A timeline of the overall project (Fig. 1) organizes the chronology of events at Coast.

## 8. Coast’s change project

In early 1995, Coast’s re-engineering of the order management process began with a two-day kick-off meeting. The core BPR planning team consisted of the Division Manager overseeing the project, the consultant, two Coast managers serving as cochairs, and the project team. Most of the project team members were first- through third-level general business and information systems (I/S) managers, all with fulltime responsibilities in addition to the BPR project. Managers from customer service, I/S, warehousing, logistics, and credit formed the core project team to define the new order management process. Representatives were also brought in from the Canadian and overseas operations as liaisons for their home country’s operations.

The Division Manager heading up the group was Coast’s North American Manager of Logistics and Warehousing. For the previous six years the Division Manager had been responsible for logistics in Coast’s southwestern region. Recently promoted to the southern California headquarters, he now oversaw all of Coast’s North American logistics. For him, the BPR could legitimate plans to integrate Logistics’ disjointed information systems and to consolidate his division with the politically powerful Sales and Marketing group.

With the aid of his Vice-President, the Division Manager convinced Coast’s executive board of the need for radical organizational change and suggested in-sourcing expertise from a consulting firm to bring in the necessary knowledge and skills to re-engineer the order management process.

The Consultant was a Los Angeles-based manager hopeful that a successful engagement with Coast would earn her a position as a Consulting Partner. As the largest responsibility in her career, her goals were to have the Coast project “deliverables completed on-time, and in-budget” [Consultant]. By personally negotiating the Coast contract with Coast’s executive management, the consultant enjoyed strong upper-level support in achieving a successful engagement. She shared project planning management activities with two co-chairs assigned from Coast.

The most active of these two Coast managers in the planning project was a secondline manager in Order Management. Rising quickly in the southern California corporate ‘campus’, she retained many of her past social connections in front-line sales and marketing. Equitable treatment and a people-oriented working environment were central organizing themes in her management style. In meetings, she preferred to mediate discussions, seeking to resolve differences and to create an open forum for equal participation and representation among the participants.

[Co-chair] Oh yeah. Yeah. I know in a year’s time or whenever the (project) is implemented, my job will go away. Or it will be merged … some of us will be effected by it. That didn’t prevent us from saying it’s the right thing to do, and it’s okay … we had to get beyond that … you’re going to have more than what you have now, and you’re going to have cross-functional opportunities.

The other Coast co-chair was also a second-level corporate manager. His restrained meeting style masked an often playful approach to managing people and projects. However, faced with the uncertainty and high visibility of the Coast planning meetings, this co-chair tended to withhold opinions and suggestions until assured of a favorable group reception.

As the project began, participants were excited by the potential for meaningful change. Despite the project being added onto their existing responsibilities, they welcomed the executives’ mandate and were looking forward to creating innovative changes. Excitement was obvious in these first meetings as participants began to see the opportunity to correct what they had long seen as problems in their workplace.

[Co-chair] I think it’s important that Coast has finally stepped up to the bar and said, “you know we need to move on with some of these decisions; quit dillydallying with it.”

Buoyed by the promise of the new information technologies, planners prepared a preliminary list of radical transformations that would guide their planning efforts:

1. Streamlined operations in order entry, inventory, and shipping

2. Electronic commerce opportunities, including electronic data interchange (EDI) and call management centers

3. Vendor managed inventories, where Coast would assume responsibilities for retail outlet inventories and stocking customer shelves

4. Order policy changes, focusing on high service for the customer

5. Improvements to product and sales forecasting and integration of systems with finance and management

6. Organizational integration of the Sales and Marketing and Logistics divisions

## 8.1. Executing the methodology: job holder interview worksheets

With this set of change opportunities in mind, planners started to implement the International Consulting methodology. The first step was to document the AS–IS scenario by developing a complete inventory of Coast’s existing work processes. To do this, the planning team was instructed to identify and record each of Coast’s order management business processes on International Consulting’s specially coded Job Holder Interview Worksheets (the ‘worksheets’; see Fig. 2 for an example).

The consultant explained that the worksheets were intended to help planners to analyze and compare their job process interview data. Standardizing the input data to the planning meetings was to facilitate data collection and presentations, enabling comparisons between operational units and generally reducing the difficulties of analyzing data. Process inefficiencies, redundant activities, and dead-end processes of the existing AS–IS organization would be identified and streamlined into the planned TO-BE organization.

The BPR process was to focus on achieving quantum leap changes in customer service and process improvement. The future vision, the TO-BE, is an analysis of the AS–IS work processes to identify gaps and opportunities:

[Consultant] … The vision targets and benchmarking tell us where to go. Customer input and stretch targets will move us from the AS–IS to the TO-BE environment. Instead of concentrating on best practices, emphasis should be on sub-par areas or ‘gaps’. Gaps are the difference between reality and the TO-BE. The interview process will follow the questions in the methods notebook. We interview for process steps, metrics, bottlenecks, and value-added activities. We are looking for ways to improve the process and probe for ways to assure all relevant people have been interviewed. This has proven successful in my fifteen years experience with BPR.

Subcommittees were formed to gather information from the existing business processes, each assigned to capture a current snapshot of the work activities performed by one of Coast’s many operational work-groups. Subcommittees consisted of a planning team member as leader and up to six subordinates from the member’s home workplace. Over the following weeks, the sub-committees interviewed job holders for each major business process within Coast’s order management process and completed the worksheets detailing the major process activities.

Descriptions of task and activities were assembled for each operational unit, a measurement metric for performance established, and baselines created for current and future levels of performance. These worksheets (Fig. 2) included columns to record current AS–IS job activities and the TO-BE stretch targets—challenging but attainable performance goals associated with individual activities. Summaries of the job holder worksheets were later presented at the on-going planning meetings.

## 8.2. Learning to talk the talk

At the project outset, most of Coast’s planners had had no prior exposure to the BPR process and few had little knowledge of basic BPR principles. This changed, however, as knowledge about BPR for the planners grew from interactions with work aids, reference materials, and from the consultant. Within a few weeks the team had acquired an operating knowledge of the BPR process and had begun to compile the interview data.

During these early meetings, the planning group’s vocabulary began to converge around key phrases and catch-words resonant with the high-risk, high-reward devisings of BPR. Phrases like ‘stretch targets’, ‘quantum leap changes’, ‘process ownership’, ‘breakthrough ideas’, and ‘worker empowerment’ appeared in meeting discussions and personal interactions among the planners. These words and phrases became important markers for participants—having command of the BPR vocabulary meant the ability to express ideas with greater legitimacy and social weight. Members ‘in the know’ were commonly attributed higher status and greater discussion time in each of these settings.

## 8.3. Low hanging fruit

BPR portrays ‘low hanging fruit,’ one of the more pervasive expressions adopted by the Coast planners, as something that adds to the value of the decision path; the quick gains accumulated along the way to radical change which bolster and propel the change process as it proceeds. At Coast the metaphor carried a slightly different significance. Low hanging fruit became synonymous with tangents hindering planning of quantum leap change. As the consultant introduced the term in the initial planning meeting:

[Consultant, initial project team meeting] Low hanging fruit (means) quick wins... it’s the way we do things and why (we’re) not going further in a particular area.

By the second core team meeting the low hanging fruit metaphor was being used frequently. As the supervisor for the order entry process presented findings to the group, the consultant abruptly cut in:

[Manager, Order management] (from the podium) We can use phone, fax, mail and EDI (electronic data interchange) for order acceptance … (consultant interrupts)

[Consultant] (taking over podium) EDI is more effective. But this is small stuff. We need a policy (to bypass) low hanging fruit like this.

As discussion turned to the need to upgrade the customer service representative position, the Coast co-chair suggested a discussion to address how to attract and retain higher skill employees. This discussion was again cut short by the consultant:

[Consultant] [These] low hanging fruit improvements are self-evident. We shouldn’t worry about getting better people or bigger salaries to get there. Let’s move on.

Low hanging fruit continued to direct the discussion, increasingly by the planners themselves. Each time the effect was to redirect the discussion away from concerns about work practices and back onto the methodology:

[Manager, Customer Service] We have problems maintaining—adding and deleting lines—in the system. But once we have the new system, these will not be an issue—they’re low hanging fruit.

Low hanging fruit became the signifier of issues that were not only a waste of time for the planners, but worse, actual distractions from real change planning:

[BPR Co-chair] (Low hanging fruit) is where we were getting bogged down... Don’t get bogged down in all the details; don’t get so bogged down in it that you never get out.

## and

[Manager, Canadian Operations] (Low hanging fruit) means not to fix what we’re using since we might not be using them in the future. Let’s not put our resources there in case we start fixing things we are getting rid of.

## 8.4. Process ownership

As the BPR meeting broke up, the consultant and co-chairs met separately afterwards to discuss the day’s meeting and to chart out the coming week. Similar to the use of low hanging fruit, the consultant introduced a new term for Coast, process ownership, to convey her sense of ‘inappropriate behavior’ in BPR planning:

[Consultant] We seem to have a problem with ownership of the re-engineering process. (An I/S team member’s) contribution is inappropriate. She lacks motivation and the ability to think independently that we need here. The group needs to have ownership of the process and not act as followers (sic). She just doesn’t have ownership.

[Co-chair] Perhaps it would help if the (Vice-President) were in the room as often as possible. Despite some ‘posturing activities’ of the team members, that might encourage people to take ownership … I’ll talk to (the I/S person) and query her expectations and thoughts about the possible outcomes for appropriateness to the group.

## 8.5. I/S loses voice

As the presentations of the worksheets in the core group continued, the importance of integrating computerization support for the work became increasingly apparent. From the beginning participants had readily accepted an integrated IT infrastructure as the implicit basis for the radical organizational changes planners envisioned in their TO-BE vision of the future.

Summarizing the role of IT in the BPR project, the Manager of Canadian Operations started the next core planning meeting with “This (BPR project) is a big push to move all that we can on-line”. This position seemed to support the interests of the I/S group. For years I/S had been pushing for resources to convert their legacy technologies; now the BPR project provided the backing to move ahead.

This initial excitement for I/S, however, was short-lived. When I/S representatives began talking about the technological transformation—raising the issue of the incremental process of IT implementation—they were admonished for ‘bogging down’ the planning and impeding ‘real’ quantum leap changes. After listening to the I/S concerns about IT redevelopment, the consultant countered:

[Consultant] The “answer” is not the system, but the policies, processes, and practices we make … This is low hanging fruit … we need to concentrate on (identifying) the real quantum leaps.

Issues of technical planning and even the feasibility of transforming the IT infrastructure were rationalized as peripheral, low hanging fruit. Resolving these details was assumed to be unambiguous and obvious; wasting meeting time to resolve technical details was presented as counter to the goals of finding quantum leap strategies. When I/S representatives continued to insist that these ‘details’ were in fact a wholesale renovation of the IT infrastructure, their push for I/S interests was portrayed as disloyal to the project and their disagreement an impediment to the process. Following this meeting, the I/S representatives quietly withdrew from attending further BPR planning meetings, shifting their efforts to the parallel SAP enterprise resource planning project at Coast.

## 8.6. Building resistance

As the data collection about the AS–IS environment began to wind down, presentations of the job holder interviews were providing a detailed picture of Coast’s existing work systems. Coast’s planners, however, were expressing concerns about the project and the value of the consultant. The consultant continued to press through the methodology, but was encountering increasing resistance as planners questioned the process and deliverables:

[Manager, Marketing] The issue is not are we doing the right process, but is there enough time to do it?

[BPR Co-chair] (to group) Well, we’re agreed to do this, so the question is how can it get done? What do you need to reach a comfort level?

[Manager, Operations] Well, we need time to analyze what we have already.

The consultant kept the group concentrated on the tasks and activities of the methodology, such as assigning metrics and stretch targets to work activities, and away from digressions on alternative analyses or issues. For example, when a warehousing manager presented her vision of customer service in the pick and pack process, the consultant scolded her for not concentrating on performance metrics:

[Manager, Warehousing] (at podium) The stretch target for pick and pack is “to be prepared to accelerate the process for each kind of customer order to meet their respective requirements in respect to product, delivery, pricing, and terms.” We need to have the right product on-hand, and the data ready-to-go.

[Consultant] (to the group) Where are the time, volume, and cost metrics in (her) stretch targets? We need to see improvement potential for each stretch targetcurrent metrics to measure attainment.

Later in the meeting, as the consultant excused herself and left the room, the group fell into informal banter. After a few minutes, the participants began to question the direction and value of International’s BPR process:

[Operations Manager] … But seriously, we need to meet (separately) with International to help them help us.

[Warehousing Manager] Yeah. I expected International would do more work that had to be done, instead of us doing it during the meeting.

However, Coast’s planners hesitated to directly challenge the consultant or to change the process to meet their needs. Calling upon the expectations of Coast’s executive management and her own expert knowledge, the consultant pushed the planners to stick to the methodology and to ‘move on’ whenever discussion digressed from the International BPR process.

## 8.7. Loss of voice

In the fourth month of the BPR project, the meetings had turned to discuss the order-to-fulfillment work process. A norm of sticking to the subject had kept the focus of attention on the worksheets and the development of stretch targets. Themes central to the BPR philosophy—most notably, cost-reduction and internal operating efficiency—were kept in focus as organizing themes during discussions and debates:

[Supervisor] When we do the interviews, the interviewees focused on issues and problems. We found obvious improvements in resolving these problems instead of documenting the way they do work now.

[Consultant] We need time, dollar and quantity measures for these. It will take multiple iterations of the interview process to get them … What metric will be used? Our next meeting will analyze the difference between current practice metrics, the AS–IS, and the expected level of accomplishment, the TO-BE …(by next week) try to come up with stretch targets for key areas; the whole point of the interviews and summaries is to quantify.

Paradoxically, discussions about new policies for vendor-managed inventory, electronic commerce, and other quantum leap changes were being shut down by calls to stick to the subject of cost-reduction and process efficiency. As discussion in the meeting turned to increasing customer value and how to identify quantum leap opportunities, this time it was the Vice-President who prompted a return the analysis of the stretch targets:

[Manager, Operations] What criteria are we using to evaluate quantum leaps?

[Manager, Canadian Operations] Quantum leaps come from exceeding in customer value areas.

[Warehousing Manager] What does the customer value?

[Vice-President] We are beating ourselves up for not getting quantum leaps. It’s the incremental “pay me now or pay me later” thing. We have the right steps, but now we need to be moving ahead.

[Consultant] Our job is to identify, analyze and quantify the opportunities, not implement them.

[Manager, Warehousing] How about electronic commerce? Something like, “we shall process all information electronically with all trading partners”?

[Manager, Forecasting] There would be benefits. Improved security, reduced order cost, reduced cycle time, increased customer service levels …

[Manager, Operations] That’s an implementation issue. EDI is always said but drops off the priority list. We need a concerted, strategic effort to push electronic commerce out onto customers.

[Vice-President] What does that mean to us? Will we have to have more training? How much? What will it cost? These are the issues. Let’s focus on the issues.

Following the BPR methodology presented problems for the planners. These proved difficult for the planners to articulate. When it appeared the TO-BE vision disconnected from business sensibilities, participants were prompted not to be concerned and to ‘move on’ through the process.

[Warehousing Supervisor] Pick and pack is our main task. We need prioritization to pick and allocate product.

[Consultant] What’s your stretch target?

[Warehousing Supervisor] No back orders. No back orders for key accounts.

[Forecasting Manager] But only if we have the right data … proper forecasts would lead to no back-orders.

(more discussion about zero back-orders)

[Division Manager, on speakerphone] What’s that? Did I hear you say no backorders? Are you really talking about a zero back-order target? That’s ludicrous. (Inventory holding) costs would skyrocket! A two-percent backorder is a target; hell, we have trouble getting below the five-percent replenishment.

[Consultant] The zero back order number is just a target. It doesn’t have to be realistic, just a target to shoot for. We have many other concerns to address. Can we move on?

Some planners expressed unease in their inability to discuss issues that did not fall directly in the realm of BPR cost effectiveness and efficiency. They were at the same time hesitant to push for changes in the process as dissent and diversion were deemed inappropriate and disruptive.

Some feared challenging the consultant’s expertise in BPR. As the meeting continued, a junior planning participant became agitated about sticking to the stretch target analysis but quickly censored himself in front of the consultant:

[Manager, Order Management] Where is the focus? Shouldn’t we be weighing the pros and cons of reinventing the business, or redefining the existing business?

[Manager, Warehousing] Creating a vision needs to be done collectively. We should have policy agreement on issues such as whether export is included or excluded, or whether backorder will be allocated either equally across customers or for key accounts have priority …

[Manager, Order Management] Well, the activities we define should be reasonable.

[Supervisor, Marketing] But we’re lacking even a half-baked test for reasonableness! We’re looking at the pieces but not at the steps or method … (glancing at the consultant) … but we’re trusting you that it will all work out.

[Consultant] Next group up.

At another point in the meeting, the participant deferred to the consultant’s claim to expert knowledge:

[Supervisor, Marketing] I would like to know about the value and workability of the BPR process … Where is the focus? It seems to me that we should be spending more time on the TO-BE (visioning), … but then, I don’t have fifteen years experience doing BPRs (as the consultant claimed to have).

Many issues of relevance and feasibility were becoming sidelined or eliminated from the discussion. In private discussions and personal interviews, planners increasingly expressed frustration with their inability to communicate what they believed to be important issues in the planning meetings. Surprisingly little of these compounding frustrations, however, were being voiced during the meetings. Sticking to the subject and the focus on metrics and deliverables had led to the emergence of an unspoken taboo on questioning the appropriateness and implementability of the BPR process.

## 8.8. Compounding pressure

As planning entered into the fifth month, planners were becoming increasingly concerned about meeting their scheduled deliverables. Planners were expressing anxiety that the project was moving too fast and that they lacked control. “I feel like a jet pilot,” said one<sup>1</sup>, “We’re flying fast and low. I don’t have the time to think ahead because all of my attention is taken dodging the things right in front of me.”

Despite these complaints the group pressed ahead. The consultant played a key role in this, continually prompting the group to move on whenever the discussion strayed from the BPR program. Discussions that began to raise problematic issues, such as Coast’s liberal product return policy, were quickly curtailed:

[Consultant] What’s the cost of handling (product) returns today? We need to document the process and measure the current costs.

[Manager, Accounting] What about other policies? Minimum order policy, et cetera?

[Manager, Marketing] Returns are a thorn in our side. They result from poor satisfaction … (interrupted)

[Consultant] We need to move on. Stretch targets give us the tasks and activities, focusing on element of time and motion. Who is presenting the next vision statement?

Participants were feeling under considerable pressure not only from the consultant, but also from Coast’s executives to stay on track with International Consulting’s BPR methodology. Apprehension was growing as Coast’s planners felt unable to properly address issues under the pressure to produce. This concern was privately related by the project co-chair during one of the interviews:

[BPR Co-chair] It’s just a matter of you get it done. And (our executives) don’t want to hear that you need to extend deadlines, because they’re working with conflicting deadlines. And all the deadlines have to be met. So it’s not something that people are real receptive to in terms of changing. I think in the BPR, (International Consulting is) probably less receptive to changes in timelines, understandably so, than anybody else.

## 8.9. Calling for repair

Late in the sixth month, as the planning process was drawing toward an end, the BPR Co-chairs called an impromptu meeting of the planning team with the project Vice-President. The consultant was not invited so that planner’s could “air their feelings without reservation” [BPR Co-chair]. The group was concerned about the heavy time requirements coupled with the impending scheduled project completion:

[Supervisor, Customer Service] We’re concerned about the time. This came to a boil last week.

[VP] There is no drop dead date. The “death march to Bataan” is not something that we need to use as a management model for this project. International is an “interactive advisor”, giving advice on how to structure the process, but not mandating the re-engineering model. (The co-chairs) are responsible for driving modifications to the process to International and working with the sub-teams to get consistent, accurate data collection. I sense that (the co-chairs) have not had the time to do that.

[Co-chair] A four-days-a-week time requirement is impossible. There are only seven days a week.

[VP] There is a danger of putting six people in a room for a half-day and envisioning the TO-BE without any detail of the existing business.

[Manager, Operations] Is International the consultants that we should be using?

They complained the methodology gave them too little time for proper analyses and no opportunity to discuss the more substantive quantum leap ideas:

[Supervisor, Customer Service] I would want to spend more time on the TO-BE side. We spend too much time filling out forms and too little time thinking.

[Supervisor 2, Customer Service] Also you’re not getting the information you need.

[VP] There is a process problem. People are pissed off. Let’s get past that and change the process.

[BPR Co-chair] (But) we don’t have time, to really close on one assignment before starting another, before being pushed into another thing.

During this meeting, away from the BPR process and the consultant, planners expressed dissatisfaction with the BPR methodology, specifically about the level of detail expected in the AS–IS analysis and the lack of discussion about the TO-BE scenarios:

[Manager, Warehousing] The interview guide is not useful—just the first sheet. It’s not our job to be going through the (methods binder).

[Supervisor, Pick and Pack] The charts are a waste of time.

[Manager, Order Management] But aren’t the charts the justification … (interrupted)

[Co-chair] … and tool for prioritizing …

[VP] … and the tools for identifying the true areas of improvement.

[Manager, Marketing] The process documentation does exist.

[BPR Co-chair] There is no emphasis on low-hanging fruit.

[VP] (interjecting) We shouldn’t be spending any amount of time on (low hanging fruit).

[Supervisor, Customer Service] We’re documenting every detail and assigning dollar costs for TO-BE justification. We’re getting the AS–IS, but not thinking about areas of improvement.

[VP] AS–IS is not to develop an awareness of the need for change. We shouldn’t go through every miniscule detail—put the low hanging fruit ideas on one overhead.

There was a growing discontent among the planning team members with the perceived limitations of the International Consulting’s methodology. The initial thrust to re-invent customer service was being reinterpreted as “internal operating efficiency for customer service, [not a] radical rethinking about our business” [BPR Co-chair] the planning participants had expected.

[VP] (Operations Manager), you raise the issue that we may be using the wrong consultant.

[Supervisor, Marketing] I’d second that.

[Supervisor, Order Management] I’m not comfortable with the process.

[Manager, Accounting] (going through the method manuals) It’s (the consultant’s) job to fit the method to the current process.

[Supervisor, Customer Service] The (process methodology), as good as they think it is, is inadequate for our function.

Despite discontent expressed by the planners, however, Coast’s executives continued to support the consultant and emphasized sticking to the BPR methodology:

[Supervisor, Marketing] Is getting another consultant realistic?

[BPR Co-chair] No, but we need time to answer questions and gain an understanding of the method we will be using in each meeting.

[Manager, Marketing] Back to the real issue … is International the right consultant firm to use?

[VP] Let me make it clear. It’s this group’s responsibility to make the process. I’m hearing we don’t like International, but the problem... (interrupted)

[Supervisor, Marketing] It’s not International, it’s their method.

[VP] (continuing, harshly) … the problem is in this group.

With one more meeting of the BPR core planning team upcoming before the BPR completion deadline, the planners focused on deliverables. The planners completed the vision statements for each of the functional areas and summarized the changes.

Seven recommendations based on the stretch targets they had identified were agreed upon.

## 8.10. The outcome: seven recommendations

In the seventh month of the project, the Coast planning team presented their recommendations for quantum leap changes to the Executive Board. These were:

1. Single point-of-contact, ‘one-call-does-it-all’ CSR responsibilities: a consolidation of customer and warehouse information for the customer service representatives;

2. Reorganization of Logistics merged with Sales and Marketing;

3. Improved forecasting capabilities: capturing additional data on sales and returns, and using statistical software to forecast warehouse demand;

4. Electronic commerce: using EDI to electronically link Coast with their upstream suppliers;

5. Distribution optimization and warehouse consolidation: replacing the existing product-specific warehouse management with geographic-specific warehousing;

6. Vendor-managed inventories and partnerships with external suppliers;

7. Stricter return policies.

Members of the planning group were clearly frustrated with the final result. After months of planning, the product they were delivering showed little improvement on the original ideas for quantum leaps they had developed at the beginning of the project. Many suggested the focus on collecting job interview data and filling out the worksheets did little to add to their original conceptions of change.

## 8.11. The outcome: the new system

The planning team’s recommendations were to be overshadowed by the announcement of the SAP development plan two weeks later. The I/S group had just finished developing plans for implementation of the SAP system for the finance and human resource divisions. Because the order management BPR plans were based on the premise of an integrated IT platform, Coast’s executives decided the SAP project would take precedence as it would also suit the BPR project’s needs.

To meet demands for ‘ubiquitous, seamless information access’, I/S planned to implement a “vanilla client–server support application” [Director, I/S] to integrate and optimize the workflow. Backed by support from Coast’s executives, the SAP implementation for the finance and human resources divisions was expanded to include the order management process. This new system could be “custom-tailored after implementation” to address local needs [BPR Co-chair].

This was disappointing for many of the BPR planning participants, who felt that Coast had “unique business needs and interrelationships” [BPR Co-chair] that would not be properly addressed by a solution that was “intended for virtually any business” [Supervisor, Marketing].

[Manager, Accounting] In actual use, we won’t be able to get any business done.

[Supervisor, Marketing] Yeah, there are many problems to a vanilla implementation, just because we are “Coast”.

## 8.12. The outcome: the Executive Board decision

Two weeks later the BPR core team received news about the Executive Board’s response to their recommendations. Of the seven recommendations presented to the Executive Board, only the one to combine the Logistics and Sales and Marketing divisions as a single organizational unit was to be implemented.

Even though many at Coast did not consider the BPR initiative a failure, it was not clear to the participants why it had not been a success. The explanation from the Executive Board was that the recommendations focused too much on streamlining existing operations and too little on creating competitive opportunity:

[BPR Co-chair] The steering committee (was) saying this is not strategic enough. This is not quantum leap enough. If you are going to do a BPR, the incremental things you can do along the way—you’re empowered, make them happen. But we would like recommendations on things that are going to put us where we need to be and give us a competitive edge. So they actually sent us back and said, “Umm, beef this up. You need to really do some more strategic type things.”

## 8.13. The outcome: post-hoc rationalizations and multiple interests

Individuals had many differing perspectives on the planning process and its results. The Division Manager of Logistics and Warehousing, whose interests were the consolidation of his logistic group operations under the politically central Sales and Marketing division, saw the process as a success:

[Interviewer] It seemed that going to International for a BPR was a really expensive and elaborate way to get a go-ahead to build an integrated system …

[Division Manager, Logistics] That’s right. And this is an insight into our organization … This is a sales- and marketing-driven organization … They’re not looking at manufacturing as being an … equivalent arm, they’re just a service group that’s got to deliver what we just did this great deal of selling.

[Interviewer] I see. So this, does this BPR basically just give you a good seat at the design table?

[Division Manager] No doubt about it.

Despite dropping out of the BPR planning, the I/S group also gained from the BPR project. By positioning the recommendations from the order management BPR as further justification for the more global SAP project, I/S was able to garner additional resources for the client–server conversion, bring more of the Coast organization under a single IT infrastructure, and avoid the difficulties of being tied to the specific demands of the BPR planning group’s proposals. Most importantly, I/S was able to foreground issues of technical development in converting the mainframe applications to a new integrated infrastructure.

The planning group’s participants were not as optimistic. As the planning had progressed, many had blamed the consultant and the methodology that was used. In post-planning interviews, they also criticized limited senior management commitment to the project.

[BPR Co-chair] My chief concern is … management commitment. We have the right people, the question is if we’ll have them to do it.

[Manager] You didn’t see any exec’s stepping up to the bar.

In another post-planning interview with one of the co-chairs:

[BPR Co-chair] It’s the same type of management, and it’s the same type of thought process that’s running the company, and no one’s taking a risk, and no one’s really doing things differently... It’s the same good-old-boy’s network that has been here forever... We’re just going to keep making the same mistakes, tripping the same way unless somebody steps up and says let’s do it differently. And you don’t see that. You don’t see that from the top down.

For her part, the consultant was able to claim success as the project achieved the contracted deliverables, on-time and in-budget. She dismissed the failure of the executive committee to implement most of the BPR recommendations to a steep learning curve for the planning participants and a lack of their understanding and their poor adherence to the BPR process.

## 9. Case analysis

Whether described as success or failure, Coast’s BPR did not create the anticipated radical changes and breakthrough ideas that initially energized the planning team. Coast’s planners had created a planning process that was conceptually constrained and procedurally limiting, making it impossible for planners to develop true quantum leap recommendations. They recognized this but continued to follow the process. What kept the planners working with such an obviously flawed methodology?

Analysis of the planning process suggests that the problems the planners faced were being shaped by the normative setting of the planning sessions—in the rules that framed the planning process, in rules that defined the appropriate goals and objectives, and those that provided a sense of the publicly acceptable ways to get things done. Shaped by the consultant’s mission to complete deliverables and propelled by the Vice-President’s urging to create radical changes, these rules disciplined the group into a manner of expression and behavior that limited their ability to create real change.

While literal conformance to analyzing performance metrics made it possible for the consultant to deliver concrete change recommendations, it was at the same time limiting the group’s time and ability to investigate alternative change opportunities. Focus on developing the ‘right’ metric concentrated the group’s attention on backorders qua activity optimization, with the adverse effect of preventing consideration of alternative solutions. Radical change opportunities and issues of substantive interest were being under-determined as planners spent their efforts on the objective, quantifiable performance measures for tasks and activities.

Although the planning methodology followed Hammer and Champy’s (1993) BPR central themes, many times the exact meaning and connotation of terms and allusions were slightly varied. The result, in most cases, was principally synonymous with popularized BPR visions but conveyed with interest-laden interpretations.

For example, the consultant’s use of the term ‘process ownership’ suggests that politically loaded terms were being used to realign rules regulating appropriate social behavior. Significant differences between the generic BPR model and Coast’s adaptation of the phrase reflected how dissent was framed as disloyal behavior, how references to expert knowledge of BPR legitimated the sanctions against dissent, and how these particular phrasings communicated rules of acceptable behavior and expression to the group. The differing interpretations of key BPR tenets like process ownership were used at Coast to create a particular conceptualization about BPR that came to shape the discussions and influence the eventual outcomes.

## 9.1. Metaphors and social rules

The metaphors of BPR, like low hanging fruit, quantum leap change and process ownership, enabled planners to make sense of the planning experience and its central organizing concepts (Kendall & Kendall, 1993; Hirschheim & Newman, 1993). The planning group was envisioned by Coast executives to be a ‘self-managing team’, ‘empowered’ to develop ‘radical transformation’ strategies in service and product provisioning. Metaphors were important in shaping the conceptualization of change, the definition of appropriateness, and the imposition of regulatory and sanctioning systems structuring the BPR planning discussions. These metaphors served to define and communicate the social rules that structured the planning process, the roles and actions of the participants, and the appropriate courses of action.

## 9.2. Low hanging fruit

Like ‘process ownership,’ the low hanging fruit metaphor carried a particularly strong significance to the planning group. It bound a sensibility to the planning process, defining the rules of the game for Coast’s planners. It homogenized vision, defined relevance, and inculcated a normative compliance mechanism onto the group. It conveyed the classificatory (‘who are we?’), evaluative (‘what is good?’) and prescriptive rules (‘how should things be done?’) that order behavior, convey perspective, and attribute responsibility.

1. Homogenizing Vision (the formation of classificatory rules). While connoting easy gains, low hanging fruit defined the terms on which the planning process was erected. The low hanging fruit trope impressed values onto the group regarding the appropriateness and legitimacy of discussion topics, creating a conceptual space that demarcated the boundaries of legitimate group discourse. Low hanging fruit created the rules framing the appropriate versus inappropriate ways to look at BPR; the metaphor defined, by separating ‘good’ ideas from peripheral ideas, the conceptual space the planning process was to occupy.

2. Defining Relevant Behavior (the formation of evaluative rules). Tied to themes of time, cost, and quality, low hanging fruit pushed the group to express their ideas in specific terms. Initially a means of defining the project and planning setting, this semantic criterion also acted to constrain the group’s planning expressions and behaviors. By proscribing activities to be considered inappropriate to the group, low hanging fruit defined what roles each of the participants should take on and how they should be conducted.

3. Creating a Norm of Compliance (the formation of prescriptive rules). Low hanging fruit also instilled a norm of tacit compliance to enforce adherence to the BPR methodology and conformance to rules of group discussion. Led by the consultant at first, the planning group as a whole ultimately adopted these self-regulating behaviors as the pressure to produce tangible results increased for everyone. These new prescriptive rules governed and regulated individuals’ public compliance with the consultant methodology and legitimated the consultant’s course of action.

## 9.3. Job holder interview worksheets

The worksheet information (Fig. 2) was the only input about work made available to the planning group. This kept the group focused exclusively on activity-based descriptions of work, defining both the material and important features of the planning process (the classificatory rules) and the goals to be achieved (the prescriptive rules). These new rules selectively omitted aspects of planning that would interfere with the achievement of functional and economic plan deliverables. Skill and resource requirements, work pacing and intensity, and patterns of worker interdependencies, for example, were not collected. Personal experience, opinions, observations, and other input were excluded as the planning focus was kept to the worksheets. Most of the planning discussion revolved around assuring completeness and correctness of the worksheets, refining and validating the measurement metrics, and recording the stretch targets.

The worksheets implicitly prescribed what goals were worth pursuing. Some planners suggested that this singular focus on the task–activity descriptions of existing work limited their ability to construct radical new TO-BE work alternatives. But alternative methods for conceiving of the TO-BE breakthroughs were not raised as the meetings pressed on. The new prescriptive social rules defined the worksheets as the appropriate means to define the project, the roles of the participants, and the acceptable actions to be taken.

## 9.4. Sticking to the subject

Compliance to Coast’s BPR model was motivated both through formal and informal prescriptive rules within the planning sessions. Statements from executives of the goals and objectives of Coast’s re-engineering change effort were constantly in the foreground of the discussions. The unusually high and visible upper level management support for the project raised the ante of implicit penalties and rewards for the BPR participants. The insistence of the Vice-President that “the problem is in this group,” as an example, heightened the risks of raising dissent against the methodology despite the VP’s own encouragement to be “empowered to change the process to meet our needs.” Being part of the high-profile project meant participants had more to gain—and more to lose—in pay, position, status and political opportunity.

Planners were motivated to comply with the BPR methodology as rules prescribing appropriate action created norms of group self-censorship. Sticking to the subject and moving along became local custom, principles that encouraged productive discussion and discouraged non-productive discussion. Dissenters faced sanctions from the group and personal censure as a renegade, a ‘non-team player’ [Consultant].

A cultural taboo of low hanging fruit, backed by the threat of social sanctioning from the other participants checked critical reflection of the on-going planning process. Planners found no voice in the planning sessions with which to express their misgivings about underestimating changes to the legacy systems. Expressions needed to self-correct the misshapen planning process had been eliminated from the planning discourse.

## 9.5. Case summary

Participants raised criticisms of insufficient time and resources, waning management support, and a poor development methodology as reasons for the failure of Coast’s BPR. These, however, are not sufficient to explain the project’s outcome. The participants’ complaints about the lack of time were, in actuality, complaints about the lack of time to discuss issues of relevance that had been cut out from the planning discourse. The lack of adequate resources was, in kind, too many resources narrowly focused on completing and validating the worksheets instead of focusing on breakthrough ideas. And complaints about the lack of executive support were, in fact, calls to repair the broken planning process, not for the lack of executive cheerleading or commitment.

Coast failed to achieve breakthrough changes because they had created a flawed process. Norms of appropriate expression and action had been set up early and not necessarily in the interests of achieving quantum leap improvements. What the BPR did do, however, was to legitimate and give voice to the interests of the Vice-President and Division Manager in consolidating their division under the politically powerful Sales and Marketing group. The project also, as a success for the consultant, demonstrated her capability to become a firm partner. These interests, which could not have been stated directly, were expressed through the results of the BPR project. To do so, the planning process was shaped to steer toward a political end and away from distraction, not by declaration or edict, but by the imposition of rules and norms guiding the process.

Three existing normative structures—carried by business process re-engineering, the consultant, and Coast’s executive management—converged in Coast’s planning sessions, pushing planners to restructure the social rules that coordinated their collective efforts. This critical juncture created uncertainty and ambiguity about how BPR planning would be characterized, what roles would be played out, who would have authority to act, and how alternative approaches would be handled.

Filling this void in normative relations depended upon the mobilization of interpretation (the discourse) and concentrated around centers of existing power, political interest and expert knowledge. The cutting off of the voice of I/S in the BPR process shows how the framing of the problem (“Once we have an integrated IT infrastructure, we can …”) imposed a constraining conceptual trajectory, in effect forfeiting the ability to question the feasibility of computerization. The high level of inference conveyed in this statement becomes clear when contrasted with alternative framings (“If we had an integrated IT infrastructure, we could…”). At Coast, expert knowledge allowed the consultant to frame the process, to quiet dissent, and to push the project along despite reservations from the participants. The adaptation of these rule systems at Coast motivated planners to adopt, and even champion, normative systems that contradicted their interests and threatened the success of their work.

## 10. Conclusions

Within BPR is a codified set of social rules that prescribes values, goals, legitimate action and defines the roles and setting for positive organizational change. Technological and organizational planning is viewed as individuals objectively applying information to make rational choices on behalf of the organization. This rationality is to be publicly held, acknowledged, and produces values and beliefs to be defended. BPR’s cohesive, albeit incomplete, set of rules entices many managers to adopt the BPR methodology as prescriptions for IT planning. In theory, planners are expected to accept and support this perspective faithfully and apply its principles uniformly.

In application, however, planners are often motivated to promote their individual goals, to protect their self-interest, and to minimize personal risk. This often includes expressing such values as expressing personal status, controlling disagreement, demonstrating loyalty, and appearing rational. In so doing, influential individuals act in ways that subordinate public good with private interests and limit dissention (Argyris, 1986).

Regulating the balance between public good and individual interest are norms. Norms regulate the means and expression of collective goals and action. The existing IT literature treats norms either behaviorally, as static factors moderating organizational processes, or sociologically, as the sum of individual interpretations emerging from shared interactions, applied uniformly and equally. Treating norms in these ways, however, risks missing important facets in the conceptualization of organizational technologies. In many important situations, norms disallow individual interpretation and expression, create conflicting modes of rationality, and result in the realization of influential interests.

At Coast we found norms are actively constructed, rather than passively emergent, from actor interactions. When participants divest old norms to engage new situations, social rule systems serve as the basis to stabilize new norms through a process of ambiguity—resolution, role-clarification, application and reinterpretation. Influential participants direct this process by providing interpretation where ambiguity and uncertainty exist and by regulating expression and dispelling dissention. Here metaphors convey and solidify public interpretation, while structured discourse (such as the job holder worksheet at Coast) erects boundaries on discussion and dissent.

Where private goals and organizational good diverge, the new norms that arise impose a rationality that can be both critically distinct from the personal interpretations and private understandings of the social group. In this case, norms disallow expression of an individual’s private beliefs in deference to public standards of polity and appearance. Bound under the interests cum norms of the influential members, participants are obliged to act publicly in accordance with established norms, yet privately harbor disagreement or subversive intent. Thus participants are obliged to act in accordance with the interests of influential members, and in the case of Coast, to act in ways that are counter-productive to the success of the group.

While constraints and parameters are necessary in planning any IT project to ensure direction and forward movement, the existence and importance of the restrictions assumed as norm are often unrecognized and uncontrolled in the IT planning process. This is because the emergence of norms occurs subdiscursively, without debate of the assumptions and conditions that shape the process. Planning IT implementations carries significance for stakeholder interests, yet these interests and their impact on the planning process are often abstracted away in technological models and methodologies.

The Coast case is a story about the construction, the inherent conflict, and the power of norms in IT planning. This study has highlighted the normative impacts of technological and organizational innovations as they exist in IT planning. As technological innovation continues to be increasingly integrated into the planning of organizations, understanding the normative adaptations of new technologies and technological methods is essential. Adding normative analysis to technical, economic, and procedural study approaches holds promise to improve scholarly understanding of organizational technologies and to refine managerial tools for technological and organizational innovation.
