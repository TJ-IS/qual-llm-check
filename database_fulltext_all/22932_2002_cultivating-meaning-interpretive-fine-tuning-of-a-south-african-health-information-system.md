# Cultivating meaning: interpretive fine-tuning of a South African health information system

M.P.A. Thompson

Judge Institute of Management Studies, University of Cambridge, Cambridge CB2 1AG, UK

## Abstract

A recently conducted piece of micro-level, interpretive IS action research has enabled the delivery of direct user benefits through the adoption of a methodological approach which focuses specifically on the interpretive generation of meaning by authorial individuals. Drawing on recent debates concerning knowledge and identity within social anthropology, IS and social psychology, this approach derives from a theoretical position which acknowledges social constraints on individual behaviour whilst according primacy to individuals’ biographically determined interpretive work. Use of this approach in the field revealed layers of interpretive interaction between users and technology which had hitherto remained invisible and problematic, and allowed several simple practical interventions which strengthened the ability of users to generate their own meanings at critical junctures in the system. It is suggested that such sensitive, low-level ‘fine tuning’ represents a new way forward for those seeking a practical, focused alternative to the more commonplace, high-level research approach within current interpretive IS literature.  2002 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowing; Interpretation; Interpretive space; Meaning; Identity; Information systems; Emerg ent; People-centred; Action research

## 1. Introduction

This paper describes a piece of action research which made use of an ethnographic style of interpretive enquiry to improve the alignment of a health information system to the requirements of its users. The research was motivated, first, by a perceived lack of in-depth IS research to date conducted at the level of the individual (Walsham, 2001), and second, by a desire to ascertain whether an ethnographic style of IS research might profitably be used together with a heavily practical interventionist focus (Senn, 1998), both areas which are currently underserved within the IS literature. The resulting methodological thrust thus was set at the level of detailed observation, and generated a view of the health information system, located within the Cape region of South Africa, as a systemically interlinked series of interpretive transactions between people, and between people and various other components of the IS, tempered at every point by a complex web of social relations.

The approach adopted in this study is similar in certain respects to Engestrom’s (1987) model of a socially-distributed activity system in its emphasis on the emergent and systemic relationship between participants’ varying conceptions of the information system. However, this paper proposes an important difference in nuance from Engestrom’s model, where social roles and rules actually mediate between the knowing of the individual and wider social structures. Instead, this author will argue in some detail for a conception of knowing as the product of individuals’ constant generation of self-identity, as whole selves, achieved through their continual interpretive work. This, people-focused, interpretive process is thus viewed as being of paramount importance for the IS researcher in understanding and intervening in the operation of any, necessarily complex, emergent interlinking between people and the wider information systems of which people form a part.

The paper seeks to make two key contributions, one theoretical and the other practical. The contribution of the theoretical section is a new focus on individuals’ continual generation of meaning within information systems. In this section I propose that the generation of emergent meaning is located at the conceptual level of knowing, authorial individuals, rather than reflexive social actors, after which I undertake a discussion of the interpretive process of knowing itself. Finally, I make a distinction between different types of interpretive activity, introducing the concept of ‘interpretive space’, which forms the basis for the empirical case work which follows. The practical contribution of the paper is an attempt to show that these theoretical ideas can be usefully applied to ‘fine tune’ the interaction between human and nonhuman components of an IS, so that this interaction—and the wider IS itself—is perceived by users as more meaningful. It is suggested that this mix of interpretive, ethnographic-style research and pragmatic intervention represents an unusual but productive approach within the IS canon.

In the next section, the theoretical and methodological underpinnings and emphasis of the research are described in some detail, since it is argued that these constitute a new type of emphasis within IS. This is followed by a more narrative description of the case material, of the theory-driven interventions carried out by the author, and the results to date of these interventions. Finally, there is a discussion and some conclusions concerning the implications of this style of research for both academic and practitioner communities.

## 2. Subjectivity shapes objectivity

## 2.1. From end-states to processes

There appears to be increasing recognition within the organizational literature that emergent, relational processes—human-technology interlinkages viewed as praxis rather than end-states—may constitute the most rewarding foci for organizational research (Bradbury & Lichtenstein, 2000, provide a useful review). This has been echoed in some of the more recent work on ‘knowledge management’ within the IS field, where emerging conceptions of knowledge as ‘the residue of thinking’ (McDermott, 1999, p.105), and an emphasis on knowing (for example, Blackler, 1995; Tsoukas, 1996) have emphasized the need to adopt a process-focused view of the organizational husbandry of knowledge. Attempts to build ‘knowledge management systems’ have presented the starkest challenge yet to determinist, technologyled systems implementation practice, since knowledge conceived as a bankable object has proved difficult to capture, generating deeper questions concerning the social and psychological dynamics of knowledge generation.

Within the IS field, an important result of all this attention has been a heightened awareness that the objective, inanimate symbolic forms in which IS trade all require human thought in order to ‘exist’ in any useful sense of the word. This has raised questions concerning the manner in which people think, and, by doing so, conjure these forms into conscious ‘existence’. For example, in a discussion of cognition in artificial intelligence, Clark (1997) proposes that linear, representationalist models of thought should be abandoned in favour of a view of the mind as a ‘leaky organ’, embodied and always relational to, and intertwined with, physiology and the outside world. It is generally accepted across several disciplines, for example in sociology (Giddens, 1991), social anthropology (Cohen, 1994; Rapport, 1993), and cognitive psychology (Bateson, 1972; Holland, 1985) that much of this ‘being-in-the-world’ derives from an ongoing interpretive process, in which the magic ingredient accounting for people’s differing understandings, responses and often behaviour, is a fund of experientially derived and individually experienced tacit context. Moreover, these and other writers, particularly from within social anthropology (see Holland, Lachicotte, Skinner, & Cain, 2001) have argued in various ways that it is at the microlevel of individual interaction that interpretive transactions occur and meaning is assigned, each time drawing upon and altering a person’s fund of tacit context, and constituting the process that is ‘being’ itself—literally self-actualising:

“Meaning is not built up through descriptions of external reality, nor does it consist in semiotic codes ordered independently of our encounters with that reality. Rather, ‘what cannot be put into words’—interchanges with persons and objects on the level of daily practice—forms the necessary condition of what can be said and of the meanings involved in practical consciousness. To know the meaning of words is thus to be able to use them as an integral part of the routine enactment of everyday life. We come to know reality not from perceiving it as it is, but as a result of the differences formed in daily practice” (Giddens, 1991, p.42–43).

The above passage forms part of Giddens’ treatment of the continual work performed by individuals in generating self-identity in the face of the uprooting demands of modern existence. This is important for the matter at hand in two ways. First, Giddens is underlining an already existing poststructuralist argument that meaning— the result of this process of interpretation and the critical difference between soughtafter ‘knowledge’ and mere ‘data’—is assigned by people, to symbols, at the everyday level of intersubjective interaction. Second, Giddens also contends that meaning is intimately bound up with the ongoing generation of individual self-identity as the fundamental process from which social life is constituted, with the individual person as the fundamental unit. Below, I seek to make use of these thoughtful insights as a foundation for constructing an analysis of the interpretive process of knowing— although later I will differ from Giddens concerning the implications of his position for the way in which this actually occurs.

## 2.2. From the collective to individuals

Although recent work on knowledge generation—particularly within the organizational behaviour literature—has progressed far in identifying “practice as both our production of the world and the result of this process” (Gherardi, 2000, p. 215), it is arguable that Giddens’ position represents a better foundation from which to address the generation of meaning within IS. This is because, in correctly identifying praxis as interactive and therefore innately social, some writers have subsequently been tempted to locate knowledge in the collective, attributing communitarian properties to a group entity whose dynamics remain resolutely subjective and personal—even, as in the case of Yanow (2000, p. 250), arguing for “conferring ontological status on the collectivity”. Such emphases lack analytical granularity, since their equation of organizations with cultures as the proposed unit of study cannot approach the individual level at which meaning is assigned. Rather, Giddens’ structurational model accepts that it is individuals that think, and not groups.

Machines, of course, cannot think either, in the practically conscious, self-actualising sense of the above passage by Giddens. Perhaps it is a similar unease, therefore, which motivates those who question the application of approaches such as Actor-Network Theory (ANT) which accord ‘general symmetry’ to the human and nonhuman elements of an IS in the face of the “continued and deep asymmetries between person and machine”, or the evidence that “humans and artifacts do not appear to constitute each other in the same way” (Suchman, 1999). The unique and pivotal role of humans within sociotechnical interaction has been underlined in various forms, including emphasis on humans’ “strategic role in knitting the network” (Rammert, 1999), the “differing capacities to initiate, to react and to reflect upon a situation” (Rammert, 1997), the uniqueness of human intentionality (Pickering, 1993), or the human role in “knowing the object” in object-centred sociality (Knorr Cetina, 1997).

It has been noted thus far that, as humans, many of us feel more convinced by accounts of human/machine behaviour which acknowledge the interpretive interactive work of individual human beings, rather than that of the collective, or of machines, as the fundamental dynamic of any information system. As a subscriber to this view, I offer below a theoretical approach which acknowledges the emergent and social nature of interpretation, but which focuses squarely on the emergent generation of meaning within IS through individuals’ interpretive work. The remainder of this theoretical section explains this focus, building on Giddens’ conception of emergent meaning, which is located at the level of the individual and always saturated in social context. However, I go further than Giddens’ model of the reflexive actor, conceiving individuals rather as constructive authors in assigning meaning to their shared environment. This is explained in From Actors to Authors below. In From Roles to Selves, I develop this position, arguing that in approaching this process of interpretation by authorial selves, the researcher should thus direct much more attention to the concept of identity, which is of at least as much importance as social roles. Finally, in Unpacking Interpretation, I address the remaining question of the interaction between the two halves—social context and peoples’ identities, or ‘tacit’ context—within the focal process of knowing.

## 2.3. From actors to authors

In Self-consciousness: An alternative anthropology of identity, the anthropologist Cohen (1994) outlines why much social theory tends to privilege the social (focusing on actors, roles, reproduction of social structures) rather than the individual (individually experienced meanings, identity, or ‘tacit’ context). He argues that many current sociological theories are a legacy of a Durkheimian essential contradiction between the individual and society, where individualism is regarded as a failure of social integration, resulting in anomie/suicide. Cohen argues that this opposition can be traced through subsequent social theoretical development, where individuals are only viewed as important as social roles/actors, as structures in themselves, or as related to structure in some identifiable way. This tradition includes structural functionalists such as Levi-Strauss, Barthes, semiologists, and symbolic interactionists such as Mead through to Goffman. Even hermeneuts such as Geertz are included: Cohen cites Geertz’ (1973) famous description of the Balinese cockfight, which represents the sort of equilibrium model—as, arguably, does Engestrom’s socially-distributed activity system—from which he is advocating that researchers should now distance themselves.

Instead, Cohen proposes a shift of emphasis to self-actualising ‘authorial selves’, where “Instead of seeing the member as being drawn by the organization out of his or her self, we would see the organization as being incorporated into the individual’s social world” (Cohen, 1994, p. 99), an inversion of the more common approach to organizational behaviour. This view is consistent with the emphasis on the knowing person proposed thus far. How does this happen? Nigel Rapport, one-time student of Cohen, adopts Bateson’s dictum that things are the epiphenomena of relations between them—i.e. that we define things by construing their boundary relations of sameness and difference to other things (as in Giddens’ comments above). Symbolic interaction is thus a process whereby individuals communicate symbolic forms to each other as vehicles to which they can attach their own significances/meanings, so as to attain ontological security/self actualisation and avoid ‘anomie’. This can only be attained via communication, since it is only through this process that form and meaning come together for the individual, and differences from pre-existent context are thus generated. The discussion returns to this important concept of difference later, in the sub-section entitled Unpacking Interpretation.

The implications of this position are that any shared symbol divorced from content does not meaningfully exist, since such animation is only possible via individuals’ ascription of such content in the form of meaning: “Moreover, it is because individual contents and cultural forms constitute one social reality that neither can be properly or ideally described in the absence of the other…it is their meeting in opposition which is socially constitutive” (Rapport, 1993, p.164–165). Although this position resembles Giddens’ structurational duality, it differs from Giddens in according a primary function to identity in determining what an individual finds meaningful. Instead of conceiving the composition of this ‘meeting in opposition’ in terms of the quasi-structural modalities of ‘interpretive schemes’, ‘facilities’ or ‘norms’ (Giddens, 1984), it is proposed that the interpretive process of knowing wherein social context and ‘tacit’ context (identity) interact comprises much more tacit context than such socially determined modalities would seem to allow. Such an interaction surely appears more authorial than reflexive; this point is developed below.

## 2.4. From roles to selves

Consider, for example, the difficulties with approaching the concept of knowing encountered by social realism, which, alongside structuration, can probably be considered the other major current theory of emergent sociality. By insisting that “relationships do have [sui generis] independent causal properties and, moreover, that such relationships, once established, are analytically prior to the subsequent rulefollowing behaviour of actors” (Porpora, 1989, in Archer, 1998, p. 201, original italics), social realists’ search for actors, rules and roles entails obvious dangers:

“Functionalist social science gave us the word ‘actor’ to use for individual. It went with ‘role’. But roles are inventions, the performance of fictions, which are supposed to mask the actors who play them, to conceal them from us as persons with other identities. We have developed a social science which, consistent with these metaphors, depicts the individual as a performing self. We cannot see the actors, because in anthropology we say that they are methodologically out of reach. ...So we settle for the script, which we invent ourselves through the ingenious use of categories.” (Cohen, 1994, p. 180).

In other words, such analysis may be falling for the seductive explanatory approach of structuralism, and merely identifying meaningless forms—an Emperor’s New Clothes of the analyst’s unwitting invention. Individuals inhabit multiple social roles, but these are all actualized—often simultaneously—through the biographical, experiential interpretive work of a single, continuous identity. To focus on any one particular role would be like looking at a cardboard cutout—a temporary mask worn by an individual for reasons infinitely more complex and interlinked than those suggested by any one researcher-observed performance. This is not to say that social life can be reduced to mere intersubjectivity; Cohen acknowledges that group behaviour can have a transformative effect on individual behaviour, but questions whether this loss of self-control denotes a loss of self. Additionally, it has been argued (Holland, 1985) that certain shared entities or movements can be granted some form of ‘otherness’—not as pure objects with independent meanings in themselves, but in the sense that there is general social agreement as to the kind of discourse they are and the kind of constraints that determine meaning within such discourses. Texts are social productions, and therefore at least partially independent of the individual. Whilst individuals are free to flout such conventions, in practice they are likely to pay a social price for doing so.

To summarise thus far, this author shares with some others a dissatisfaction with seemingly functionalist approaches to IS which encourage a focus on end-states rather than processes, on reflexive actors rather than strategic authors, on roles rather than complex, ‘whole’ selves, and on form rather than content, and which are thus unable to articulate with emergent meanings as they are generated in the field. The response in this paper has been to build on Giddens’ insights concerning the selfactualising enactment of everyday life, but also to go further than Giddens, stressing the pivotal importance of tacit context as the motor of the interpretive process of knowing. The result is a view of knowing which is socially situated yet individually authorial and identity-driven, which contrasts with Giddens’ more attenuated view of the reflexive social actor, who performs social roles within the social space afforded by modalities which, on close examination, appear more ‘structural’ than ‘individual’.

The approach developed in this paper focuses on individuals’ exchange of commonly shared symbols as the motor with which to generate meaning and purpose, and thus not only explains what happens but, importantly, also why. The model attempts to be methodologically clear about what it is that should be studied, since the location of this process at individual level precludes any focus on actors, roles, equilibria, ontologically identifiable collectives, or other functionalist labels as tropes which are unable to articulate with the generation of such meanings. Instead, it is argued that to approach the interlinkages between humans and wider IS with any clarity of focus, the IS researcher should locate him or herself down at the level of individuals’ generation of meaning.

Having, it is hoped, established several good reasons for adopting a consciously people-centred approach towards IS research, it will probably be necessary to increase the magnification still further in order finally to convince the reader of the title of Section 2, namely that ‘subjectivity shapes objectivity’. It is an explanation of what may constitute the interpretive process itself—the motor of self-actualisation—that will, it is hoped, finally confirm the usefulness of such a consciously people-oriented focus. The final part of this theoretical discussion suggests just what may be occurring when authorial individuals ‘interpret’, and develops this to distinguish between different levels of interpretive activity. This distinction, supported by the low-level approach to meaning outlined earlier, then forms the basis for the empirical work described in the next section.

## 2.5. Unpacking interpretation

The ‘feedback’ explanation of the interpretive process as proposed by Gregory Bateson has proved useful and consistent with the approach set out in this paper, since it provides a detailed explanation of the dynamics of interpretation, whilst retaining a people-centred approach to explaining the ecology of systemic information ‘circuits’. In Steps to an Ecology of Mind, Bateson (1972) portrays the process which has been referred to throughout this paper as knowing as a chain of metabolically powered events in which informational difference travels round a complete circuit, returning, altered, to the place from which it first started:

“Consider a man felling a tree with an axe. Each stroke of the axe is modified or corrected, according to the shape of the cut face of the tree left by the previous stroke. This self-corrective (i.e. mental) process is brought about by a total system, tree-eyes-brain-muscles-axe-stroke-tree…more correctly, we should spell the matter out as (differences in tree)-(differences in retina)-(differences in brain)- (differences in muscles)-(differences in movement of axe)-(differences in tree), etc. What is transmitted around the circuit is transforms of differences. And…a difference which makes a difference is an idea or unit of information” (Bateson, 1972, p. 317–319, original italics).

Such processes are clearly distinguishable from events in the physical world, which are seen instead as the result of cause-and-effect energy transfer. By contrast, “the negative character of cybernetic explanation” (p. 408) allows the differentiation of these difference-related, internal processes from physical events in that the former are negative: i.e. based on a person’s perception of difference from a pre-existent, ‘tacit’, mental context. Thus, “without context, there is no communication”(p. 408). Bateson thus emphasises the need to view interpretation as embedded within a complex circuit of events, which may encompass systemic factors external to the individual. Importantly, however, he nonetheless retains a distinction between the nature of internal (thought) and external (wider-system) mechanisms of ‘IS’ transmission; although embedded within circuits, people remain different from machines.

In current parlance, therefore, the position advanced in this paper is that the selfactualising process of knowing involves peoples’ formation of difference from preexistent, tacit mental context, in order to generate meaning. This position is consistent with the argument thus far. In the first place, it was proposed that there is a need to devote more attention within the field of IS to the generation of meaning within information systems. Second, I have argued that meaning is generated through people’s continually emergent need to self-actualise (as in the contributions of Giddens, Cohen, and Rapport discussed earlier; see also Holland et al., 2001, for a thorough ethnographic treatment of the link between identity and meaning). Third, I have suggested that tacit context plays a vital role in animating this self-actualising process, or knowing, and that this has implications for Giddens’ concept of the reflexive actor, namely that people are more authorial and identity-driven in their interactions than Giddens’ view of actors’ reflexive performance of roles would seem to allow. Fourth, I have sought to further increase the magnification by unpacking the process of knowing itself, which, following Bateson, I have defined as the metabolically powered formation of difference from pre-existent, tacit mental context.

It remains to develop this view of knowing one step further, to allow distinctions between different types of interpretation, since it is this ability to distinguish between higher and lower levels of individuals’ assignation of meaning to shared forms within information systems which forms the primary tool of the case analysis presented in the next section. Holland (1985) has developed Bateson’s feedback into the following model, which has been adapted for purposes of this discussion.

This model differs from Bateson’s example in that, rather than consisting of one circuit, there is in fact a hierarchy of sub-circuits within the mechanism of knowing, each of which refers to a different level of difference (and therefore differently generated layer of meaning). Fig. 1 shows a circuit of knowing, which in this case includes one hierarchical sub-circuit. These sub-circuits, or successive comparisons of difference, can be seen as the motor driving the knowing process itself. Thus, as shown in the diagram, it is impossible to know or become consciously aware of a stimulus at all, without drawing upon tacit context, which may include a habitual way of seeing something, or even physiological, embodied characteristics, as recently underlined by Mingers in relation to the work of Merleau-Ponty (Mingers, 2001).

In Fig. 1, a stimulus is thus only ‘perceived’ at Comparison 1 via its conjunction with certain invoked pre-existent physical and tacit characteristics (Standard 1) of the perceiver. Therefore, although historically pre-existent, such characteristics, or ‘tacit context’, can only ‘exist’ consciously as part of a situated—and, therefore, unique—combination with a stimulus, as part of the process of knowing. This combination, or ‘meaning’, can then be passed onwards to other circuits of comparison as required (Comparison 2), becoming the stimulus for which more tacit context (Standard 2) is invoked. These subsequent circuits can be called ‘higher’ in that they operate at progressively higher levels of symbolic abstraction, invoking more and more tacit context, and hence increasing the amounts of subjectivity introduced into the circuit.

The important implication of this step-by-step unpacking of the interpretive process is that peoples’ ‘self actualisation’—the continual generation of meaning for themselves via knowing—occurs through biologically driven comparisons of difference (see also Maturana & Varela, 1980) which rest in turn upon subjectively determined comparators (their tacit context). Moreover as we have seen, it is possible to distinguish between those forms of interaction which invoke only relatively lowlevel interpretive circuits (such as engrained habits, or simple numerical operations), and other, ‘higher’ forms of interaction which require the invocation of higher circuits of tacit context in order to generate meaning for action. I propose the term ‘interpretive space’ to describe this distinction between lower and higher levels of interpretive activity, and have used this term to structure the research interventions outlined in the following section.

Within an IS context, interpretive space can be conceived as the varying opportunity or encouragement given to individuals within the design of an IS to perform such comparisons of difference. The concept has its roots in the following statement by Jones (1998), which I have found influential in identifying the problematic addressed in this paper:

“…interpretation is central to the process of information systems design, implementation, and use. Material agency influences, but does not determine, the opportunity space for these interpretations. Rather, interpretations depend on emergent, situated social/social social/technical interactions, which will be influenced by preceding context. Since the context and conditions for action will vary between individuals, interpretations are also idiosyncratic”(Jones, 1998, p. 298, my italics).

In performing the interventions based on the concept of interpretive space, the researcher focused on ‘whole’ individuals’ biographical interaction with their tacit context, or identity (as opposed to the enactment of social roles), to identify junctures where those more complex higher level comparisons of difference needed to be invoked. Where this was the case, was this process adequately recognized and supported by the IS? If not—and this had become apparent through the generation of information that no-one found meaningful—how could this be improved? By contrast, where higher level comparisons of difference were not required since there was consensus amongst users about the ‘straightforwardness’ of the transactions being performed, how could the need for people to invoke higher interpretive circuits (introducing greater levels of subjectivity into the data) be reduced?

To summarise, the theoretical section has established a variant of the activity approach which centres on the emergent process of knowing, which is located firmly within ‘whole’, authorial identities and their interplay with their systemic environmental context. As stated in the title of this section, subjectivity does, indeed, shape objectivity, in that all conscious, ‘objective’ perception is revealed as a process comprising subjective, as well as objective, components. Having argued that knowing should form the focus of any low-level action research, it was then necessary to unpack this process to offer an account of how people interpret and assign meaning to shared symbols. The resulting conception of interpretation as peoples’ invocation of progressive comparisons of difference in relation to their own tacit context allowed the development of the practical concept of interpretive space.

Interpretive space distinguishes between two types of interpretation (although they are perhaps best conceived as opposite poles in a continuum). In some cases, it is argued that people need to supply a high level of subjective input (invocation of higher circuits of comparison with tacit context) to generate the meaning that they require to interact as people with the outside world (or IS) of which they form a part. In such instances, the IS must provide sufficient allowance and support—or space—for such interpretation to occur, or risk being perceived as literally ‘meaningless’ and peripheral to everyday activity. In other cases, there may be certain transactions for which an agreed routine exists which require less explanatory escalation within the minds of users (e.g. numeric operations) where an IS should be fairly transparent or risk being perceived by users as a time-wasting irritant. In the latter case, the need for such interpretive work should be contracted—a reduction of the interpretive space.

The following section describes the empirical use in the field to which the conceptual tools and approach outlined here have been applied. As a researcher with a background in IS consultancy undertaking action research, I have adopted a deliberately practical, indeed, ‘realist’ tone for the empirical section, which differs from the more eclectic, theory-building approach used thus far. My aim in following this ‘two papers in one’ structure is to underscore the pragmatic, action orientation to which theory-grounded interpretive IS research, so often conducted at the high level of post-implementation review, can usefully be directed.

## 3. Health information systems project

## 3.1. Background and focus of research

Initiated in 1995, the Health Information Systems Project (HISP) is currently a collaborative venture between University of the Western Cape (UWC), which has traditionally focused on issues concerning the disadvantaged in South Africa, University of Oslo, supported by the Norwegian government through NORAD, and health administrations within the South African government. Based in Cape Town, HISP consists of a set of integrated data collection and reporting applications written in MS-Access, based on the Microsoft Office product set, with MS Access forming the ‘back end’ data repository, MS Excel allowing the manipulation of indicators using pivot tables, and MS Word and Powerpoint forming the reporting ‘front end’. The objectives of HISP are to roll out an action-led district based health management information system which allows health staff in local clinics to take control of, and benefit from, their own health information. Basic health data tracked includes total clinic attendees, prescriptions issued, TB treatments, diarrhoea cases, and various indicators relating to maternal and child health, sexually transmitted diseases (including HIV), etc. This low-level, ‘empowerment’ approach is distinguishable from the legacy of centralized, top-down health information systems within South Africa, and resonates with the Government’s current emphasis on promoting initiatives in primary health care (Braa, 1997; Braa & Hedberg, 2000).

Although HISP has met with some opposition from other stakeholders within the health information system field who maintain reservations about the workability of its devolved structure, the author spoke to a wide selection of informants from across South Africa who considered that the project had been generally successful to date in rolling out the system from a densely installed base in its ‘home’ province of Western Cape to other provinces across South Africa. HISP is also currently being implemented in Mozambique and has received enquiries from several other so-called less developed countries (LDCs). Notable amongst its successes is the development of the first ever unified health information dataset on the African continent, establishing the basis for state-wide comparisons of health indicators.

In spite of the overall success of HISP to date, the City of Cape Town Health Department, an important HISP partner and stakeholder for health information within Cape Province, had identified an important remaining obstacle to the successful deployment of the health information system in some of the most disadvantaged areas within the City of Cape Town region (South Africa is organised into nine provinces at the highest level, followed by regions and then districts). This obstacle might be summarised as the continuing inability of certain of the most disadvantaged clinics to collect and use health data which they found meaningful, resulting in the submission of inaccurate, often meaningless data to the Department—often relating to just those geographical areas where such information is needed most. This perception—specifically, that data was often perceived as meaningless—had also emerged from studies conducted by both UWC and University of Oslo (Braa, Heywood, & Shung King, 1997; Rendall-Mkosi, 1999). This need to address the problem of meaning generation in the clinics, acknowledged by all HISP’s institutional stakeholders, thus presented a suitable field environment within which to test the low-level, meaning-oriented research approach outlined in detail in the preceding section of this paper.

Following a three-week period of exposure to HISP stakeholders from across South Africa, the author therefore agreed to work intensively in partnership with the Health Department of Cape Town Administration, City of Cape Town over a further period of seven weeks. This work consisted first of obtaining a high-level understanding of the reporting process across several clinics, in both prosperous and disadvantaged, ‘township’, areas: in all, twelve clinics were visited. Next, the author focused in on Langa and Nyanga, two of the most disadvantaged ‘township’ clinics (where the problem was most marked), observing at length the detailed, low-level interaction between clinic staff, their information systems and their wider contexts, before redesigning and initiating the pilots of certain adjusted elements of these systems. It should be noted that no staff from either the Health Department or from HISP had to date been in a position to conduct any such low-level analysis of these interactions, although all agreed that such a study was overdue.

## 3.2. Research methodology

An action research methodology, where the “independent variable is constituted by the actions of the researcher” (Gill & Johnson, 1997, p. 60) was selected for several reasons. First, an explicit aim of the research was to demonstrate that a highly interpretive, theory-driven approach can generate insights that may profitably be applied to improve the alignment of information systems to the requirements of their users. Second, the practical value of the concept of interpretive space, derived through theoretical argument, could only be assessed through its application to a userdefined problematic environment, which was then monitored for feedback. Third, it is argued that the complex social events under scrutiny would have remained invisible to alternative instruments such as surveys, questionnaires, interviews, or even non-participant observation, since these required a comparison of pre- and post-intervention observations. Moreover, under these circumstances, it was my view that an open acknowledgement of my involvement in the ‘double hermeneutic’ (Giddens, 1984) would be the most appropriate form of engagement. Fourth, a genuine aim of the research was to provide some practical benefit to the research hosts, who would have benefited little from another high-level interpretive review or purely theoretical paper.

The action research approach adopted resembles the dual research cycle proposed by Mackay and Marshall (2001), in which the research interest remains outside, although highly interactive with, the problem solving interest. Thus reflection about, and refinement of, the concept of interpretive space, together with the other theoretical ideas presented in this paper, occurred before, during, and after the duration of the field engagement. McKay and Marshall use this factor to distinguish between ‘true’ action research and other interventionist approaches such as Checkland’s (1981) soft systems methodology, prototyping (Alavi, 1984), and other, primarily problem-solving, methodologies.

Data collection thus occurred alongside and throughout the period of field involvement, and took the primary form of longhand notes, supplemented by detailed, extensive analysis of both the software-based and manual aspects of the health IS. Interactions took the form of ethnographic-style observation, meetings and interviews with a wide variety of stakeholders (over 30 clinic staff and managers from around the country, 3 City of Cape Town Health Department representatives, 2 administrative staff, 3 HISP members, and 2 academics at UWC). Although conversational exchanges of particular interest (such as those which appear later) were documented in full, the main medium was a set of detailed analytical notes, added to and revised on a daily basis. Although ethnographic in style, the research differed from ‘pure’ ethnography in that the focus was not to document and explain the meanings attributed by people to their interactions, but was instead to observe and map the junctures within HIS at which interpretive activity was clearly occurring, and to use these observations to explain and address those points where the data transmitted was inaccurate or meaningless.

Data analysis was conducted with many of these stakeholders (on the problemsolving track) and by the researcher (research interest track). In the case of the latter, extensive notes were analysed and separated into first-order concepts (empirical observations) and second-order concepts (hypotheses), and second-order concepts reworked and developed throughout the period of analysis (Hammersley & Atkinson, 1995). Where conversations with stakeholders concerned data analysis (i.e. where stakeholders were also actively involved in discussion of some of the issues presented here), these were included as second-order concepts along with my own hypotheses. As certain key hypotheses (second-order concepts) emerged from the data, these were fed back into the action research interventions and thus affected the first-order concepts that were subsequently recorded.

## 3.3. Findings of observation stage

The perceived loss of meaning within the health information system (HIS), identified by both data gatherers and data users, suggested that HIS might be a suitable environment in which to assess the practical use of the conceptual tool of interpretive space developed in the previous section. However, in order for this to occur, it was necessary first to identify those points within the IS which required human interpretive input—i.e. to generate a conceptual view of the system expressed in terms of users’ generation of meaning. Accordingly, nine key points were identified at which meaning was always transformed within HIS, viewed as a cybernetic chain of transformations: these are summarised at Fig. 2. By this, I mean that either the data itself or its intelligibility to the next user was observed to alter or become lost at each point, as elaborated below. It should be noted that this map, created as a result of the theoretical approach outlined earlier in the paper, is almost unrecogniseable from HISP’s own high-level application architecture diagram. In particular, the map identifies several major interpretive junctures which lie outside the software components of the system (which is enclosed in the diagram by a dotted line), and which thus do not appear on HISP’s diagram at all.

The mechanism for capturing observed symptoms/dispensed treatments ‘on the ground’ was a series of weekly tally sheets, where clinical staff (doctors and nurses) ticked check boxes or ‘tallies’ to record each interaction with patients, which they then added together and placed in a totals column, at the end of the week. Tally sheets were organised according to data type. At the end of the week, all tally sheets were gathered up by an orderly and presented to a more senior nurse, typically the deputy to the Sister-In-Charge (Doctors travel around clinics as required, and fall outside the internal hierarchy of clinic staff), who entered their totals onto a ‘master sheet known as the Routine Monthly Report (RMR). At the month end, all these weekly totals were in turn added together, usually by the same senior nurse, to obtain a monthly figure for each data. Only then were these monthly figures entered onto the HISP software—often by the Sister-In-Charge herself. Following data entry, which entailed a mandatory software-driven data validation procedure, in which staff were asked to evaluate the ‘reasonableness’ of the data in relation to previous months’ statistics, the clinic or ‘health facility’ passed on its aggregated monthly data to the district level, after which it was passed upwards in turn to the provincial and national levels. It was observed that each of these junctures required considerable human interpretive input, which involved individuals’ evaluation of the ‘meaning’ of data at that particular juncture in the system. This interpretive work in turn affected the nature of the data transformation which occurred during transmission to the next juncture.

During the detailed observation at Langa and Nyanga, the research focused on the four interpretive junctures marked with a star in Fig. 2. The reason for this was that these were the most accessible to the researcher as a non-clinician based at health facility level; moreover, as the bedrock of HIS, the tally sheets, and the way these are validated upon entry to the HIS software, constitute the actual interface between daily reality in each clinic and the version of this reality which is captured and reported at district, regional, provincial and national levels; hence they involve the most interpretive input from people. The key observed problems relating to the operation of each of these four interpretive junctures within HIS are now summarised in turn. This is followed by a short, second-order interpretive account of some of the underlying reasons for these problems, viewed from the perspective of the research approach advanced in this paper, before a discussion of the interventions which were based on this theoretical interpretation.

## 3.3.1. Approximation of intersubjective reality to tally categories (completion of tally sheets)

As mentioned earlier, Tally sheets were split up functionally according to the clinical area of the nurse collecting the data (examples included ‘TB’, ‘Women’s & Reproductive Health’, and ‘Child Health’). In practice, this meant that, although in reality health staff were interacting with whole people with developed identities, where symptoms were often interrelated, HIS required the separation of these into ‘slices’ of people, or functional ‘roles’ (‘TB patient’, ‘child for weighing’, etc). This meant that those nurses who dealt with ‘Child Health’, for example, were not encouraged to make any systemic linkages—which they would have been professionally capable of performing—between the few categories which appeared on their tally sheets and any other aspect of the patient: for example, the child’s weight, which may have been dropping in relation to another, linked, symptom (the most renowned linkage in the Cape Peninsula area was that between HIV and TB, for example, which can also result in weight loss). Divorced of their context, the small data segments to which nurses had access were often viewed as meaningless, with the result that several nurses submitted incomplete data, duplicate data which overlapped with other categories to which they were denied access, or just invented the data altogether: in the words of Sister Udemans, Sister-In-Charge at Nyanga, “people have just been submitting rubbish”. The link between staff motivation and perceived meaning in this context had already been recognized by one of the District Information Officers (the two individuals tasked with supporting the submission by large numbers of clinics of health data to the Health Department within Cape Town Administration, usually termed DIOs), who explained her approach:

“We explain and sit down with/talk to our people, so that they understand all the data and can do it themselves…the Epidemiologist (has) started coming and talking to the clinic and area managers, and saying: ‘this is what we do with the data’”.

The parallels of this situation with the discussion earlier in the paper of the loss of meaning attendant on any separation of knowing individuals into actors, rules and roles is striking—in particular, my comment about how a focus on any one particular role would be like looking at a cardboard cutout. As knowing individuals themselves, clinic staff were assigning meaning in their interactions with patients on the basis of the relational organization of phenomena around other situated, knowing individuals. In turn, staff looking at this data later on were attributing meaning to the numbers on the same basis. Staff therefore felt they were balkanizing a situation with relational meaning into meaningless categories; in turn, other staff later confronted with the data were often unable to assign their own meaning to these forms. Note that the meaning itself is not transmitted and ‘reconstituted’ by the receiver; however, data that is recorded in a fashion that one person finds meaningful is more likely to be perceived as meaningful by another.

This situation was exacerbated by the clear observational evidence, supported by other studies (for example, Werbner & Ranger, 1996) that in affective, close-knit communities such as those at Langa and Nyanga, both staff and patients were often already deeply embedded in complex, cross-cutting social relationships, to which that of ‘staff’ and ‘patient’—let alone ‘staff-segment’ and ‘patient-segment’—was secondary. Much of the important social context to the meaning generated in staff/patient consultations was thus bypassed by the functional fractions which appeared on the tally sheets—the first ‘rung’ of the HIS ladder.

## 3.3.2. Aggregation of tally sheets to RMR

There are in fact three interpretive transactions within this juncture, which have been placed together for ease of description. Having been recorded in the manner described above, data within each category was first totalled at the end of the week by a designated member of staff, and the totals entered in the totals column on the tally sheet. From here, totals were then aggregated—again, at the end of the week— into the weekly column of the RMR. Finally, at the month end, all weekly figures are added on the RMR to derive the monthly total. Once captured, the system should have been ensuring that any transformations of data which occurred were restricted merely to the additions demanded by these simple operations.

However, instead, the non-uniform layouts of tally sheets, each of which in turn dealt only with components of a patient, meant that it was extremely difficult to identify the same data field across tally sheets during the addition process. Individuals tasked with such additions were therefore compelled to interpret what they saw and to form their own conclusions about the original intentions of the staff member who had completed the sheet, often based on their knowledge of this individual as a person. This was evident in the way in which the individual’s impression of the author’s identity would affect the way they completed missing fields, in the sense that that person would surely have ‘meant’ to have done. This was often difficult, since, in the words of one participant: “As soon as it leaves the clinic, half the meaning (of the data) is lost and it becomes just numbers”. Emergent, narrative (Boland, 2001), and necessarily subjective meanings were being assigned to what at first glance appeared to be ‘unproblematic’ numbers. Rather than simple translations and transformations of data, therefore, the aggregation of tally totals on the RMR often consisted of a complex dance of artifactual design, data, subjective human narrative based on a judgement of the identity/original intentions of the author, and environmental conditions, the total figure being affected by the particular relational combination of these factors at any one time.

The results were often extreme: when the author was given the opportunity to examine a completed pile of tally sheets, surmounted by the completed RMR, serious discrepancies emerged. For example, on one tally sheet, 326 patients under 5 years of age had been ticked; the number entered in the totals column was 216, and the total number transferred to the RMR was 378. For the ‘Patients Over 5 Years’ category, a total of 107 on the tally sheet had become 1295 on the RMR. For ‘Babies Examined up to and Including 6 Weeks’, the figure recorded on the tally sheets was 0 (a very improbable figure), and 29 (much more probable) on the RMR—indicating a retrofitted approximation on the part of the senior nurse performing the aggregation. Twelve other such serious errors were recorded in this pile of tally sheets alone.

## 3.3.3. Entering of RMR data onto HIS software

Staff members exhibited markedly different levels of skill and experience in interaction with PCs, let alone the HIS software. At one end of this extreme variation in PC literacy were the few who were able to enter data from the RMR into the system unaided by the DIOs. At the other end, individuals required support mouseclick by mouseclick, field by field, without which a breakdown in dialogue (Suchman, 1987) regularly occurred. In the words of one DIO, when introduced to the PC, “some were terrified of computers—they were even scared to touch the mouse—we had to tell them it wouldn’t bite!” Due to the monthly spacing of the data entry process—which generally lasted about one hour—individuals lacked the opportunity to practise on the computer and develop more experience. At one of the clinics studied, Nyanga, there was no PC at all, and the Sister-In-Charge had to travel to her DIO’s office in order to enter the data on the DIO’s PC.

## 3.3.4. Validation and alteration of data

Before storing data which had been typed into a data entry field, the HIS software performed a validation function against maximum and minimum parameters defined as ‘acceptable’ by the previous user. Should the entered data fall outside these parameters (which, due in part to the previously discussed problems, was sometimes over 50% of all entries), a warning flag would appear and the user invited either to reset the ‘acceptable’ parameters or to re-enter a different figure. An audit trail of previous entries was then available, together with a regression figure for these, to assist in the required interpretive judgement.

As in the case of aggregating tally sheet totals onto the RMR, a complex, relational interplay of factors governed this interpretive process triggered by outlying data (itself often the result of the problems discussed). In the first instance, the user had to ask themselves for the likely reason for the return of ‘exceptionally’ high or low figures, which was often a difficult task since there was no audit trail leading back to the individual who had initially completed the tally sheet. Divorced from the immediate context of its capture, the data had thus often become meaningless, and it was therefore difficult for another person to ascertain its validity. Comments such as “Ah, now, let me see...what can this be due to?” were common in these situations, as staff constructed ongoing narratives (as we saw in the aggregation of tally sheets) to attribute meaning to abstracted, hence ‘meaningless’, phenomena. Such interpretive work had to be performed alongside a lack of faith in the accuracy of the data, so that if no immediate explanation lay within reach, the figures were often assumed to be inaccurate.

Additionally, before a decision could be made regarding the most appropriate change to the data (or parameters), there was common concern with the possible material consequences for the clinic of superiors’ future interpretation of the data, again divorced from the explanatory context (and meaning) in which it had been entered. This concern applied in particular to overall figures for clinic attendance, nurse consultation rates, and any campaigns running at the time, for example condom distribution. One particular exchange provided a good example of this ‘informating’ (visibility-increasing) effect:

Sister-In-Charge: “All my data is really accurate from this month onwards, as I’ve started a clean slate. So can’t we just wipe the previous month’s data?” (only half-joking).

DIO: “Nooooo, sister, unfortunately that’s why the system is here. That’s what it does”.

Sister-In-Charge: “But what about my bad doctor? He’s been fiddling the system and seeing 70 patients in 2 hours, then leaving! Do I enter this onto HIS?” DIO: “Yessss, sweetie, you do.”

Sister-In-Charge: “But the last time there was a proper survey of our data, they came and took away our nurses and doctor time, so what are you people doing with the data?”

The relational dance at this juncture therefore involved previous interpretations (stored as ‘acceptable’ parameters), the regression provided by the software, lack of faith in data accuracy, loss of meaning ‘upstream’, and the imagined consequences of possible loss of meaning ‘downstream’. To these was added the tacit interpretive judgement of the individual required to mediate between these factors, as well as the ‘policing’ influence of the DIO.

## 3.4. Interpretive account of underlying reasons for problems

I argue that the people-focused, meaning-oriented interpretive approach outlined earlier in this paper allowed these issues to become visible in the first place, and also that it offers a consistent explanation for them. Clinic staff were being prevented from understanding, trusting and using their own data—from benefitting fully from the explicitly emancipatory agenda of HISP. This was because HIS’s predominantly data/software-driven focus had taken insufficient account of those junctures of the system—the completion of tally sheets, and validation and alteration of data—which required a high level of interpretive input from users. Data about people, collected by people, to be interpreted by people, was being split up along functional lines so that its relational, contextual qualities were being obscured. As a result, both the people transmitting and receiving the data felt that meaning was being filtered out of the process. However, in order to perform the interpretive work required of them at these junctures, users needed such relational context in order to invoke the higher interpretive circuits within which the data would acquire meaning; there was, quite literally, less to interpret. Thus the IS was presenting users with insufficient raw material support—functional ‘slices’ of people—to perform their interpretive work with any completeness, or consistency. The result was a loss of meaning for all concerned. In short, several important interpretive junctures within the IS had gone unrecognized and hence were unsupported by the IS in the way that was required to generate meaning for its users.

By contrast, at another juncture in the system—the aggregation of tally sheets onto the RMR—the inconsistency (varying layouts), incompleteness (‘parts’ of patients), and anonymity (no available audit trail or ownership) of data had resulted in much more interpretive work becoming necessary to make sense of the data than otherwise would have been required. Rather than forming a routine—a lower-level circuit within the mind of the interpreter—the need to guess at, correct or complete data had invoked higher-level circuits of interpretation. These higher circuits necessarily involved ever more subjective components, as more of the interpreter’s tacit context or ‘whole’ identity was brought into play to generate the differences that constitute knowing, as discussed in the theoretical section of this paper. A good empirical example of this is the scenario given earlier of staff who were faced with having to add incomplete or inconsistent tally sheets using their subjective understanding/opinion of the person completing the sheet to make inferences concerning data accuracy. Although such inferences are arguably present to some degree within any information system, they are usually less desirable than the accurate transmission of the intentions of the originator of the data. Interpersonal judgements— personality judging personality—are much more highly subjective events than the routine addition of numbers to pre-established rules.

To summarise, the theoretically derived observations and analysis generated a hitherto unrecognized view of the health IS as consisting of a series of interpretive transformations, some of which supported too little interpretive activity on the part of users, (incorporated too little interpretive space), and some of which encouraged too much (incorporated too much interpretive space). In contrast to tally sheet completion and data validation/alteration, where it has been argued that insufficient materials were being provided to people in their recording and use of data to generate the level of meaning required for the system to function effectively, the process of aggregating tally sheets onto the RMR was providing too much of a reason for people to invoke unnecessary levels of interpretive process; they were assigning irrelevant levels of meaning to a ‘lower-level’ function which should have remained purely clerical. It is argued that this provides an explanation for the problems being experienced by City of Cape Town Health Department, to which the field research had been initially directed. Within the same theoretical paradigm, the interventions which presented themselves therefore demanded, first, a recognition by users of the heavily interpretive nature of the IS, and second, a ‘fine tuning’ of the IS to support the optimal balance of interpretive space. These interventions are described below.

## 3.5. Interventions and results to date of pilots

This subsection summarises the interventions carried out to the tally sheets on the basis of the analysis outlined above, which were then piloted in the two largest of the most disadvantaged clinics, Langa and Nyanga, both of whose data had generally been submitted latest each month, in a more incomplete/inaccurate condition than data from the other facilities. As in the preceding subsection, I address each of the four interpretive junctures in turn. This is followed by a summary of the results to date of the pilots.

3.5.1. Approximation of intersubjective reality to tally categories (completion of tally sheets)

The objective of intervention within this juncture was to increase the amount and relational quality of information, and hence the opportunity to generate meaning—the interpretive space—available to staff members busy approximating intersubjectively shared reality to a series of ticked boxes on tally sheets. By redesigning tally sheets so that staff had access to all of the information about a ‘whole patient’, rather than various, isolated clinical ‘roles’ to which a patient had been assigned, the relationships (or differences) between data items became visible, and staff were observed to find the tally sheets (and hence the activity associated with their completion) to be more meaningful. For example, during training sessions for the redesigned tally sheet, staff who had previously regarded the information system as ‘meaningless and an administrative waste of time became more enthusiastic as they began to see the relationships between data items emerge for the first time. The Sister-In-Charge explained that patients may fall under several data categories simultaneously, so that a newborn baby might require ticks in the categories ‘Child Under 5 Years Weighed’, ‘Baby Examined First Time Before 6 Weeks’, and ‘Children Under 5 Seen For Prevention’ as a bare minimum, in addition to any additional categories related to any possible clinical condition. As they were being told “if you tick this, then you must also tick that”, this new, relational understanding was generating meaning for nurses, who could now see the point of the system, and several people expressed a new desire at the end of the training session to ensure that they completed their tally sheets properly.

By contrast to these ‘whole’ patient tally sheets, those data items which related rather to locations within the clinic (such as total headcount, prescriptions dispensed, and TB treatments given) were moved to the reverse of the sheet. All such data items were high-volume and difficult to capture at speed; a typical scenario might be the need to record a large group of patients entering the clinic at the same time, whilst also greeting them (as ‘whole’ person to ‘whole’ person within a shared social environment) and issuing them with instructions. To facilitate the capture of such data items within these difficult circumstances, a series of hand-held tally counters, such as those commonly used to count stock, were issued to those locations of the clinic where these data items were recorded. In this way, data recording no longer interfered with the person-to-person interaction which formed a wider and essential part of the transaction of clinical business. Accuracy was thus improved.

## 3.5.2. Aggregation of tally sheets to RMR

Whereas the purpose of intervention in the previous juncture was to increase the available interpretive space so as to assist people to generate meaningful data, the purpose of intervention at this juncture was to reduce this space wherever possible. This is not to say that the intention was to restrict human interpretation, since in the light of the theoretical discussion of this paper, this is clearly impossible. Rather, the intention was to lessen peoples’ need to invoke higher circuits of interpretation and, by doing so, introduce progressively more subjective components into the data. What was required was therefore to alter the system so that it became more transparent during routine aggregation tasks; a decrease of the interpretive space. The look and feel of tally sheets between clinics was thus standardised, so that staff moving between clinics were able to develop a shared symbolic vocabulary, reducing their need to ‘interpret’ forms. The arrangement of tally rows was altered to make addition simpler and quicker, and the now uniform positioning of all totals boxes eliminated the need to spread tally sheets over several tables to try and identify corresponding (or duplicated) data.

In addition, the redesigned tally sheets were issued on a personal basis to each staff member, who was required to ‘own’ his or her data. In addition to creating the additional incentive for staff to understand and complete the tally sheets that might be expected by any organizational observer, this personalisation also created an audit trail back to the person who completed the sheet, so that explanatory contextual information, relationally organised, could be sought by others interpreting data values at later junctures in the system. This further reduced their need to invoke higher circuits of interpretation, since the original context of a set of data, together with the intentions of its author, had become more freely available.

## 3.5.3. Entering of RMR data onto HIS software

No specific interventions could be achieved within this juncture, since improvements in PC literacy required sustained exposure to the data entry activity on a more frequent basis than had hitherto been the case. However, the author confirmed the view already held by the key stakeholder within the Health Department of City of Cape Town of the critical importance of his two DIOs in sustaining tenuous humancomputer dialogues, and of the urgent necessity to provide them with more support in the regular training of health staff in PC literacy.

## 3.5.4. Validation and alteration of data

As in the completion of tally sheets, the objective of intervention in this juncture was to increase the ability of people faced with outlying data which had been captured by someone else, to interpret that data so as to be able to take a decision. Improvements here were effected by previously described interventions, which, by improving data accuracy, had reduced the amount of outlying data, and, through creating an audit trail, had preserved a link between a data value and its original context, should this be required. However, little could be done by the author about peoples’ concerns regarding potential ‘downstream’ interpretation of their data by others, and this is likely to remain an issue affecting the accurate transmission of data to management level without attention from City of Cape Town Health Department.

## 3.5.5. Results to date of the pilots

At time of writing, the redesigned tally sheets and hand-held tally counters have been in operation for nine months. Feedback has taken several forms. First, conversations were held during the initial pilot stage with all the major stakeholders, as well as several clinic staff. The two DIOs were very enthusiastic about the redesigned materials and had high expectations for their success, particularly in the possibility of reductions in their workload owing to improved accuracy of collected data. The epidemiologist at City of Cape Town Health Department, the major ‘consumer’ of aggregated data, cautiously welcomed the innovations but wanted to reserve his judgement until the pilots had been operational for several months, when any successes might more easily be measured in terms of improved accuracy of collected data. The two HISP staff with whom discussions were held during the pilot were enthusiastic that the piloted alterations were the result of field-based observations, which they agreed had been long overdue, and also agreed broadly with the principles of increasing clinic staff’s understanding of the data by increasing the visibility of the interrelationships between data items. Like the epidemiologist, however, their judgement would be reserved until a sustained improvement in data accuracy had been recorded. Additionally, nine conversations were held with clinic staff, using open-ended questions such as “how do you feel about these new materials?”, thus making greater allowance for the possibility of negative as well as positive comments. However, although staff also tended to want to reserve final judgement until the pilot had been in operation for several months, initial impressions were that the redesigned tally sheets were easier to use, and that it was helpful to be able to see all the data collected together on a single sheet. Several staff commented that they now found it easier to relate to and understand the data they were being asked to collect, the routine aggregation tasks were being performed with less errors, and that the tally counters had proved popular. In particular, the Sister-In-Charge at the other clinic has seen the interventions as successful. An initial exception was one of the Sisters-In-Charge, who was initially skeptical about the possible value of the redesigned tally sheets, but who has since come to support the initiatives, since her clinic moved in the first month of the pilot from being the last in her district to submit data, which was often inaccurate, to being the first, with no major inaccuracies reported by her DIO.

The second form of feedback occurred during observation of the aggregation of data categories from the redesigned tally sheets onto the RMR at Langa clinic. Having observed this procedure in operation the previous month at Langa and Nyanga using the ‘old’ materials, it was readily visible that this important task had become quicker to perform and less chaotic. The senior nurse routinely assigned to this task agreed that corresponding data items were now easier to locate; queries were now resolvable by consulting the source of the data (thus eliminating the need to introduce subjective ‘guessing’); it appeared that less glaring anomalies in recorded data were being submitted (indicating greater accuracy); and the process was completed in about 60 min, as opposed to between 90 and 180 min previously.

The third form of feedback has taken the form of sustained telephone, email and letter communications with several stakeholders subsequent to my return from the field. Three such communications will be highlighted here. First, following the third month of the pilot, the City of Cape Town Health Department epidemiologist, who is responsible for the operation of HIS throughout the Cape Town region, and who processes and interprets data at regional level, wrote that “quality of data seems to have improved for both Langa and Nyanga”. Second, following the eighth month of the pilot, the chief software engineer within HISP, responsible for the overall architecture of the application, commented to the author on the telephone that the redesigned materials appeared to have been successful. Third, nine months after the initiation of the pilot, one of the DIOs has just written to the author that the redesigned materials have now been rolled out across additional clinics and are now operational across the entire Cape Peninsula area. This indicates that the pilot was considered a success and that the desired sustained improvements in data accuracy have been achieved.

## 4. Implications and conclusion

## 4.1. Practical implications

The practical contribution of this paper is an attempt to place a concern with users’ generation of meaning squarely at the centre of a practical interpretive analysis of an information system. It is argued that the generation of meaning lies at the intersection between people and the wider information systems of which they form a part, and that in consequence, an in-depth analysis of the mechanism whereby individuals generate meaning within an IS context has been long overdue. Moreover, it is arguable that a useful index of the value of an IS investment should be the level of required generated meaning that it is able to support—since a ‘meaningless’ IS has little value for anyone. This can be defined as the extent to which the IS is able to support individuals’ interpretive input where such input—the invocation of peoples’ higher-level interpretive circuits—is inevitable or necessary. This is as opposed to those junctures where such higher-level input is unnecessary or even misleading, as shown in the detailed examples discussed in this paper. Put simply, to be perceived as valuable, an IS should be ‘balanced’ so as to incorporate enough interpretive space where required.

It is of course acknowledged that defining where interpretive work is more, or less, required within an IS may be subject to contention, due to the inevitably contested relations of power within any reasonably complex information system. However, it should be emphasised that the approach described in this paper does not attempt to evaluate qualitatively between the aims of competing stakeholders, but rather distinguishes between, on the one hand, highly numeric or routine transactions for which there is a highly developed set of shared expectations, and, on the other, those transactions for which a high degree of subjective interpretive input can be identified by the low-level observer, in the way outlined in the empirical section of this paper.

The major practical implication of this position is that an analysis of the type that has been outlined in this paper should form an important component of the system design process. At the highest level, such a procedure would involve, first, mapping the interpretive chain of an IS in a similar manner to that shown at Fig. 2. Second, the analyst should distinguish between those points which require high levels of subjective interpretive input, make these explicit, and ensure that people are supported by the IS—given adequate interpretive space—to generate the required levels of meaning. Third, are there any points where people are currently required (by their own estimation) to ‘over problematise’ something which should be relatively simple, even routine? And should this function not be automated in any case?

Although the empirical material presented in this paper was pitched at a low level, and the system under scrutiny was relatively simple, it is argued that the underlying principle established—that the function of IS should be to support the generation of meaning by self-actualising individuals—remains applicable to IS of any complexity. Moreover, it is arguable that however ‘black boxed’ and complex the intervening processing between two user interfaces (of which there was very little in the example used here), the complexity of meaning generation at any one human-computer interface is likely to vary only to a limited extent (human, physiologically-embedded characteristics are less infinitely elastic than those of computers) and that the ‘fine tuning’ outlined in this paper is likely therefore to remain a way of improving performance at (reasonably similar) human-computer interfaces within more complex IS. This approach contrasts with more traditional, ‘waterfall’ approaches to systems development, where functionalist abstractions of user roles are mapped out in the form of ‘business processes’, whose streamlining or replication within a new IS remains the object of focus for systems designers. Whilst such mapping is likely to remain a core component of any large systems integration project, what is being proposed is a complementary, relatively simple method of understanding and finetuning the ability of the system to support its users’ meaning generation activity at the human-computer interface—a critical measure of value of any IS investment. Whilst the importance of ‘user-friendliness’ is a well-recognized feature of most IS, it is argued that the interpretive fine-tuning exercise proposed here, and the theoretical grounding which explains how this may best be accomplished, represents a new practical contribution to the IS field.

The concept of interpretive space has broad applicability wherever people interact within the standardizing routines of wider information systems. Consider the implications for Orlikowski’s (1993) well-known study of the reaction of employees within two organizations to the introduction of CASE tools. In passages which imply a loss of meaning for those she studied, Orlikowski comments that “some…felt less personally involved in their work”, and notes the observation of a senior analyst that using tools amounted to a “form-filling exercise”, concluding:

“The theoretical framework generated from the empirical findings suggests that the intentions and actions of key players… as well as the social context into which tools are implemented, critically influence what changes are associated with CASE tools. This proposes a different approach…one that takes into account the interaction over time of intentions, context, process and action around the technology” (p. 337).

It is argued that many of Orlikowski’s (1993) observations are explicable through an analysis of meaning such as that undertaken in the first part of this paper, which unpacks intentions and actions within the context of the emergent, interpretive activity with which people generate meaning. Moreover, it is possible that the interpretive analysis of the type demonstrated here represents a practical, low-level application of the approach that she calls for. One might conclude that an analysis of interpretive space would form a useful precursor to the introduction of any form of procedural standardization such as those associated with the introduction of ‘best practice’, since it would allow for greater accuracy in targeting those activities which would benefit from a reduction in interpretive space, as opposed to those where support of such space should remain, or even be augmented.

## 4.2. Theoretical and methodological implications

In addition to these practical implications, it is argued that the theoretical contribution of this paper is a new interdisciplinary, detailed analytical framework which is complementary to other, more high-level, and usually retrospective, interpretive studies. The value of this contribution lies in its focus on the interpretive work of ‘whole’ people as the subject of analysis, which enables the researcher to engage at last with emergent relationships of knowing, and thus to begin properly to address the generation of meaning within IS with some clarity. That this should be an important aim—and one which requires the adoption of a new, granular approach to interpretive processes—has been highlighted in particular by the often confused terminology within the recent literature dealing with the shortcomings of attempts to ‘manage knowledge’ via IS.

Indeed, it is argued that an understanding of the interpretive process of knowing as developed in this paper makes the concept of ‘knowledge management’ appear clumsy, and ill-defined. By emphasizing the pivotal importance of meaning to the realization of value from IS investments, its generation from the relationally organized activity of knowing, and suggesting a practical way of generating increased meaning from information systems, it is hoped that this paper has made a small contribution to the literature dealing with the generation and husbandry of organizational knowledge.

It is hoped also that the research described in this paper is clearly locatable within the broad discussion within the discipline concerning the ‘Cartesian split’ between knower and known, subject and object, ‘agency’ and ‘structure’, which arguably represents the most fundamental problem with which the social sciences must contend. Within the IS field, this has manifested itself in the ‘answerability’ of the existing debates between the poles of technological and social determinism, to which any IS researcher must respond in terms of locating their work. A good review of some of the most important positions adopted along this continuum is provided in Jones (1998). Against this background, the activity-based approach adopted in this paper seeks to build on Pickering’s (1995) ‘middle-road’ insights regarding the temporal co-emergence of human and material agency, yet differs from Pickering’s conclusion that the two are so ‘mangled’ together in practice as to become inseparable. Although, like Pickering, I have sought to distinguish clearly between the type of work performed by humans and machines within an IS, according primary importance to human intention, I have also shown that it is possible to alter the prior, structural properties of an IS to influence the interaction of these factors in practice. This approach rests on the continuing separability of the two throughout the interactive process, a separability which is arguably needed for any action research, which requires an ability to distinguish between different variables. Although space precludes a full discussion here, I thus hope to have shown that an emergent ontology can be combined with a methodology which continues to distinguish between the two elements of a dualism, giving “due recognition to both the technological and social elements of information systems, while acknowledging their differences” (Jones, 1998, p. 299).

Finally, there is an interesting methodological implication associated with the application of these concepts at the level described in this paper. This is the ability of the low-level interpretive researcher to perform interventions as a result of his or her research findings, where both findings and interventions have been generated as a result of the explicit application of theory. Whilst it is not argued that this creates an opportunity to empirically ‘test’ interpretive theory (such a view would run counter to the theoretical foundation of this paper, which highlights the subjectivity of human experience, based on the generation of meaning through difference), it is certainly possible that limited interventions may offer the interpretive researcher opportunities to clarify or shape his or her own emerging hypotheses about the nature of IS. As such, approaches of this type should present the opportunity to develop both a new style of interpretive IS research focusing on users’ emergent generation of meaning, and to enable the interpretive IS researcher to make a new type of contribution to practice in the form of theory-driven intervention.
