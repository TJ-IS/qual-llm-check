# An investigation of the effects of decisional guidance and cognitive ability on decisionmaking involving uncertainty data

Lois S. Mahoney ∗, Pamela B. Roush, Dale Bandy

School of Accounting, University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL 32816-1400, USA

## Abstract

In their roles as users and providers of uncertainty data, decision-makers are confronted with choosing among a plethora of data formats offered by the decision support systems on most personal computers, but are offered little in the way of decisional guidance for choosing the most effective display format. This research investigated when and why decisional guidance should be provided. We found that in tasks involving uncertainty data, the decisionmakers were more accurate and responded faster when symbolic tasks were matched with tabular displays and spatial tasks were matched with graphical displays. We also found that when subjects were provided with both matched and unmatched display formats, field independent individuals were more accurate for both spatial and symbolic tasks. When decisional guidance restricted the display formats to match the task types, field dependency did not significantly affect performance. This suggests that cognitive ability or field dependency is not a factor when the mental representation formulated is consistent with the problem representation.  2002 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decisional guidance; Uncertainty; Cognitive fit; Display format

## 1. Introduction

As information technology evolves, decision-makers are challenged to use uncertainty data. Financial and managerial decisions are often based on information that is dependent on a variety of rules, estimates and conventions. Traditionally, decisionmakers have presented this information as discrete data point estimates when in reality the underlying measurement process is probabilistic<sup>1</sup>. Various professional groups have questioned the usefulness of discrete numbers for decisions involving uncertainty in financial data<sup>2</sup>.

The integration of risk into problem solving has created a demand for information presented in a way that helps decision-makers to effectively incorporate uncertainty into their judgments. The availability of software applications that run Monte Carlo simulations on desktop computers allows more decision-makers to generate and use uncertainty data. Types of decisions benefiting from the use of uncertainty data include cost–risk analysis, capital budgeting, risk-based financing estimates, optimal project plan decisions, new venture risk assessment, and option pricing. Although the technology exists to create and use uncertainty data, there has been little study of questions such as whether alternative presentations of uncertainty data can improve the accuracy and time of decision-making or whether cognitive ability and data presentation affect performance. The answers to such questions can guide systems designers and perhaps lead to improved decisions.

Decision-makers must choose a data format for uncertainty information from among the options provided by the computer’s decision support system (DSS). Decision-makers must determine which data format will lead to the best decisions. This is especially true in today’s business environment where the use of powerful desktop computers and software applications has shifted control of display choices from trained specialists to mainstream users. The risks associated with end-users controlling displays is exacerbated by the proliferation of display formats from which to choose and the lack of decisional guidance provided to prescribe when, where, or why to use particular displays (Wilson & Zigurs, 1999). As current software makes the generation of probability information relatively easy, business decisionmakers may benefit from decisional guidance as they work to gain the expertise needed to effectively utilize the statistical concepts associated with probability information.

Decisional guidance, either deliberately or inadvertently provided by the DSS, is the manner in which a DSS guides users as they choose among and use the system’s functional capabilities (Silver, 1991). Silver views decisional guidance provided by the system as a meta-support because it supports more effective use of the DSS, which itself supports more effective decision-making. Deliberate decisional guidance, where intelligence or expertise is embedded in the system, advises or directs the user to the best use of the DSS.

Decisional guidance research should lead to an understanding of the consequences of guidance, which in turn contributes to understanding how DSSs affect decisionmaking (Silver, 1991). We focus on the consequences of embedded deliberate decisional guidance to explain how the DSS affects the decision process of professional decision-makers solving problems involving uncertainty data. We follow Silver’s (1991) three step approach to address behavioral questions concerning decisional guidance for uncertainty data: (1) when and why system designers should provide decisional guidance, considering the opportunities, motives, and means for guiding; (2) how designers can provide guidance by using a three-dimensional typology for deliberate guidance; and (3) the consequences of decisional guidance— its effects and reasons for its effectiveness.

Using an embedded single guidance mechanism based on the theory of cognitive fit (matching task type with appropriate data display), we investigate the effectiveness of theory-based decisional guidance for decision-making involving uncertainty data. Simultaneously, by incorporating the Group Embedded Figures Test (GEFT) to represent cognitive ability, we investigate the behavioral consequences of this type of guidance on the performance of professional decision-makers with varying cognitive abilities.

As noted, probability software can generate output in a variety of formats including tables and graphs. The distinctions between decisional guidance and system restrictiveness are outlined by Silver (1990). System restrictiveness is the degree to which and the manner in which a DSS limits the user’s decision-making processes to a subset of all possible processes. Decisional guidance is the degree to which and the manner in which a DSS guides users in constructing and executing decision-making processes, by assisting them in choosing and using its operators. If a single display format is best for decisions made utilizing probability information, it follows that the system should restrict users to that single display format. On the other hand, if performance varies between tasks, decisional guidance could help users choose the appropriate data display for the task at hand. Cognitive fit theory suggests that the tabular presentations of data should benefit users performing symbolic tasks, and that graphical displays should benefit users performing spatial tasks. If alternative display formats can produce better decisions for different tasks, it is important that a system not restrict options in such a way as to prevent users from availing themselves of display formats suited for the specific tasks they perform. Instead users should be guided to display options that suit the task at hand. As a result, it is important to determine whether certain tasks can be better performed utilizing alternative data formats, and whether the theory of cognitive fit can be used to guide users to the data formats that better suit the tasks they are performing.

While prior studies examined the effects of matching task with display type based on the theory of cognitive fit, we extend the research in four ways. First, we examine decisions involving uncertainty data to determine whether the theory applies to such data. Previous research has not addressed this question. Second, we present two measurements of effectiveness, accuracy and time, which allow us to assess time/accuracy trade-off effects as suggested by Luce (1986). Prior studies relied on manual tracking of time and accuracy by multiple researchers or assistants (Frownfelter-Lohrke, 1998; Vessey & Galletta, 1991). In our study time was automatically tracked by the computer program. Third, we consider whether the benefit from decisional guidance based on the theory of cognitive fit varies with cognitive ability. That is, do field dependent individuals benefit more from decisional guidance that is based on the cognitive fit theory? We use one type of skill, cognitive ability, in a multivariate test to assess both its main and interactive effects with task and display type on performance. We then test whether performance is improved for field dependents when theory based decisional guidance is applied for display type. Fourth, we use business professionals rather than students for subjects in order to better test the business applications of such systems.

We found that embedded decisional guidance based on the theory of cognitive fit does significantly improve decision-making by increasing both accuracy and time when the task and display are appropriately matched. We found a marginal significant trade-off effect between time and accuracy when task and data presentation are mismatched. In addition, we found that cognitive ability significantly influenced performance. Field independent individuals outperformed field dependent individuals. However, when theory-based decisional guidance was applied, guidance used to direct the user’s choice of display format, we found no significant difference between the performance of field independents and field dependents. Thus our study provides evidence that decisional guidance may make up for a lack of display choice expertise.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: in Section 2 the opportunity, motive, and means for decisional guidance is presented. In Section 3 we discuss the methodology used in the study. Next, Section 4 presents the results. The final section is the discussion and conclusion based on the findings.

## 2. Opportunity, motive, and means for decisional guidance

## 2.1. Opportunity for decisional guidance

When users need to make discretionary judgments, there is an opportunity for decisional guidance to help the user to choose the appropriate data display from among the available outputs of the DSS (Silver, 1991). Current support for most DSSs is more “mechanical” than “decisional” because business software offers a plethora of data display choices without providing guidance on how to choose the appropriate display for a particular task. Thus there is an opportunity to provide decisional guidance for DSSs involving uncertainty data because: (1) multiple data display choices were offered by the DSSs; and (2) the choice of data display can affect performance. The theory of cognitive fit suggests that the selection of DSS data display format, such as graphs or tables, can affect the performance of the decision-maker.

The theory of cognitive fit explains the relationships between display format and performance. The theory of cognitive fit proposes that a specific skill has its greatest effect on performance when the problem representation and the task emphasize the same type of information needed for the skill (Vessey & Galletta, 1991). Performance is enhanced because the mental representation formulated is consistent with the problem representation, task and skills. Performance declines when the problem representation and task do not match, because the decision-maker must compensate by using less than optimal problem solving processes.

Mismatching the problem representation and the task can lead to one of two results. An unmatched problem representation may cause the decision-maker to develop an inappropriate mental representation that must be transformed before a correct solution can be found. As a result of the transformation, the decision process slows and becomes more error prone. Alternatively, the decision-maker may base the mental representation on the task (Umanath & Vessey, 1994; Vessey, 1991). In this case, the data must be transformed into a mental representation suitable for the task solution. In both situations there is the potential for a decline in performance.

In applying the theory of cognitive fit to display formats, Vessey (1991) and Vessey and Galletta (1991) divided tasks into spatial and symbolic categories. Spatial tasks involve an assessment of the problem as a whole and require making associations, perceiving relationships and the interpolation of values. Symbolic tasks involve extracting discrete data values and are better accomplished using analytical processes. Asking a user of financial data that includes reported profits for several years whether profits are increasing or decreasing is a spatial task, as it requires comparing prior and current profits. On the other hand, asking the user to determine the current profit is a symbolic task, as the user is required to extract a specific value. The theory of cognitive fit suggests that spatial tasks are better supported with graphical display format while symbolic tasks are better supported with tabular display format.

If the theory of cognitive fit holds true for spatial or symbolic tasks involving uncertainty data, there is an opportunity for the DSS to guide users who must choose between display forms.

## 2.2. Motive for decisional guidance

Having demonstrated an opportunity, we next address the motive for providing decisional guidance for uncertainty decision-making. Silver (1990, 1991) suggests two sets of issues that affect the decision to provide or not provide decisional guidance: concerns about supporting decision-makers and concerns about influencing them. For each concern the need for decisional guidance is a function of both the system and its users. Decisional guidance may support users as they interact with the system and confront its complexities. Decisional guidance helps users choose among and interact with a system’s capabilities. Usually more complex DSSs and less structured tasks, dealing with uncertainty and risk, need more guidance for making predictive judgments. Users may need guidance to choose among competing solution techniques or among alternative methods of processing information to form a more appropriate decision-making process from the DSS. However, if the guidance mechanisms are too complex, they can make the DSS more difficult to learn and operate.

Our motivation for providing deliberate decisional guidance is also based on the simple guidelines of the cognitive fit theory. If the matching of data displays improves decision-making for spatial and symbolic tasks using uncertainty data, then the system could serve as a vehicle for encouraging the behavior of matching tasks and displays to influence the way decision-makers reach a decision. This leads to the first research question we investigate to establish opportunity and motive for providing decisional guidance within the DSS for spatial and symbolic tasks involving uncertainty data: Does matching the data display with the task improve decisionmaking for spatial or symbolic tasks involving uncertainty data?

This research question deserves further investigation for several reasons. The first is to test the theory of cognitive fit with uncertainty data. Other studies’ symbolic questions dealt with a prediction of a discrete quantity. Their spatial questions involved assessing an increase or decrease of a discrete quantity or bond rating category over time (Frownfelter-Lohrke, 1998; Vessey & Galletta, 1991; Wilson & Zigurs, 1999). Answers based on uncertainty data require the decision-maker to assess the probability of the event happening. Therefore, our symbolic questions asked the prediction of the probability of occurrence or mean of an event and the spatial questions asked for the assessment of the ‘more likely’ occurrence between the possible events. With increased technical capability creating the opportunity to incorporate uncertainty into the business decision process, decision-makers not only use data displays for making their decisions, they also provide data displays to other decisionmakers, perhaps through software based decisional guidance developed for clients and employees. This research will extend prior research by investigating whether decisions involving uncertainty data are improved when tasks and display types are matched, thus providing support for using theory-based decisional guidance.

The second reason is the conflicting results of prior business research that examined the relationship between format and decision quality<sup>3</sup>. A possible reason for the conflicting results, as postulated by Frownfelter-Lohrke (1998), was the lack of a theoretical base for explaining the relationship between presentation format and task type on decision quality. Controlling for user characteristic variables, Frownfelter-Lohrke investigated the theory of cognitive fit, but did not find a significant improvement in accuracy or efficiency of decision-makers when tasks were matched with presentation displays. On the other hand, Wilson and Zigurs (1999) found significant improvement when they used the theory of cognitive fit as the basis for theory-based decisional guidance embedded in a software application. They found a significant improvement in subjects’ overall performance for theory-based decisional guidance over user preferred display formats.

A comparison of the Frownfelter-Lohrke (1998) and Wilson and Zigurs (1999) studies highlights the intricacies of investigating the effects of decision aids on the decision-maker and the need to control for confounding effects. The conflicting results may be caused by variation in tasks complexity between the studies. Campbell (1988) suggests defining task complexity based on task acts and the information cues needed to perform the tasks. It is possible that the more complex tasks used by Frownfelter-Lohrke required more substantive business expertise<sup>4</sup> for task performance and, therefore, were beyond the student subjects’ abilities to solve even with the information cues provided<sup>5</sup>. MBA students, in Frownfelter-Lohrke’s study, were required to analyze a full set of financial statements using ratio analysis to predict the financial condition of a firm (spatial task) and to forecast the company’s subsequent year’s earnings per share (symbolic task). She assumed all students in the study had the required knowledge to perform these tasks<sup>6</sup>. Considering that the average business experience of the group was less than a year, and that the students attended five different institutions, it is possible that the students lacked the ability to recognize and interpret the financial information cues, no matter what display format was presented. A review of ratio analysis before the task performance may have mitigated this potential problem. On the average, it took the students 1.5 h to complete the experiment.

Utilizing a less demanding task, Wilson and Zigurs reduced reliance on business or educational experience. The information cue was based on a table or bar graph with the tasks requiring subjects to use the data displays to identify a specific quantity for symbolic tasks and to determine whether there was increase or decrease in quantity for spatial tasks. The subjects’ average completion time was approximately 30 min. They used both undergraduate and graduate students from the same institution who had completed coursework in producing and using computer data displays. To equalize students’ abilities to use the information cues and solve the task, the software used to administer the experiment included a tutorial of sample practice tasks in which subjects were shown the mechanical procedures required to operate the display choice conditions.

Another potential reason for conflicting results includes a possible measurement bias for efficiency. Because Wilson and Zigurs administered the experiment on the computer, there is possibly less bias in their performance measurement of time.

Frownfelter-Lohrke relied on the five different experimenters to track the student task time, which was recorded as a student completed each case.

The final possible reason for the difference in results was the inclusion of several personal and task characteristic variables as covariates in the Frownfelter-Lohrke study, including cognitive style as measured by the GEFT (Witkin, Oltman, Rask, & Karp, 1971) that were not included in Wilson and Zigurs’ study. Therefore, individual differences in psychological traits and cognitive ability (Ho & Rodgers, 1993; Libby & Lewis, 1977; Pincus, 1990) may have contributed to the differences in performance.

Focusing on the effects of user preferred display formats on performance, Wilson and Zigurs (1999) did not concentrate their study on theory-based decisional guidance. They compared user selected display formats to both randomly selected data displays and guided assignments. Even though their findings infer theory-based decisional guidance is superior to random display, Wilson and Zigurs did not compare improper assignment of task to display (where task and display do not match) to guided assignment (where task and display are always matched). Therefore, in our study, we investigate the effects of assignment of task to display format on decision performance to test our first hypothesis based on the theory of cognitive fit.

Although the theory of cognitive fit has not been studied in connection with probability data, it has been tested in other business situations. The theory of cognitive fit was extended to accounting models by Dunn and Grabski (2001). They compared the traditional Debit–Credit-Account (DCA) model with the Resources–Events– Agents (REA) model. The REA model is predominately spatial in nature, revealing relationships between objects, while the DCA model is predominately symbolic in nature, as it generates specific numeric data. This study asked subjects to explain how they would complete a series of tasks utilizing each model. For example, one task required subjects to explain how they would determine the sales of specific products within a specific time period. In the DCA model subjects were required to explain how to identify the line items on invoices that were associated with the products in question and to total the amounts on those lines. With the REA model subjects could explain how to formulate queries that isolated the products’ sales for the dates in question. Dunn and Grabski found that subjects using the REA model were generally more accurate but slower when compared with subjects using the DCA model.

The theory of cognitive fit was applied to the use of maps and tabular data by Dennis and Carte (1998). Subjects were asked to determine the relative desirability of possible sites for a new business by comparing the proximity of the sites to existing businesses. They concluded that subjects using maps tended to estimate answers while subjects using tables calculated answers. Tables were found to be more useful in solving containment tasks that only required subjects to consider information relating to a single geographic area. On the other hand, maps were found to be more useful in solving adjacency tasks that required subjects to consider information relating to both the local and surrounding geographic areas.

The theory of cognitive fit and the results of the cited studies suggest the first hypothesis:

H1: Decision performance (both accuracy and time) involving uncertainty data will improve when the problem representation matches the task.

If matching task and data presentation leads to better decision-making compared to unmatched tasks and data presentations, we will have shown the opportunity and the motivation to use decisional guidance within a DSS when dealing with spatial or symbolic tasks involving uncertainty data.

## 2.3. Means: how can decisional guidance be implemented for spatial and symbolic tasks?

Once the opportunity and the motive are established, the next step is to address the means for providing deliberate decisional guidance. For a large complex system, incorporating many functional capabilities into a system is much easier than providing guidance on its use. Silver (1991) discusses several problems related to delivering guidance to the user including tracking user behavior and ensuring that contextsensitive messages are presented at the appropriate junctures. Because most decisionmakers’ knowledge of the means for constructing and delivering such guidance is limited, a simple approach for spatial and/or symbolic tasks is demonstrated in this research following Silver’s (1991) typology for building guidance into a DSS.

First we begin by identifying what is being guided, which Silver calls the target of the guidance. The target may include two aspects of decision-making that require human judgment: structuring the process or executing the process. Structured guidance helps decision-makers choose operators to decide what to do next (or first). When the chosen outputs of the DSS require human judgments, execution guidance may prompt or guide the user in making the necessary judgment. Because we help the user choose operators, the target of our research was structured guidance.

Next we chose the form of guidance: either suggestive or informative. Suggestive guidance recommends how the user should act by suggesting what the user should do next or what input values to use. Informative guidance provides information that enlightens the user without suggesting how to act. Because our goal was to develop simple structured guidance, we used a suggestive form. Since suggestive guidance recommends how users can respond to the judgmental opportunities they encounter, structured decision-making process recommendations may include a single operator, a small set of operators, a rank ordered list of operators, or not using certain operators. Our structured decisional guidance suggested a single operator.

The final choice of Silver’s typology is the mode of guidance. Designers may provide predetermined, dynamic or participative guidance. For the predetermined mode the designer predefines the specific suggestions or information display(s) and builds them into the guidance mechanism. The other two modes require interaction with decision-makers’ choices to select displays or generate suggestions available in the guidance mechanism. Our guidance is a predefined mode because we prepared data displays and embedded them directly into the system. This mode of guidance was appropriate because we based our suggested display selections following the theory of cognitive fit.

How well users perform when they use personal preference to choose display formats, when they are guided to their choice, and when display format is randomly chosen was studied by Wilson and Zigurs (1999). Subjects were assigned several spatial and symbolic tasks. First subjects were allowed to choose from a limited menu of display formats (personal preference). Second, display format and task were randomly assigned. Third, subjects were assigned a single display format per task based on the theory of cognitive fit. The study found that theory-based decisional guidance improved subjects’ overall performance substantially over personal preference or random assignment.

Our study differs from that of Wilson and Zigurs (1999) in four ways. First, the subjects in our study are professional decision-makers rather than students. This allows us to test subjects from the working environment who would use the simple embedded theory-based decisional guidance. Second, we test the effect of theorybased decisional guidance on decision-making involving uncertainty data. Third, as is previously noted, we specifically test the effectiveness of the theory of cognitive fit as a basis for decisional guidance by comparing the effects of an improper assignment of task to display (mismatching of task and display) to a guided assignment of task to display (matching task and display) on performance. Finally, we extend the research to examine why the decisional guidance based on the theory of cognitive fit is effective by simultaneously examining subjects’ cognitive abilities. This is discussed in the next section.

## 2.4. The behavioral effects of deliberate decisional guidance for decision-making involving uncertainty data

Silver’s (1991) final step is the assessment of the effectiveness of decisional guidance. We investigate whether theory-based decisional guidance improves decisionmaking by individuals with differing cognitive abilities. A possible explanation of how matching display format with task improves performance involves linking cognitive ability to theory of cognitive fit. According to Kogan’s (1973) taxonomy of cognitive characteristics, cognitive ability relates to knowledge encoding, retrieval, and information processing within a particular context and is evaluated in terms of accuracy and value judgments. Prior studies (Casey, 1980; Gul, 1984; McGhee, Shields, & Birnberg, 1978; Mock, Estrin, & Vasarhelyi, 1972) have demonstrated that differences in decision-makers’ cognitive characteristics are important factors in decision-making and performance<sup>7</sup>.

The Group Embedded Figures Test (GEFT) developed by Witkin et al., (1971) is most widely used to assess the cognitive ability of individuals (Ho & Rodgers, 1993). The GEFT distinguishes between field dependent and field independent subjects on the basis of their ability to differentiate an object from its background. Individuals who achieve ‘low’ scores on the GEFT are called field dependent and are considered to have limited differentiation ability. Individuals who score ‘high’ on the GEFT have heightened differentiation ability and are called field independent. Benbasat and

Dexter (1985) found that field independent subjects performed well regardless of the display format while field dependent subjects relied on the display format to improve performance. Other studies have found field independent subjects and field dependent subjects differed in their preferences for presentation of information<sup>8</sup>. Witkin, Goodenough, and Oltman (1979) found that field independent decision-makers perform better than field dependent decision-makers when tasks involve spatial information. This leads us to our second research question: Are field dependent individuals more adversely affected when display format and tasks are not matched?

One of the risks associated with shifting responsibility for display choices from specialists to end-users is the potential of inadvertently placing certain users at a disadvantage. Cognitive research has shown that individuals vary in their ability to use display formats for problem solving. Matching problem solving skills to both problem representation and task has a greater effect on performance than matching only problem representation or task (Vessey & Galletta, 1991). They found that individuals who are field independent were more accurate when spatial tasks were matched with graphical displays and were more efficient when symbolic tasks were matched with tabular displays. Some individuals are able to adjust inappropriate display formats, that do not emphasize the same type of information needed for the task, into a usable format. Theory-based decisional guidance is important to all endusers, but it is especially important to those individuals who are less adaptable to inappropriate display formats. It is doubtful that flexibility of choice would solve this problem if inappropriate displays were among the choices. Prior research shows that using inappropriate displays negatively affects performance by increasing the problem solvers’ cognitive load and associated cognitive costs (Jarvenpaa, 1989; Tan & Benbasat, 1990; Todd & Benbasat, 1994; Umanath & Scamell, 1988; Vessey & Galletta, 1991). Other research suggests that having a large variety of displays to choose from may actually be detrimental (Vessey & Galletta, 1991; Wilson & Zigurs, 1999).

Based on the above discussion, we state our second hypothesis. We propose that cognitive ability or field dependency will affect decision-making performance when the decision support system includes display formats that do not match the tasks. When display format and task do not match, we expect field independents will perform better than field dependents.

H2: When the DSS includes display formats that are not matched with the tasks, cognitive ability (field dependency) will affect performance.

Cognitive ability and the theory of cognitive fit are naturally linked in the decisionmaking process. Cognitive ability addresses how the decision process works, and the theory of cognitive fit explores how data presentation affects the decision process. Collectively, cognitive ability research (Jarvenpaa, 1989; Tan & Benbasat, 1990; Todd & Benbasat, 1994; Umanath & Scamell, 1988; Vessey & Galletta, 1991) has shown that people who are field dependent process information differently than people who are field independent and that field dependents are more reliant on how information is presented. Cognitive fit research has shown that matching tasks with the appropriate support data display format enhances performance because the mental representation formulated is consistent with the task. Matching task and data display format increases the decision-maker’s time and/or accuracy.

Therefore, in our final hypothesis we combine the two theories. When theorybased decisional guidance is provided, in the form of matched display formats and tasks, the mental representations formulated will be consistent with the tasks helping field dependents more than field independents. The matching compensates for the field dependents limited differentiation ability.

H3: When only matched display formats and tasks (theory-based decisional guidance) are provided, cognitive ability (field dependency) will not affect performance.

## 3. Method

A laboratory experiment was conducted using a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial design (display format by task type and field dependency). Each subject completed eight treatments during the experiment (two treatments in each of two task types in two different display formats). In each treatment, subjects answered a question using a display format where the order of display formats and task types were random. Subjects completed the GEFT test before starting the experiments.

## 3.1. Experimental procedures

The computerized portion of the experiment was administered by the same researcher to all subjects on a one by one basis using custom software running under Microsoft Windows on laptop computers. The software supported standard Windows conventions, including a graphical user interface and standard mouse and keyboard operations. Prior to administration the software was pilot-tested by graduate auditing students as part of a class exercise, and minor changes were made based on their feedback.

Each subject in the study worked through the software at his or her own pace. The software explained the experiment as an investigation of interpretations of uncertainty information displays and provided a review of probability density and tabular displays of uncertainty information. The software instructed subjects to answer each experimental question accurately and quickly. The test administrator remained in the room and restricted subjects from talking or arbitrarily leaving their seats during the experiment. The small number of questions asked by subjects during the experiment were answered by the researcher and did not appear in any way to interfere with accurate measurement in the experiment. All but one subject successfully completed the experiment. The Group Embedded Figures Test (GEFT), a pencil and paper exercise, was administered prior to the computerized portion of the experiment.

## 3.2. Subjects

The subjects were 90 members of the Institute of Management Accountants in a large metropolitan area. A total of 114 members were contacted to participate in the study. Of these, 90 participated in the study resulting in a response rate of 78.9%<sup>9</sup>. All subjects held advanced degrees and were employed in the accounting area. The mean full-time employment experience was 14.2 years (with a range of 0 to 47 years and a standard deviation of 9.8 years); 63% held either a CPA or CMA certification; 72% held present positions as a supervisor/manager or above while 28% were presently employed in staff positions.

## 3.3. Independent variables

## 3.3.1. Task type

Two task types were manipulated in the experiment: spatial (which required the subject to compare components of the display) and symbolic (which required the subject to provide a numeric answer using the information acquired from the display). We used theory based definitions that were validated in prior studies (Umanath & Scamell, 1988; Vessey & Galletta, 1991) to develop the task types in the study. In order to simulate a variety of task/display situations, each task type was operationalized using two questions. The two spatial questions were: “Which is more likely, a profit or a loss?” and “Is the probability of a profit between \$x and \$y less than or greater than the probability of a profit between \$y and \$z?” The two symbolic questions used were: “What is the probability of making a profit?” and “What is the mean profit?” Fig. 1 shows an example of tabular and graphical displays provided to the subjects for one question. This provided a total of two treatments per task type for each display choice condition.

Task complexity was minimized to avoid the potentially confounding effects of knowledge and business expertise (Bonner et al., 1996) and strategy shifts as suggested by Vessey (1994) and Frownfelter-Lohrke (1998). The tasks are representative of questions normally asked when analyzing uncertainty data. Due to the subjects overall business experience and academic background, the participants were judged to possess the knowledge and expertise needed to perform the tasks. In addition, the support system provided subjects with a review of statistical concepts used in the study.

## 3.3.2. Cognitive ability (field dependency)

We used two levels of cognitive ability: field independents (indicating heightened perceptual differentiation) and field dependents (indicating lower perceptual differentiation). The field independent group had a GEFT score of 14 or higher, while the field dependent group had scores less than 14. This same cutoff score was used by Lusk (1973), Doktor and Hamilton (1973) and Benbasat and Dexter (1979, 1982) and was a commonly observed mean in a large sample of subjects with business backgrounds (Benbasat & Dexter, 1982).

## 3.3.3. Display format

We used two types of display formats: probability density (graphical) and table (tabular). These are common display formats found in uncertainty data software.

Prior to task performance, a tutorial reviewing the data formats and statistical terminology used in the tasks was administered via the software program to minimize the potentially confounding effects of learning (Frownfelter-Lohrke, 1998) and prior or differential knowledge (Bonner et al., 1996; Libby & Frederick, 1990).

## 3.3.4. Decisional guidance

For hypothesis 3 task and display formats were combined to represent suggestive decisional guidance versus no decisional guidance. A single display was assigned to each treatment based on the theory of cognitive fit, which prescribes matching spatial tasks with graphical displays and symbolic tasks with tabular displays (suggestive decisional guidance). In addition, a single display was assigned for each treatment that did not follow the theory, where spatial tasks were matched with tabular displays and symbolic tasks were matched with graphical displays (no decisional guidance). The same suggestive decisional guidance was used by Wilson and Zigurs (1999) as the underlying rules are validated by empirical research and simple to apply in practice.

## 3.4. Dependent variables

Accuracy and response time were used as performance measures, as recommended by previous data presentation researchers (Jarvenpaa, 1989; Vessey & Galletta, 1991; Wilson & Zigurs, 1999). Accurate responses for spatial questions were defined as the correct selection from yes/no choice and for symbolic questions as a numeric answer within ±5% of the correct answer (Wilson & Zigurs, 1999). The computer tracked the response time in seconds from the initial presentation of the display screen through the time that the subjects completed a response on the response screen.

## 4. Results

The mean and standard deviation for accuracy and time are shown in Table 1. The results are presented for each task and display format. The results are reported separately for field dependent and field independent subjects.

The hypotheses were analyzed using a mixed design, repeated-measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) for the performance dependent variable, measured as both accuracy and time, with repeated measures of task and display format and between-group analysis of cognitive ability (field dependency)<sup>10</sup>. The Box M statistic (P = 0.375) showed no homogeneity of variance violation. Univariate analysis and between-groups t-tests were also performed.

The use of multivariate analysis in combination with other experimental controls addressed the problems noted in the previous section. Certain task and personal characteristic effects on performance were simultaneously tested by including both task type and cognitive ability in the MANOVA model. Confounding personal and task characteristics such as knowledge and business expertise were minimized by using less demanding tasks, professional subjects with similar educational and business experience, and a pretest review of statistical concepts and data formats. The administration of the test by a single individual using a computer program to track time and responses controlled for measurement bias, especially for time.

## 4.3. Hypothesis 3: when display formats are matched with tasks (theory-based decisional guidance), cognitive ability will not affect performance

We examine the behavioral consequences of decisional guidance by combining the cognitive fit theory with cognitive ability in hypothesis 3. As previously noted, for suggestive decisional guidance a single display was assigned to each treatment based on the theory of cognitive fit, which prescribes matching spatial tasks with graphical displays and symbolic tasks with tabular displays. In addition, for nondecisional guidance a single display was assigned for each treatment that did not follow the theory, where spatial tasks were matched with tabular displays and symbolic tasks were matched with graphical displays.

Follow up between-groups t-test on the effect of field dependency on performance when display formats include only appropriate matches for spatial and symbolic tasks indicates that field independents were not more accurate $( t = 0 . 2 1 2 , P = 0 . 8 3 3 )$ or faster $( t = 0 . 6 0 2 , P = 0 . 5 4 9 )$ than field dependents. Fig. 3 shows that there is no difference between field independent and field dependents’ accuracy and time when decisional guidance is provided (matched display format and task). Theory suggests that field independents will outperform field dependents on tasks that require restructuring and involve ambiguity (Witkin & Goodenough, 1981). It is possible that only unmatched cases provide an opportunity for field independents to outperform field dependents. Therefore, the results support hypothesis 3.

## 5. Summary and discussion, recommendations, and limitations

## 5.1. Summary and discussion

In their roles as users and providers of uncertainty data, decision-makers are confronted with choosing among a plethora of data formats offered by the decision support systems on most personal computers, but are offered little in the way of decisional guidance for choosing the most effective display format. This shift in responsibility for display choice from professional graphic designers to end user decision-makers increases information risks if choice of data presentation makes a difference to the decision outcome. Research has demonstrated that display choices made by end users have a significant potential to affect organization productivity and that theory-based decisional guidance is more effective than user preference for display choice (Wilson & Zigurs, 1999). Problem solving skills were also shown to affect performance when the problem representation and the task are similar (Vessey & Galletta, 1991).

This research investigated when and why decisional guidance should be based on the cognitive fit theory in an effort to demonstrate the opportunities, motives, and means for providing guidance. We used a structured, suggestive predetermined approach as a means of providing theory-based decisional guidance for tasks involving uncertainty data. Using an embedded single guidance mechanism based on the theory of cognitive fit, we investigated the behavioral consequences of this type of guidance on the decision process of professional decision-makers for tasks involving uncertainty data. By simultaneously testing cognitive ability and the theory of cognitive fit, we focused on why theory-based decisional guidance is effective and necessary. The results indicate that the theory of cognitive fit extends to probability data and also that decisional guidance could point users toward data presentations that produce better decisions.

We found that in tasks involving uncertainty data, the decision-makers were more accurate and responded faster when symbolic tasks were matched with tabular displays and spatial tasks were matched with graphical displays. Therefore, our findings strongly support the theory of cognitive fit for tasks involving uncertainty data. The effectiveness of a problem representation should be considered in the context of the task to be solved (Vessey & Galletta, 1991). As more business decisions require the use of probabilistic information, it is important to show that display choice based on the cognitive fit theory helps decision-makers effectively incorporate uncertainty into their judgments.

When examining performance, where display format includes inappropriate matches for task type, evidence suggest a marginal time-accuracy trade-off as noted by psychological research where accuracy is frequently associated with slower times (Luce, 1986). The theory of cognitive fit indicates that a specific skill has its greatest effect on performance when the problem representation and task emphasize the same type of information needed for the skill. The matching of task and data presentation reduces the mental processing needed to perform the task increasing both time and accuracy. Mismatching data presentation and task forces decision-makers to compensate by either using the unmatched data presentation or mentally converting the unmatched data presentation. This slows the process and introduces errors.

The lack of a time-accuracy trade-off when display choice matched task type may be a result of assigning the display format rather than allowing the decision-maker to choose. When allowing subjects to choose among multiple displays versus given an assigned display, Wilson and Zigurs (1999) found that subjects took longer to perform a task. While our findings support limiting the display choices of end-users when designing embedded decisional guidance, research has shown that end-users prefer guidance that does not limit their options and suggests using a form of decisional guidance that is not perceived as limiting options (Wilson & Zigurs, 1999).

We extended the assessment of the behavioral consequences of decisional guidance to simultaneously examine the effects of cognitive ability (field dependency) and the theory of cognitive fit on performance to investigate a potential explanation for the effectiveness of theory-based decisional guidance. We found that when subjects were provided both matched and unmatched display formats, field independent individuals performed better for both task types in the random condition. The decision process for field dependents was more prone to error than for field independent individuals. These results, combined with the finding that field independents used tables more successfully than field dependents for spatial tasks, suggest that field independents are more successful than field dependents when inappropriate display formats are matched to tasks. This is consistent with the idea that the processes necessary to formulate the mental representation play a key role in mediating the dual matching process of problem representation to mental representation and mental representation to task. The lack of a “cognitive fit” when inappropriate display formats are used increases the problem solver’s cognitive load and associated cognitive costs (Jarvenpaa, 1989; Tan & Benbasat, 1990; Todd & Benbasat, 1994; Umanath & Scamell, 1988; Vessey & Galletta, 1991).

We then examined whether a decisional guidance mechanism based on the theory of cognitive fit would eliminate the performance difference between field dependents and independents (cognitive ability). When decisional guidance restricted the display formats to match the task types, cognitive ability (field dependency) did not significantly affect performance. This suggests that cognitive ability or field dependency is not a factor when the mental representation formulated is consistent with the problem representation, possibly reducing cognitive load caused by unmatched conditions. Additionally, when we analyzed the data using only unmatched task types and display formats, a possible cognitive overload condition, field independents were more accurate and marginally slower than field dependents.

## 5.2. Policy recommendations

The results of this study emphasize the importance of decisional guidance to decision-makers in several ways. First, in a technological business environment where personal computers provide multiple display format choices, decisional guidance helps to alleviate information risk by guiding the user to more effective decision support. However, complications are introduced for decisions involving uncertainty data where the decision-maker must incorporate risk, or the probability of the event happening, into the answers. Although many decision makers have a computerized decision support system that is capable of factoring risk into decisions, such as asset valuation or choosing the appropriate mix of derivative instruments for hedging purposes, it is doubtful that very many decision makers are comfortable choosing among competing solution techniques or alternative methods of processing information. The challenge is to provide a guidance mechanism for such decisions that is not so complex that it makes the DSS more difficult to learn and operate. Those involved in designing the guidance mechanism choice should consider the relationship between task complexity and informative versus suggestive decisional guidance designs and the need for theory-based guidance.

Second, decisional guidance may alleviate information bias for particular groups who tend to be more field dependent such as females. Research<sup>11</sup> within the field of psychology has shown males and females differ in how they perceive, process, and encode information when making a judgment or decision. National studies have shown that the average female GEFT score is significantly lower than the male score. We also found that female accountants in the study had a significantly lower GEFT score than the male accountants. This suggests that as a group females are usually more field dependent than males, and therefore are more dependent on proper use of display formats.

Third, for uncomplicated tasks, simple guidelines from empirical data presentation literature, such as matching display format to the task at hand, incorporated in a simple decisional guidance mechanism can improve decision-making in a more cost– effective way to some alternatives such as training personnel and clients.

## 5.3. Limitations

Our research is not without limitations. We used simple spatial and symbolic questions and a non-complex guidance system. Possible future research could examine the effect of complexity of a guidance system to see if more complex systems make the decision support system easier or more difficult to operate. The experiments could include other modes of guidance that require interaction with decision-makers’ choices to select displays or generate suggestions. In addition, complexity of the questions could be increased to examine learning effects or expertise. However, we may have inadvertently increased task complexity by using uncertainty data. For many business professionals the use of probability information is a relatively new experience. It is not clear how the learning that occurs with use will change the relationships we observed. It is clear, however, that there is a need for decisional guidance for users of probability information at the present time.

Finally, future research could examine the ability of decision-makers to use uncertainty data versus discrete data for decision-making. Additionally, because subjects were from a single metropolitan area, the generalizability of the results may be limited.

## 5.4. Conclusion

As current software makes the generation of probability information relatively easy, the integration of risk into problem solving has created a demand for information presented in a way that helps decision-makers effectively incorporate uncertainty into their judgments. We suggest that decision-makers may benefit from decisional guidance as they work to gain the expertise needed to effectively utilize the statistical concepts associated with probability information. We have shown the benefits of embedded suggestive decisional guidance to guide the choice between alternative presentations of uncertainty data. Theory-based decisional guidance can improve the accuracy and time of decision-makers.

We also contribute to an understanding of the consequences of guidance, which in turn contributes to understanding how DSSs affect decision-making (Silver, 1991), by showing how cognitive ability and the theory of cognitive fit are linked in the decision-making process. When theory based decisional guidance is provided in the form of matched display and tasks, which allows the mental representations formulated to be consistent with the tasks, we found that field dependents performed as well as field independents. Thus, the main contribution of our research is the assessment of consequences of decisional guidance-not only its effectiveness, but a reason for its effectiveness.
