# Beyond the walls of the asylum: information and organization in the provision of community mental health services

B.P. Bloomfield <sup>a,</sup>∗, C. McLean b

<sup>a</sup> Centre for the Study of Technology and Organisation, Lancaster University Management School, Lancaster LA1 4YX, UK

<sup>b</sup> Manchester School of Management, UMIST, P.O. Box 88, Manchester M60 1QD, UK

## Abstract

In the last twenty years or so the UK National Health Service (NHS) has witnessed tremendous changes in the provision of mental health services. As the vast Victorian asylums have progressively been run down and closed attention has switched to the planning and delivery of services based on the idea of ‘care in the community’. This paper explores some of the temporal and spatial dimensions of the problems of organizing that have marked this transition. Drawing on research conducted within a hospital department responsible for psychiatric services in the community, it addresses some of the issues faced by mental health professionals in struggling to put in place and maintain mental health services while simultaneously under pressure to demonstrate their professional accountability. In this regard, particular attention is paid to the role of technology (principally information and the information systems) in managing services in the context of the transition to care in the community as well as addressing some of the problems generated by it.

## 1. Introduction

The growing interest in time and space across a range of academic disciplines continues apace and in this regard the information systems field has proved no exception. The literature dealing with information systems, organizations, time and space, conveys a rich variety of theoretical commitments. For example, attention has been paid to understanding the broad world historical significance of information and communications technology (ICTs) (Castells, 1996; Mosco, 1989); the political, social, and cultural dimensions of IT, time and space (Rasmussen, 1997; Sahay, 1998); the experience of time and place among IT professionals (Schulze & Boland, 2000); or the philosophical dimensions of spatio-temporal organization in an electronically mediated world (Kallinikos, 1995; Sotto, 1996). Here, we explore the nature of the interrelationship between people, time and space, technology and organization. In particular, we are interested in understanding how the organization of space may support a specific organization of time (and vice versa) and how information and information systems are deployed to address the problems of organizing across fragmented spaces and times.

The context that sets the backdrop for our deliberations is the transition from the Victorian asylum to ‘care in the community’ as the preferred means of delivering mental health services.<sup>1</sup> This shift is marked by a contrast between the (relatively) closed world of the asylum, what has been posited as a ‘total institution’ by Goffman (1968), and the scattered and fragmented spaces outside the asylum where mental health patients may now typically (though not exclusively) reside.<sup>2</sup> We do not seek to account for this transition from one regime to the other. Rather, we pursue a more modest task. We take the shift to care in the community as a given (though acknowledging that different accounts of it exist) and seek instead to understand the role of technology—specifically, information and information systems—in the management of the spatial–temporal problems of organization associated with the transition. Our object of inquiry then is the interrelationships between time and space, technology, organization, and human subjects (patients or staff) in the field of mental health care. Taken together these are considered as part of a shifting problematic<sup>3</sup> that has served to shape the approach to, and practical realization of mental healthcare provision. This problematic comprises a set of mutually supportive concepts and associated assumptions, practices, subject positions and technologies. For example, the role allotted to a particular technology (e.g. psychoactive drugs, assessment techniques, or information systems) presupposes particular subject positions (for example patients, managers, and clinicians), as well as organizing procedures. Conversely, a particular view of patients (again, a specific subject position) can be connected to particular assumptions about technology or organizing.

Historically, one of the primary goals of the mental health services has been the maintenance of social order and control and this has involved particular forms of knowledge and discipline (both of patients and mental health professionals) and technology. This accords with the more general view that sees modern technology as typically associated with the problem or ordering, with the desire to realize and maintain control (Bloomfield, 2001; Law, 1994). By removing the subjects of mental health care to places of incarceration, society and by implication its social categories were to be protected from those whose presence would (seemingly) cause disturbance. Over time different technologies have been incorporated into the task of pursuing this order.<sup>4</sup> In actor network terms (Latour, 1992) what was once delegated to the asylum, its architecture and disciplinary (spatially and temporally ordered) routines, later came to involve pharmacological drugs, particularly after the 1950s, but now also encompasses a prominent use of information and information systems.<sup>5</sup> These systems are deployed to monitor patients, assist the determination and recording of their needs, plan and standardize care, and ensure that the services provided by mental health professionals are compliant with particular procedures and routines.

Such is the importance of the role played by information and information systems in the management and delivery of mental health services in the community that in this paper we propose to consider the subjects of modern psychiatric services as members of a virtual asylum. In this regard, it is important to stress that we do not wish to suggest that care in the community is a virtual organization, much less that it has been planned as such. Rather, we propose that the virtual characteristics of the organization of community based care can be understood in two important senses. First, care in the community represents a spatially dispersed and temporally diverse organization, one in which the comparatively fixed or uniform spatial–temporal order arising from the regimentation and physical confinement of the Victorian asylum has been replaced by a more fragmented ensemble of practices, knowledge, bodies, organizations, artefacts/technologies, and locations. In this regard then, we might say that the ‘virtual asylum’ is virtual by way of what it lacks (Sotto, 1996)—not only the physical architecture, but also the (more) fixed spatio-temporal order of the Victorian asylum. Secondly, the work of mental health professionals now (more than ever before) involves practices of information gathering and information management (Bloomfield & McLean, 1996), the purposes of which include: (a) reporting the accountability of the service (e.g. to colleagues, management, health commissioners, the government, and the clients/users of the service); and (b) assembling/disassembling clinical activities (or patients) textually (i.e. on paper or on the computer screen) as a prelude to transforming them according to notions of best practice, efficiency or effectiveness.<sup>6</sup> This increased emphasis on inscribing the world in terms of information marks a virtualization of the world of professional practice for clinicians and managers alike and thus the organization of which they are a part.

Developing the issues highlighted thus far, the argument in the rest of the paper comprises four main sections. Section 2 introduces some basic ideas concerning the connection between space and time in the specific context of organizing. Section 3 examines the changes in the spatial and temporal ordering of mental health services in the move to care in the community. Section 4 then draws on empirical fieldwork to illustrate and develop the theoretical ideas thus laid down. Specifically we examine two areas: the introduction of an initiative known as the Care Programme Approach (CPA) which was meant to have a central role in the provision, management, and audit of clinical services; followed by an examination of some of the problems deriving from the temporal disparity between the allocation of staff resources and the actual manifestation of mental health problems. In Section 5 we present a more general discussion of the issues raised in the paper.

## 2. Organizing time and space

Power is articulated directly onto time; it assures its control and guarantees its use. Foucault (1979, p. 160)

Though the understanding of space and time and their interrelationship has of course been a long-standing preoccupation of philosophy and science, interest in the social sciences is rather more recent (Adam, 1992; Elias, 1992). At the risk of being brutal to a large and complex area of enquiry we concentrate here on sketching out certain aspects of time and space in relation to the specific problem of organizing. In contrast to the abstract universal notion of time posited in (much of) the natural sciences and engineering, among the social sciences<sup>7</sup> the emphasis has often been placed on the particular temporal ordering of social life in different contexts. Anthropologists, for instance, have revealed a rich cultural variety in the conceptualizations of spatial and temporal order. Rather than regarding social life as a sequence of events which are mapped onto the universal (physical) notion of time, social activities and events and their interconnections are seen as defining the temporal order of the community concerned. For example, in Evans-Pritchard’s study of the Nuer, time reckoning is described in terms of the daily activities of milking cattle:

The daily timepiece is the cattle clock, the round of pastoral tasks, and the time of day and the passage of time through a day are to a Nuer primarily the succession of these tasks and their relations to one another… Nuer generally use such points of activity, rather than concrete points in the movement of the sun across the heavens, to co-ordinate events. Evans-Pritchard (1940, pp. 101, 102)

Spatial and temporal orders, are therefore, intimately interconnected, the daily routine of tasks/events take place in prescribed spaces. The regular (i.e. temporally ordered) association of persons and activities with particular spaces comes to imbue a particular spatio-temporal order in experience.<sup>8</sup>

In the context of technologically advanced societies it is evident that the development and spread of (ever more) accurate technologies for time keeping have become part and parcel of the spatio-temporal complexity that is the modern organization. Indeed, time and time reckoning were important features of the emergence of industrial organizations. For Foucault the organization of space and time can be viewed as part of the wider emergence of disciplinary power—regimented bodies presupposed regulated times and spaces

In the first instance, discipline proceeds from the distribution of individuals in space. Foucault (1979, p. 141)

While time, once a medium of religious control, became the object of new techniques and orders of measurement.

For centuries, the religious orders had been masters of discipline: they were the specialists of time, the great technicians of rhythm and regular activities. But the disciplines altered these methods of temporal regulation from which they derived.

They altered them first by refining them. One began to count in quarter hours, in minutes, in seconds. Foucault (1979, p. 150)

Discipline then is understood in two related ways: on the one hand, as drill, training and control of the body; and on the other, as knowledge in connection with the fields of investigation (the human sciences) that developed in part through the opportunities for surveillance opened up by the control of disciplined bodies within institutions such as schools, prisons, factories, asylums, and hospitals. Thus, the discipline of psychiatry was shaped by the fact that it was able to observe and experiment on the captive subjects of the asylum (see also: Rose, 1986, 1990).

The emphasis on the historical importance of religious orders in time keeping as well as the elaboration of the latter during the industrial revolution is also discussed by Mumford

The instrument [the clock] presently spread outside the monastery; and the regular striking of the bells brought a new regularity into the life of the workman and the merchant. The bells of the clock tower almost defined urban existence. Timekeeping passed into time-serving and time-accounting and time-rationing. Mumford (1946, p. 14)

The orchestration of the processes of production and distribution according to an abstract universal notion of time presupposes a means of organizing in which time— past, present, and future—can be inscribed and manipulated. That is, if abstract time became a condition for the efficient operation of industry it could play this role only if it too was made the object of practices of organizing. As suggested previously, the introduction of accurate clocks in Western societies enabled an increased objectification of, and control over, the temporal dimension of social and economic life. Previously, the constraints on the span of control derived in part from the absence of a shared unified time. During the industrial revolution the discipline of the workforce required workers’ subordination not only to the factory machines of which they became an extension, but also to the machine that is the clock.<sup>9</sup> In fact, Mumford (1946, p. 15) suggests that the clock is the ‘foremost’ machine; the accurate clock became an exemplar in relation to which other machines, and organizations, derived a standard of perfection, as suggested by the phrase ‘to run like clockwork’.<sup>10</sup> Take the development of the railways. The efficient movement of people and goods on a national scale involved complex problems of timetabling. But, particular locales within the larger railway system could only operate a unified timetable if there was a sufficient distribution of clocks whose time keeping was in regular agreement (Clark, 1997). In terms of Zerubavel’s (1979) distinction between symmetric and asymmetric temporal orders, and thus, between integrated and non-integrated social worlds, we might say that as a means of integration a unified timetable required a symmetrical system of time keeping across the country. In a nutshell, all station clocks had to be synchronized as close as possibe, when it was 3 o’clock in London it had to be 3 o’clock in Manchester. In this connection the notion of grammatocentrism envisages an ensemble of writing practices (timetabling, filling in forms, record keeping, lists, schedules etc.) which institute and regulate the spatio-temporal dimensions of organizational life (Hoskin & Macve, 1986). Thus, we might say that during the course of industrialization there was a shift from the organization of production to the production of organization (Cooper & Burrell, 1988).

Such ideas are, of course, not confined to industrial organization, but provide a more general insight into the character of organizing. In particular, they have a bearing on our understanding of how space and time are configured together through technology. Research that specifically addresses time and the organization of hospital life includes that of Zerubavel (1979) and Barley (1988) where the sequence, duration, temporal location and rate of events or activities are explored in uncovering the temporal rhythms of work and organizing. Barley’s work is particularly relevant here because it centers on organizational change following the introduction of digital X-ray technology.<sup>11</sup> Barley details the intricacies of temporal ordering within professional work cultures, drawing attention to how the introduction of the new technology shifted the temporal organization and thus social order of the hospital concerned.<sup>12</sup> In the argument developed here, however, we adopt a somewhat different approach in as much as it is not the temporal consequences of the introduction of new technology that interest us, but rather the spatio-temporal ramifications of the transition from the asylum to care in the community; and in particular the specific role allotted to information and information systems in trying to, as it were, hold everything together. Of course the consequences (intended and unintended) associated with any technology may be important and indeed we do touch upon the temporal aspect of professional work culture associated with the new information based procedures. But it is the overall problematic within which technology fits that is the focus of attention; we want to explore the conditions of possibility wherein technology comes to be seen as an answer to the problems of organization. In other words, we are more interested in what shapes technology than what is shaped by technology. This is why we focus on the problematic that interrelates space, time, technology, organization and people in the context of the pursuit of social order. In the following section we will consider the specific spatio-temporal orders inherent in different ways of orchestrating mental health services and the associated discipline of patients and staff that these entail.

## 3. Care in the community: problems of organizing in time and space

Every institution captures something of the time and interest of its members and provides something of a world for them. Goffman (1968, p. 15)

The past 20 years have witnessed a radical restructuring in the provision of mental health services. The vast Victorian asylums have been progressively run down or closed and attention has switched to the planning and delivery of services based on the idea of caring for the mentally ill in the community. The principles of care in the community were published in the White Paper Caring for People: Community Care in the Next Decade and Beyond (Department of Health, 1989). Though the seeds of this transformation date from the late 1950s and early 1960s,<sup>13</sup> the pace of change is widely seen to have gathered substantial momentum during the 1980s and 1990s. For example, drawing on official statistics Jones (1993, p. 243) notes that between 1976 and 1992 the number of residents in mental illness hospitals in the UK fell from 83,320 to 45,100.<sup>14</sup>

Divergent arguments have been put forward to explain the transition from the asylum to care in the community, though the reasons commonly cited are not necessarily mutually exclusive. On the one hand, one might consider the influence of neoliberal discourse on the individual rights of the mentally ill, or the calls for a more humane regime of treatment—these being particularly prevalent in the wake of serious scandals involving ill-treatment and brutality on the part of staff (for example, see Jones, 1993).<sup>15</sup> On the other, there is the fiscal crisis of the state and the concomitant desire to reduce costs. For example, within the larger programme of National Health Service (NHS) reform inaugurated by the Conservative government of 1979 onwards. But also important are the notions of a pharmacological revolution which centered on the (contested) belief that new drugs would enable the management of previously intractable conditions, as well as developments in psychiatric discourse (see Bean & Mounser, 1993; Busfield, 1986; Gittens, 1998; Rogers & Pilgram, 1996; Rose, 1986, 1990).<sup>16</sup>

Interesting though the debates about the causes of the transition might be, they are not the focus of this paper. As suggested earlier, instead of seeking to identify the main causes of the move to care in the community our task is to understand the shifting problematic of technology, subjects, time, space and organizing. For our purposes here what is notable about the belief in the efficacy of new drug treatments for mental illness is that by implication this technology was delegated the task of controlling the mentally ill. That is, those who could not control their own behavior would have it modified through the power of psychoactive drugs.<sup>17</sup> For some, the more severe cases would mean the continued residence of their now more docile bodies within the asylum.<sup>18</sup> But for others there was the prospect that they might no longer have to be confined in an institution, but might be able to return to the community.

The rhetoric of the pharmacological revolution found expression amongst the medical profession and politicians alike. For example, in 1961 the Minister of Health Enoch Powell believed that new drug therapies would enable the process of deinstitutionalization (Bean & Mounser, 1993, p. 19; see also Rose, 1986, p. 66) while a decade later in 1971 the then Secretary of State for Health Sir Keith Joseph opined:

Psychiatry is to join the rest of medicine… the treatment of psychosis, neurosis and schizophrenia have been entirely changed by the drug revolution. People go into hospital with mental disorders and they are cured, and that is why we want to bring that branch of medicine into the scope of the 230 district general hospitals that are planned for England and Wales. Cited in Jones (1993, p. 184)

Aside from the very important arguments that challenge the claimed clinical benefit of such drugs,<sup>19</sup> what is pertinent for our purposes here is the set of assumptions about the context within which the new regime of treatment would be realized. The delegation of cure/order to the new drugs rested not only on the assumption that the patient was physiologically amenable to control by the administration of suitable medication, but further that they and the staff administering it could be brought into compliance with the treatment. Both patients and staff would have to act appropriately as subordinate subjects of the practice. Moreover, the faith placed in the transition to the new regime of community care presupposed patients who would (continue to) be disciplined vis-a\`-vis the administration of their prescribed medication. But this begs the question of how this could be ensured if patients were no longer the captive subjects of a hospital institution. In fact, though the move to community care originally presumed a growing role for pharmacological drugs it has now become supplemented by the increased use of information systems of various sorts. These have emerged as one means of compensating for the apparent deficit in order and control brought about by the move to care in the community. To develop this aspect of our argument we must consider more closely the transition away from the enclosed world of the asylum and, in particular, its specific configuration of bodies, discipline, space, and time.

The Victorian asylums constituted a distinct spatial and temporal order for both staff and patients alike. In this regard architecture, and therefore, the specific organization of space, was a distinctive feature of many institutions.

High walls and long drives kept patients away from the outside world. Long straight corridors made for easy surveillance. At Rainhill hospital, near Liverpool, built in 1851, the main corridor was one-third of a mile long. Jones (1993, p. 119)

The sense of social separation and exclusion (from mainstream society) that characterized asylums, as well as a sense of their scale (literally dwarfing the individuals who resided therein), was evident in a critique of such institutions in 1961 by the Minister for Health:

There they stand, isolated, majestic, imperious, brooded over by the giant water tower and chimney combined, rising unmistakable and daunting out of the countryside. Enoch Powell cited in Rogers and Pilgram (1996, p. 70)

Whatever the problems of internal disorder within the confines of the old asylums, for instance in terms of the abuse of patients or patients’ abuse of other patients, in representational terms their austere appearance and (in many cases) geographical isolation stood as a hallmark of society’s control over the mentally ill.

Boundaries between reason and madness, order and chaos were tightly defined and rigidly enforced; arguably such a dichotomy enabled people to think that all irrational aspects—of themselves, their families and communities—were safely locked away somewhere else. Gittens (1996, p. 25)

But the separation from the rest of society, the confinement behind the high walls, were just one aspect of society’s attempt to exercise control over and thus organize madness. For instance, the organization of space tended to reflect and reinforce class and gender boundaries.<sup>20</sup> In some cases asylum architecture, as with hospitals more generally, schools or even factories, indicated the need to be able to survey the denizens, to render them visible to the gaze of staff. In the case of hospitals and asylums there was an ongoing need to prevent the possible dangers associated with fire. Historically, it would appear that fears of pollution have also been much in evidence when it comes to the treatment of the mentally ill—on the presumption (it would seem) that mentally ill patients were somehow contagious. In some cases this became manifest in the design of asylum laundry facilities (Gittens, 1998, p. 21); that is, the management of pollution (associated with mental illness) required a specific organization or ordering of space so that it could be contained. The local form of boundary work manifest in the organization of laundry facilities was, of course, a reflection of the wider separation of categories—the pure (reason) and the impure (madness)—that asylum based care instituted.<sup>21</sup>

Thus, the organization of social/institutional space represented one aspect of the organization of patients’ lives. They were also subjected to a high degree of regimentation and a concomitant punctuation and sequencing of time (Zerubavel, 1979) with the purpose of bringing some structure, what was seen as a therapeutic order, into their lives. Thus, the asylum represented a distinct spatio-temporal order. It was a particular site of psychiatric practice where a specific form of discipline was enacted.

[A]ll phases of the day’s activities are tightly scheduled, with one activity leading at a prearranged time into the next, the whole sequence of activities being imposed from above by a system of explicit formal rules and a body of officials. Goffman (1968, p. 17)

In contrast to this picture of an ordered and regimented institution, the move to community care represented a diminution of centralized order and control. Indeed, long before the major emphasis on community care in the 1990s the term ‘institutionalized’ was often applied to mentally ill patients on their return to the outside world because they were seen as not being capable of looking after themselves. This was not because of their illness as such, indeed the return to society presumably meant that their illness was in remission or was sufficiently controlled by drugs, but the fact that the asylum as an institution had done so much for them in terms of the ordering and control of daily activities that they could not fend for themselves. Over the past four decades the institutionalization of mental health patients became one of the central arguments against asylums and for alternative approaches, such as care within the community (Busfield, 1986).

No longer confined by the walls of a ‘total institution’ (Goffman, 1968) mental health patients are now increasingly dispersed across various sites within the community—from day care and rehabilitation centers to bedsit appartments in the private rented sector, from emergency/acute beds in hospital psychiatry departments to (in some cases) living on the streets.<sup>22</sup> The very fact of the latter is indicative of a seeming loss of order that the closure of the asylums has brought about. Of course in a sense the notion of care in the community was all along very misleading for there was no single site, the community, as such in which patients could be cared for, but rather dispersed and fragmented locations within the wider community. The shift from the asylum to care in the community presupposed that a whole raft of services, materials, spaces, times, and people would be in place and would be properly co-ordinated (in time) so as to ensure that the initiative would stand a chance of working. The fact that in many instances these were simply not in place or were insufficiently co-ordinated or resourced, inevitably gave rise to some of the problems associated with life outside the asylum.<sup>23</sup>

The move from the asylum to the community has been paralleled by other societal changes that have impacted on the service: for example, increasing levels of homelessness or the increasing number of offenders with mental illness now treated within the wider health service. The overall effect of such changes, as seen by one academic consultant psychiatrist writing in The Times Higher Education Supplement, are tantamount to a crisis:

Psychiatrists are continually ‘fire-fighting’, unable to manage patients because of factors beyond our control. Adult psychiatry has no boundaries: we are a refuge of last resort for society’s flotsam and jetsam, young and old, homeless or rootless, mad or bad. Deahl (1996)

In the limit the order and control of some patients in the community has effectively evaporated and there have been a number of tragic and highly publicized cases in which patients have put themselves in great danger, have caused significant harm to others, or even death. In one tragic case a mentally ill patient (Christopher Clunis) killed a perfect stranger (Jonathan Zito) on a railway station in 1992. In the public imagination, fuelled in part by the media, such cases have come to symbolize the apparent loss of order associated with care in the community. Though such incidents have been rare there has now been an official acceptance of the wider failings of the actual realization of care in the community:

Care in the community has failed because, while it improved the treatment of many people who were mentally ill, it left far too many walking the streets, often at risk to themselves and a nuisance to others. A small but significant minority have been a threat to others or themselves. Secretary of State for Health, Foreword to Modernising Mental Health Services (Department of Health, 1998)

In practical terms the shift from one regime or mode of organization to another presented new and significant problems of management, co-ordination and control for the services concerned. In contrast to the previously more controlled environment, there are far less controls over patients’ treatment regimes, but also under certain circumstances less flexibility as regards mental health professionals’ options.

The community psychiatrist’s main option—and perhaps the only one when confronted with someone out of control—is to admit to hospital, perhaps on a compulsory order, and sedate immediately. Bean and Mounser (1993, p. 125)

Taking a less extreme case we might consider the problem of trying to ensure that patients in the community keep appointments with the mental health services. In the community the dispersion of patients in space is compounded by a fragmentation in time. In the absence of the temporal symmetry (Zerubavel, 1979) imposed by the regimen of the asylum, the possession of a watch or a calendar is simply not enough to guarantee that patients will keep hospital appointments. Indeed, the use of a watch or calendar, as with any technology, presupposes a user who (to a greater or lesser extent) will operate the technology in accordance with the purposes for which it was designed. Time keeping technology alone cannot determine a patient’s attendance for an appointment. Rather, what is required is a commitment on the part of the patient, and a discipline that enables them to use the technology to keep to the place and time of the appointment.

In a significant number of cases such discipline is simply absent. In the community setting the patients potentially have much greater discretion as regards their interaction (or not) with the mental health services. Without notification they might move to another neighboring health authority, or even further afield. The comparatively simple problem of organizing medication in the context of an asylum becomes a formidable one when the physical barriers and the temporal order that went with it (e.g. the routine of daily medication) are no longer in place. Moreover, a person in an asylum may find it far more difficult to resist medication than someone who is not hospitalized and who can simply fail to come to a clinic for medication. As such a continual problem for mental health professionals is that of trying to secure a correspondence between the now fragmented (individualized) spaces and temporal ordering of patients’ lives and the spatio-temporal organization of mental health services. (Indeed, the problem of ‘patients and service users not remaining in contact with services’ has been highlighted as one of the failings of care in the community (Department of Health, 1998).)

The difficulties involved in trying to keep track of a dispersed and non-captive patient population have been compounded to a certain extent by the extant spatial organization of mental health service provision. The official inquiry into the Christopher Clunis case reported (among many other findings) that there was a tendency

to allow geographical boundaries to interfere with or curtail proper provision of care. (Department of Health, 1994a)

In other words, some staff within the mental health services saw their responsibilities as being delimited by the geographical areas covered by the particular health authorities that they worked for. The obvious danger in this is that if staff no longer assume responsibility for patients once they have moved out of their patch then they may become lost to the mental health service as a whole. Accordingly, spatial boundaries and by implication the mindset that went with them, were seen by the inquiry as something to be overcome. The inquiry was also scathing in its criticism of the lack of monitoring of the provision of mental health services.

No one has a means of telling whether the community service provided is effective or an efficient use of resources. The old certainties and principles of care within traditional institutions which might have been too rigid have disappeared but have not been replaced… In our view it is essential that the Department of Health sets up proper procedures for effective monitoring of psychiatric services within the community. (Department of Health, 1994a)

What complicates the picture is the fact that the dispersion due to the policy of care in the community has had a consequence in terms of the various groups, institutions and professionals that now have an enhanced responsibility as regards the provision and monitoring of services for the mentally ill.<sup>24</sup> Thus, the dispersion in space has generated an increased differentiation of the sites of psychiatric knowledge and practice. This has given rise to further potential sources of disorder—for example, a patient might become lost in the gap between NHS psychiatric services and social services—and in turn this has led to calls for networking and crossinstitutional/professional teams.

The picture that emerged in the aftermath of the Clunis affair was of mental health services that (at least in some districts) were in need of considerable tightening up and to achieve this would necessitate that they were made the object of external inspection. In turn this would require that both the provision of services and the management of such provision would have to be made visible. But in the fragmented times and spaces of care in the community such visibility would not be easy to achieve. This is where information and information systems have come to be seen as playing a crucial role in planning and documenting care as well as monitoring its delivery.

## 4. Order through information

Having discussed some of the issues surrounding the organization of mental health services in the NHS, let us now turn to some specific empirical illustrations of the argument to see how organization, time and space, subjects and technology are configured together. The material presented in the following section is drawn from qualitative research conducted within the psychiatry department of a large hospital responsible for the management of mental health services within a deprived inner city area. The department had a limited number of psychiatric beds, but also ran services within the community including the management of a number of small units that served as ‘half-way houses’ between hospitalized care on the one hand and full independence on the other. The initial contact with the department arose during the course of a larger piece of comparative research (1988–1995) on the deployment of IT within NHS hospitals when the department concerned had been identified as a suitable site for more intensive ethnographic work directed at following and understanding technology in the making (Latour, 1987). This particular department (and indeed the hospital of which it was a part) had something by way of a history as regards being proactive in relation to the introduction of IT, with some of the staff envisaging a number of areas in which new technology could potentially improve the management and delivery of services. Moreover, the senior manager in the department at the time (the clinical director) had published an academic book on mental health services and was very open to the idea of conducting research on what they were trying to achieve through IT.

The research was conducted with varying degrees of intensity between 1994 and 1997. A variety of research methods were employed during this research process, including 40 formal semi-structured interviews, participant observation (e.g. attendance at meetings, supporting the implementation of IT, membership of an audit team, etc.), informal discussions, and the review of a wide range of documentary material. The research commenced with a series of initial interviews conducted by the authors and was centered on some of the senior members of staff. The research then moved into a more intensively engaged phase in which one of the authors became a participant observer within the department. This particular role stemmed from an invitation to help out with the installation of a new computer system that would be used to manage and audit the delivery of services according to the CPA (see subsequently). In addition to the latter, which included somewhat ‘technical’ work, this dimension of the research also involved participation in various discussions about IT throughout the period and two major pieces of audit work. The first involved membership of a medical audit team charged with responsibility for assessing clinicians’ compliance with the recording procedures instituted through the CPA; the second centered on a review of a form used for needs assessment procedures. Becoming an accepted member of the team allowed the opportunity to conduct interviews with managers, consultant psychiatrists, nurses, IT staff, and other professionals covering a range of issues connected with IT and the management of mental health services. The issues arising from the research—both practical ones arising from the tasks associated with being a participant observer as well as academic ones—were discussed by both authors on a frequent and continuing basis.

In the remainder of this section we concentrate on two aspects of the research. First, the institution of the CPA and secondly, the temporal organization of mental health services in the context of emergency situations.

## 4.1. The Care Programme Approach

Against the backdrop of seeming disorder in the operational realization of care in the community,<sup>25</sup> the Department of Health and mental health service professionals have increasingly looked to information systems as a way of (re)asserting control: to monitor patients; maintain a register of those deemed to be at risk (to themselves or to others);<sup>26</sup> trace their movements across the boundaries of geographical and institutional spaces; to provide a safety net lest they fall through the network of care. In one way or another the information systems (paper based and electronic) introduced in mental health services serve as a means of making the subjects of psychiatric services (whether patients or mental health professionals) visible and accountable.

The CPA was published by the Department of Health in 1990 and became binding on health and local authorities on the 1st April 1991.<sup>27</sup> It stipulated that mental health care should be organized in a more systematic and co-ordinated way for those patients living in the community and should do so by ensuring a multi-agency approach that was offered on a continuous basis. Within the CPA guidelines three principle features stand out. First, an increased level of documentation in terms of inscribing mental health practice (e.g. needs assessments, reviews, care plans, etc.). Secondly, a greater sense of standardization particularly in following routine chronologically ordered processes (i.e. the requirement to comply with practice and complete forms in a certain order and within a stipulated time period). Thirdly, an improved level of patient involvement in the needs assessment process through participation in the derivation of care plans.<sup>28</sup> In short, the CPA was presented as a way of overcoming some of the problems of managing and organizing mentally ill patients who reside across multiple sites in the community through a process of documentation, co-ordination, communication and participation.<sup>29</sup> Since the inception of the CPA the Department of Health has continued to promote the importance of IT within the initiative:

All effective CPA information systems are likely to have a computer at their heart. (Department of Health, 2000, p. 20)

In other words, to be effective requires technology; technology that will help carry the burden of maintaining orderly procedures. Though there has been some variability across the service in this regard, our case study department embarked on the computerization of its CPA implementation as early as 1994.

The emphasis on documentation—on what we might describe as the writing of practice and hence, its opening up to surveillance, to inspection and audit—was a common interpretation of the CPA within the case study site. For example, one manager described the CPA in the following terms:

It [the CPA] is a way of ensuring everything is written down; which is big at the moment [presents the file produced by the Department of Health on ‘How to keep records’]. So when you go into court you can show that everything is written down. It’s no good saying that certain things about the patient had been discussed and decided upon, because unless it’s written down it won’t stand up in court. Psychiatric Service Manager (September, 1994)

In other words it was no longer enough merely to follow good practice, for the CPA required the increased documentation of certain practices—the production of a parallel textual or documentary reality—through the use of various forms and information systems. For example, documentation of the care planning process was operationalized through a specific CPA form, and when a patient was assessed the clinician concerned was required to indicate that the patient would be reviewed within a given time period (often six months). For some staff the CPA was seen as a way of documenting what was already done anyway.

Reviews are not new to the psychiatric service, especially to the CSS [Continuing Support Services] who perform regular reviews of the patient’s progress. The CPA is just a means of writing down that a review has taken place. Consultant Psychiatrist (October, 1994)

However, while the process of review may have been considered to be an integral aspect of everyday practice the increased level of standardization and formalization represented by the CPA process was more than a mere paper exercise. Arguably it represented a translation (in actor network terms) of mental health care. For example, as an inscription device the CPA form assembled together information on patients scattered across the city area for which the department was responsible, represented the assessment carried out by the mental health professionals, and also stipulated future actions. Moreover, patients who did not attend appointments (or scheduled reviews) became the subjects of a new category—did not attends. This helped to highlight possibly problematic cases, providing a focus for professional effort both to assist the individuals involved and to try and circumvent the risks (and thus potential disorder) associated with patients in crisis out in the community. In addition, when it came to reviews stipulated in CPA forms, whether these actually took place or not was the object of another CPA checking procedure at a later date.

The burgeoning paperwork associated with initiatives such as the CPA has increasingly served to render staff as providers of information and to the extent that some of the information is meant to inform their own practice, mental health professionals have also become managers of information (Bloomfield & McLean, 1996). Not surprisingly, there has been resistance to the increasing burden of paperwork across the NHS. For instance, one consultant psychiatrist (interview, October, 1994) argued:

There is an opportunity cost. As we spend more time filling in forms there is less time available for patient care.

While another consultant psychiatrist (not from the case study department) opined in The Guardian national newspaper:

Forms and committees have a part to play in the support systems, but are nothing but a hindrance when it comes to the primary task. You cannot cure schizophrenia with piles of paper—and if you try to give medical treatment by committee, you only get confusion. Launer (1996) our emphasis (see also Department of Health (1999, p. 86)).

This quotation expresses frustration at the way in which excessive paperwork can divert the clinician from direct or ‘hands-on’ clinical practice, it is also perhaps indicative of the time pressure and resultant stress experienced by those faced with the rising demands of patient case loads. Furthermore, as we shall see subsequently, systems put in place to standardize and document practice may not just displace time available for working with patients face-to-face, but might also distort aspects of professional practice if not designed to take proper account of the situated nature of that practice.

## 4.1.1. Forms, temporality and practice: the needs assessment schedule

As indicated earlier, the CPA promoted the participation of patients in the determination of their needs. Accordingly, the needs assessment schedule developed locally and introduced as part of the CPA aimed to be a comprehensive questionnaire to determine patient needs, assess outcomes, and document the entire process of care. It was meant to be completed by clinicians and patients together following a preset sequence of needs/problems each of which contained a list of prompts (i.e. questions) and a box in which the clinician rated the problem within a five-point scale. Additionally, the patient was requested to provide a rating of their level of satisfaction with the service provision for each given aspect of their care. Within this assessment process the lives of the patients were dissected in minute detail: the categories ranging from matters of self-care and diet to occupation, leisure and social contacts; from issues about alcohol and other drugs to sexual expression.

Asterisked items on the Scoresheet, ‘Self-Care and Diet’ and ‘Psychological Health’ are items which must be reported to the appropriate Consultant or Doctor immediately, if risk is suspected or apparent. This may be impulsive or deliberate self-harm or violence to others, wandering, maltreatment by or of the carer, severe self-neglect, or similar. Risk may not have been detected until the NAS interview, in which case you may be the first person to elicit it—so be sure to report it! Be specific, and say precisely and evidentially what you think the nature of the ‘risk’ is, to or from this client. Needs assessment schedule document (1996)

On completion, the form would then be sent to the CPA co-ordinator who would transfer certain details onto the CPA system database. These included items such as name of general practitioner, consultant psychiatrist dealing with their case, keyworker (i.e. the clinician or social services professional assigned to their case and delegated responsibility for managing it within the context of a multi-discipinary team), at risk status, a list of needs, and future review date. The upshot of the procedure was that patients were becoming subjected to a greater degree of scrutiny that rendered them thinkable and manageable in particular ways (Miller & Rose, 1986).<sup>30</sup> Moreover, insofar as the completed forms could then be subject to audit procedures, clinicians too were also being drawn into an enhanced regime of surveillance. Indeed, great significance has been placed on the need to conduct audits of the implementation and operationalization of the CPA in order to ensure accountability, and to address the sort of deficit (or lack of order) in the management of cases/records highlighted by the inquiry into the Clunis case. For example, in so far as the completion of the form stood as an account of professional’s work it could provide evidence of good (or alternatively bad) practice, thus enabling the comparison of staff performance over time. Following Power (1994), we might suggest that the audit dimension of the CPA represented the exercise of control over control (i.e. through the process of documenting good or bad practice).

Following a decision in the department to review the use of the needs assessment schedule this task was seen by staff as an obvious extension of the existing participatory research on the audit of the CPA. Accordingly, the material presented subsequently draws upon a series of interviews conducted with clinicians as part of this review, with the use of the form for the needs assessment schedule being the main focus of attention. Though the needs assessment schedule was a tool still undergoing development (rather than a finished technique) a discussion of its early use in practice is useful because of the issues it highlights, for example, in the area of patient and professional surveillance, as well as the tension between the demands of information gathering and the situated reality of professional practice.

The designers of the form had understood that it could not be applied mechanically. Indeed, clinicians were cautioned that what was meant to be part of the establishment of a relationship with patients ought not to degenerate into an interrogation. Nonetheless disparities existed between clinicians’ views of good practice and the constraints arising from the actual use of the form. In terms of the concerns of this paper, the form embodied a particular ordering derived from the specific sequencing of the areas/needs addressed. As inscribed in the form this order configured the patient–clinician relationship in a particular way. Thus, the form contained some 20 pre-defined categories, to be followed (as stated earlier) in a pre-set order according to a set of accompanying instructions. It was meant to be completed within a two week period. Thus, the specific organization of the form represented an orchestration of the interaction between patient and clinician. In turn, this represented a particular organization of time. However, this implicit temporal ordering conflicted with the professional knowledge and experience of a number of the clinicians interviewed. For example, one argued that the Schedule was useful only if carried out within a clinically appropriate time period:

It’s all right if you do the Needs Assessment Schedule over a longer period of time cos you can talk over some of those issues at each meeting.... Otherwise you get very superficial answers really, because the guy doesn’t really know you and you don’t know them, or how they’ll respond. So you know, they just say something and you just accept that. Continuing Support Services worker (March, 1997)

Similarly, the requirement to provide a quantifiable rating for all the 20 areas of need within the given two week time period was regarded by some of the community psychiatric nurses to be very problematic in certain cases. For some the practice of producing a patient rating without first being confident of the account presented by the patient, was actually to go against good practice:

You wouldn’t go against good practice to produce a rating. I would just hold fire before making a final judgement on the score until you can do more exploratory work to see what reality is… If you don’t have a reasonable therapeutic relationship with the person you won’t get past the first question anyway. Senior Clinical Nurse (March, 1997)

In other words, there was a mismatch or asymmetry between the implicit temporal order governing the design and stipulated use of the form and the variability in the length of time actually required to get to know a patient and understand their needs. This meant that if professionals had not completed the form within the stipulated two weeks that might indicate the difficulty of achieving a therapeutic relationship rather than bad practice.

Another area of concern arose in respect of the conceptualization of risk and the appearance (or not) of warning signals. Unlike previous attempts at needs assessment in the community served by the psychiatry department, the needs assessment schedule sought to address issues of risk in a more detailed and prescribed way. In part, this was a response to the greater demands placed on psychiatry departments to provide more formal and documented assessments of risk.<sup>31</sup> The mental health professionals had to assess the patient in the state in which they presented themselves during the completion of the Schedule. Based on this assessment they had to provide a score for this category (as with the others) on a scale of 1–5, and predict the level of risk in the future. Some of the difficulties posed by this are summed up in the following reflections on the procedure by one of the nurses:

We assess risk all the time but risk assessments are very dodgy things in a way. The most important thing to ensure is consistency of reporting and recognising that each individual you assess is going to have very clear differences…. You have to do risk assessment, but it has to be a very flexible issue and anyone who has done risk assessment will tell you that risk assessment is only valid for that point in time. I could assess you for risk now and you’ve not had a drink and then you could go home have a bottle of whisky and be a totally changed person. We are all potentially at risk to others. We go out in our cars and do some stupid things and get involved in road rage and also be at risk of harming someone. Risk assessment is a very difficult and tricky issue. We have to do it but when you get to the issues of scoring, does it mean that you have a flowchart and when someone scores five you immediately consult with the doctor and get them admitted? Community Psychiatric Nurse (April, 1997)

What this alerts us to is, again, the potential disparity between the implicit temporal order of the needs assessment schedule and practitioners’ knowledge of the variable temporal manifestation of at–risk behavior. Detecting patients at risk and capturing this in an information system may have been an important and laudable aim, but it was not a guarantee that individuals would be prevented from harming themselves or others.

What these disparities indicate is a tension between the demands of form filling and the established requirements of situated professional practice. On the one hand, we have the imperative of gaining information for the purposes of instituting/following the CPA, managing it and rendering it accountable—in other words, activities of categorizing and coding that we might describe as belonging to the order of information. On the other, we have the clinical knowledge and experience of the practitioner—that is, the embodied and situated order of professional practice.<sup>32</sup> The tension is another example of the commonly found divergence between the demands of record keeping conceived in the abstract (e.g. on paper or in the minds of the auditor) and situated reality (Garfinkel, 1967). And to the extent that the world of situated practice fails to conform to the paper world of the audit team an ongoing dynamic between the two is thereby sustained.

In this example we have been primarily concerned with information and information systems in relation to accountability and the temporal order of professional practice. In the following, we explore space and time in a more general sense in connection with the case of patients in the community seeking ‘out of hours’ care, as well as the further delegations to technology that have arisen in response to the demands for round-the-clock services.

## 4.2. Out of hours care in emergency situations

We are increasingly said to be moving into a so-called ‘24/7’ society, one in which access to goods and services is available 24 h a day, seven days a week. However, (at least in the past) this has definitely not been the case when it comes to healthcare services (or indeed many other services). One of the long-standing problems facing the organization and management of healthcare stems from the disparity between the temporal provision of medical resources (staff, beds, operating theatres etc.) and the actual manifestation of ill health. This applies across all the health services and not just those dealing with the mentally ill. Though some illnesses may well result from the temporal ordering of the modern world (e.g. shift work) the symptoms of disease are not confined to the temporal regularities according to which society is orchestrated. In broad terms, a heart attack occurs when it occurs irrespective of whether or not health professionals are available to attend. Similarly, acute signs of mental distress occur when they occur, during the day or night, during the week or at weekends. In short, the temporal patterns of illness and the temporal provision of care have been frequently poorly synchronized. For a long time demands for 24 h services were translated within the NHS as an unrealistic cost; the NHS just could not afford it. Put another way, the rationing of healthcare resources (lack of money) meant a lack of time (in terms of the provision of services). In practice then it was simply not possible to provide the same level of cover 24 h a day and so historically the bulk of resources have been organized according to the conventional notion of normal working hours.

Yet, research (e.g. Gray & Baulcombe, 1996) has indicated that the paucity of out-of-hours cover poses a significant problem both for health professionals as well as patients.

Out-of-hours mental health services were a great concern to most service providers….Lack of flexible resources, particularly staff, and a lack of provision for clients not requiring hospitalisation and those with challenging behaviors, were widely voiced concerns…. Poor communication within and between services, in particular between night and daytime services within multidisciplinary teams, added to the confusion for clients. Gray and Baulcombe (1996, p. 25)

The issue of service provision outside of normal working hours was also highlighted by clinicians within our case study psychiatry department. In particular, staff reported that the problems associated with the long-term reduction in the number of in-patient beds became most notable during the nights and over the weekends when patients could be in crisis and needed access to professional services. At the time of the research individuals who required treatment during these periods would typically attend a hospital accident and emergency department where they would (usually) be seen by doctors with limited psychiatric training (e.g. a junior doctor). Any preexisting information concerning the patient’s details, including suggested strategies of care in such situations, would be unavailable to the doctors on duty. Going back to our earlier discussion of needs assessment, we might say that even if needs were captured on paper they tended to remain fixed in place and were not mobile or accessible in all those contexts where they might prove useful to other health professionals. As one psychiatric nurse described the problem:

To work effectively you need live access to someone’s notes. A classic case. I saw someone on Friday who I assessed and I’ve got a good knowledge of. They described very clear symptoms of illness, however I did not feel overly concerned about them and planned to see them in a week’s time. That person, after 5 p.m., presented himself at casualty. The junior doctor [who] saw them didn’t have access to my notes cos they are locked away here, didn’t have access to the person’s general notes because the consultant had taken them home to review them. Community Psychiatric Nurse (April, 1997)

When mentally ill patients presented in casualty departments outside of normal working hours the doctors on duty were placed in a difficult situation as regards the best course of action to take. The circulation of official NHS reports and recommendations emphasizing the need to treat all patients who appear to be a danger to themselves or society encouraged doctors to select the least risky option available to them— namely, to admit such individuals as in-patients. The nurse continued:

the doctor was left with no intimate knowledge of this client and had to admit them. So this person ended up out of area… Whereas, if they [the junior doctor] had access to my notes which showed that I’d seen them that day and felt that there was no risk whatsoever... then they could have made a much more informed decision and sent the person home. We’re looking at all sorts of issues such as hospital days, costs, and a totally inappropriate assessment. Until these sort of systems come into place, we can assess someone until the cows come home and it makes not a blind bit of difference.Community Psychiatric Nurse (April, 1997)

In this example the patient was admitted as an in-patient, but to a unit located outside the district—that is, out-of-area. There were only a limited number of beds within the district available for the admission of psychiatric patients, and when this capacity was reached any such additional patient had to be relocated in units outside the operational area covering the department. Not surprisingly some patients and/or their relatives did not like this arrangement and it would appear that in some cases they even deferred seeking help out-of-hours in order to avoid the possibility of being sent out-of-area. The latter represented a further element of uncertainty for the patient as well as providing potential travel difficulties for their relatives. Expressed theoretically, when patients were sent to in-patient beds in another area the problem of temporal disparity between the provision of services and patient needs became translated into a spatial dis-location; out-of-hours became out-of-area. So, space and time are intimately connected, changes in one have ramifications in the other and vice versa. Hence together, in any particular instance, they represent a spatio-temporal order.

Staff in the department were acutely aware of the unsatisfactory nature of the practice of sending patients out-of-area. Indeed, we might say that in a sense locations have an identity, from the perspective of patients with a history of mental illness they are populated with familiar faces, with staff who are likely to know something about them, and families who have easier access in terms of visiting them. In fact, there may be an intimate connection between the shaping of identity of those experiencing mental distress and the particular geography of services with which they interact (see Parr & Philo, 1995).

The difficulties posed by medical decision making in the context of emergency care were further complicated by financial considerations. In-patient beds are costly to maintain, but the out-of-area referrals also represented an expensive variable cost to the department and the hospital of which it was a part. Accordingly, for some time the department had been considering ways in which service cover in out-ofhours periods could be improved while simultaneously keeping costs under control. One idea was to put together teams of professionals to provide emergency out-ofhours cover, but this too was seen to represent a costly option; while another was to significantly improve the degree of integration of services. The latter was expressed particularly strongly by a Service Manager in charge of the Rehabilitation group within the department who felt that:

There is a real need to pull together this very disparate service which is managed separately and operates more on a basis of opportunity than through any strategic plan, especially for patients who need attention during the night, over the holiday periods and the weekend. Psychiatric Service Manager (November, 1994)

This manager also believed that the provision of a team of workers to cover out-ofhours situations would reduce the number of in-patient stays and provide a more efficient and effective service.<sup>33</sup> In other words, the cost of such teams had to take into account more than the immediate issue of staffing costs; it must be balanced against any benefits in terms of patient care and indeed the prevention of longerterm demands on resources arising from the limited level of care available out-ofhours. One suggested way forward was the notion of a community based team together with the development of a fully integrated information system linked to the casualty department, thus providing a more general access to basic clinical details relating to the diagnosis of the patient and any plan of action for emergency situations. In particular, it was felt that it would have helped in overcoming some of the problems associated with the mismatch between the temporal provision of mental health services and the actual manifestation of mental health problems.

The deliberations about temporal access to services can be seen to highlight another aspect of time namely, as a discursive device, a symbolic resource in arguments about how organizations should be organized.<sup>34</sup> Hitherto the financial arguments about the cost of such care within the NHS have held sway, but it would seem that this is no longer the case for in fact the need for access to services 24 h a day, seven days a week, has now been accepted at the highest level. It is to be a feature of the ‘modernization’ of mental health services, in general, and the CPA, in particular:

The principle is getting people to the right place for the right intervention at the right time. (Department of Health, 2000, p. 4, emphasis in original)

This modernization centers on a 3-year programme involving some additional £700 million of expenditure on mental health services.

[W]e are preparing radical changes to improve the treatment of mental illness so it provides a system in which patients, carers and the public are safe and where security and support is provided to all who need it… we propose 24 crisis teams to respond to emergencies… access to NHS Direct for 24 hour help and advice. Secretary of State for Health (Department of Health, 1998: foreword)

NHS Direct is a new initiative that is currently being implemented across the service. Essentially, it is a sophisticated telephone service (a national set of ‘call centers’) based on the integration of telephony and computing. It is staffed by nurses who give health advice and also deal with emergency calls, filtering out those cases that can be dealt with via online advice and reassurance from those that genuinely require hands-on expert medical intervention. In this aspect of its operation decision making by staff at NHS Direct is supported by software. In terms of the concerns of this paper, NHS Direct (as with other examples of telemedicine) addresses the problem of scarce medical resources coupled with the spatial and temporal distribution of people with health related problems. Thus, a small number of expert staff with access to knowledge bases and decision making software can seek to address the demands of a large population; the experts cannot be brought (physically) to the people with problems who need their expertise (in all locations and at all times), but through the technology they can (in a sense) be brought together (virtually). Thus, the NHS Direct initiative represents yet another development in the chain of delegations to technology experienced within the mental health services. By implication the network of the virtual asylum, and its spatio-temporal order, is extended further to include both the NHS Direct call centers and the people who access them from a multiplicity of sites across the community.

## 5. Discussion

The ideas aired previously are not confined to the specifics of mental health care and so in this the penultimate section we discuss a number of points of more general relevance to the study of information, technology, and organization.

First, our approach here has not been concerned so much with the social consequences of the introduction of technology on mental health services as much as understanding the role of technology within a given problematic of organizing. When patients resided in an asylum they could in effect be observed at will, but in the context of care in the community this is no longer possible. Instead they are rendered visible though information. Visibility in this context acts as a condition for the exercise of control. In addition, the work of the various mental health professions is meant to be co-ordinated and has become subject to audit—requiring that the professionals too must become visible. Accordingly, the temporal and spatial reordering of mental health care has led to an emphasis on the surveillance and networking capabilities of information technology: surveillance to track the movements of patients and their interaction with various mental health services, and to record the delivery of services according to prescribed standards; and networking in order to allow a degree of integration of the otherwise disparate services and agencies. Technology then has become a vital part of managing in the context of care in the community, in theory it seems to offer the prospect of overcoming temporal asymmetry and spatial fragmentation across diverse professionals and patients in the community. Having stated that however, we must stress the heterogeneous character of the problem domain—that is, technology, the audit of professional practice, bodies, professional boundaries, inscriptions, spaces, and times, as well as notions such as value for money or the desire for a more humane system of care, are inextricably intertwined. Technology then is not a cause or determinant of social order, but rather should be understood as an integral part of a given problematic in the context of organizing. If we highlight the role of technology we must do so in the context of understanding the conditions of possibility whereby it comes to be seen as the solution to particular problems of organizing. The challenges of mental health care and the transition from the asylum to care in the community set the context that has shaped the specific configuration of people, technology, time and space we addressed in Section 4. And of course once set in train, the deployment of technology (in the form of information practices and information systems) has helped shape its context of use.

Following on from this, a second point derives from the ongoing dynamic that is associated with delegations to technology. The examples discussed earlier were used to illustrate the emergence of a problematic concerning the management of mental health services in which the deficit in control and discipline is addressed through more technology. Thus, the delegation to architecture and to pharmacological drugs is now complemented by a (much enhanced) delegation to information and information systems. Through these patients are inscribed, assessed, monitored, and managed, and at the same time staff are rendered accountable. But technology tends to beget technology; delegation to a technology is not an endpoint, but merely a link in an ongoing chain of expanding associations (Bloomfield, 1995). To take a different example, consider an organization that makes its first significant investments in computer based information systems. The technology becomes so significant that its management becomes the object of strategic attention i.e. it is deemed necessary to have an IT strategy. The move to e-mail and Internet access brings forth demands for security in the shape of firewalls, anti-virus software, and written policies on legitimate usage. Increasing dependence on the technology becomes an issue of risk management and demands to put in place measures to provide system back-up in the event of serious service outage. Accordingly, we might suggest that implementing new technology often turns out not to be a one-off investment, a singular event in time, but rather becomes part of an ongoing process marked by increasing heterogeneity, and therefore, increasing complexity and interdependence between people, space, time, technologies, inscriptions and assorted materials, and so on.

Thirdly, we have seen that there is something of a gap or disparity between official moves to manage or control mental health services and practice on the ground (i.e. within the multiplicity of sites in the wider community). In a sense this is another example of the frequently observed tension between theory and practice. Managerial initiatives formulated on paper, for example in the shape of Department of Health directives such as the CPA, seek to effect changes in practice, to reconstruct the world of professionals and patients according to the image of organization implicit in the text. But inevitably the world fails to conform to such paper prescriptions (Bloomfield & Vurdubakis, 1995). Returning to the previous point, we might surmise that whatever the panoply of technologies that is put in place some of the patients of mental health services are likely to continue to fall through the network of care. Moreover, in respect of the needs assessment schedule we argued that the gap between theory and practice can be viewed as a disparity between the implicit temporal order of practice conceived in the abstract and that of situated reality. Thus, in Akrich’s (1992) terms, the inscribed use/user of the technology failed to take account of the particular temporal order often necessary to establish a relationship of trust with patients suffering from acute conditions. These observations connect to the wider discussions within the information systems field about end-user resistance to new technology and thus, the need for effective forms of participation in IS design. In other words, we might add asymmetry of temporal order (between inscribed design and situated practice) to the list of issues that complicate the implementation of IS.

Finally, a fourth point concerns the notion of the virtual organization. Our discussion of care in the community points to an interesting contrast between some of the rhetoric promoting the concept of the virtual organization (Davidow & Malone, 1993; Mowshowitz, 1994) and the problems of organizing in the setting of the virtual asylum. According to the prophets of the virtual organization, the dissolution of conventional ways of organizing in time and space (so-called bricks and mortar) is seen as empowering or emancipatory for business organizations. However, as we have seen here, in the context of care in the community, the spatial and temporal fragmentation resulting from the loss of the institutional co-ordination and specific physical configuration of space and time represents a significant challenge to the management of services. It is precisely the melting away of the spatio-temporal order of the asylum that can be seen to pose so many difficulties for staff and patients alike. While we would not deny the well documented problems of asylum based care, it is evident that for some patients temporary residence in some form of hospital unit may prove a lifeline. Accepting the many obvious differences between these two examples of virtuality might the problems of the virtual asylum nonetheless inform the more general debate on virtual organizations? In particular, insofar as the view of time and space held by proponents of virtual organizations reflects a physical/natural science conceptualization it thereby tends to neglect the social or cultural dimensions of spatio-temporal order. Accordingly, talk of overcoming the constraints of space and time—and thus, in effect rendering time and space as objects of organization at an ever more abstract level—ignores the fact that human subjects are always situated. In other words, the concept of the virtual business organization can be seen as a denial or even erasure of context (see also Walsham, 1994). Yet context and the spatio-temporal order that goes with it form part-and-parcel of the system of meaning vital to any human activity and sense of orientation in the world. Accordingly, we might note that in the call center industry one can find examples in which an operations center in one location provides a service to users that are located in a different region, country or even on a different continent. In certain cases the lack of common context (perhaps marked by an asymmetry of space, time, accent and culture) is addressed by giving the call center agents specific situated information pertinent to the distant domain (e.g. meteorological conditions or social/cultural events) that they can convey to users (alongside that specifically associated with the service) in order to foster a sense of collocation between them. For example, it has been reported that agents in India dealing with North American users have been given accent training and might chat about the weather—say, perhaps, about the heavy fall of snow last night—when they as individuals are far removed from the particular experience referred to. Thus, in such cases the lack of context can be addressed though not entirely solved; but in others it might prove a much more significant barrier to effective communication. Thus, we might note that in the case of NHS Direct the call center nurses tend to be risk averse with certain categories of caller or sets of symptoms—after all they cannot see the caller nor appraise the context—and refer such cases to accident and emergency departments at general hospitals. Virtuality has its attractions but also its limitations and knowing what they are is crucial.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper has addressed the problem of organizing in space and time with specific reference to the management of mental health services in the context of the transition from the asylum to care in the community. That transition has taken place against a background of other fundamental changes in the NHS—including the drive for efficiency and the demand that services should be audited. The twin demands of efficiency and effectiveness (particularly as regards the problem of patients becoming lost to the system and the dangers that this can pose) have had important consequences both for mental health professionals (and patients) and the various information systems that have been developed and implemented in mental health services. Given the demands for accountability together with the difficulties posed by the growing fragmentation of time and space in the community setting, it would seem that the further deployment of information systems in the delivery of mental health services is inevitable. As such is likely to provide a continuing focus of academic interest.
