# Networks, negotiations, and new times: the implementation of enterprise resource planning into an academic administration<sup></sup>

S.V. Scott <sup>a</sup>, E.L. Wagner <sup>b,</sup>∗

<sup>a</sup> Information Systems Department, The London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton St, London WC2A 2AE, UK

<sup>b</sup> School of Hotel Administration, Cornell University, 340 Statler Hall, Ithaca, NY 14853-6902, USA

## Abstract

Higher education is a sector entering an era of IT-enabled modernization in which it may have to cope with an influx of unfamiliar corporate concepts and practices. This paper analyzes one of the first Enterprise Resource Planning implementation projects within the academic administration of an Ivy League university. We contribute to existing qualitative literature in information systems by developing the theme of temporality within actor–network theory to support our analysis. This enables us to extend process-oriented ERP research by focusing on the identification of temporal zones and creation of durable work times designed to re-order priorities between competing visions for the future of higher education. We analyze detailed negotiations during periods of controversy to reveal how standard work practices come to be created and recreated. We consider how the ERP that emerges is affected by progressive trials of strength during the project and analyze the achievement of order as an on-going process. Our findings highlight the distinctive contribution that a ‘temporal turn’ can bring to longitudinal research studies by providing insight into the technical agency of ERP packages and how its temporal inscriptions shaped the emergence of a socio-technical information system. This reordered organizational work life and created a hybrid temporality that still needs to be negotiated into the working rhythms of the University’s actors.  2003 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Interpretivist perspective; IS implementation; Enterprise resource planning; Negotiation; Organizational change; Actor–network theory; Sociology of time; Narrative; Academic administration

## 1. Introduction

The widespread adoption and appropriation of enterprise resource planning (ERP) technology across a variety of organizational contexts and geographical locations in recent years is well documented (Davenport, 2000; Soh, Kien, & Tay-Yap, 2000; Hossain, Patrick, & Rashid, 2002). A significant body of work has emerged identifying the issues that organizations confront in localizing these standard packages (see Rogowsky & Somers, 2002), emphasizing the importance of our continued efforts to understand the effects of large systems projects such as ERP on organizational agendas. This literature provides the necessary groundwork for thinking about how technologies are introduced, shaped, and reshaped over time and with significant organizational consequences.

In this research paper we attempt to contribute to the development of theoretical approaches focused on this process of technological design and implementation. Our case study highlights how contradictions are created by attempts to include multiple perspectives into an ERP system that is expected to be used in daily operations by diverse stakeholders in an Ivy League university. We draw on and extend contemporary thinking about technology design, actor–networks, and agency by foregrounding implicit temporal features involved in negotiating and translating interests. Our aim is to understand how organizations negotiate with ERP technology in an attempt to create a local information system. We begin by providing some background into the ERP information systems phenomena.

The last decade of the 20th century heralded ‘The Enterprise Resource Planning Revolution’ (Ross, 1998) with enterprise systems implemented within most Fortune 500 companies (Kumar & van Hillegersberg, 2000). Business leaders, persuaded by the concept of an emerging global marketplace (see Castells, 1996; Held, 1999), were convinced by the technology’s promise to streamline organizational activities, eliminate duplication of effort and data, and co-ordinate business operations across geographically dispersed locations (Davenport, 2000; Markus, Tanis & Tanis, 2000). International management consultancies were a driving force (Walsham, 2001) behind the proliferation of the trend, as they worked with software vendors to sell ERP as an appropriate ‘solution’ for multiple markets (Soh, Kien, & Tay-Yap, 2000; Walsham, 2001). Fuelled by media coverage of the feared year two-thousand (Y2K) millennium bug, the trend increased as a mass of organizations from a variety of industries, jumped on the ERP bandwagon (Kremers and Dissel, 2000; Kumar & van Hillegersberg, 2000). By early 2000, ERP-related sales generated \$40 billion in revenue split between software vendors and consulting firms (Willcocks & Sykes, 2000) and literature claimed that “the business world’s embrace of enterprise systems may in fact be the most important development in the corporate use of information technology in the 1990s” (Davenport, 1998, p. 122).

While a great deal of support can be generated for adopting the fashionable business trends of ERP, Swanson (2003, p. 24) notes that these grand visions offer promises that distract organizations from ‘knowing why they selected the ERP, and how to successfully realize its promises’ as a workable socio-technical infrastructure. The financial investment required to implement ERP technology is exponentially higher than other IS initiatives with costs reaching over \$500 million (Markus, 1999) sending companies into litigation (Nash, 2000; Boudette, 1999; MacDonald, 1999) and bankruptcy (Nash, 2000; Montoya, 1998). This level of investment has the potential to increase the risk position of even the wealthiest organizations and intensify the pressure for the ERP to deliver breakthroughs in organization-wide efficiency. High expectations sit upon the shoulders of project teams mandated to fulfill whole-house requirements in which functionality must be robust within and across individual software modules. In sum, organizations are finding themselves caught between the perceived need to implement ERP and the challenge of realizing the benefits from them.

The scope of ERP projects and their inherent risks challenge IS researchers to draw insights from the analysis of historically situated studies to inform the work of practitioners (Markus, 1999). In this paper we respond by proposing a theoretical approach that sheds light on negotiating multiple agencies of change and order over time and provides insight into the relationship between technological design and organizational work life. Theorizing the technical agency of information technology, and how design decisions shape the emergence of a socio-technical infrastructure and its accompanying work practices, is fundamental to conceptualizing the boundaries of the technology and ways in which human agency fits within its borders. We author three project narratives identified during our analysis that highlight the complex interplay between actor networks and the ERP technology over time as they work to achieve their preferred future during the flow of events surrounding this program of enterprise-wide IT-enabled change.

Although we identify the preferred futures of different actor–networks, our conclusions do not suggest a simple polarity of winners and losers. Instead, the findings presented indicate a messier hybrid socio-technical system in which conditions of dependence come to be recognized and uncomfortably accommodated with repairs, bolt-on or shadow systems in order to ‘make it work’. Whether or not this constitutes a success or failure becomes an expression of political position amongst those involved. Despite internal organizational turmoil or politics this ERP is a present day ‘matter of fact’, a part of on-going work experience, and the work involved in making it function has to be reluctantly absorbed by those whose work lives it touches.

We suggest that this highlights the significant social ‘cost’ that may be associated with the process of replacing an existing infrastructure with another. Through our narrative analysis we reveal the effort involved in establishing new forms of permanence that, for better or worse, must become accepted as ‘part of the furniture’. The main theme that we explore centers around the question: what constitutes the achievement of order in the context of this organization-wide ERP project? Trials of strength between the competing actor–networks we describe over the course of a three-year ERP project highlight on-going processes of ‘ordering’ and ‘changing’.

Our temporal turn within ANT provides insight into popular notions such as windows of opportunity (Orlikowski & Tyre, 1994), emergence (Mead, 1980) and drift (Ciborra, 1996), not only through a study of ERP in context, but by explicitly examining information systems implementation in the flow of time. We suggest that effective ERP implementation is not just about involving stakeholders, but also about the timing and nature of that participation. Finally, we maintain that it is important to understand the process through which some things go forward and others get left behind during an ERP project as this influences the quality of continuity and permanence achieved. In the next section we present the theoretical foundations of the paper and introduce key terms and concepts used in the analysis.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

In this section we outline the theoretical foundations of the paper, providing a brief overview of actor–network theory (ANT) before focusing on its conjunction with the theme of temporality. We chose ANT because we were studying a program of extensive IT-enabled modernization at Ivy and ANT is a theory dedicated to understanding change and order (Monteiro, 2000). ERP is such an alien corporate entity for the academic sector, it seemed likely that it would bring considerable upheaval; ANT frames controversial events and presents them as a hub to study the negotiations associated with moving from one state to another. For actor–network theorists the achievement of order is on-going; it is an actively negotiated outcome. As a consequence, the maintenance of order comes at some cost within a community. We adopted a narrative approach to data gathering to document these negotiations and take into account the compromises or ‘cost’ at which order was achieved. As the ERP project at Ivy was designed to produce global standards for academic administration we also became interested in how the intention to bring about order can have unintended side-effects and frame conditions for subsequent developmental phases or prime future controversies. ANT supports an analysis of these issues, and through its particular treatment of both technological and human agency helps us better understand the implications that can be drawn from developments like ERP. A brief overview of the basic tenets of ANT may help those readers unfamiliar with it.

Actor–network theory was originally conceived by Bruno Latour (1987) and Michel Callon (1986) as part of the field of science and technology studies (STS) and has been applied by a variety of IS process-oriented researchers (Boland and Schultze, 1996; Ciborra & associates, 2000; Hanseth & Braa, 1999; McMaster, Vidgen, & Wastell, 1998; Quattrone & Hopper, 2001) to remind us that technology is not just a static artifact, able to be introduced within a variety of organizational contexts without conflict. Rather, these non-human entities are interpreted as political actors, capable of being shaped by domain experts but also limited by design in the way they can be modified. Actor–network theoreticians advocate a broad sense of agency because they argue there is no longer a clear delineation between the purely social and the technical. Instead, a network metaphor focuses attention on the relationships between entities as they express political and social interests and attempt to influence others over time.

Actor–network theory’s overall premise focuses on how the work of society is accomplished (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987). Rather than viewing society as consisting of static entities and structures within which scholars can conduct research, the proponents of this approach conceptualize human and non-human actors as working together to create social environments. The localization of technology into the everyday fabric of an organization is conceptualized as an achievement of order resulting from successive negotiations between heterogeneous actor–networks (Monteiro, 2000). Focusing on these processes foregrounds the emergent and progressive manner in which order is achieved and relationships are established (Kavanagh & Araujo, 1995). Insight into these negotiations can be gained over time if researchers follow processes of enrollment and translation (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1999; Law, 1992), where the actors’ interests become aligned with others.

We began our empirical research of the computer-mediated change initiative at an interesting moment in Ivy’s history where top leadership enrolls the University community into a high-profile modernization initiative. As such, a group—or network—is formed that connects the interests of multiple actors such as Ivy, Oracle Corporation, US higher education institutions, the ERP software product, and legacy information systems. This network is constituted by the relationships between these actors and indicates that other groups are being dismantled as a result of the enrolment with the VP and his vision of an ERP-based infrastructure.

The procurement of standard ERP software from a multinational vendor represents the strengthening of one network and the weakening of connections between those in the university who preferred to upgrade legacy systems. Similarly, alternative network alliances with vendors such as PeopleSoft are closed down when the University signs a contract with Oracle Corporation. This moment of enrollment is an interesting starting point for inquiry into the change process. As Latour says “let us study the bombardment of offers from contradictory social groups because by sitting at the controversy of group formation we can compare group making to group making” (Latour, 2001a).

The creation of one group over another is an act of enrollment and a translation of interests: a process that illuminates the back and forth transformation between collectives of human and non-human actors (Latour, 1999). Following acts of translation provide insight into how individuals and groups negotiate for the dominance of their working rhythms, ordering of the world, and pace of activity. In other words, translation is a process of reordering the dominant temporal landscape and cycles of activity by making decisions about “what will be carried forward into the future, and what will be left behind to make room for the new” (Latour, 1999, p. 71). This transformation is a key tenet of actor–network theory where translation results in the formation of a new network configuration where the present is similar but different from the past.

Some authors have begun to explore the conjunction between ANT and temporality (Kavanagh & Araujo, 1995). The theme of time is key to all the issues of interest to ANT mentioned above: change, multiplicity, past, present and future. Indeed, leading ANT scholars have hosted conferences (Latour, 2001b) exploring the conceptual interweaving of ANT and temporal theories. Despite this there are relatively few publications exploring this conjunction, which we attribute to its esoteric philosophical complexity and the semantic challenges that it presents.

However, there is a body of literature that provides authors interested in time as a lens through which to examine IT-enabled modernization in organizations with a basis from which to explore this area further (Butler, 1996; Clark, 1995; Das, 1992; Hassard, 1990; Lee, 1999; Lee & Liebenau, 1999; Mosakowski & Earley, 2000; Orlikowski & Yates, 1999; Sahay, 1997). These authors choose to draw the complexity of time into their studies, not because they accord it greater importance than other phenomenon, but because focusing on time provides us with further scholarly purpose. Engaging in the ‘temporal turn’ generates distinctive analytical insights that highlight potentially valuable perspectives on organizational processes and patterns.

Time is already incorporated in research in many ways, but usually centered around clock time as a resource and a structural parameter. Subjective views of time and the temporal perceptions of actors are generally ignored, yet we know that concepts of time vary dramatically across individuals and cultures (Mosakowski & Earley, 2000). In this study we build upon the theoretical stance that clock time is only one of multiple times that come to bear on our lives simultaneously (Adam, 1990). We can illustrate this point in the following way: the ERP project in our study took place over 3 clock years, however within this it was constituted by multiple subjective times (see Table 1), for example the perception that they were experiencing a period of uncertain industry transformation and the sense that this was “the right time for change”. The perception of subjective time is key to the notion of multiple times used in this paper and we follow Sorokin (1943) in suggesting that they determine the meaning attributed to specific events within a sociological system, affecting individual and collective behaviors.

From within the richness and complex mass of multiple times we cluster together particular subjective temporal perceptions to construct a notion of project times in order to explore the extent to which they may have shaped the distinctive implementation of the ERP system in our study and its consequences for those involved. We define project times as an identifiable ebb and flow in meaning constituted by shifting actor network processes that affects responses and conditions outcomes. We suggest that one project may hold within it different and often conflicting definitions of its meaning. An important part of these actor network dynamics are the negotiations that take place about resources over time as they work to achieve their preferred future during the flow of events surrounding this program of enterprise-wide ITenabled change.

We view this process of negotiation between actor–networks through the emergence of ‘temporal zones’ and, for the purposes of analysis, organize the presentation of our analysis by bracketing three specific temporal zones: the historic vision surrounding the ERP system procurement; the creation of a ‘project’; and the attempt to achieve order. These temporal zones mark different social realities and shifts in meaning, one could regard them as relationships and rationalities expressed in temporal form. Temporal zones are scaleable, which means that either an organization, project or person may hold together a temporal zone.

A university illuminates the notion of a temporal zone nicely. An academic year is organized around semesters and classes each of a specified duration. There are familiar traditions and rituals, like graduation ceremonies. Yet in each repetition we find difference as every person, clique and year group imbues events with their own meaning. Student careers last a few years, while academic and administrative professionals stay longer. As actors we have to flip between temporal zones and be able to manage potential tensions or collisions between multiple times in our lives as we traverse temporal zones (Kavanagh & Araujo, 1995).

The particular organizing logic of a temporal zone emerges as delegate actors within actor–networks strengthen the connections between selected temporalities (perhaps the rhythms, routines, flow, or pace) in order to realize a preferred future. Artifacts are inscribed to hold particular temporalities in relation to one another, for example a school bell ringing to mark the change of class. Work flow and the rhythm at which the pace of the day must be lived are fused together to create a hybrid: a human–nonhuman collective. For example, a final examination is constituted by students, teachers, exam papers and a particular arrangement of furniture.

We see in this last example, where students have to subject themselves to a schedule of exams, that it is not always possible for multiple times to democratically coexist. Power relations are implied in the organization of temporality. Inscribed times often initiate an unintended trial of strength (Callon, 1986, 1998b; Kavanagh & Araujo, 1995; Latour, 1999) between actor networks associated with a temporal zone or its key features. During these trials different agencies negotiate and some come to dominate resulting in the ability to shape possible future opportunities. Allies are called upon from the past, present, and future in an effort to persuade others to subscribe to and adopt the embedded temporality (Kavanagh & Araujo, 1995; Latour, 1999).

Trials of strength engender controversies indicating the absence of a stabilized knowledge base (Callon, 1998a). It is during this time that various actors become involved and present different interpretations of how they see the future. A controversy is resolved if actors agree on a proposed solution and metaphorically pass through an obligatory passage point (Callon, 1986). The translation of interests through this obligatory passage point marks a significant moment where change was negotiated and the interests of actors are reordered (for example a doctoral student must pass an exam before advancing in their career. In moving through the obligatory point of the oral defense, the student and examiners come to a shared understanding of the thesis in relation to future research endeavors).

Translation is often accompanied by compromise as only some interests will be enrolled and persuaded to pass through the obligatory point. It is the connections between what is abandoned, what is transported over time, and what is created in the process that provide insight into negotiating change. Throughout this process of transformation there are moments of emergence or meaning-making. In other words, as the present emerges the actor makes sense of it, adjusting their interpretation of the past and future accordingly (Adam, 1990; Mead, 1980). This represents a further level of re-ordering our understanding of the world—a revised notion of ‘the now’.

The temporalities that survive (or are created through) the trial of strength develop strong characteristics of irreversibility (Callon, 1991) as a result of their multiple inscriptions throughout the translation process. This durable time comes at a cost; the negotiations and compromises that were necessary to enroll interests may be primers for potential controversies ahead and condition the next phase of development. Each trial of strength builds upon the other in a progressive, but nonlinear manner. In this way the future may become colonized by a series of past events (Kavanagh & Araujo, 1995). This is of particular importance to the study of information systems where material inscription of interests result in the design of an operating environment with particular temporal logic. As Latour (1991) writes, “technology is society made durable”; by following multiple agencies we can gain insight into the ways in which Ivy University moves from an orderly state, through change, toward a re-ordering of their temporal zones and the crafting of durability.

## 3. Research methodology

This paper presents the results of an interpretive research project conducted between June 1999 and August 2000 at an Ivy League university. The field research was designed with the aim of providing a theoretical abstraction of IT-enabled change as it is experienced and made sense of by organizational actors. An interpretive epistemology means that we understand reality to be a social construction that can be articulated as a result of human sense making activities (Walsham, 1993). Access to these interpretations is gained through the collection and analysis of language, symbols and artifacts. These data are made meaningful through a hermeneutic process of interpretation defined as “the fundamental principle of interpretive field research” (Klein & Myers, 1999).

Hermeneutics suggests that the world can be interpreted as text-analogues—or narratives—where meaning is appropriated through iterative ‘readings’ of the situation. In other words, we come to understand the whole (the research context) as informing, and being informed by its constituent parts (individual stories, technologies, business processes). Similarly, theoretical generalizations are informed by instantiations of practice and vice versa. In this way theoretical explanations derive from descriptions of empirical data:

If we display a socio-technical network—defining trajectories by actants’ association and substitution, defining actants by all the trajectories in which they enter, by following translations and, finally, by varying the observer’s point of view— we have no need to look for any additional causes. The explanation emerges once the description is saturated. (Latour, 1991, p. 129)

To obtain such explanations we sought out multiple perspectives and analyzed the ways in which these network interests interconnected to impact coordinated action (Callon, 1991; Latour, 1999).

Attending to narrative texts over time can provide insight into multiple agencies of change and order that come to constitute a project initiative. When analyzing interview data, the aim is not to find the interviewee/s who gave the answer closest to the truth, but rather to understand the processes and patterns revealed in multiple interpretations. Narratives tend to be constructed as a convincing explanation of ‘why things are the way they are’ (Bruner, 1986, 1990; Czarniawska, 1998). Collectively analyzing these narratives provides insight into how coordinated outcomes are/are not achieved during complex IT-enabled change initiatives (Boland, Tenkasi and Te’eni, 1994).

We recognize the production of narrative texts as a performance where events are retold to an audience in a specific order and for particular effect (Boland & Schultze, 1996; Czarniawska, 1998). Narrating events is the act of selectively retelling a story where the ordering of the plot need not be commensurate with the way in which events occurred (Bruner, 1990). Rather researchers should interpret narratives as “artifactual representations of the intentions, actions, or goals of actors situated in time” (Wagner, 2003a). The researcher’s role is to interpret these artifacts in order to come to a finer reading of the situation and provide a plausible theoretical explanation of how things are.

From a hermeneutic perspective, we all enter situations with prejudices based on our pre-existing stock of knowledge, our interpretation of context is deepened through reflexively creating shared meaning within a community (Boland, 1987). As such we briefly describe the relationship of the field researcher to the case organization. These details provide the reader with insight into the hermeneutic circle at work within the study. Unprecedented access to the university was gained by one of the researchers as a case organization for her doctoral study. After a series of meetings with senior management, the Vice President (VP) for Finance and Administration gave complete access to confidential materials and the freedom to publish articles as the researcher saw fit. The only condition stipulated that the research be designed with a longitudinal focus in order to interpret the ERP project over time.

We attribute the unprecedented access as a result of multiple issues including: the request coming at the right time, the VP’s desire to be a part of research conducted at a world-renowned British ‘Ivy’, and the researcher’s professional reputation as testified to by Ivy leaders. She had been employed as a financial analyst within the University’s Medical School between 1993 and 1995 and returned in 1998 as a summer employee. This positioned the researcher a year later to begin conducting a timely, interpretive field study. Entering the field site with an informed understanding of cultural values and norms meant the researcher was able to communicate with Ivy actors by appropriating local language, symbols, and practices. Gaining access to these individuals and garnering their trust was timely because she was viewed as an ‘insider/outsider’ by the local community. This enabled her to establish credibility and develop interpretations of context with greater depth than would likely have been the case at an unfamiliar field site.

However, this insider/outsider status was not without its difficulties. Having worked within Ivy’s powerful medical school, the field researcher entered the university with a prejudice informing her interpretation of the political relationships comprising organizational interactions. The study was designed to help develop an awareness of, and accountability for these biases by selecting a narrative strategy that sought out multiplicity and silent voices. The field researcher consistently documented her perspective and refined her understanding through interpersonal communication with interlocutors, discussions with her co-author, and repeated readings of official documents, and interview transcripts.

One hundred and twenty-nine interviews were conducted with 53 project members and the wider university community. In addition, the field researcher directly observed 11 project meetings. The majority of the interviews followed the narrative interviewing (NI) convention, which provides a temporal frame of reference (for example, ‘Describe the last project phase to me.’) and then allows the interviewees to narrate, without interruption, their interpretations of important moments of negotiation in front of them at that time (Bauer, 1996). All the interviews were taperecorded and verbatim transcripts were produced which facilitated the process of analysis (Riessman, 1993).

Upon producing the verbatim transcripts, the researcher ‘re-transcribed’ the tapes in order to communicate the ‘spoken features of discourse’ such as tone, mood and pace of the narration (Riessman, 1993). We felt that formatting text in this way was important for understanding the storytelling characteristic of the texts. In this paper bold text is used to indicate a raise in volume of the narrator’s voice. Italicized words connote inflection in the pitch and tone of voice and a combination of bold and italics indicates these characteristics were occurring simultaneously. In addition, detailed notes were recorded within a hardbound journal to supplement the transcripts with information about pre/post interview banter, non-verbal cues, and the researcher’s interpretation of events. Throughout the fieldwork critical meetings were observed and documents related to the project were collected and analyzed as other organizational narratives.

Regular intervals of intensive fieldwork included consistent repeat interviews with actors. Important issues and actors referred to in narrative accounts set the agenda guiding us to the next round of interviews. This allowed us to gain multiple perspectives by interviewing not only organizational allies but also controversial actors who may have otherwise remained ‘silent voices’ (Star, 1991). When a reference was made to a group, or to non-human actors, the researchers would arrange to interview a delegate actor (Pouloudi & Whitley, 2000). This incremental and participant-led process meant that the researchers took up opportunities to reach interviewees and collect narratives that reflected their in situ sense-making and rhetorical perspectives.

Any collection of narratives is likely to be partial; on a practical level one cannot collect data all the time, from everyone. However, when abstracting from the empirical material the researchers found that the majority of narratives clustered around important political processes occurring at the time; for example, impending deadlines, user group requirements or controversial work practices. The analysis of these hot situations highlighted the variety of perspectives that existed within Ivy at a given moment in time and we organized them into trials of strength to illuminate the socio-technical processes at work. The three temporal zones in our analysis focus on resolving collisions between multiple times and take us ‘backstage’ to provide insights into the complex processes of socio-political negotiation whereby conflicting interests worked to colonize organizational reality.

We often found that interviewees would not want to discuss the issues that they had made the agenda of the last interview, since in their minds ‘the world had moved on’. What had dominated their focus at one point in time was now, in the words of one interviewee ‘a done deal’; what had been an open controversy during a certain period had become black boxed (Vidgen & McMaster, 1996) and a matter of fact (Latour, 1999) for the interviewee. This phenomenon directly linked to the principle of emergence where the narrator’s understanding and articulation of reality is based on the present moment and her relation to the past and potential futures. As the present emerges she makes sense of it, adjusting her interpretation of the past and future accordingly. For example, as we began the last phase of interviews, one project team leader said that she would be ‘fascinated’ to read her own transcripts from interviews over the last 2 years, because her understanding of the project, and what it meant to Ivy University, had changed so much over time that she actually couldn’t remember how she had made sense of it previously. A key reason for this is that, understandably, the temporal frame of interest to the interviewee tends to be radically different from the researcher.

The juxtaposition between our academic time zone and that of the frenetic project management world highlighted the importance of developing a subtle understanding of network dynamics and project work through an analysis of the interconnectedness of each situated trial of strength. In the next section the empirical material is presented in the form of a case study. Constructing a case study description from qualitative data can be challenging; whose narrative does one privilege? How does one ensure that the personal voices of the interviewees speak through the text? The researchers take responsibility for ‘authoring’ the version of narrative that is read in the case study and the three temporal zones characterizing our analysis. The organization of the empirical material necessarily reflects their sense-making process to a certain extent; albeit one that has representativeness as its goal. A full version of the case study is documented in a longer publication (Wagner, 2003b) if the reader wishes to refer to it. The account that follows therefore is a necessarily selective, crafted version of the case which serves as background and ‘base camp’ for the presentation of the analysis.

## 4. Description of empirical material: Ivy University’s project initiative

The Vice President (VP) for Finance and Administration was newly arrived at the University when he began communicating to a core group of middle managers his vision to “partner with industry in order to create an administrative center of excellence, putting the school on the map and creating a state of the art system” (interview with VP). ERP software companies were seeking to quickly penetrate untapped markets in order to widen their customer base by selling Y2K compliant technology. Several vendors approached Ivy about developing an ‘industry-specific solution (interview with project manager) that could be sold to universities worldwide. The VP was eager to have Ivy chosen as the model organization over its rival institutions and when offered the opportunity, university officers unanimously approved the initiative.

In summer 1996, Ivy created an alliance with Oracle Corporation to become their ‘showcase customer’ (contractual documentation, May 1996) in order to develop and implement flagship technology. Ivy wanted to implement an integrated financial infrastructure that would unify its silos of administration. The choice to work with a single ERP vendor was predicated on Oracle’s interest in developing two modules to support a comprehensive administrative platform for universities: Oracle Grants Management (OGM) and Labor Distribution (LD) would integrate with its existing Government Financials suite (OGF) to create an ERP package specifically for the University context. The nature of the University’s grant business is quite complex. The vendor required Ivy’s expertise if they were to be successful in integrating their existing software into the grant environment. Ivy added to this complexity by aiming to be the first university to successfully achieve an ERP ‘big bang’ implementation. University leadership expected to simultaneously ‘turn on’ all ERP modules while curtailing the use of its legacy systems. In October 1996, this initiative was officially announced to the Ivy community by the university provost and VP who reported the project’s expected completion date as October 1998.

After this announcement the project structure was formed and a leader with experience implementing ERP software in large business organizations was hired to guide the initiative. However, his inexperience with university operations meant that dayto-day leadership of the initiative was driven by the VP’s core group of middle managers, the majority of whom were considered functional business experts with only a cursory understanding of ERP technology. The development priorities of Oracle were to modify existing ERP technology quickly and inexpensively while the core group spent time considering possible business process changes that would support a new administrative model. They were later criticized for being focused “30 000 feet above sea-level and not opening the box and installing the software to see what it did” (interview with technical director).

In October 1997, the university contracted external auditors to conduct an assessment of the project’s progress. The rapid approach of Y2K and the slow pace of system configuration and design, led to a modification of the deadline. What was previously supposed to be a single, big-bang implementation was replaced with a phased approach where all applications had to be functioning by the ‘drop dead date’ of July 1999 (official documentation). This date marked the beginning of Ivy’s year 2000 fiscal calendar and meant any legacy systems still in operation were putting the university at risk from the millennium bug. In an attempt to meet these dates, the VP secured local ERP expertise by hiring a technical Director of Administrative

Systems. The VP told the Director that his number one priority was to “meet the Y2K fiscal year deadline—no matter what” (interview with Director). The VP was so concerned about Oracle’s development delays that he traveled 3000 miles by plane to their corporate headquarters to inform top management that the partnership would cease if the custom ERP modules were not delivered in a timely manner and with high functionality.

In July 1998 Ivy implemented the purchasing and accounts payable applications which ran on converted, legacy financial data because Oracle had yet to complete the design of the OGM and LD modules and integrate the applications with the government package. When the original October 1998 deadline arrived Oracle was still developing OGM and had begun redesigning the entire Labor Distribution application. In the spring of 1999, Ivy received ‘bare bones’ technology from Oracle which excluded the LD module. Oracle had been unable to complete the design of this application in time for Ivy’s 2000 fiscal year. Ivy worked from the spring to June 1999 to design basic labor distribution functionality but this meant that only ‘essential’ parts of the ERP were operational on the first day of the new fiscal year.

While a small group of departmental end-users and faculty were consulted throughout the initiative, the phase-one system very much reflected the interests of Oracle and the VP’s core group of managers. For example, as direct consumers of the July 2000 phase-one system, academic administrators could not answer faculties’ fundamental question: “how much money do I have left to spend?”. The crucial principal investigator report (PI report) detailing financial commitments on grant awards was not available to faculty or staff. Suddenly, a group who had been told to expect a “reduction in hassle factor” (interview with faculty member) as a result of the new system, felt unable to accomplish key aspects of their job. From July 2000 the VP’s core group entered into an unexpected back-and-forth process of report development with faculty. At the time of writing in May 2002, the university remains unable to break the hold of shadow systems because the remaining project team members have yet to design a functioning ERP solution that incorporates an efficient business process and effective reporting tools. Almost 2 years after going live with the phase-one system, faculty administrators must still import data from the ERP system into customized spreadsheets in order to report to faculty the amount of money they have available for research.

The PI report illustrates the extent to which the ERP project initiative was driven by the high-level agendas of Oracle and the project team. Despite the promise of “higher quality administrative services to faculty, students, staff, alumni, donors, and sponsors” (official Ivy announcement of initiative, October 1996), the system still neglects aspects of university administrative practice. In the next section we analyze three temporal zones to highlight the multiple times entangled in the PI report controversy. We emphasize that the creation of a working ERP system is not just a story about the powerful ‘winning’ or the dominance of the technology over its users as has been suggested by other authors studying ERP (Hanseth & Braa, 1998; Cornford, 2000; Soh, Kien & Tay-Yap, 2000; Koch, 2001); that would be far too straightforward. Although aspects of the ERP have become ‘part of the furniture’ at Ivy, an enterprise-wide administrative system has yet to be achieved. Rather, local shadow systems sit beside the ERP and reflect the constitution of interests that have yet to be articulated within the context of the official system development.

## 5. Analysis

This section presents an analysis of the multiple and interpenetrating times constituting the ERP system at Ivy. This is organized as three temporal zones representing the various agencies at work during the project initiative. The empirical data is used to illuminate the organizing logic of the temporal zones and the way in which it becomes inscribed into artifacts to create hybrid collectives that reinforce it. As discussed in the theoretical section, power relations are implied in the organization of temporality and we analyze a selection of progressive trials of strength each one of which builds upon the other conditioning subsequent phases of the ERP system development.

Our analysis highlights the achievement of order, which often emerges with the creation of project times, however we also emphasize the transitory and shifting constitution of this achievement. Each manifestation of project time attempts to shape the ERP system and make the ERP global standards work within the organizing horizon of its temporal zone. We consider the cost that these on-going compromises and negotiations have on the naturalization of the ERP system. The first subsection begins by analyzing the crafting of an historic ERP vision and the efforts by a specific actor–network to manifest a particular preferred future for Ivy. We have chosen to make the Vice President the primary delegate for this group and organized this narrative around his ERP proposal.

## 5.1. Crafting an historic ERP vision: The vice president’s temporal zone

In this temporal zone we reveal the effort involved in initiating an ERP project that achieved sufficient enrollment to realize a watershed program of IT-enabled organizational change. The temporal forms characterizing this zone are marked by an effort to bring a particular interpretation of the phenomenon constituting global times (Adam, 1995) into account in the local organizational times of Ivy. Global times are the heightened perception of an increased internationality among communities, for example a growing interconnectedness of marketplaces. They are also shaped by historically situated events and trends such as the dawning of the year 2000 or the 9/11 attack.

The horizon of the 1990s was overshadowed by fears of computer viruses associated with Y2K, the hubris of the ‘internet economy’ and enterprise-wide organizational transformation (see Financial Times, Dempsey, 1999). Wide currents of change toward the automation and “commercialization of higher education” (Noble, 1998a, b) existed as influential narratives within the educational context of the US (see Noble, 1998a, b; Winner, 1997). These themes and trends marked a ‘sign of the times’ where software vendors, consultants and university administrators, “hoping to get a piece of the commercial action … giving their institutions a fashionable forward-looking image” (Noble, 1998a), began developing powerful, strategic alliances. Together these groups formed powerful networks that attempted to colonize an unknown future in light of advances in computer-mediated learning and integrated technological infrastructures (Agre, 2000; Cornford, 2000; Noble, 1998a, b; Silver & Silver, 1997).

In 1996, a newly arrived VP was charged with modernizing Ivy’s administrative infrastructure and equipping it to thrive in the next century. His prestigious corporate background meant that the VP related to the emergence of these commercially oriented actor–networks and regarded them as an expression of current best business practice within an increasingly competitive university marketplace. These were the global times that the VP believed Ivy must take into account if it were to achieve a robust strategic position. They formed his sense of the ‘global present’ in which he lived out his particular appropriation of ‘global times’. In making his assessment of what was required to lead Ivy into a more robust strategic position, the VP resolved to bring this same sense of the global present to Ivy.

Rather than aligning himself with Ivy’s diffuse network of grass roots development expertise, which had always characterized previous IT projects, his agenda was to “partner with industry” which he believed would provide important contributions influencing the nature and pace of change. The VP’s break from tradition marked the beginning of a trial of strength where he worked to enroll the University to accept his priorities and distinctive hybrid rationality. In the process he created a glocal (Robertson, 1992) temporal zone shaped by his effort to adapt his global outlook to local conditions in order to realize his preferred future for Ivy.

So, how did the VP and his network of corporate Oracle contacts translate the local interests within Ivy into a belief in the ERP solution that persuaded them to leave other options behind? Historically within the university, IT decisions fell within the domain of the chief information officer and his staff. However, this changed with the VP’s agenda to position the university as an administrative leader by embracing recent technological trends that were dominating the corporate, business world. The VP connected his ERP strategy with the reputation of first-class international software vendors and the media-fuelled sense of urgency to replace information technology in the approach to Y2K. The Ivy community was acutely aware of the inability of their legacy IT systems to cope with the demands of the contemporary higher education sector. The VP enrolled them in his belief that ERP would expertly equip them to face the challenges of increasingly complex finances, litigious hazards, and potential reputation risk.

The sum was a compelling temporal zone that conscripted local and global nonhuman actors into an extended network announcing the sources of legitimacy for his leading-edge logic. Perhaps the VP’s most ‘adhesive’ appeal was to the Ivy community’s prestigious intellectual ethos. Boardroom negotiations were swayed by the opportunity to inscribe Ivy’s local work practices into Oracle’s higher education solution. Ivy would co-author the definitive global standard, a gold standard, for academic administration and thereby influence future development of universities around the world effectively colonizing the unknown global future. No longer was it a story of ‘just another IT system’; rather, university leaders became enrolled into the VP’s network through the belief that seeking outside expertise secured a prosperous future for them all. This is expressed during an interview with the VP where he begins the story of Ivy’s project initiative by placing Ivy as an actor within a network of universities all trying to establish relationships with powerful software vendors during the same period of time:

… I remember once at the IBM conference centre at Palisades, New Jersey a number of universities met to see if we could agree on a joint vendor and collaborate. We had a number of vendors come in and talk with us and—you know—it was different people, at different places, at different times, and we had different priorities and that to some extent led to very different answers. I remember at that meeting people were there from [three Ivy League universities and two large research schools] and—you know—after the meeting if you had like two months later asked where everyone was, it was kind of ironic, [one University] was pursuing a relationship with SAP, [another Ivy League] was with PeopleSoft. [The research university] decided that they were just going to enhance their existing system and maybe consider PeopleSoft later and [we] moved towards Oracle…I’m not trying to cast stones because—I mean—I think circumstances are different and quite often—you know—organisations are organisms just like our bodies are and quite often—you know—they stop things or start things because the timing isn’t right and that’s ok—but the other [universities] just haven’t been able to move forward as well on their projects.

The VP’s commentary as an indication of his role as delegate for Ivy within a network that spans organizational boundaries. He justifies a move toward ERP technology by telling of the emerging trend within other university networks. He evaluates Ivy’s experiences in relation to other institutions and his story illuminates his belief that the non-traditional relationship between Ivy and Oracle was the “right move at the right time”.

The strategic partnership between Ivy and Oracle was formalized by a contract which created an obligatory passage point. This was a moment of emergence; Ivy’s understanding of its past and potential futures was re-ordered by the launch of the initiative. In voting for the contract, Ivy’s leaders in their capacity as delegates for the University community, publicly subscribed to the VP’s narrative and lent a degree of stability to his network by inscribing the new temporal zone within artifacts such as initial seed funding, purchase of Oracle’s software licence, and press releases. After a history of following trends, the VP’s ERP narrative offered Ivy an opportunity to become pioneers; an opportunity that inspired an institution dedicated to excellence. They trusted his corporate expertise to guide them in the modernization of their frustrating legacy systems. An expression of the success of his narrative within Ivy was the way in which his enthusiasm seeped into common parlance captured in the fieldwork interviews. Broad networks of actors became delegates for his vision and were able to communicate the need for ERP in language reminiscent of his enthusiasm using phrases like the “VP’s vision” or his “number one baby” (multiple interviews with Ivy community).

These inscriptions of the project into everyday language and contractual artifacts, added stability to the ERP project and created a durable temporal zone. This had the effect of black boxing (Vidgen & McMaster, 1996) the negotiations leading up to the purchase of Oracle’s ERP technology. The appropriateness of a standard software package for university operations was unquestioned, and took on matter of fact status within the organization. The irreversibility of this network proved quite phenomenal over time as it maintained the enrollment of almost the entire University community who, in adopting this durable time, became unable to recall a time when alternatives to this vision existed. This is expressed by one end-user at the medical school:

We all know that nobody builds their own systems anymore. It just isn’t an option—life is too confusing now, so why reinvent the wheel?...the ERP is [the $\mathrm { V P } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s } ]$ baby as evidenced by the huge project budget and the number of staff it’s been given.

In the next section we analyze the irreversibility of the VP’s durable time when confronted with additional trials of strength. We see that this durable time primed Ivy for potential controversies ahead and conditioned the next phase of development.

## 5.2. Creating the project collective: achieving durable project times

If the VP’s project initiative was to acquire qualities of irreversibility and enroll others through the ERP as an obligatory passage point within Ivy, a project temporal zone would need to be created that successfully joined together actors with varied expertise. The technological and functional complexity of ERP meant that the creation of a hybrid collective was necessary if the VP’s vision was to be successfully mobilized. The agency of this project team would be far greater than that of its constituent actors if such a hybrid collective could be formed through the fusing of multiple perspectives. Ivy opened its doors to delegate actors representing the alien ERP assumptions and timeframes of project, process and milestones. External ERP experts lacked contextual understanding of Ivy’s ethos and workflow, making it difficult to develop a common basis for a collective work focus. Ivy worked to inscribe a project temporality through the allocation of space, state of-the-art technology and a generous financial budget. However, the creation of a project hybrid required its constituent actors to learn how to flip between time zones in order to communicate with one another. A crucial dialogue had to open up focusing on developing divergent knowledge (Baskerville, Pawlowski, & McLean, 2000) about one another’s expert domains. A core group of Ivy’s middle managers translated their knowledge of embedded and embodied university work-times to a broader project temporality in an attempt to create a shared Ivy–Oracle project time.

However, the creation of a hybrid collective imbued with the momentum of durable Ivy–Oracle project time was difficult to achieve. The VP ‘back filled’ the permanent jobs of the core group of Ivy managers, thereby enrolling their full attention on the project and curtailing many of their pre-project, network alliances. This worked to create an all encompassing project temporality for Ivy actors. However, Oracle experts were only partially enrolled in Ivy’s project. These ERP delegates saw project X as a small network amongst many, whose temporal markers and commitments had to give way to more powerful networks concerned with macro-corporate goals of return on investment. These actors were less willing to ‘make room’ for a shared Ivy–Oracle project temporality and as such a series of trials of strength between the various actors ensued as each struggled to maintain preferred temporal priorities. For example, Oracle understaffed the project, its programmers were unreceptive to learning Ivy’s business, and Oracle experts rarely worked on-site with Ivy actors. While the Ivy core group was constantly attempting further enrollment of the ERP and its ambassadors, the temporal resources of Oracle delegates were often pulled toward ‘higher priority’ commitments.

In many ways the future of Ivy was shaped by Oracle’s noted recalcitrance in this regard which threatened the irreversibility of the VP’s vision. Oracle remained an unknown to Ivy, a global actor that fought localization. In response to Oracle’s absence the local core group developed their own durable project time:

The difference between now and back in the days of home development is we’re not our own masters. Our code is provided by vendors who have their own agendas. [Ivy] cannot dictate how it wants to do its business by itself. [Ivy has] always been accountable to federal regulations but more and more it’s had to work with vendors providing code, vendors providing services, vendor needs … So there is—you stand back and ask yourself this question—‘if nobody did anything once we implement the ERP, what would happen, what would inevitably happen?’ [pause] There is what I call the osmosis factor—the osmosis factor was that in the old-days if we did nothing—nothing happened. The osmosis factor today is that the world keeps changing and there’s nothing you can do about it —gotta keep up—change with it.

Inevitably, the project time that gained stability was a hybrid as certain aspects of Oracle and Ivy interests remained fused together, but the pace, rhythm and focus of the work practices were dominated by Ivy core group logic. This project time reflected Ivy’s intellectual spirit and the core group got lost in envisioning Ivy’s future operating environment. The first year of the project was spent conceptualizing how the technology should be designed in order to create a universal global standard for higher education. Ivy’s development partnership with Oracle had introduced unknown definitions of what standard work practices were, the times and places in which these practices should be enacted, and with whom the responsibility should lie. Without Oracle expertise at hand, the team had difficulty determining essential legacy functionality that should be carried forward into the ERP environment. A frequent phrase during the first year of the initiative was ‘now is the time’, which was underpinned by a powerful narrative of organizational transformation that implied a fleeting ‘window of opportunity’. The innovations brought to the table by Ivy team members were added to the development agenda, creating ‘scope creep’ and putting additional pressure on the already recalcitrant ERP project management timeframe, the result was technological development got out of step with conceptual work:

The technical teams have to work through their functional leadership who in many cases may be motivated by getting what they want in the system but don’t understand the difficulties it takes to get that kind of stuff coded … So you know I think very highly of the folks who lead the functional teams but they won’t necessarily have a technical perspective. The technical guys need someone to hear it from—independent of what their functional leader said—to what we call ‘open the box’. Oracle hasn’t been that voice—their modules are still vapourware and I’m convinced they haven’t even staffed this project properly. Back at home it’s been like buying Microsoft Excel and looking at the box and saying ‘you know— I wish Microsoft Excel would do this’, ‘I wish Microsoft would do this’, ‘I wish Microsoft would do this’, but you never actually opened the box and installed the software to see what the hell it actually did—two years in and the box isn’t open! [pause] I want the box opened—so we could essentially say ‘this is what it does and this is what you get’. While [the core group] are debating whether [the system] should do this, or whether it should do that, or whether it should do this—[the technical teams] are waiting on Oracle for permission to say, ‘this is what it does and this is what it’s going to do’. (Senior programmer)

With the approach of an immovable Y2K deadline, the VP became concerned with the project’s progress envisioning the universal global standard has escalated and technological development of localized standards had fallen behind. He decided to change the constituency of his local network to pull it back on to his timeframe and definition. The VP accomplished this by hiring a Technical Director of Administrative Systems to subvert Oracle’s monopoly on technological developments which was regarded as inhibiting the process of naturalization. Tensions in this regard culminated in the VP’s flight from the east coast to visit Oracle at their home base in California. The VP informed Oracle that Ivy would pull all support from the strategic partnership denying Oracle their industry experience. He threatened to complete the implementation locally if the pace of development did not increase. The VP’s ultimatum began a trial of strength between the two players. The outcome of these negotiations resulted in the insertion of a new temporal zone. This zone represented a phased development agenda dividing in two what Oracle defined as essential and non-essential ERP functionality. The shift from a ‘big-bang’ implementation to a two-phase delivery fractured the VP’s strategic vision and made the value of Ivy’s administrative work practices subject to recategorization in terms of what Oracle could achieve by Ivy’s Y2K fiscal year:

The VP, who was the father of this project … was willing to compromise on the strategic goals that he wanted to achieve to get to the end game—which was— to get it done … more than anything else, failure is not an option! Regardless of whether it’s pretty and whether various people are happy with it—it’s got to get done! The project couldn’t be a failure. (Technical Manager)

Project time was reconstituted by this trial of strength; many of the core group’s conceptualization efforts were left behind, a new logic shaping the pace of development by Oracle (phase-one/phase-two) was brought forward. The VP compromised both the naturalization of the ERP within Ivy and his global vision of designing a ‘state-of-the-art’ administrative system for higher education. He had achieved order, which in a moment of emergence, he defined as pulling Ivy’s ERP project closer to schedule, but as Law (1992) reminds us order is likely only to be temporary and to come at a cost. The VP may have ‘won’ this trial of strength but engendered the conditions for potential future controversy. The VP’s act of recalcitrance toward his global network alliances temporarily limited Oracle’s influence within other university contexts. Ivy was without a robust phase-one system, but so too was Oracle without local expertise to aid in the completion of its global university package. The next task for the project team and VP was to face the wider university community with the phase-one ERP system to see how robust their negotiations had been. A key goal of the Ivy–Oracle strategic partnership was to draw upon local Ivy expertise and develop a global standard for academic administration. Ivy’s investment in this strategy was, at its most basic, to naturalize the Oracle software. When the ERP was rolled out to the academic enterprise it, therefore faced its most fundamental trial of strength: would the output of the project be accepted?

## 5.3. Attempts to create order: taking professional academic times into account

When measured in clock time the project took place over 3 years. As we see from the previous two subsections, project time has meant many different things during this period and been reconstituted as a result of a progressive trials of strength involving different actors. The ERP that has emerged reflects the complex agencies that were at work during this time. During these 2 years faculty have been living their own multiple times and their temporal zone is organized around a different logic. The temporality of their tenured career priorities overflows the horizon of a single project. Their temporal zones are characterized by the slow rhythms of research, dominated by cycles of the publication and grant application process. The project team had organized several faculty presentations over the previous 3 years however the turnout was minimal. For faculty the ERP was being built during those years to support the academic enterprise. The introduction of the ERP to faculty administrations marked a moment of emergence for both the project team and faculty.

Ivy’s academic temporal zones first confronted ERP project time when faculty administrators were unable to generate reports for Principal Investigators (PI) detailing their remaining balance. The inability to answer the fundamental question of faculty: ‘how much money do I have left to spend?’ stalled the progress of the ERP system. This report was a crucial artifact within the academic timescape and the inability to generate it on command impacted the professional work life of Ivy faculty. Faculty realized that the project had been developed on time, but at their expense. The meaning of past project time for them was radically revised as they took their present situation into account. When they projected the current issues into the future they saw that the ERP had introduced considerable uncertainty into their working practices. It would disrupt the flow of their research plans and their scarce resources would continue to be used to fund an administrative system that didn’t serve them.

The compromised phase-one ERP system failed to enroll the wider university community and reminded the project team and the VP that their efforts had been communicated as an initiative to benefit the entire enterprise. While the project initiative had defined the ‘right time’ to inscribe the core group’s priorities into the ERP, its members neglected important ‘other times’ (Adam, 1995) held by the wider university community. The project team reordered their understanding of past events and future possibilities when they realized that the business-oriented design was at odds with the nature of administrative work within the academic faculties. The academic faculty had created an obligatory passage point through which the ERP must travel if its preferred future was to become the local university standard.

This controversy not only tested the irreversibility of the VP’s original vision to shift Ivy toward a more corporate operating platform, it also broke an implicit set of trust relationships between the project team and the wider university community. Powerful faculty conscripted narratives from Ivy’s past to use as delegate actors on their behalf in order to remind the VP of his official promise to improve university working rhythms for the entire community, through the new system. The faculty leveraged their own actor–network, to make their voices heard in the broader project narrative, and achieve tactical concessions in the flow of organizational strategy. The VP’s vision and the project team’s phase-one system were negotiating change with Ivy’s dominant academic work times. Faculty and their administrators demanded that the legacy system which generated the ‘old world’ PI report remain live until new tools were developed as part of phase-two implementation. This compromise was particularly hard for the project team to accept since from their perspective the PI report inscribed ‘a checkbook mentality’ which was interpreted as an outdated mindset in conflict with the phase-one design. The VP’s core group had purposely excluded this functionality from the enterprise system in preference of a more corporate approach. A senior financial manager bluntly articulates this:

I would say that the mentality that we’ve had … for managing is primitive to say the best and it’s very old-fashioned … the corporate world left it many years ago … Many faculty … think of things fundamentally wrong … we want to move people towards a management model where we’re going to ask [them] to put together a time-phased business plan.

However, the project team quickly realized that a working PI report was an important omission in their development priorities for the phase-one system. Oracle had deemed the management model and business plan as non-essential development items and rescheduled them as a phase-two deliverable. Without an alternative network solution in place, a legacy system concession had to be allowed. This ‘old world’ technology became a powerful delegate for the academic faculty and positioned legacy working rhythms beside ERP technology; this had the potential to significantly reconstitute the VP and core group’s durable project time.

The failure of the core group to envision this trial of strength during the project initiative and negotiate faculty interests into the phase-one ERP system was an omission that had cost them powerful allies. Unable to conscript Oracle into design efforts, the team worked to increase the stability of the phase-one system through a series of quick, trials of strength. Their immediate goal was to create a temporary ERP solution as quickly as possible. The project team shifted phase-two development priorities and took a crude intermediary step in which the old style PI Report was temporarily ‘bolted on to’ the ERP system. This move had the effect of fusing academic and project temporalities into a single integrated system. They then conscripted users into their network by closing down the legacy system and creating an obligatory passage point through which they expected to enroll faculty interests. While this trial of strength forced a temporary user migration to the new technology by silencing the legacy system, it failed to translate user interests. Academic administrators found the ERP process cumbersome and inefficient and as a result, a new wave of shadow systems emerged.

The ‘bolt on’ remained unused by administrators who conscripted actors from their past and present (e.g. legacy reports, spreadsheet tools, faculty advocates) in order to create an alternative to the ERP system. In this way they were trying to actively redefine the future of Ivy’s administrative environment. Resourceful administrators exported data from the bolt-on and imported it into Microsoft Excel enabling them to recreate silos of activity, answer faculty questions about funding availability, and maintain their academic temporal zones. These local shadow systems were shared amongst academic departments and resulted in the mobilization of a grassroots network that fused Ivy’s historical working rhythms with ERP generated data. The faculty’s success in achieving compromise on this issue prioritized their temporal working rhythms within the constitution of durable project time. The PI report which once appeared to be a ‘border skirmish’ between conflicting administrative and academic networks came to look more like a revolution.

This revolution is exemplified in the following narrative gathered during a followup email interview in May 2002 almost 3 years since the phase-one system went live. The narrator is a delegate for the powerful medical school who generate 80% of the university’s total grant revenue:

What has not been explored here may be the most interesting aspect of the entire implementation—how and why did [Ivy’s] faculty get caught in the crossfire between central and distributed management? Time, money and expertise. The folks doing the development did not set out to not meet the business needs of their users, I am sure of that, they just didn’t get enough of the right work done in the right amount of time for the right amount of money. That said, by the time it was ‘time’—no fully integrated, fully supported system existed. Starting over was not an option (apparently) so we still do not have either system fully in place. No [checkbook] replacement, no fully developed [ERP] approach.

This group is tired of working around durable project time and its members are now designing a full-scale alternative to the ERP’s grant reporting system that will inscribe their academic temporal zones. The narrator continues:

Department [administrators] clearly understand that the faculty are their customers, and try to provide them the services they most need and value. Why has central management become invested in changing, for an extremely large revenue stream, from a tried-and-true system, proven to be both efficient and well-accepted by the faculty, to one that has not been tested with these types of accounts, and is less efficient and useful for the faculty? My guess is that there is too much water under the bridge to go back. Which is why we are building our own. This is the most fascinating part of the whole story! I wish I knew how it turns out, or even how to turn it around.

The investment in the project initiative has grown so large that the ERP system as a whole is indisputable but the extent to which new ‘process-based’ working rhythms will underpin this multimillion dollar administrative infrastructure is highly questionable as we see in the final piece of narrative text:

I see the central university continuing to call us bad partners, I see them too far down the path to admit they should have zigged when they zagged, and I see them never fully developing what would be required to even make [the ERP] really work ... I hope you understand that it’s not [the ERP] itself that’s the issue. It’s the lack of understanding and regard for the people bringing in the money and the people doing the work that is so frustrating.

Proponents of an enterprise-wide temporality must inscribe this vision if they hope to maintain the durability of their network. Surely, opponents to this agenda are involved in constituting a durable alternative.

At the time of the interview quoted above the durability of project time is severely at risk due to fractured development efforts, loss of project momentum, and evolving network configurations. The VP has moved to another university and few of his core members remain as full-time project employees. The VP saw the ERP become a naturalized object; ‘part of the furniture’, but only as part of an idiosyncratic community design. The ERP is a working everyday manifestation of Ivy’s multiple and conflicting temporal zones. Durable project time was reconstituted from his original vision and became an achievement of negotiated order that reflects the intermingling of the past with the present and the tensions of Ivy’s future.

The VP’s original vision to create an integrated system that would form the basis for an administrative center of excellence remains illusive. Despite the combined power of the ERP’s global work times and the momentum of the university’s multiyear project initiative, this story will never be one of the powerful winning. Nor should it read as the victory of David over Goliath for this research suggests that it is rarely that neat and tidy. As Latour (1999) says, when their goals are frustrated actors take detours through the goals of others resulting in a general drift. The temporality of one becomes intermingled with the rhythms of the other and the drift that emerges represents not solution two overtaking solution one, but a fusing of multiple interests.

The implementation of standard ERP technology within Ivy foregrounds the collision of conflicting temporal zones and illustrates the political artistry involved in working to achieve an information system that will accommodate multiple temporalities. The extent to which the ERP should be cast as David or Goliath is a matter of timing where the past and future are recast through the result of emerging trials of strength. Rather than prescribing an outcome of the ERP project for Ivy’s unknown future this study has sought to illuminate the “complex and subtle processes of creating and recreating” (Sahay, 1997) a working information system.

## 6. Conclusions

Three years of clock time were invested in the establishment of a working ERP system for academic administration by the prestigious actor–network of Ivy and Oracle. The public announcement that the system was ‘live’ gave the impression of an achievement of order to the outside world and sent ripples of expectation through the academic community. The Ivy project has helped Oracle sell ERP systems and fuelled the trajectory of management careers in Ivy and Oracle. Although outwardly the clock time invested in this ERP system might be taken as an implication of its quality we have suggested clock time is only one of multiple times experienced during a software project.

We have explored the way in which multiple times, especially subjective temporal perceptions may shape an IT-enabled program of organizational change. For example, in our case study we have shown that the determination of the VP’s actor network to integrate ‘global times’ into Ivy’s local social present was highly significant as was his claim that ‘now is the time’ to implement ERP. From among the multiple temporal zones mingling with the Ivy social form, we chose to cluster particular subjective temporal perceptions and construct the notion of ‘project times’. We maintain that multiple project times came to bear upon the ERP implementation process simultaneously each articulating different interpretations of priorities, interests and rationales. The manifestation of a distinctive project time at Ivy was shaped by the efforts of an actor–network to influence the ordering of issues such as: whose priorities should be taken into account when deciding the project deadlines? When should users be involved? What is the timeframe for acceptable levels of Oracle involvement?

The ERP that has emerged affects the progressive trials of strength that occurred over such issues during the 3-year project at Ivy. These processes of negotiations were not discrete events, but were deeply embedded in the respective organizational contexts of Ivy and Oracle; each actor network called upon allies based upon their understanding of the past, their perception of the present, and their projection of the future in an effort to persuade others to subscribe to their definition of what project time should mean to Ivy. Compromises crafted during these times about what goes forward and what gets left behind account for the distinctive consequences of the ERP at Ivy and influenced the quality of order achieved. Our micronarrative analysis of these negotiations are, we maintain, an illumination of the dynamic constitution of phenomenon such as windows of opportunity (Orlikowski & Tyre, 1994) and project drift (Ciborra, 1996) in the academic literature.

The implementation of ERP systems is particularly vulnerable to becoming entangled in multiple project times because by design they traverse multiple communities attempting to connect up previously discrete silos business activity into an enterprise view. ERP infrastructures have to enroll multiple levels of meaning, interest, and functionality across time if they are to achieve durability (Star & Ruhleder, 1996). The technological capability of the ERP itself forms an important part of the broad range of agencies across which actor networks negotiate as they seek to have their priorities and interests accommodated into the ERP standard. However, in direct contrast to the other analyses, which regard ERP as uncontrollable, “everybody’s enemy by resisting all organizational change” (Hanseth and Braa, 1998, p. 195), our findings illustrate the strength of the local. We find evidence that the voice of supposedly silenced can penetrate the technological monster (Monteiro, 1999) and create a hybrid working rhythm that is inscribed into its socio-technical infrastructure.

An ERP may become ‘part of the furniture’ but as many organizations have found its effective naturalization is a complex process, whilst collective harmony sought for the ERP can at times exist as hybrid dissonance. For example, according to a 2001 survey of over 100 firms the level of productivity decreased within 75% of ERP-enabled organizations (Cooke, Gelman, & Peterson, 2001). We interpret such statistics as artifacts representing a specific time in the history of ERP technology. While this information is helpful for quantifying the effects of a trend, quantitative measures also have the effect of freezing a moving target by labeling evolving sociotechnical infrastructures as static. Our research approach supports an analysis of the ebb and flow of agencies in the flow of time and take into account both human and non-human actors.

What does the Ivy case teach us about the participatory design approach that they attempted to use? We suggest that practitioners extend the now standard consideration of who and what to enroll and engage with the more subtle question of when to involve. For example, although it was difficult to enroll the faculty in the early phases of the project, subsequently silencing them once their interest became apparent led the medical school to splinter off and create a costly alternative to the ERP. The lack of temporal insight about when to enroll and inscribe the interests of actors diminished their initial attempts at a participatory approach. Researchers have acknowledged the side effects that may emerge from ‘letting the concrete set’ on ERP technological standards (Hanseth & Braa, 1998). However, we call for further research into the legacy engendered by the temporal features of human actors network inscription, as well as non-human, to redress the balance. The temporal turn within actor network theory provides an helpful methodology for such an endeavor highlighting the effort involved, not only in initiating and managing large-scale programs of IT-enabled change, but also the social cost of living with them on a longterm basis.

Swanson (2003) notes that when the discourse of a technology trend winds down, organizational actors are left to pick up the pieces and move forward in an attempt to redefine their failures. Our research highlights this point through the study of bolton and shadow systems necessary to make the ERP system work at Ivy. The intense negotiations between actor networks surrounding this ERP repair process make claims that its implementation is a ‘success’ or ‘failure’ highly situated. For example, whilst the VP imposed order through the negotiation of a phase one/phase two demarcation enabling his actor–network to claim ‘successful progress’ this stability was quickly lost once the ERP interacted with the professional academic community at Ivy. We therefore conclude by proposing that judgments of ERP success or failure are closely related to the timing of evaluation and the vantage point from which such observations are made.
