# The expert system: constructing expertise in an IT/management consultancy

Matthew Jones \*

Judge Institute of Management, University of Cambridge, Trumpington Street, Cambridge CB2 1AG, Cambridge, UK

## Abstract

Despite widespread uncertainty, if not scepticism, about the precise nature of the expertise that consultants bring to bear in their client engagements, organisations continue to employ consultants in large numbers, especially in information technology-related projects. Drawing on a participant observation study of IT/management consultancy practice, this paper seeks to explore the different ways in which consultants are seen as experts in the light of the pressures of the consultancy market place. The ongoing construction of consultancy expertise through the interaction of consultants and their clients with an array of elements, including technical knowledge, skilled practice, individual and collective experience, and commercial interests is discussed. It is suggested that, rather than being a relatively-enduring property of individuals, based on their mastery of a body abstract knowledge or their skills at managing the perceptions of their clients, the precarious and multi-faceted character of their expertise means that consultants are continuously engaged in its active construction through their situated practice.  2003 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Expertise; Consultants; Professionals; Impression management; Participant observation

## 1. Introduction

Within a generally rapidly expanding market, the use of consultants in the adoption, development and management of IT has been widely identified as a major area of growth in both the private and public sectors (Rassam & Oates, 1991; Bloomfield & Danieli, 1995; The Economist, 1997; Sturdy, 1997; O’Shea & Madigan, 1999). A significant factor identified as motivating the employment of consultants is that they are perceived to be experts, possessing a specific body of knowledge that they are able to deploy to their clients’ benefit (Buchanan & Boddy, 1992; Clark, 1995; Rassam & Oates, 1991; Tisdall, 1982). Apart from the somewhat atypical case of “expert” consultants (Robertson & Swan, 1998), who are employed specifically for their specialised technical knowledge, however, the nature of this expertise is often considered elusive (Caulkin, 1997; Clark, 1995; The Economist, 1997; Shapiro, Eccles & Soske, 1993), not least by consultants themselves (Rassam & Oates, 1991; Tisdall, 1982). The industry thus continues to be sensitive (Rassam & Oates, 1991) to the jibe that a consultant is someone who borrows your watch to tell you the time (Townsend, 1984).

Assuming that companies employing consultants do not do so out of charity, however, then insight on the “expertise” that consultants provide to their clients would seem important in accounting for the growth in their use. To date, however, relatively little attention has been paid in the academic IS literature to the study of consultants and their work (the limited exceptions, such as Bloomfield and Danieli (1995) and Sturdy (1997) have appeared in general management journals, or, as in Orlikowski (1991, 1993); Alvesson (1995) and Deetz (1998), have focused on consultancy firms themselves rather than consultants’ interactions with their clients). This paper seeks to remedy this deficiency with an in-depth case study of consultancy practice, focusing on the way in which consultants come to be perceived as “experts”. To set this case in context, however, it would first seem necessary to consider the nature of expertise.

## 2. Expertise

According to Scarbrough (1996: 23) “expertise has traditionally been viewed as the near exclusive property of professional groups”. For consultants seeking recognition of their expertise, therefore, the professions constitute an often implicit, but sometimes explicit (Rassam & Oates, 1991; Tisdall, 1982), model.

Professional expertise is generally seen to be based on a body of knowledge and practice, the acquisition of which is identified as an essential feature of professional formation, both individual (Atkinson, 1981; Bucher & Stelling, 1977) and collective (Larson, 1984; Macdonald, 1995). Abbott (1988) suggests that it is the abstract character of professional knowledge that constitutes its essential quality, warranting the jurisdictional claims of professions. The certification and credentialling of the capability to deploy appropriately particular forms of abstract knowledge thus often serves to control professional membership, and, in some cases, the right to practice. Such regulation may be supplemented, as Harries-Jenkins (1970) discusses, by ethical injunctions on the proper use of professional knowledge.

As Abbott also recognised, however, professional judgement does not rely solely on abstract knowledge, but requires a balance with concrete knowledge: too much abstraction risks “mere formalism”, while too much concreteness gives the appearance of being no more than a craft. Professional formation, therefore, may also often involve training in, and evaluation of, performance of particular concrete work practices (Abbott, 1988).

## 2.1. Consultancy and expertise

In contrast to professionals, consultants generally lack the major and acknowledged body of specialist knowledge, approved training programmes, or officiallyrecognised authorities that are seen as sustaining professional expertise. Clark (1995: 90), for example, argues that an agreed body of knowledge is “precisely what is missing” in consultancy. Rather there are multiple and competing knowledges, none of which possess sufficient authority to provide the basis for occupational certification. Clark attributes this to characteristics of the consultancy market, with its weak barriers to entry, heterogeneity of suppliers and high turnover. As a consequence, there is little control over who can call themselves a consultant or what expertise, if any, they provide. Moreover the intangibility and perishability of consultancy services, the heterogeneity of service forms, and the dependence of delivery on interaction with clients (which make it difficult to establish ex ante quality criteria), gives particular significance to reputation and to client-consultant relationships in establishing and sustaining consultants’ claims to expertise.

In the absence of a recognised professional body to validate their expertise, Clark (1995) also suggests that consultants have a particular need to convince clients that they are “authoritative, competent, expert, knowledgeable, skilled and can deliver a quality service” (Clark, 1995: 93). This active shaping of clients’ perceptions is echoed by Broomfield & Danieli, (1995:28) who argue that consultants “do not so much target themselves at a particular niche as seek to create a niche and persuade clients that they are within it” and by Elkjaer et al. (1991:152) who argue that consultants seek to “construct [the situation for which their expertise is appropriate] in ways that are intended to ensure the relevance, and thereby the material value of the [expertise]”. Clark (1995: 93) thus proposes a view of “consultancy activity as a process of construction of meaning and impression management”. Starbuck (1992) also draws attention to the importance of impression management in discussing the difficulties that clients have in assessing the quality of advice offered by experts (amongst whom he includes consultants). As a consequence, he suggests, clients tend to make judgements of the value and quality of experts’ services on the basis of “generic symbols of expertise”, such as the expert’s qualifications, use of impressive analytical techniques, and the expert’s dress, confidence and general demeanour.

In highlighting the differences between professional and consultancy expertise and in emphasising the importance of impression management to consultancy practice, however, writers such as Clark (1995) would seem to neglect questions about the technical basis of professional expertise and its performative, and rhetorical (Alvesson, 1993) character. Thus Crozier (1964) argued that rationalised knowledge cannot be a sufficient basis for expert power, as the agreement of an abstract, formalised definition of an expert’s knowledge makes it vulnerable to replication and commodification, undermining the expert’s claim to exclusivity and exceptional ability. In contrast to the emphasis of Abbott (1988) on the abstract character of professional knowledge, Jamous and Peloille (1970) proposed an alternative categorisation, distinguishing between technicality and indetermination, and arguing that professional expertise is marked by high levels of indeterminacy. Hence Larson (1977) suggests that professional expertise consequently involves certain criteria of talent that “cannot be taught”. Thus, according to Jamous and Peloille (1970), professional selection and training typically includes an emphasis on “individual and social potentialities, experience, talent and intuition”.

Further evidence of the importance of informal knowledge is provided by microlevel studies of professional training, such as Becker et al. (1961); Bucher and Stelling (1977), and Atkinson (1981) on doctors, or Power (1991); Coffey (1994); Macdonald (1995) and Grey (1998) on accountants. These suggest that, in addition to, and even sometimes in opposition to, the acquisition of a body of abstract knowledge, professional formation involves the development of practical, sometimes tacit, skills and socialisation in particular forms of behaviour and self-presentation. That is, trainees learn to “act professionally”. Similar observations are made in relation to less well-recognised professional groups, such as Dingwall (1976) on health visitors. Thus professionals, as much as consultants, may be seen as using symbolic resources to establish their expert status.

A perhaps more significant distinguishing feature of consultancy expertise, not widely commented on in the literature, however, would seem to be its dynamic character. Reflecting the fluid and highly competitive nature of the consultancy market, firms need continuously to develop new and proprietary knowledge “products” and to promote these as providing the most efficacious solution to managements’ problems (Clark, 1995; Darwent, 1988; O’Shea & Madigan, 1999). Such pressures have been identified as reinforcing tendencies towards faddishness in management thinking (Abrahamson, 1996; Gill & Whittle, 1993; Pascale, 1991) as each consultancy seeks to establish the primacy of its offerings and to supersede (or maybe, failing that, to emulate) the “solutions” of their competitors. Consultancy expertise thus lacks not just agreed criteria of expertise, but positive incentives towards fragmentation and change. Consultants would therefore seem to face particular problems in presenting themselves as experts compared to accredited professionals who are able to lay claim to a recognised and relatively slowly-changing body of authoritative knowledge. These problems would seem likely to be highlighted, moreover, in situations where a consultancy is actively developing a new product. This is therefore the focus of the case study to which we now turn.

## 3. Research methods

The description presented in this paper is based on six months of participant observation by the author in an IT/management consultancy, referred to here, for reasons of confidentiality, by the pseudonym ABC. During this period, the author worked, unpaid, at one of the local offices of ABC which played a central role in the development and delivery of a new consultancy offering. This provided opportunities for discussions with a number of people who had contributed to the development of the offering as well as active participation in group meetings of the consultants who were involved in its delivery; preparation of presentations to market the offering to new clients, and a project applying the offering for an external client. The adoption of such methods thus enables us to gain insight on the practice of consultancy expertise and answers the call by Sturdy (1997) for more empirical research, especially studies providing access to client-consultant interactions.

The data sources include: contemporaneous field notes (including notes of informal discussions and meetings with consultants and clients); internal company documentation; email correspondence; project documentation; and meeting minutes. The description also draws on some published sources, but these are not referenced to preserve confidentiality. A draft of the paper was sent to the consultants at ABC for their comments. No substantial errors or omissions were identified, however, other than to note that the description, in focusing on consultancy expertise, reflects only a small part of the work of a consultant on a project. Other roles such as project management, client liaison and marketing were identified as equally, if not more, significant aspects of a consultant’s activities.

## 4. The ABC case

ABC Consultants is an international IT/management consultancy with nearly 10,000 employees worldwide. During the period 1995–1998 they had developed, through collaboration with a client, an approach to managing IT investments (hereafter referred to as The Approach), which, it was believed, could substantially enhance the likelihood of achieving business benefits from IT. The Approach was made up of 3 main elements: a conceptual modelling technique for representing and analysing the full range of activities (both IT and non-IT-related) required to achieve desired business outcomes; a method for evaluating the contribution of alternative investment options to the achievement of the outcomes; and a management process for planning and implementation of the chosen options. In line with the practice of other consultancies, a book about The Approach aimed at a popular management audience (hereafter The Book), was written by a group of ABC consultants, lead by one of the team originally involved in its development. This was launched through an international promotional tour by the lead author, and by tie-in publications in the business press.

While it drew on a number of elements of ABC’s existing techniques, in particular the mapping methodology was an established component of their suite of system development tools, The Approach, as a whole, was a substantial new departure for the company. In particular, it was recognised to involve a broadening of ABC’s focus, which had traditionally been based around software development, to address the whole process of achieving business benefits from IT investments. This was captured in a catchphrase, as a shift from “design to delivery” to “concept to cash”. Such an enlargement of the scope of ABC’s consultancy practice was recognised as bringing ABC into more direct competition with the major management consultancies, especially those with a strategic focus.

The Approach was, however, also seen as a major innovation in IT/Management consultancy practice, which gave ABC a distinct advantage, even with these new competitors. The importance of The Approach to ABC was emphasised in internal newsletters where it was described as ABC’s “flagship”, its “unique” or “key” “differentiator” in the consultancy marketplace, and as becoming so integrated into their delivery solutions that all ABC’s services would involve its use.

## 4.1. A practical approach

A major theme of The Book is that The Approach is “not just another academic theory”, but “a practical approach developed, tested in the field and successfully used” with an international roster of clients. This is reinforced, as is typical in the popular management literature, by the inclusion of vignettes, described as “windows on the real world: client stories”, illustrating aspects of its use in named companies at the end of each chapter. The practical nature of The Approach and its pragmatic orientation is also emphasised in the Foreword, where it is stressed that it “is not theoretically perfect”, nor does it “claim to possess the one true management doctrine”, but it is “client-tested”.

The modesty, or perhaps realism, of this position is justified by criticism of “silver bullet” thinking, which, it is argued, has tended to pervade management’s attitude to IT investments. It is also argued that The Approach is “not a quick fix”, nor a “cookbook”, but needs to be adapted to each organisation. Neither is it “purely mechanistic”, rather it is intended to support business judgement, not to replace it. The possibility of future changes to The Approach, as further experience is accumulated, is raised.

The careful qualification of the claims made for The Approach may be seen as serving both to differentiate it from the messianic rhetoric of approaches such as business process reengineering, and to draw attention to the central importance of practical experience in its development and use. Moreover, as internal documents admitted, The Book itself had been specifically written so as not to give too much away—“we had to produce a book that was rich in contents, that didn’t reveal too much of our proprietary knowledge to competitors, and, most importantly, would sell”. Thus being an expert in The Approach would seem to require more than “going by The Book”. What additional qualities were involved in its practical application, that could not be, or were not, discussed in The Book, may be illustrated through an examination of its deployment in a project undertaken for a government agency (the Agency).

## 4.2. Practicing the approach

Faced with limited resources, especially of senior management time, the Agency was considering which of a range of possible initiatives, that had been identified in an earlier IT strategy exercise, it should implement. Unfortunately the contribution of these initiatives to the Agency’s business outcomes was unclear from the documentation produced by the earlier exercise and the process for selecting and implementing the preferred initiatives undecided. On the recommendation of one of the Agency’s senior managers who had attended one of the launch events for The Book and had been convinced by the argument of The Approach, ABC was asked to submit a proposal to provide assistance in addressing these issues. The proposal was prepared by a consultant who had previously worked with this manager, and was accepted by the Agency CEO.

ABC’s team for the project comprised two experienced consultants, a “Principal” and an “Associate Director”, who had both used elements of The Approach, especially the mapping tool, in previous assignments, but neither of whom had conducted a full Approach project using all of the elements. This reflected the scarcity of consultants with full-range experience within the local practice, but also a wish to enable these consultants to acquire that experience as quickly as possible, with support, if necessary, from local and regional experts. ABC were also keen to establish as many projects using The Approach as possible, so as to establish reference sites in a range of sectors which could be used in future selling. The researcher, who was familiar with The Approach from The Book and had held discussions with a number of leading practitioners, but had no practical experience of its use, was assigned to the ABC team as a supernumary and uncharged resource, which role was disclosed to the client.

The ABC team negotiated use of several offices on the executive floor of the Agency building as their workspace, as well as access to a computer, printer, telephone and Agency email and voice-mail accounts. A large meeting room on the same floor was subsequently provided as an additional workspace. The proximity of these spaces to the CEO’s office meant that the ABC team’s presence was very visible to staff meeting with the CEO and provided opportunities for informal clientconsultant interaction.

The first phase of the project involved using the ABC mapping tool to represent the relationships between the IT initiatives identified in the strategy exercise and the Agency’s stated business objectives. This exercise soon revealed that, according to the conventions of the mapping tool, the relationships were often weak and in some cases possibly non-existent. It was therefore considered necessary to re-validate the objectives at a meeting with the Agency’s most senior managers and then to work back to identify the range of initiatives, both IT and non-IT, that might be expected to contribute to achieving them. The relatively weak semantics of the mapping tool meant that this process involved a fair element of personal judgement by the ABC team. The extent of this was clouded somewhat, however, by the prior commitment that the Agency management appeared to have established to a particular view of IT strategy as a result of the earlier exercise (if only because of the amount of work they had put into it).

Having established a representation of the relationship between the initiatives and the Agency’s business objectives, the next task was to validate this model with the senior management team. This was done through a two-day workshop in which groups of senior managers worked through the model with the assistance of the ABC team. Although most of the Agency managers felt that the overall model was too complex to be understood in its entirety, they found that they were able to comment effectively on smaller components (comprising only the initiatives contributing to particular business outcomes), especially where these related to their personal area of experience.

Taking these component “programs” as the main focus of analysis, attention was then turned to an assessment of the relative performance of their constituent initiatives on 3 criteria: risk (the likelihood that an initiative would not deliver the expected business results), alignment (the extent to which an initiative would contribute to business outcomes) and financial worth (the net present value of the expected costs and benefits). Although ABC had developed generic assessment instruments for each of these measures, because business outcomes, risk factors and financial models are specific to each organisation, it was necessary for the consultants to “calibrate” the instruments to the Agency’s particular situation. At a second workshop, the programs were assessed using the calibrated instruments by teams of managers in the relevant area of business, lead by an appropriate senior manager and supported by the ABC team.

From the results of this analysis the ABC team, drawing on examples used in previous projects, devised a graphical plot that enabled the comparison of initiatives in terms of their performance on the three dimensions. This was used in a third workshop with the senior managers to select the initiatives with the greatest expected contribution to desired business outcomes, the greatest financial return and the least risk of failure as the priority for implementation.

This assessment procedure, identified as the final deliverable of the project, was seen as providing the basis for the management of future investments, i.e. it would provide a consistent means of evaluating initiatives seeking funding in the future. Similarly, with initiatives and their contributions to business outcomes defined, accountability for results could be clearly established and ongoing monitoring of project progress maintained. In this it was seen that ABC consultants could play a role in supporting the assessment and governance process. Having defined the “necessary” initiatives, ABC would also be in a strong position to provide technical services in delivering them.

## 4.3. The reflective practitioner

In conducting the project, the two ABC consultants regularly reflected upon the nature of expertise. This was largely prompted by concerns about being recognised as experts in The Approach, both by clients and internally within ABC, but also by a more general response to the experience they were undergoing in applying The Approach. Client recognition was seen to be important in establishing their personal credibility and also that of ABC as a whole. Internal recognition was seen to be related to reward (formal and informal), status and career progression. This was brought to the fore during the course of the project by an internal exercise at ABC to identify the local experts in The Approach for marketing purposes. The consultants were aware of the significance of The Approach in terms of ABC’s positioning and skill profile, and this gave added impetus to their reflections on using it, especially as it related to their own particular career experience. At a more personal level, being an expert was also acknowledged as being tied to their own identity. Thus, sensitive to the view of consultants as “snake-oil salesmen”, they sought to establish their knowledge as real and significant, for example by emphasising the practical skills and experience they brought to the application of The Approach.

The status of expert was seen to have important bearing on the consultant:client relationship. This was expressed in a variety of ways, from relatively simple steps, such as adopting a dress code that meant that the consultant should always be slightly more formal than the client, and ensuring the highest standards of presentation in all materials, to more substantive issues of the form and content of interactions with the client. On this, however, the two consultants held quite different opinions. For one, the characteristic of expertise was self-confidence. It was therefore essential not to permit any evidence of self doubt to be shown to clients. All statements should be unambiguously positive, there should be no suggestion of any possibility of error, or doubt, about the consultant’s actions, and all interactions should be solely on the consultant’s terms. As it was explained: “we are steering the ship, they are coming on board”. The other consultant was much more cautious by nature, often carefully qualifying his remarks (sometimes much to the exasperation of the other consultant) and seeing the relationship with the client as more one of partnership.

## 4.4. Personal reflections

Further insights on the nature of consultancy expertise were available by virtue of my own active participation in the Agency project. Thus, when first assigned to the project I would not have claimed any level of expertise, either generally as a consultant or specifically in The Approach. Although I had read The Book, had participated in the preparation of presentations for potential clients, and discussed The Approach with a number of those who had been involved in its development, I had never received any training in the techniques, nor used any of them in practice. The Agency project consultants were aware of my position and research interests at ABC and I had worked with one of them on preparing the presentations. They seemed happy, however, to accept my participation in the project, despite my lack of experience, and to “sell” my involvement positively to the Agency. It seemed, therefore, that my academic status and familiarity with The Book was sufficient to qualify me as an expert, of sorts, even though my known skills amounted to little more than an ability to prepare fairly basic Powerpoint slides.

Despite my wish to avoid being seen as an academic expert, however, there were two occasions in which my status appeared to have a direct bearing on the conduct of the project. The first was in discussions of the assessment instruments in which I was aware that my knowledge of the literature on decision-making gave my comments an authority greater than would normally have been the case for someone of my experience. The second was an awareness that the CEO appeared a few times to be seeking my opinion as an independent party rather than as an ABC consultant. In both cases my ABC colleagues were aware of the situation, and it was not apparent that these incidents significantly affected the outcome of the project, but they may serve as a reminder that neutral participation is not possible.

While a passive observational role might have been possible, it was assumed by both the Agency and the ABC consultants that I would make an active contribution to the project. Thus, without there being any formal allocation of tasks, I took on responsibility for working with the modelling tool to develop the maps of the Agency’s activities. In part this may have reflected my demonstrated competency with using software packages, but also the fact that I had the time and the inclination to undertake the detailed work that this involved. As a result, I became “the expert” on the models, not just for the Agency staff, but also with the consultants.

## 5. Constructing consultancy expertise in the agency project

In order to structure the discussion of the ABC case, a variety of different ways in which the Agency project consultants came to be seen as experts may be identified. These processes may be seen as operating at every stage of the project, from its initial selling, through the conduct of the work itself, to the final results, and as involving both clients’ perceptions and the consultants’ self-belief. It is not claimed, though, that these categories necessarily provide a complete description of how consultancy expertise is constructed, but rather that they appeared helpful in understanding the practice of the ABC consultants in the Agency project. Where appropriate, supplementary case data is presented to elaborate the illustration of the categories.

## 5.1. Expert is as expert is sold

Perhaps the most direct way in which consultants came to be seen as experts was simply by being sold as such. Thus, ABC’s proposal to the Agency, for example, described the project team as “experienced consultants with strong expertise in our Approach”. Whether intentionally, or not, this formulation accurately described the consultants’ considerable knowledge of The Approach, whilst not directly stating that their experience was necessarily in applying it. The association of the claims of experience and of expertise in The Approach, however, particularly in the context of a proposal framed in the language of The Approach may be seen as helping to create a perception on the client side that the ABC team were experts in it. Implicitly, or explicitly, therefore, a proposal constructs a particular representation of the consultants’ expertise that clients are invited to accept.

As was noted, for the Agency project the proposal was drafted by one of the consultants. More typically, however, proposals would be developed by “client executives”, dedicated sales staff whose job was to generate work for ABC’s consultants by developing relationships with particular types of client. Since the primary interest of client executives was in winning business, it was recognised that there could be problems with them exaggerating the expertise that ABC could provide in their eagerness to close deals. This was exacerbated in relation to The Approach, where client executives’ limited knowledge of this new offering meant that they had little understanding of what expertise it required or was available within the company. The expertise offered in a proposal may therefore reflect more what is perceived to be the client’s wishes than a realistic picture of the resources available. This may be significant, however, in establishing expectations to which the consultants, if the proposal is accepted, may have to live up.

## 5.2. Expert is as expert is bought

The initial perception of consultants as experts, however, was not solely a matter of how they were sold to clients, but also of what the client believed they were buying. Thus the Agency senior manager who had championed The Approach within the Agency, might reasonably have expected that consultants provided by ABC would be experts in it. He may also have had a personal incentive for wishing to believe the consultants to be experts, as successful completion of the project might have been expected to enhance his own standing in the Agency. Whatever claims are made for the expertise of the consultants, therefore, may be reinforced by the client.

## 5.3. Expert is as client believes them to be

The client’s belief in the consultant’s expertise may extend beyond their interest in having bought the right experts. They may also need to believe that the consultant’s practice demonstrates expertise. Thus the Agency project relied upon the clients “buying in” to The Approach and accepting that it provided a useful analysis of data that they had themselves provided to address a problem they had themselves defined. The clients’ awareness of this came out in one workshop, where an Agency manager observed that “all you [the consultants] are doing is telling us what we know already”. He immediately followed this, however, by saying “but we wouldn’t have worked it all out like you did”. The ability to represent the Agency to itself was thus delegated to the consultants and became a demonstration of their expertise.

This process was perhaps even more striking when The Approach lead to a representation which differed from that of Agency staff, for example regarding the relationship between departments. Although these differences were sometimes a subject of debate, both between Agency managers as well as with the consultants, the broad structure of the model and assessment process were not significantly challenged. While this may have reflected a strategic decision by managers to “play the game” according to the, CEO-supported, rules of The Approach, or the weak commitment of the managers to a particular conception of the Agency, it may also be seen to demonstrate the managers’ willingness to see the consultants as experts in understanding their organisation. Clients may therefore not simply want to believe they are buying expertise, but they may also be inclined to interpret consultants practice as evidence of this.

## 5.4. Expert is as expert bills

In the absence of alternative, external quality indicators an important indicator of the quality of a consultants’ expertise may be seen to be its price. The higher the billing rate, the greater their expertise may be assumed to be. Thus in the Agency proposal the consultants’ billing rate was related to their seniority, with the “Principal” being charged at a lower rate than the “Associate Director”. Despite both being described as having “strong expertise” in The Approach, therefore, the billing rates may be seen as implying that the more senior consultant had greater expertise.

The whole question of billing rates was a matter of some sensitivity at ABC. For example, there was concern that client executives seeking to close a deal would have an incentive to charge consultants at the lowest rate possible, which could devalue their work in the eyes of the client. This was seen to be particular issue in relation to The Approach where the attempt to re-position ABC against the strategic management consultancies could be under-mined by failing to bill at a comparable, and in ABC terms exceptional, rate. Clients would not believe that they were getting credible expertise at lower rates.

It was not just with clients, however, that billing rates were a potentially sensitive matter. Internally too, billing rates were seen as an important indicator of status, even if only indirectly linked to personal rewards. Although there were no formal rules, there appeared to be a general understanding that a certain level of seniority should attract a certain billing rate. To be charged out at rates below this was seen as undermining an individual’s status and credibility. At worst, it could reduce an individual to a “body”, a bundle of skills for hire, lacking individual initiative. Since consultants’ “performance” as experts, depended, to some extent at least, on their self-belief in their own expertise, perceptions that this was under-appreciated by the company could negatively affect client interactions.

For the individual consultant, therefore, a high billing rate was generally considered desirable, although not without costs. Thus while it could enhance their selfesteem and credibility, it was also a burden, demanding that their work justify it. Within ABC this was represented in terms of the need to define a “value proposition” that could substantiate claims for returns on a consultancy project exceeding the fee, or of a consultant’s interventions being as valuable as that of member of the client’s staff commanding a salary equivalent to the annualised billing rate. In a commercial market, billing rates may thus stand as a measure of consultants’ expertise, relatively independently of any evidence of capability, both for the client and the consultants themselves.

## 5.5. Expert is as expert believes themselves to be

As the question of billing rates indicates, expertise was, at least partially, a matter of self-belief. This could lead to differences between consultants in their level of expertise even though they might have the same knowledge and experience. Thus expertise was not measured simply in terms of levels of training and experience, but was also seen to involve an assessment of capability to deliver in practice. This could lead to discrepancies in both directions, i.e. individuals who considered themselves more expert than their practice would indicate, but also, more rarely, individuals whose performance exceeded their perceptions of their own competence. This was a potential issue in the internal exercise to identify local experts in The Approach where consultants’ views about their own level of expertise might lead them to expect to be placed in a category other than that to which they might be assigned. The need for careful handling of this exercise to recognise individuals’ sensitivities on this question was acknowledged by ABC management. In the absence of reliable external criteria, therefore, expertise may be to some extent a matter of individual judgement.

## 5.6. Expert is as expert is trained

A formal solution to such discrepancies in consultants’ perceptions of their capabilities might seem to be to base assessment of expertise solely on evidence of completion of internally-recognised training courses. As was noted, however, training on its own, was not considered sufficient to enable a consultant to become an expert in The Approach. This is not to say that training was regarded as unnecessary, indeed its role in providing knowledge of the techniques and insight on the practical issues in their application was recognised as very valuable, but that practical experience was also required before a consultant could be considered an expert.

Perhaps a more direct influence of training on expertise arose with those consultants involved in providing the training, where their role served to reinforce their status as experts, both in their own eyes and those of the trainees, but also in the company as a whole. Within such courses the ability to draw examples from personal experience was seen as lending essential authority and also as enabling the training to be more aware of the complexities of real client engagements. Thus, the receipt and provision of training may be a relatively tangible indicator of expertise, although not, in itself, enough to ensure expert status.

## 5.7. Expert is as expert does

From the point of view of the client, one of the clearest manifestations of a consultant’s expertise may be the practice of technical skills, such as operating the software that supports The Approach, or carrying out the activities involved in the various stages. At one level this may be seen to reflect the need of clients for visible evidence of expert action. Thus, while they may recognise that expertise involves the deployment of bodies of specialised knowledge, possibly based on extensive training, clients may primarily be aware of these only through the observable actions of the consultants. Thus, in the Agency project it was apparent that, even though the senior managers were able, after some initial familiarisation, to understand conceptually the mapping and assessment techniques, they were impressed by the consultants’ ability to use the techniques of The Approach to translate their views into its framework.

One product of the Approach technique that appeared to be particularly convincing as a demonstration of expertise was the ability of the consultants to develop a rapid understanding of the “big picture” of the Agency’s business. While this was a necessary product of the strategic orientation of The Approach, for many of the senior managers whose primary concern was their own department, the holistic view of the Agency provided by the consultants was more abstract, complex and at a higher level than they were familiar with. Thus, although the CEO was able to comprehend the whole map of the Agency’s activities, most senior managers found it easier to grasp those programs that related most directly to their work.

From the consultants’ perspective, however, it was not the technical skills themselves that were particularly valued, but rather the ability to use these in a flexible way to suit the particular conditions of the client site. A capability to apply the basic techniques was taken as given. It was the ability to demonstrate sufficient mastery to be able to adapt them to local circumstances or to improvise in unexpected situations that was seen to mark expert behaviour. The consultants on the Agency project described this as “getting it”—an ability to transcend the particulars and to appreciate the essential features of The Approach in ways that enabled its creative application. Although such an appreciation might be achievable through formal training, it could only be demonstrated and developed in practice.

The consultants recognised that this meant that expert behaviour was not codifiable since improvisation would depend on the context. Individual experts might also vary in their response to a situation depending on their particular knowledge and experience. This was seen to have implications for the assessment of levels of expertise as, beyond a certain level of competence, the criteria for identifying expert practice could not be standardised and would only be evident in their instantiation.

The consultants on the Agency project suggested, however, that not all individuals would necessarily “get it”. In particular, it was felt that some more technicallyoriented consultants, might have difficulty in adopting the more holistic, strategic mindset that expert use of The Approach required and would persist in mechanistic application of the techniques. For those who did “get it”, however, improvisation was seen to be one of the most challenging, but also most rewarding, aspects of consultancy work. During the Agency project, for example, the development of the assessment instruments provoked wide-ranging, and sometimes quite heated, discussion about the underlying theoretical assumptions of The Approach. Practice therefore appeared to be significant both as an instantiation of expertise, but also the medium for its development.

## 5.8. Expert is as expert talks

Consultants’ expertise was also seen to be manifested in their mastery of the language and rules of The Approach. Although it does not have a very elaborate or obscure terminology, nor, as has been noted, are the semantics of the modelling technique particularly strong, there were a number of situations where, particularly to promote consistency, certain formulations of the problem were insisted upon by the consultants. For example outcomes in the model were always expressed in terms of an acronym, MEDIC, indicating whether they would Maintain, Eliminate, Decrease, Increase or Create some activity. Although, in themselves, the terminology and rules were not particularly sophisticated, the consultants’ knowledge of them served to differentiate them from the clients and, in insisting on their application, served to emphasise their expert status.

Another aspect of talk that appeared to contribute to the consultants’ expert status was their reference to personal experiences. Their ability to tell “war stories” of their use of The Approach, or its component techniques, in other companies, served not only to demonstrate the depth of their understanding, but also to provide external validation of their views. An appeal to what had worked in another organisation was often enough to clinch the discussion when the appropriate course of action was under question. Expertise thus appears to have a significant rhetorical, as well as practical, character.

## 5.9. Expert is as expert behaves

The Agency project consultants were very aware of the significance of performance in the demonstration of expertise. While this was most forcefully expressed by the consultant who argued that clients should never be given any indication of error, or doubt, the other consultant also recognised that they were engaged in performing to clients, albeit in a different style. Both acknowledged the importance of confidence in achievement of an “expert” performance, even if expressed in different ways. Such confidence arose not just from belief in their knowledge of The Approach, and hence in their capability to deploy it effectively, but also in the basic validity of The Approach and its potential value to clients. This was related to a more general, if not uncritical, belief in ABC and its distinctive character as a company. Practically, what this appeared to provide was reassurance about the quality of work that lay behind the Approach, and knowledge that there was a considerable reserve of experience that could be drawn on if need arose. Given their belief that they had “got” The Approach, they were also able to call on their own experience in other projects and on their more general qualities as a consultant to enable them to feel confident in their performance as experts.

This performance was seen to involve not just the tone and rhetoric of the script, but also many other aspects of a consultant’s behaviour and approach. Thus the importance placed on dress and demeanour, on careful preparation of all meetings (down to the level of ensuring that all papers were placed in precisely the correct alignment on the meeting room table), and on the quality of the “props” (to the extent of insisting on a last-minute reprinting of a number of maps to ensure exact colour matching). Although this was clearly an exercise in impression management, and recognised as such by the consultants, there was also a substantive content to much of this behaviour. Thus the meeting preparation included rehearsals of the argument to be presented, to ensure that team members had a common understanding of all details of the material, or data projectors would be tested to ensure that images would be legible from all areas of the meeting room.

Although the impression management efforts were mainly directed at the set-piece interactions with clients, the interim meetings as well as the full-scale workshops, the demarcation between presentations and day-to-day activity was far from clear. In some senses, there was no time when the consultants were in the Agency building that they were not potentially “on show”, especially given the proximity of their workspace to the CEO’s office. Thus, while the Agency staff showed some acknowledgement of the consultants’ workspace as a distinct region, for example knocking on the door before entering (even though it was typically left ajar), there was felt to be an expectation that consultants were engaged in work on the Agency project whilst they were present there. As a result, the consultants sought to maintain as high a level of “face time” as possible at the Agency offices, and days when the consultants worked after hours were seen to be particularly meritorious in this respect.

An important aspect of work practice that was identified by the consultants as sustaining their status was their process skills, such as in making presentations, running meetings and workshops, and managing the relationship with the client. The care taken in preparing for presentations has already been noted, but the consultants were also assiduous in ensuring that good relations were maintained with the Agency. This was done both formally, in regular meetings with the CEO, and informally, for example by engaging in social discussion with Agency staff. The state of the relationship, both generally and with specific individuals, especially the CEO, was also a source of frequent discussion in the consultancy team.

Although it was recognised that there could be different styles in consultants’ process skills, as the contrast between the two Agency project consultants illustrated, they were nevertheless considered essential to effective practice. They were also seen to be only loosely related to technical skills. Thus a consultant with strong technical skills, but weak process skills was considered likely to be less effective than one with poorer technical skills, but excellent process skills. Consultancy expertise was therefore seen to involve not just the possession of technical knowledge, but also the ability to convey this knowledge to clients and to sustain a positive relationship with them. A consultant’s status as an expert could not be taken for granted, but required continuous effort and monitoring to ensure that it was recognised by clients. Awareness of the performative character of expertise and the active cultivation of the client’s reception of this performance thus appears to play a significant role in consultants’ understanding of their expertise.

## 5.10. Expert is as expert delivers

The importance placed on performance might seem to suggest that consultancy expertise was more about impressions than substance, but for the ABC consultants there was a clear recognition that it also required a demonstrable capability to deliver results. While it was acknowledged that the responsibility for achieving results rested with the clients, whose enthusiasm, ability and capacity to follow the consultants’ recommendations could never be guaranteed, the idea that an effective engagement should be capable of yielding benefits substantially exceeding the cost of the consultants fees (the value proposition) was strongly maintained. Evidence of the achievement of such benefits was highly prized.

Given the uncertainty about whether the client would pursue the recommendations, however, other measures of the effectiveness of the consultants’ intervention might also be adopted. At minimum, it was seen to be necessary to demonstrate that all deliverables had been supplied, as per the original proposal. In the Agency project this required some negotiation when it became apparent that one of the deliverables was not actually required. On its own, however, relying on fulfilment of contractual obligations was usually considered unsatisfactory, since it could indicate a breakdown in relations with the client and might not be associated with payment, in full, for the services provided. Even getting paid, however, was not enough to indicate that the consultants’ expertise had been valued. The real test was seen to be whether the project led to further business. This was especially valuable if the client would be willing to provide a reference stating their satisfaction with the work done. Evidence that they had done all that was expected of them, that their work had contributed to specific outcomes, and that these were, if possible, valued by the client thus served to reinforce consultants’ assessment of their own status as experts, especially in their future engagements.

## 6. Elements of consultancy expertise

The process of establishing the ABC consultants on the Agency project as experts would therefore appear to be based on more than just: the evidence of their accomplishments, such as their credentials or training; or their practice, such as the quality of their performance; or their self-belief; such as the conviction with which they held themselves to be experts; although each of these may play a part. It also appeared that consultants’ credentials, performances and beliefs needed to be accepted by clients and that this, in turn, helped to sustain their expert status. It was therefore in the interaction of consultant and client that the consultants’ expertise was actively constructed.

If this suggests how consultancy expertise appeared to be constructed in the Agency project, then another important aspect would seem to be the elements from which it appeared to be constructed i.e. what consultants’ expertise consisted of. In contrast to much of the literature, however, that focuses either on the performative or the substantive character of consultancy expertise, especially when comparing consultants with professionals, the ABC case would appear to indicate that there was a complex array of capabilities, practices and perceptions, each of which was involved in creating and sustaining the consultants’ expert status, as will now be discussed.

## 6.1. Technical knowledge

The ABC case would seem to suggest that technical knowledge, for example of specific techniques, was generally recognised as an important element of the consultants’ expertise. What the Agency project may also be seen as illustrating, however, was that, as Bloomfield and Danieli (1995) and Sturdy (1997) report, such knowledge may be insufficient, on its own, to sustain expert consultancy practice and that additional skills are essential. Thus, even if technical knowledge may be necessary for expert practice, consultants also need to be able to display this knowledge in ways that are persuasive to clients.

As Starbuck (1992) and O’Shea and Madigan (1999) warn, this may provide an incentive for consultants to dress up their practice in elaborate language to disguise its mundane character. If technical knowledge is not visible to clients, however, it may not be recognised at all. Of course, this may be the case too with professionals, but, lacking institutional accreditation of their knowledge base, consultants would seem especially in need of performance skills to ensure that clients recognise the technical knowledge on which they draw in their work.

As the ABC consultants’ concept of “getting it” illustrates, moreover, consultants need not just to be capable of deploying their technical knowledge effectively but also to transcend the mechanical application of techniques to apply them creatively to new situations. Thus method serves as a starting point for reflection, rather than as a prescriptive guide to practice. This ability to go beyond the prescriptions of method suggests a supra-rational view of expert practice, similar to that of Dreyfus et al. (1986). The improvisatory character of this practice may also be related to the concepts of tinkering and bricolage discussed by Ciborra (1999). Again, this would not necessarily seem to be unique to consultancy expertise, but the particular emphasis on novelty and customisation needed to persuade clients that they are getting a distinctive service would seem to place a premium on flexibility and creativity in the application of knowledge, rather than following standard practice.

## 6.2. Skilled practice

These problems with status of the consultants’ knowledge base may contribute to the promotion of practice, rather than abstract knowledge, as the essential measure of a consultant’s expertise. Thus ABC promoted The Approach as practical and client-tested, as opposed to “academic theory” and the Agency project consultants suggested that “getting it” was practically demonstrable, even if indefinable. Similar arguments are quoted by Sturdy (1997) from an IT consultancy’s publicity material which stated that “it takes practical hands-on skills well beyond the conceptual to achieve complete implementation…XYZ people are seasoned professionals…we encourage them to shun orthodoxy and think creatively and above all practically”. Hence practicality is contrasted with, and elevated above, conceptual thinking, and allied to creativity.

While this may not be so different from the indeterminate character of professional expertise highlighted by Jamous and Peloille (1970) and Larson (1977), the unstable and fragmented nature of the consultancy knowledge base, combined with the intangibility of the services provided would seem likely to place greater emphasis on practice as the primary means by which a consultant’s expertise can be demonstrated. Thus, while the common, recognised and relatively stable knowledge base of a professional may need to be demonstrated in practice, the proof of consultants’ proprietary, uncredentialled and constantly-changing knowledge will depend primarily on what they do, rather than what their claimed technical knowledge might indicate that they are capable of. Proficiency in the language and rules of The Approach, for example, while not necessarily critical to a consultant’s effectiveness may have a significant impact on the clients’ perception of the expertise being provided.

One effect of the emphasis on the practical nature of consultancy expertise may be to play up the importance of visible effort as necessary evidence of its manifestation. In contrast to views of expertise that conceive it as enabling effortless accomplishment, for example the expert doctor who is able to home in on a diagnosis with minimum investigation, consultants’ concern with hands-on practice means that their work needs to be seen to be done. The importance placed on “face time” in the Agency project may be seen as an example of this.

## 6.3. Experience

Another important element of consultancy expertise would seem to be experience. This is not to say that experience is not also significant in sustaining professional expertise, but the lack of external certification or ex ante quality criteria for consultancy practice, may be seen as making experience the primary basis for assessing competence. Evidence of the successful completion of equivalent projects is needed if a consultant’s claim to expertise is to be accepted. As the Agency project consultant’s argument on “getting it” suggests, however, expertise may not be directly related to the amount of experience. Thus while the importance given to experience may encourage the creative re-interpretation of consultant’s past careers, it may not necessarily guarantee greater expertise.

A further dilemma for consultancies in emphasising experience as a measure of expertise may arise from their attempts simultaneously to present themselves as highly innovative. The continuous launching of “new” services weakens claims to long experience. As The Book and the Agency project proposal illustrate there was therefore a fine balance to be struck between stressing the novelty of The Approach on the one hand and ABC’s experience in applying it on the other. This may be particularly significant in relation to the implied claims for the experience of individual consultants, especially in view of persistent complaints that consultancy companies sell projects with senior consultants, but staff them with juniors (Shapiro, Eccles & Soske, 1993; Caulkin, 1997), suggesting that clients particularly value individual experience. While this creates a need to ensure that individual consultants rapidly acquire experience in new services, it would also appear from the ABC case that collective experience underpins individual experience, for example by promoting confidence in The Approach and providing a safety net should problems arise.

## 6.4. Company reputation

Collective experience may also be seen as an aspect of reputation, and thus as a preliminary selection criterion for clients seeking to choose a consultant. No amount of individual experience may matter if this initial hurdle is not overcome. The importance placed at ABC in the value proposition for The Approach and the need to ensure that client executives sold it correctly may be seen as efforts to ensure the strength of its reputation, and thereby that of the company as a whole. Reputation may be particularly significant given consultancies’ interest in “selling on” (Rassam & Oates, 1991; Sturdy, 1997). Such repeat business not only reduces marketing costs, but can also be seen as demonstrating success in previous projects, further reinforcing reputation.

## 6.5. Consultants’ self belief

Whatever the significance of technical knowledge, skilled practice or experience in consultancy expertise, there is also a vitally important element of active impression management of which consultants are well aware and to which they pay considerable attention, for example in terms of dress, demeanour and quality of presentations. In carrying off their “performance”, consultants, given the lack of external validation of their expertise, would seem more than usually dependent on their own self-belief. This may be reinforced by evidence such as billing rates and access to senior client management. As the views of one of the Agency consultants illustrates, this may lead to a concern to achieve as much control as possible over the client relationship. In other contexts, as Sturdy (1997) reports, it can lead to consultants being perceived as arrogant.

The need for consultants continually to maintain their appearance, and confidence in themselves, as experts would seem to be increased on projects, such as at the Agency, where face time and visibility are high. In the absence of any significant “back region” (Goffman, 1990) in which to relax, the expert performance must be continually sustained. While this may contribute to the pressures of consultancy practice, however, visibility may also be a means of demonstrating status. Thus in the Agency project, the proximity of the consultants’ workspace to the CEO’s office was recognised as conferring significant symbolic value. Even had the workspace not been so strategically placed, the establishment of a dedicated workspace in the client’s building and the provision of necessary facilities was seen by the ABC consultants as an essential indication of a client’s commitment and their acknowledgement of consultancy expertise.

## 6.6. Clients’ belief in the consultant

This viewpoint may be taken further, as Bloomfield and Danieli (1995) argue, in suggesting that the distinction between technical and process skills may be seen as being constructed by the consultants themselves. Thus, consultants help clients to understand their situation in terms of particular problems to which the consultants have the solutions. This would seem to be clearly illustrated with The Approach, especially as it is presented in The Book. It was also evident in the Agency project, however, in the way that the Agency’s understanding of their organisation and its activities was shaped by The Approach techniques, such that they could be seen to provide the rational solution for the Agency to follow. While the assumptions involved in The Approach were generally made visible, particularly through the workshops at which staff worked with the detail of the models, in practice this appeared to be more a process of getting client “buy in”, through allowing local reconfiguration, whilst the overall conceptualisation, because of its abstractness and complexity, was largely unchallenged.

Such a view should not be taken to suggest that consultants’ clients should be understood as being manipulated by the devious practices of the consultants they innocently employ. It is important to recognise that client’s also have an important role in constructing their interactions with consultants. It was noted in the Agency project, for example, how clients may have an interest in perceiving the consultant as an expert. Whether for internal political reasons (Bloomfield & Danieli, 1995) or the desire to find an answer to a problem for which no solution is locally available (Sturdy, 1997), the client puts their faith in the consultant’s expertise.

Similarly, viewing consultancy as providing help on the content, process or structure of tasks rather than being responsible for doing the task itself (Steele, 1975), then the implementation of a consultant’s solution also depends on their client’s commitment to action. Since the decision to employ a consultant may, in some circumstances be a manifestation of this commitment, then the role of the consultant might be seen as encouraging, or at least avoiding damaging, the client’s confidence in their own capability to act. Thus in the Agency project the commissioning of ABC may be seen as reflecting an existing commitment to invest in new forms of IT, and the role of the consultants was to provide an acceptably rational mechanism for reaching common agreement on priorities. The “solution”, if such could be seen to exist, would be in the mind of the Agency staff, rather than provided by the ABC team.

In order to enable the client to feel that they are getting more from the consultant than a reflection of their own understanding, however, it may be necessary for the client to deny their own capability. Such a process, it has been suggested, may be evident in the Agency staff’s attitude toward the mapping and assessment process. While recognising that it was their views that were expressed, they wished to delegate the authority for the implications to the consultants. This may also be politically astute in avoiding responsibility if the process is seen as a failure—a not unlikely event based on the Agency’s, and many other organisations’, experience.

## 6.7. Consultants’self-identity

If clients are seen to be active in enrolling consultants as agents for their own actions, consultants may not be unresponsive to such efforts. Shapiro, Eccles and Soske (1993) and Sturdy (1997) suggest that this may provide an incentive for consultants to recommend whatever their clients want to hear. This did not appear to be overtly the case in the Agency project, although there was an awareness that the managers who had understood the implications of The Approach techniques engaged in strategic interventions in the process to “help” it to come up with the answers that they were seeking, for example by choosing weightings in the assessment process that assisted their position.

While this perspective might seem to indicate a congruence of interests between consultants and clients, it must also be recognised that these may not always coincide. Thus the consultants’ construction of the client’s problems in ways that are amenable to their solutions is not a neutral process, necessarily in the client’s best interests, and the consequent tensions may contribute to insecurities in consultants’ identities (Sturdy, 1997). Thus the Agency project consultant’s desire for control may mask anxieties about the uncertainty of consultancy work. More generally, conceiving of themselves as helping their clients, there is a desire to feel that their contribution is both valuable and valued (McGivern, 1983). The view at ABC that consultants should be capable of delivering value to their clients exceeding the annualised cost of their fees may be seen as seeking to validate the consultant’s role.

## 6.8. Commercial interests

At the same time, consultants are likely to have an interest in promoting their individual expertise and in “selling on”, to advance their own status as well as that of their company. Sturdy (1997) suggests that this may be resolved by reference to professionalism, of which ethical standards, exemplified by the doctors’ Hippocratic oath, are seen to be a key feature. Since this is an area in which consultancy is notably weakly served, however, such appeals may have little influence. More important appears to be belief in self and the company as guarantors of good faith. While the Agency project provided no evidence to suggest that this was a problem, whether, as O’Shea and Madigan (1999) discuss, this may be sufficient to prevent abuse is open to question.

The incentive for inflation of claims to expertise, or of its efficacy, if not of exploitation of the client relationship, may be seen to be increased by the highly competitive nature of the consultancy market. As was discussed, the absence of entry barriers, or an agreed knowledge base or quality criteria provides encouragement for consultancies to make strong claims for the unique capabilities of their offerings. As Elkjaer et al. (1991) argue, notwithstanding its sometimes “critical” rhetoric, consultancy expertise needs to sell itself in a competitive market. Thus, even while The Book rejects “silver bullet thinking” it needs to convince readers that The Approach is both distinctive and an, if not the only, effective general solution to the problems it defines. Competition may also reduce the longevity of even genuine innovations, both by dissipating their originality with “me-too” products if they are successful and by seeking to replace them with new concepts as the fashion cycle moves on. Consultancies therefore need to “strike while the [concept] is hot”, with perhaps a narrow window of opportunity to achieve a return on their investment. Under such circumstances, the temptation to over-sell their ideas and the expertise of their consultants may be understandable. Whether this is sufficiently prevalent to justify the cynical assessment of consultants voiced by Townsend (1984) would seem, on the basis of the ABC case, to be unproven. That this assessment has sufficient plausibility to evoke a frisson of recognition by both consultants and their clients, however, suggests that it touches on a continuing sensitivity in the consultancy business.

## 7. The dilemmas of consultancy expertise

The access to client-consultant interaction provided by participant observation in the ABC case highlighted the active nature of expertise construction by both parties and the above analysis suggested that this process involves the interaction of an array of different elements. While it has been noted that these features may not be wholly absent in the case of professional expertise (compare, for example, Atkinson’s observations on the role of patients in constructing the expertise of doctors in training (Atkinson, 1981)), there would seem to be a number of particular characteristics of their practice that increase the need for consultants to engage in the continuous maintenance (in interaction with their clients) of their expert status. These may be seen as relating to particular dilemmas of consultancy practice, a number of which have already been referred to in the discussion of the elements of expertise.

Traditionally, consultants have been seen as facing a central dilemma that has been variously framed in terms of performance vs substance, impression management vs technical knowledge or practice vs theory, with the characteristics of the consultancy market being seen as accounting for the difficulties in establishing agreement on the substantive content of consultancy expertise and the consequent emphasis on performance. Indeed, as has been noted, consultants may actively promote this view through their elevation of practicality as the essence of their expertise, thereby undermining their substantive knowledge claims. The ABC case, however, suggests that consultants face a number of additional dilemmas that may contribute to the instability of their expertise.

## 7.1. Standardisation vs customisation

Among the more significant of these, would appear to be that between standardisation of consultancy expertise and its customisation. This dilemma would seem to be actively reinforced by both consultants and their clients. Thus, from an internal consultancy perspective there is an interest in simplifying and formalising expertise, such that it can be easily communicated and transferred within the organisation, whether to train consultants in new services or to enable client executives to sell them. Standardisation may also enable work to be carried out by less-experienced staff, thereby increasing potential margins on projects sold. While this would seem unlikely to be welcomed by clients, unless associated with fee reductions, they may also have an interest in standardisation if it improves service reliability and consistency and quality of delivery.

At the same time, however, from the individual consultants’ perspective, standardisation restricts their scope for creativity, turning them from skilled practitioners, providing their own distinctive contribution to a project, to mechanical operators following a pre-defined formula. As the sensitivity about billing rates and being treated as “bodies” illustrated, this was something to which the project consultants were strenuously opposed. Unless consultants are convinced of the inherent superiority of their company’s products and feel they gain status from their association with them, therefore, standardisation may risk undermining the confidence on which consultants’ effective practice depends. Standardisation is also not without its drawbacks from the consultancy’s perspective in that it makes their products more easily replicable by competitors. Clients may also feel that the distinctive needs of their organisation are unlikely to be satisfactorily addressed by a standard method.

A similar choice, between what they call codification and personalisation, is addressed by Hansen et al. (1999) in their discussion of consultancy knowledge management strategies. Their conclusion, that companies should focus on either one or the other, however, would seem to be questioned by the findings of the ABC case, which may be seen to that suggest both standardisation and customisation are necessary in sustaining consultants’ claims to expertise. Too much standardisation risks expertise being seen as trivial and mechanistic, too much customisation risks it being seen as unreliable and lacking in substance. This would seem to be supported by practitioners’ critical commentaries (Pagani, 1999; Yu, 1999) on Hansen et al. (1999). Consultants therefore walk a fine line in balancing the two, arguably incompatible, positions, needing to present their expertise as simultaneously standardised and customised.

## 7.2. Novelty vs experience

A second significant dilemma in consultancy expertise, highlighted by the focus of the ABC case on the rolling out of a new consultancy product, is that between novelty and experience. As was noted, novelty is an important differentiator in the consultancy market place. Individual consultants are also likely to want to be involved in leading edge projects. While seeking to position themselves as innovators and to provide opportunities for their consultants to acquire experience of new practices, however, consultancies need to reassure clients that their products are “tried and tested”. Rhetorical strategies emphasising the depth of practical experience on which new products are based, such as those adopted in The Book, may not be sufficient to achieve this, though, without the support of clients’ ambivalent attitude towards novelty. Thus Sturdy (1997) argues that, while clients may express a wish for a distinctive and innovative service, they may simultaneously seek conformity with current practice, making them receptive to consultants’ claims.

## 7.3. Visibility vs intangibility

A similar tension may exist between visibility and intangibility in respect of consultancy practice. Thus, on the one hand, clients may wish to have recognisable evidence that consultants are delivering value, but at the same time to feel that their contribution is in some way exceptional and ineffable. In the Agency project, for example, face time and visible effort were positively viewed, but so too was evidence of the consultants’ holistic understanding of the Agency and mastery of The Approach techniques. Equally, consultants want their practice to be valued and appreciated by clients but also to feel that they offer some indefinable extra capabilities that both justifies the fees they charge and distinguishes them from the clients employees. Hence the attention paid to preparations for client meetings and the close monitoring of the CEO’s perceptions of the project, but also the consultants’ emphasis on “getting it” and concern with control.

Clearly, this is closely tied with the security of consultants’ self-identities. The importance of consultants’ self-belief to their effective performance as experts, and of clients’ belief in the consultants in sustaining this, points to the ongoing active construction of consultancy expertise and its sensitivity to loss of confidence on either side. Thus, for example, internal recognition of capabilities (or perceived lack of it), both directly in the audit of expertise, but also indirectly in measures such as billing rates and invitations to contribute to training, acquired a disproportionate significance in consultants’ eyes. Similarly, while a certain level of client dissatisfaction could be dismissed as expected evidence of resistance to change, the efforts devoted to cultivating client approval were notable. While professionals are no doubt not without their anxieties, the precariousness of consultancy expertise would seem considerably greater than suggested by writers such as Reed (1996) who view consultants as just one class of generic experts.

As the earlier comments suggest, another aspect of the visibility/intangibility dilemma relates to the question of the value of consultancy services. Interestingly, it would appear that often the more intangible the service the more highly it is valued. Thus technical IT consultants generally attract lower fees than strategic IT consultants. Since the value contributed by intangible services is, by definition, largely invisible, fees reflect a “going rate” based on the willingness of clients to pay and the confidence of consultants to charge. Hence, as was noted in the ABC case, to charge too low a fee for intangible services may raise questions about their quality. The value of such services is therefore not set, on some objective criterion, by either the consultant or client, but constructed through their interaction, reflecting market conditions and indirect quality indicators (including the fee itself).

## 7.4. Individual vs collective expertise

One further dualism evident from the ABC case was that between individual and collective expertise. While internal advancement and status were related to some notion of individual expertise, the client may also be interested in collective expertise and experience. Moreover, the Agency project may be seen as illustrating that the expertise drawn on by the consultants was not wholly individual, but was, in some senses, distributed through the organisation. Thus the experience of The Approach present in ABC, both directly in the back-up available from its originators, but also more diffusely in the common practices and beliefs of consultants provided a resource that shaped and supported the activities of individual consultants. It may therefore be possible, following Hutchins (1996), to consider expertise as being located not within the individual consultants, but in the system of relationships, practices and technologies of which the consultants were a part.

The picture of consultancy expertise that emerges from the ABC case, therefore, suggests that, even in an area such as IT/management consultancy in which technical knowledge might be expected to play a significant role, it is considerably more performative than is generally suggested in the literature. Rather than performance and impression management being the essential quality of consultancy expertise, as alternative writers such as Clark (1995) have suggested, however, the ABC case may be seen as illustrating its ongoing construction through practice. Clients may be identified as having an active role in this process, which draws on an array of elements including technical knowledge, skilled practice, individual and collective experience, and commercial interests. Balancing conflicting pressures for both practicality and theoretical significance, standardisation and customisation, novelty and experience, visibility and intangibility, and individual and collective capability, consultants’ expertise is thus continuously constructed in interaction with their clients.

Locating consultancy expertise in these interactions, and exploring the tensions within consultancy practice that contribute to their dynamics, therefore, enables us to reconceptualise expertise from a relatively-enduring property of individuals to a situated practice, actively sustained by consultants and their clients.

## 8. Conclusions

While expertise has traditionally been associated with professionals, the highly competitive and open nature of the consultancy industry and the lack an agreed body of knowledge, let alone the institutional structures to sustain it, makes consultants’ claims to expertise particularly precarious. Pressured by industry dynamics to engage in a continuous search for new, distinctive and plausibly effective “solutions”, their formal expertise is often transitory, proprietary and intangible. This last quality may indeed be deliberately promoted to protect competitive advantage, perhaps even at the expense of weakening reasonable claims to substantive knowledge and placing greater reliance on visible effort as a manifestation of their expertise.

Emphasising practicality, consultants also increase the importance placed on experience, despite recognising that this may only be a necessary, rather than sufficient, condition for expertise. Such experience may even be, in part, vicarious, where the company track record provides reputation and enhances confidence. Selfbelief would seem essential to the consultant’s expert performance, enabling them to manage impressions, through the deployment of a variety of symbolic outputs, in ways that seek to convince clients of their expertise.

Viewing impression management, and the process skills that support it as the core element of consultancy expertise, however, may overlook the extent to which technical and process skills are interlinked, and indeed the constructed nature of the distinction between them. Furthermore, this may be seen as but one aspect of the active role of both clients and consultants in the production and reproduction of consultancy expertise. Thus, rather than seeing expertise as something that a consultant has by virtue of their training, involving the acquisition of a body of technical knowledge and skilled practice, or that the consultant performs in managing clients’ impressions, it may be seen as created in the interaction of client and consultant, drawing on a system of elements, including technical knowledge, skilled practice, individual and collective experience, and commercial interests. The character of consultancy expertise would therefore seem likely to remain elusive and doubts about its content and practice unlikely to be definitively dispelled.
