# Stakeholders, soft systems and technology: separation and mediation in the analysis of information system requirements

Richard Vidgen

Department of Computation, UMIST, PO Box 88, Manchester M60 1QD, UK, email: rvidgen@co.umist.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper describes the application of stakeholder analysis and soft systems thinking for an investigation of information system requirements. It is argued that it is appropriate to approach IS development as an exercise in managing complexity (soft systems) and pluralism (stakeholder analysis). A framework for investigating IS requirements is proposed that contrasts the current situation with the future situation and the real world with conceptual thinking about the real world. These aspects are viewed as outcomes of the process of IS requirements analysis, for which the metaphor of mediation is adopted in preference to presenting requirements analysis as a binary distinction between social construction and objectivity. The IS requirements analysis framework is applied in action research and the findings and learning that arose are presented.

Keywords: Mediation, requirements analysis, soft systems methodology, stakeholder analysis

## INTRODUCTION

Despite attempts to make software development more rigorous, a considerable proportion of computer system development effort results in products that do not provide user satisfaction. Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) suggest that the level of IS failures is about 75%. They identify three traditional types of IS failure: correspondence failure, process failure and interaction failure. Correspondence failure reflects a predominantly management view and occurs when stated design objectives are not met; process failure occurs when the IS development process cannot produce a workable system, or when a system is produced but at a high level of budget or time overrun (Gladden, 1982); and interaction failure is indicated by low levels of use and poor user satisfaction (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Eason, 1992). Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) identify a fourth type of failure: expectation failure (which subsumes the three traditional types of failure) in which there are no general failures, as any IS failure must pose a problem for someone or some group. Following Mason and Mitroff (1981) and Freeman (1984), we refer to these groups as stakeholders and adopt a stakeholder-centred approach to IS development (Lyytinen, 1988).

Any information system development has the potential to affect many stakeholders, often cutting across the hierarchical intra-organizational structure as well as enabling inter-organizational working and even new ways of organizing. However, not all the stakeholders in an IS development will necessarily share common interests and have common beliefs. A more appropriate assumption in IS development perhaps is pluralism. Flood and Jackson (1991) (following from Burrell & Morgan, 1979; Morgan, 1986) define pluralism as a situation in which stakeholders are perceived to have a basic compatibility of interests; not necessarily agree on ends and means, but be able to achieve compromise; act in accordance with an agreed objective; and have some divergence of values and beliefs (p. 34).

Flood and Jackson (1991) also categorize problem contexts as simple or complex (pp. 33–34). In our view, IS developments are best viewed as inherently complex undertakings. An IS development typically comprises many elements (which have many interactions and are loosely organized), evolves over time and is subject to behavioural influence — all of which contribute to uncertainty of outcome. Where structured methods, such as SSADM (CCTA, 1990), appear to address complexity, they do so primarily through tackling technical complexity, using the tools of reductionism, rather than recognizing sociotechnical complexity and the need for a wider, systemic approach. All in all, we prefer to perceive a problem situation as a sociotechnical ‘mess’ rather than a technical ‘difficulty’ (Ackoff, 1974).

In the ‘Winterton Co-operative Development Agency’ case study, Flood and Jackson (1991) describe the use of strategic assumption surfacing and testing (SAST) (Mason & Mitroff, 1981) in conjunction with soft systems methodology (SSM) (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990). This combination of approaches is recommended as suitable for situations considered to be pluralist/complex: SAST copes with the issue of pluralism and SSM provides a way of addressing complexity (see also Flood, 1995 for further case studies). A combination of soft systems thinking and stakeholder analysis has been adopted in Vidgen (1994) as an appropriate way of organizing an investigation into information system requirements.

In this paper, we report on the practical experience of combining stakeholder analysis and soft systems thinking within an IS-specific framework for requirements analysis. The structure of the paper is as follows: the second section provides a rationale for combining stakeholder analysis and soft systems thinking in the IS development process; in the third section, a framework for the investigation of IS requirements is presented; in the fourth section, the research method, action research, is described and justified; in the fifth section, the fieldwork is described; the sixth section is concerned with the learning that arose from the research; and conclusions and recommendations for future work are presented in the last section.

## STAKEHOLDERS AND SOFT SYSTEMS

Stakeholder analysis and soft systems thinking have a common provenance in Churchman's (1968, 1971) work on inquiring systems. Significant characteristics of Churchman's work are an emphasis on participation ('sweeping in') and a recognition that any world view is restricted. The systems approach is to incorporate as many different perspectives as possible and to tackle problems through enlargement and synthesis rather than by focus and analysis (Freeman, 1984). Given this common background, we argue that it is reasonable, from a theoretical viewpoint at least, to combine stakeholder analysis and soft systems thinking for the purpose of investigating IS requirements in problem contexts perceived to be pluralist/complex.

## Stakeholder analysis

The origins of stakeholding have been traced back to the Stanford Research Institute who in 1963 defined stakeholders as ‘those groups without whose support the organization would cease to exist’ (reported by Freeman 1984, p. 31). This definition recognized the need for organizations to consider employees, customers, suppliers, lenders and society and constituted a significant movement away from the unitary view of organizations in which a single stakeholder group, the shareholders, are privileged, and action is legitimated through an appeal to a unitary and economic objective, such as profit maximization. The basis of the Stanford definition is a view of stakeholders as a necessity, a view reflected in Ansoff’s (1965) early work, in which stakeholders represent a constraint on the level of the objective (profit maximization) that can be attained at any particular time. The systems literature embraced stakeholding as a response to diversity of interests and the need to consider the environment in which the organization operates, seeking to involve stakeholders as participants rather than treating them as constraints (Ackoff, 1974).

The stakeholder concept has been generalized to the point where Freeman (1984) proposed that ‘a stakeholder in an organization is (by definition) any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization’s objectives’ (p. 46) and, more recently, Mitroff and Linstone (1993) defined stakeholders to be ‘any individual, group, organization, or institution that can affect as well as be affected by an individual’s, group’s, organization’s, or institution’s policy or policies’ (p. 141). Stakeholding emerges from a strategic perspective in management and organizational theory, as evidenced by the titles of two influential works: Freeman’s (1984) Strategic Management and Mason and Mitroff’s (1981) Challenging Strategic Planning Assumptions. It might be argued that, while stakeholder approaches are relevant to the formulation of IS strategy, they are not appropriate for IS development projects.

However, there is evidence that the identity of organizations is changing, making strategy an issue for all levels of organizational unit. Clegg (1990) has argued that we are in an era of epochal post-modern organizations, which are characterized by niche marketing, multiskilled workforces and flexible manufacturing. Clegg (1990) takes a modernist approach to the study of post-modern organizational forms, while other authors have argued that post-modernist concepts are needed in the study of organizations (Cooper & Burrell, 1988). These post-modern organizational forms are compatible with the central metaphor of evolved theories of stake-holding: the organization as a web of stakeholder relationships rather than the organization as an autonomous entity. Wicks et al. (1994) propose a series of reworked metaphors, whereby conflict and competition, objective strategy and hierarchical power are replaced by communicative and collective action, solidarity between agents and stakeholders, and decentralization and empowerment. This view is supported by Mitroff and Linstone (1993) who propose that an organization comprises the entire set of relationships it has with itself and its stakeholders (p. 142); it is also sympathetic to Vickers' notion of appreciation and a concern with relationship maintaining and judgement rather than the 'poverty-stricken notion of goal-seeking' (Checkland & Casar, 1986). In this sense, organizations become verbs rather than nouns and organization becomes an active process of maintaining identity and viability — a theme central to Beer's work in cybernetics (Beer, 1979, 1981).

Mason and Mitroff (1981) and Freeman (1984) offer well-developed and well-documented approaches to stakeholder analysis. For the purpose of this research we have focused on Mason and Mitroff's strategic assumption surfacing and testing (SAST) method, since it has a particularly strong provenance in the systems thinking tradition. SAST comprises the following stages: group formation; surfacing of assumptions; dialectical debate; and synthesis. In assumption surfacing the groups identify stakeholders, articulate their assumptions about stakeholders, and then rate the assumptions using two dimensions: the degree of importance to the intended change and the level of certainty that the assumption accorded to the stakeholder by the group is justified. The groups then present their positions and engage in debate, following which a synthesis is attempted through a process of negotiation.

## Soft systems thinking

Soft systems methodology (SSM) (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990) has brought considerable richness to the development of information systems (IS). SSM has been adopted within the Multiview IS development methodology (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990, 1991; Wood-Harper & Avison, 1992) and has been adopted and adapted as a front end for SSADM using the work of Wilson (1990). Systems ideas are used in SSM to organize thought about a problem situation; one seeks to avoid the limitations of a means—ends orientation by focusing on problem formulation and what needs to be done rather than on problem solving per se. In its basic form, SSM comprises a seven-stage model in which: a problem situation is considered problematic; the problem situation is expressed (this often entails the development of a rich picture); root definitions (including the CATWOE mnemonic) are formulated; conceptual models of purposeful activity are developed; the conceptual models are compared with the real world; changes are proposed — these should be systemically desirable but must be culturally feasible; and, lastly, action to improve the situation is taken. The use of conceptual models and root definitions, together with the CATWOE mnemonic, encourages reasoned, rigorous and internally cohesive modelling that encapsulates most of the concepts important in systems thinking, while the 'Weltanschauung' (world view) concept encourages consideration of different perspectives. In its evolved form, this logical stream of analysis is complemented by cultural analysis in which social and political metaphors are employed (Checkland & Scholes, 1990, p. 29). SSM is a participative approach in which the aim is to achieve an accommodation concerning what action should be taken.

## A FRAMEWORK FOR REQUIREMENTS ANALYSIS IN IS DEVELOPMENT

We have argued that it is appropriate to view the IS development domain as pluralist/complex and that stakeholder analysis and SSM are suitable and compatible methods to use in an investigation of IS requirements. However, there are two particular shortcomings of using stakeholder analysis and soft systems in IS development. The first concern is that neither approach explicitly addresses the role of information technology (IT) and the widening impact that IT has on organizational work (Orlikowski et al., 1996), together with its informing capability (Zuboff, 1988). The second concern is that there is little practical guidance for the elicitation of information system requirements from real world stakeholders (see Macaulay, 1994 for details of a practical approach to IS requirements capture). Thus, in the horizontal orientation of Figure 1, the real world of user requirements is contrasted with the conceptual world of soft systems and stakeholders and 're-visions' (Vidgen et al., 1994) of the problem situation. The vertical orientation contrasts the current situation with some desirable and concrete future that incorporates both people and (information) technology (Avison et al., 1994). The role of technology in the IS requirements analysis process should be made explicit because those technologies used in the current situation might serve as a constraint on what action can be taken, while in considering a future situation new technology might enable radically different business processes and forms of organization (Davenport, 1993). The minor interactions between the four aspects reflect further dependencies. For example, conceptual models relevant to a problem situation are used to reinterpret the current situation, to make sense of real world requirements articulated by stakeholders, and to re-vision the problem situation. The capture of real world requirements allows the analyst to identify issues and constraints in the current situation, to provide a basis for assessing the relevance of conceptual models and to identify problems and specify requirements to be (re-) solved in some future situation.

Double-headed arrows have been used in Figure 1 to show the four aspects of an investigation of IS requirements as co-present, co-dependent and emergent (Giddens, 1984). We are suspicious of a technical rationality that assumes an inexorable temporal flow from current situation to future situation and find it equally difficult to accept a complete separation of conceptual world and real world, preferring to view the four aspects as outcomes of the requirements analysis activity. Consequently, IS requirements analysis is represented in Figure 1 as a process of mediation (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1991, 1993). According to this view the object and subject worlds of requirements capture and conceptualization are inseparable (as are the current situation and a desirable future situation), being but two sides of the same coin. A successful requirements analysis process leads to the appearance of separation such that a plausible, although post-rationalized, account of the IS requirements can be presented.

## RESEARCH METHOD

Galliers (1985, 1992) and Galliers and Land (1987) have classified research methods into two categories: scientific and interpretivist. The scientific approach assumes that phenomena can be observed objectively and rigorously; good research is legitimated with reference to the virtues of repeatability, reductionism and refutability (Checkland, 1981). In contrast, the interpretivist approach considers the methods of natural science to be inappropriate where human beings are concerned, and it is recognized that different stakeholders (including researchers) can interpret a situation in different ways. These two views of research can be characterized as positivism, which is concerned with reducing the area of investigation in order to be able to make reliable predictions, and interpretivism, which is concerned with making a reading of history in order to gain understanding. Given the speculative and informal nature of the framework in Figure 1, experimental method is inappropriate at this stage of the research. However, case study without intervention is also inappropriate, since the framework and the combination of analysis methods were not in use in an organizational domain. Consequently, the author decided that an interventional approach — action research — would be appropriate, allowing ideas about information systems requirements analysis to be explored through practical application.

Action research provides a way of building theory and descriptions within the context of practice itself. The theories are tested through intervention in an organizational context, through ‘experiments’ that bear the double burden of testing hypotheses and effecting some desirable change in the situation (Argyris & Schön, 1991). This approach to research is neatly summarized by Baskerville and Wood-Harper (1996): ‘The gaining of knowledge is seen as an active process, such that our beliefs are redefined in the light of the outcomes. A means for dealing with reality is more desirable than a representation of reality. Action research is a pragmatic approach which desires to ‘come to terms’ with the world’ (p. 239). A pervasive model for action research is from Susman and Evered (1978) and Susman (1983). This model involves the establishment of a relationship between researchers and the participants’ (clients’) infrastructure in support of a five-phase cycle of action. Checkland (1991) describes a seven-stage model applicable to action research that stresses the importance of declaring a framework of ideas (F) and a methodology (M) that will be used to investigate some area of application (A).

Checkland (1991) sees the declaration of an explicit methodological framework as a vehicle for establishing the validity of the action research approach and for making the results of an action research project ‘coherent and potentially transferable’ (p. 402). Baskerville and Wood-Harper (1996) make the point that the declaration of a framework of ideas and methodology are important if action research is to be distinguished from consultancy.

With respect to the research presented in this paper, the framework of ideas (F) is expressed in Figure 1 through a view of requirements analysis as mediation; the methodology (M) is represented by the content of Figure 1, which comprises current situation analysis, systemic stakeholder analysis, requirements capture, and future analysis. In the next section the action research is presented, followed by reflections on the learning made with respect to the methodology and the framework of ideas.

## THE FIELDWORK

## Background to the situation

The fieldwork was conducted in an organization involved with all aspects of aircraft design and manufacture. For the purposes of this paper, the organization is referred to as ‘Eurospace’. The UK division of Eurospace has headquarters in Manchester with further sites throughout the UK, the major centres being Birmingham and London. The author joined a project, code-named ‘X52’, concerned with the development of software for wind tunnel operation. The sponsors of the software development project were the Wind Tunnel Department (WTD) and the sponsors of the action research were Eurospace’s Software Quality Group. For Eurospace the presence of in-house wind tunnel facilities has the benefit of guaranteed and controlled access to test facilities and is considered to be a strategic requirement by Eurospace senior management. Eurospace have concentrated the physical wind tunnel facilities at Manchester, from where test facilities are provided to aerodynamicists based in Manchester, Birmingham and London.

The aerodynamicists require data to help them solve aerodynamic problems that are associated with operational requirements, such as aircraft manoeuvrability. The work can range from designs for a new aircraft to minor modifications, such as the positioning of a wing flap. WTD and aerodynamic personnel often need to work together closely and in many cases aerodynamicists wish to be present while a test run is being conducted, especially in situations where a new design is being evaluated and there is significant uncertainty about the outcome. As a result of concentrating wind tunnel facilities on the Manchester site, there are increasing geographical difficulties for aerodynamicists based in Birmingham and London. The WTD also carries out a small amount of external work, such as wind tunnel tests of motor cars and sea-craft. This work tends to be infrequent and is not currently a significant source of revenue.

## Wind tunnel operation

A brief description of wind tunnel operation is included to give the reader a feeling for the application domain, wind tunnel technology and aerodynamics, for which software was to be developed. A typical low speed wind tunnel has a continuous (circular) flow with a speed range of $5–105 \, m s^{-1}$ . The flow is supplied by a fan. To conduct a wind tunnel test, a model of an aircraft is placed on a 'sting' in the tunnel and the wind turned on (Figure 2). The sting mounting system traverses the model through a predefined range of incidences (up-and-down movement) and sideslips (side-to-side movement). The combination of incidence and sideslip is known as the model attitude. In general, one factor is designated as the test variable, such as incidence, and all the other variables, such as sideslip, tunnel speed and model configuration are held constant for the duration of a particular run. A common test is to move the model through a range of incidences, while keeping the other variables constant — this type of test is known as an 'incidence traverse'.

The forces and moments on the model are measured using transducers in a strain gauge balance located inside the model. Wind tunnel test data is collected using a data acquisition system (DAS), which comprises hardware, such as amplifiers and transducers, and software. The stream of analogue data collected by the DAS is sampled and converted into digital form and presented in engineering units — this is raw data. The raw data is then transmitted from the UNIX workstation to a mainframe suite of programs, known as TRIPOS, where corrections are applied to compensate for the artificial aspects of the wind tunnel (scaling, tunnel characteristics, etc.) resulting in processed data. It is this processed data that is the best approximation of the aerodynamic characteristics of the full-size target object.

However, there is a further complication to wind tunnel operation. To allow compensations to be made to the data for the weight of the model a tare traverse is taken with the wind turned off — this set of values is then subtracted from the readings obtained when the wind is on. Tare values are typically taken at coarser incidence intervals than the wind-on data as the tare values are a smooth function and can be interpolated. The tare run is done after the wind-on data collection run, which means that processed data cannot be made available to aerodynamicists until the tare run and mainframe post-processing are complete.

## Project X52

The current mode of low-speed tunnel operation is labour intensive and requires manual integration of the various wind tunnel components, such as sting movement, DAS interfacing and tunnel speed control. The current high level of manual intervention means that considerable experience of wind tunnel operation is needed and that operator errors can occur. The WTD consider that data quality and operational reliability can be improved through further automation and integration of wind tunnel components. Prototype software is being developed using a graphical user interface (GUI) that will provide a single window on tunnel operation, consolidating all of the various subcomponent monitoring and control systems on a single workstation.

A further aim of the WTD is to promote flexible working and to reorganize around functions rather than operating each of the tunnels as mini-wind tunnel departments. WTD management consider that a common DAS design and common wind tunnel control systems, particularly in the area of user interfaces for wind tunnel operation, are needed to support a flexible, facility-based approach to WTD activity. This proposed change represents a more general movement within Eurospace towards a customer-facing ethos rather than one organized around the supplier's view of operations. However, this is a major change in working practice and is expected to have a significant impact on the nature of organizational work in the WTD.

## Entering the problem situation

Prototyping of the wind tunnel control software was well under way when the researcher joined project X52 and the expectation was that the research would continue in parallel with software development — the research was not expected to result in changes to the terms of reference or the scope of the project. A simple strategy would have been to place the tunnel operation software at the centre of the perceived world and to identify stakeholders from that perspective. Obvious stakeholder groups would be tunnel operators and project supervisors, since these are the people who are the primary users of the wind tunnel software. Undoubtedly, these are the main users of the software in terms of their closeness to the computer system and the work that it supports, helping them to carry out their day-to-day duties. However, a central aim of the research was to locate the X52 project in a larger organizational context in order to understand the wider implications of this information system development project. The organizational analysis therefore began at the level of the Wind Tunnel Department (WTD). The software development team and even some WTD personnel found it hard to see why the scope of the research should be quite this wide. As the research unfolded, the benefits and implications of this broad scope became apparent.

## The intervention

The action research is described using the structure of Figure 1 and consists of the following activities: current situation analysis, systemic stakeholder analysis, requirements capture and future analysis.

## Current situation

The rich picture is an established SSM technique, which provides on a single sheet a graphical and heterogeneous representation of complex and messy situations (Lewis, 1992). It supplements and often replaces the thick textual descriptions that are more typical of feasibility reports and preliminary analyses. Figure 3 is a rich picture developed by the researcher to represent salient features of the WTD and X52 context.

A generic stakeholder map (Freeman, 1984) was used as a template together with the rich picture to make a context-specific stakeholder map (Figure 4). Stakeholder subcategories were introduced to provide structure: for example, customers are categorized into external and internal customers, with internal customers being identified explicitly, that is London, Manchester and Birmingham. Human stakeholders and non-human allies (for example, the mainframe computer system) are included in the stakeholder map in order to keep organizational and technological issues together. A benefit of this approach is that constraining and enabling aspects of technology can be considered jointly with proposals for organizational change.

## Systemic stakeholder analysis

The aim of systemic stakeholder analysis (SSA) is to develop SSM models that provide a rigorous approach to the analysis of stakeholders and assumptions made about stakeholders. SSM was used in ‘mode 2’ (Checkland & Scholes, 1990, pp. 280–285), in which the SSM practitioner uses the ideas of SSM to structure the analysis of a situation, while not necessarily training those involved in the theory, principles, language and methods of SSM directly. This approach was adopted contingently since formal training in SSM would not have been perceived as meaningful by the X52 project team. However, the researcher found that by asking the basic questions ‘what is being done?’ and ‘why should it be done?’, it was possible to initiate debate that informed the formulation of root definitions and CATWOEs, particularly once subsidiary questions such as how, who, when, and where were added. A range of primary task models that might be appropriate for debate about the purpose of a WTD were created and a working model emerged:

'An Aerodynamic Technology owned system operated by the Wind Tunnel Department in conjunction with aerodynamicists to acquire aerodynamic data by carrying out a series of tests on a model in a wind tunnel in order to understand the aerodynamic characteristics of a target object, subject to the limitations of scaling effects, tunnel flow representativeness and model accuracy.'

Associated with the root definition is a CATWOE (Table 1). Conceptual models of purposeful activity were developed for the root definition/CATWOE and performance measures identified (efficacy, efficiency and effectiveness). Although the root definition might appear rather mundane, it proved surprisingly difficult to formulate this basic model of purposeful activity; the WTD had been established for many years and it was ‘obvious’ what they did and why – at least, it was until the researcher attempted to discuss purpose explicitly.

To understand the viewpoints of other stakeholders better and to gain an insight into how an intervention such as X52 might be perceived, we developed further conceptualizations of purposeful activity. For example, wind tunnels produce noise as well as aerodynamic data and this noise can be invasive for local residents (Figure 5). Should the WTD decide to operate the tunnels through the night, or during weekends, then local residents might take action, perhaps to lobby the local council for restrictions to be placed on the hours of wind tunnel operation (for example, no test runs after 18.00 h and no weekend operation). Although one would not think of local residents as a customer, by formulating a root definition that is relevant to this stakeholder group, it is possible to cast them as an SSM Customer (in this case victim) (the conceptual Customer of SSM is distinguished by a capital C from the real-world customer, which has a lower-case c). The general and ongoing assumption with respect to X52 might be that local residents will not campaign for shorter hours of operation.

As with the primary task analysis of the WTD, there are many views that might be taken of the X52 project — for the centralized purchasing department, project X52 might be appropriately regarded as an opportunity for enforcing procurement procedures.

In the SSM orthodoxy (Checkland, 1995), soft systems-inspired models are not intended to describe what the WTD is or what the X52 project is. Neither should it be assumed that SSM models describe what the WTD or X52 ought to be — SSM models are devices that serve as a basis for coherent and informed discussion, for the surfacing of assumptions and for the creation of a shared understanding (possibly a consensus, but at least some accommodation of interests). Having discussed what the purpose of the WTD might be, it was possible to generate key assumptions about the stakeholders identified in the current situation (Table 2). Stakeholders are not fixed and, as the project unfolded, further relevant stakeholders were recognized. For example, the WTD was subject to a quality audit as part of gaining third part accreditation under ISO9000 and the procedures, audit trails and authorizations required for conducting wind tunnel tests were scrutinized closely. Certainly, failure to take account of the quality auditors' requirements of X52 would have had severe and far-reaching consequences. There was now an understanding of what the purpose of the WTD might be and the potential role of the X52 intervention. An SSM conceptual model of human activity was developed from the primary purpose root definition and CATWOE. By starting at the level of the WTD, it was possible to provide a context for the operation of a wind tunnel. However, by looking up into the context of the WTD, it was also possible to re-vision the role of the WTD within the Eurospace organization.

Re-visioning. The transformation outlined in Table 1 has been constrained by a 'how', since the Weltanschauung reflects the assumption that aerodynamic data is collected in a physical wind tunnel. This is a deep-rooted assumption of many wind tunnel personnel, some of whom have been working in the department for 20 or 30 years. However, there are other ways of satisfying the need for aerodynamic data, such as the use of computational flow dynamics (CFD), in which forces and moments are simulated using computer-based models. Rather than descend into a reductionist analysis of how the collection of aerodynamic data might be improved through technology, the analysis was opened out, thus allowing the context of the WTD to be explored. In SSM models the success factor effectiveness ('E3') is concerned with the longer term aims of the system. To address this question, it is necessary to understand how aerodynamic data might be of value to aerodynamicists. To discuss the purpose of the aerodynamics department (and thus to provide an organizational context for the WTD), models of purposeful activity appropriate to aerodynamicists were formed, including the following root definition:

'An Aerodynamic Technology owned and operated system to create (modify) object designs that have acceptable aerodynamic characteristics by building mathematical models of aerodynamic characteristics of an object and testing those models through simulation prior to flight test in order to meet the operational needs of Airframe Technology and other external customers safely and cost-effectively, subject to airworthiness requirements.'

The transformation was expressed as:

object design → object design with acceptable aerodynamic characteristics

with a Weltanschauung:

new operational requirements arise that require new and modified aircraft (objects). Cost and safety concerns mean that any new design must be demonstrated to be aerodynamically sound before manufacturing takes place.

The formulation was of a system to solve aerodynamic problems with one of the activities in the conceptual model being concerned with the acquisition and management of aerodynamic data (Figure 6). In using this model, the assumption that wind tunnels (physical simulations) are used for collecting aerodynamic data was surfaced and debated. Data can also be collected from third-party wind tunnels, from CFD simulations and from flight tests. In this reformulation, the role of a WTD might be to act as data managers, responsible for the collection, storage, distribution and quality of aerodynamic data. A suitable measure of success might then be the level of reuse of aerodynamic data (a current problem is that aerodynamicists request wind tunnel tests to be repeated because they cannot access the original data easily or simply don't trust the original data).

An intervention based upon the above re-vision would change the role of the WTD significantly. This does not mean that the process ought to be redesigned on the basis of the re-visioning — the aim was to show that a focus on the operation of wind tunnels can result in a narrow view of the world that constrains the opportunity for radical change. In SSM terms, one might, for example, consider it systemically desirable to view WTD as data managers (especially if physical wind tunnels are replaced by computer simulations), but if such a view is not culturally feasible, then even small changes in the organization that are based upon this view are unlikely to be successful.

## Requirements capture

Although SSM gives a powerful means of reconceptualizing the problem situation and SAST allows stakeholders to be identified and the assumptions those involved in the intervention make about stakeholders to be surfaced, debated and shared, neither approach provides detailed and practical guidance for the identification of specific requirements and qualities demanded by real-world customers. In order to gather the ‘voice of the customer’ a quality requirements workshop was held at each of the Aerodynamic Technology locations (Manchester, Birmingham and London). Each site was asked to supply around six representatives for a workshop. The workshops were conducted by the researcher supported by the WTD representative and followed guidelines set out by Bossert (1991) for gathering quality requirements.

The basic approach was the same for each half-day workshop. The purpose and aims of the workshop were explained and then issues in the current situation were explored by getting the attendees to annotate a photo-enlarged copy (A2 size) of the rich picture (Figure 3). Attendees were then asked ‘what are the qualities demanded by aerodynamicists of an excellent wind tunnel department?’ No more guidance was given, other than to ask the attendees not to be constrained by existing operations, organization structure and cost factors. Responses were recorded on PostIt notes and placed on a large conference table. The aerodynamicists were encouraged to record their requirements directly onto the PostIts, the aim being to capture the customer’s words using the customer’s terminology and phrasing and to minimize unsubstantiated assumptions made by the researcher or the WTD representative about the meaning of the text. These requirements are known as customer verbatims.

Once sufficient ideas had been captured, the aerodynamicists were asked to conduct affinity grouping, which involves participants in grouping PostIt's that appear (to them) to be related in some way. A PostIt was then chosen to represent the group; if there was no suitable PostIt in the group, then a new PostIt was created to act as a group header. These requirements were taken away and the results of the three workshops were used as the basis for the creation of a tree-structured list of customer-demanded qualities (for example, ‘supplies wind tunnel data in a standard format’, ‘gives rapid delivery of test plan estimates’, ‘has conscientious WTD staff’). The demanded qualities were used to develop a quality plan (King, 1989). The quality plan was distributed as a questionnaire to aerodynamicists who were asked to rank the importance of the qualities, to assess the WTD’s current performance and to compare the WTD to its competitors.

Clearly, further requirements capture could be carried out for other stakeholders. However, in this context the WTD viewed aerodynamicists as its principal customer and the requirements capture process was focused on this stakeholder group. As the analysis became more specific about wind tunnel operation then those stakeholders whose organizational work was affected by the X52 intervention were involved more closely (e.g. tunnel operators). The impact on work was addressed through the sociotechnical analysis of the Multiview method (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990, 1991), but is outside the scope of this paper.

## Future analysis

Using the understanding of the current situation, the real-world requirements of aerodynamicists and the conceptualizations and re-visions of soft systems thinking, different futures could be envisaged with respect to the WTD and project X52. Three areas in which technology could be enrolled were deemed to be of particular importance: the provision of aerodynamic data in real time, virtual working and communication/group working.

Real-time data. A major issue for the aerodynamicists was the ability to see wind tunnel test data being produced in real time. With the current systems (Figure 2), processed data could only be accessed once the run was finished and the data sent to the TRIPOS mainframe system for processing. This means that there is a delay in seeing the results of a test run and that an aerodynamicist sitting in the tunnel has to wait until the run has completed before a judgement can be made as to whether the data is usable. In experimental situations, speculative changes are made and the aerodynamicist can often tell if the data will be of any use from the first few data points. If the processed data are made available in real time, then a run can be halted, further changes made and another test run started — subject to the tare run to compensate for the weight of the model being executed with the wind off prior to the test run proper being conducted with the wind turned on. This approach would result in significant improvements in tunnel throughput and enable the aerodynamicists to work more effectively.

In the current operation, the wind tunnel supervisor checks the test data for reasonableness before releasing it to the aerodynamicist, thus introducing a further element of delay. Clearly, there is a reluctance to release data that might be unreliable, and part of the justification for new DAS hardware and software was related to improved data reliability. However, the reluctance to release test data reflects a WTD attitude of 'they can have the data when we are happy with it and ready to hand it over'. The requirements workshops highlighted a strong customer requirement for real-time data and a recognition that competitor facilities already supply this service. Prior to the workshops, the WTD attitude was 'we know what our customers want' — the workshops demonstrated the importance of listening carefully to what customers say. As the soft systems analysis and requirements capture progressed, it became apparent that the distinction between the data collection on UNIX workstations and the data processing on a mainframe was an artificial boundary constituted by the limitations of 1980s computing. The advances in workstation processing power meant that data processing could now be moved from the mainframe into a workstation environment, allowing test data to be processed in real time and presented both as text and as colour graphics. The mainframe computing was originally outside the scope of X52, which had been conceived as a project to improve efficiency; changing the scope of X52 to include real-time data would allow the project to contribute to the effectiveness of the WTD and, based on the requirements workshops with aerodynamicists, to a significant improvement in customer satisfaction.

Virtual working. It was also clear from the workshops that the aerodynamicists were keen to cut down unproductive time spent travelling and sitting in the tunnel, leading us to envisage a situation in which data and video links would allow aerodynamicists to monitor test runs from their local offices. Video-conferencing would allow operators and aerodynamicists to communicate while the model is prepared in the tunnel and during the test run; real-time processed data in graphical form could be displayed locally on the aerodynamicist's workstation; fixed elevation cameras could be used to display the model in the tunnel, and hand-held cameras for the tunnel operator to show specific test modifications, such as a lump of bluetak stuck on to the model. Going further, we envisaged a situation in which the aerodynamicists would operate the wind tunnels remotely — the user interface for the wind tunnel control system could be displayed remotely on the aerodynamicist's workstation, with robots being used to install and modify a model in the tunnel. Although this might be slightly fanciful in terms of today's technology (and also the large element of craft needed in setting up a wind tunnel model), the scenario did provide a liberating way of thinking about the possible futures for wind tunnel operation.

Communication. The aerodynamicists also wanted to be kept informed of the status and progress on a test series. We envisaged the use of a groupware product that the operators and supervisors would use to maintain a log of activity related to the lifecycle of a test series. If aerodynamicists were given access to the groupware, then they could find out the status of their test series and the individual test runs without needing to send faxes, make telephone calls, and so on. This would also free WTD personnel from the need to take action to inform customers of progress, as well as providing an online log book of actions taken in the wind tunnel during test runs, something required by quality management systems such as ISO9000.

## Exiting the research situation

New ways of working supported by new technologies were explored in the future analysis allowing us to envisage different and exciting scenarios for the WTD. However, change in the WTD was constrained by cultural feasibility, technological capability and economic factors. Given that WTD personnel and IT developers did not have any expectation that the research would lead to changes in project X52, the researcher was realistic about the extent to which the intervention would result in change in the problem situation. Despite this, the action research did contribute to the scope of X52 being widened to include real-time data processing. The proposals concerning new technologies such as groupware for improved communication were considered to be outside the scope of X52 but were incorporated into the WTD IT strategy, which could now draw upon customer needs gathered in the quality requirements workshops to legitimate organizational change and further IT development in the WTD.

## DISCUSSION OF THE ACTION RESEARCH

In this section the learning from the action research is recorded (Checkland, 1991). The learning is presented at two levels: the first level is concerned with the lessons from the application of the methodology (M) in the action research and relates to the content of Figure 1; the second level is more abstract and relates to the framework of ideas (F) that govern the form of Figure 1, i.e. IS requirements analysis as mediation.

## Learning about the IS requirements analysis methodology

## Stakeholder identification

With respect to the current situation, the development of a rich picture provided a useful starting point for the identification of stakeholders and for gaining a pre-understanding of the problem situation. The sequential presentation in this report may give the impression that there was a single rich picture that could be put to one side once an initial view of the problem situation had been sketched; in practice, the rich picture evolved over the life of the action research as the environment changed and reinterpretations and redescriptions were made as a result of requirements workshops, systemic stakeholder analysis and future analysis. This reflects the dynamic aspects of IS development, suggesting that the identification of stakeholders and the appreciation of relationships needs to be viewed as an on-going dialogue with an evolving problem situation and reflected in a series of rich pictures.

## Using SSM in mode 2

In the action research SSM was used to organize thought about the problem situation ('mode 2') and to a lesser extent as formal step-by-step guide to intervention ('mode 1') (Checkland & Scholes, 1990, p. 281). At first this was a pragmatic response by the researcher to a situation in which a formal application of SSM would have been considered an unwarranted intrusion into the WTD and the X52 project and consequently the researcher found it necessary to present SSM models using language that was meaningful to those involved. For example, the Technical Computing manager was comfortable with formulating models that addressed 'focus' (what is to be done – transformation) and 'purpose' (why it should be done – Weltanschauung). The use of language that was meaningful to those in the problem situation was an essential element of using SSM in this instance, suggesting that mode 2 application is the norm and mode 1 the special case. Unfortunately, to use SSM in mode 2 requires that the principles of SSM be internalized by the analyst, and this is a long-term endeavour that is only likely to be achieved through extensive practical experience.

## On combining SSM and SAST

In systemic stakeholder analysis it is proposed that SSM and SAST be used in concert. In SSM there is a clear distinction between the conceptual world and the real world of the perceived problem situation. Therefore, there might be unease concerning the development of SSM models that are associated with different stakeholders. However, we hold that stakeholder groups are also conceptual – stakeholder groups do not exist as objective real-world entities any more than an SSM model reflects an objective reality. When constructing SSM models Customers (C), Actors (A) and Owners (O) are given explicit consideration. But these are not individual, real-world people – they are conceptual groupings. Thus, generating assumptions from SSM models and ascribing these assumptions to stakeholders on the basis of C, A and O roles is essentially an exercise in conceptual modelling that might serve as a basis for debate about a real-world problem situation. Thus, depending on the perception of the problem situation, it might be as meaningful to categorize aerodynamicists into those who wear brown suits and those who don’t as to categorize them on the basis of geographical location. When we turn to the real world and requirements capture we find that an individual could be aligned with more than one conceptual stakeholder group, subscribe to those groups to a greater or to a lesser extent, and not necessarily behave in a manner consistent with the imputed values and beliefs of the group. For example, an individual aerodynamicist participating in a requirements workshop might also be a local resident and as a result possibly experience a conflict of interest. We therefore argue that systemic stakeholder analysis does maintain the distinction between problem situation and conceptual thinking about the problem situation. It is the requirements capture process that must address real-world representatives of stakeholder groups.

## Customers and customers

The WTD believed that the aerodynamicists were their customer and the action research did not result in this view being changed. In one clear sense the real-world customer of the WTD is indeed the aerodynamicist since it is difficult to imagine the existence of a WTD in the absence of an aerodynamic design activity. In conceptual terms the SSM Customer is valuable because it allows the WTD to debate its role in the organization and to identify beneficiaries and victims (such as local residents) who have a stake in WTD activity other than aerodynamicists.

## Requirements capture as relationship building

Requirements capture was found to be complementary to conceptual thinking about the problem situation, aiding the resolution of disputes concerning assumptions about stakeholders and providing a concrete input to the production of a requirements specification. Before the action research the WTD did not consult their customers in a formalized manner, the attitude being that they had been running wind tunnels for a long time and knew what their customers wanted. The requirements workshops were useful since they showed the importance of listening carefully to what customers are asking for, asking them why they are asking for it, and gauging how important it is to them. This has the double benefit of legitimating the activities of the WTD and the system developers (for example, a business demand for real-time data could be substantiated) and strengthens the relationship between the WTD and its customers. This last aspect is a double-edged sword insofar as it raised the expectations of the aerodynamicists that the WTD will be responsive to their needs.

## Learning about the IS requirements analysis framework

At the methodological level the balance of real-world requirements capture and conceptual thinking juxtaposed with current situation and future situation provided a helpful way of moving around the requirements analysis space, with each element helping to refine and shape each of the others. However, in the interests of readability the fieldwork has been presented sequentially, starting with the current situation, going on to conceptual thinking, moving from conceptual thinking to real-world requirements, and finally ending up with visions of future situations. This presentation is useful in making sense of the methodology employed in the action research, but it obscures the framework of ideas. In the early stages of the IS requirements process ‘The trail is hot. Information is scarce, contradictory, asymmetrical, and difficult to interpret and use.

Uncertainty rules the day.' (Callon, 1991, p. 154). As yet there is no clear separation between conceptual world, real world, current situation and some desired future situation. As the network of human stakeholders and their non-human allies becomes aligned and interests converge then the purified forms emerge and a plausible story of the requirements process can be told. In Figure 7 the outcomes of requirements analysis are shown as separations that have been achieved through a process of mediation; real-world and conceptual world are now synchronized and an unblocked route can be seen from the current situation to a desirable future situation.

Mediation cannot be explained away as a dialectical bobbing back and forth in which conceptual world and real world, current situation and future situation are imagined to be interconnected by circular movements of single-headed arrows. Iterations of SSM (conceptual world) and requirements capture (real world) do not explain the requirements analysis process. The SSM models we had developed affected the questions we asked of attendees at the requirements workshops; our conceptualizations affected the way in which we made sense of the answers and spurred us on to ask yet more clarifying questions. As we found out more about the real world new ideas for conceptualizing and re-visioning were generated. The real world and the conceptual world were inseparable, but we needed to make a separation to make sense of the situation, regardless of how temporary or artificial that separation might be. Once we had achieved a separation then the requirements specification could be seen to have an internal consistency; whether it would provide a sound basis for the implementation of an information system depended on the story told being sufficiently plausible to appeal to the interests of the human stakeholders and their non-human allies.

Figure 7. Mediation and separation in the IS requirements framework.

However, if we accept IS requirements analysis as mediation, then what are the implications for the requirements analysis methodology? From a theoretical viewpoint it can be argued that the methodology brings together paradigms that are incommensurable; Burrell and Morgan (1979) have argued that ‘Contrary to the widely held belief that synthesis and mediation between paradigms is what is required, we argue that the real need is for paradigmatic closure’ (p. 397). On the basis of the argument that the IS requirements analysis process is neither subjective nor objective one might conclude that Burrell & Morgan are wrong and that what is needed are new methods that will indeed support and promote mediation and paradigmatic synthesis. However, this author does not follow this line of argument and proposes that, from a methodological viewpoint, paradigmatic closure is indeed still desirable since the aim of requirements analysis, the outcome, is separation. We should strive to apply purely conceptual ideas to construct a re-visioned social reality and at the same time record faithfully the real world, objectively given verbatim requirements of IS stakeholders. In doing so we continue to do what we have always done (Latour, 1993), acting as if we can separate the subjective/organizational and objective/technological, while recognizing at the same time that they are inextricably interwoven and that any talk of one without the other is telling but half the story.

## SUMMARY AND FUTURE DIRECTION

The framework for investigating information system requirements presented in Figure 1 has been used to inform an action research project. Systemic stakeholder analysis, being a combination of SSM and SAST, has been used to conceptualize the problem situation on the grounds that it provides an approach for addressing pluralism and complexity. It has also been argued that systems-based approaches, such as SSM and SAST, do not provide adequate guidance for addressing the role of technology and the elicitation and specification of requirements that would inform the design and implementation of a computer-based artefact. A central theme of this paper has been requirements analysis as mediation rather than separation, which has been posited as an outcome of a successful IS requirements analysis activity. The mediations that have been used to guide the analysis are current situation and future situation and real-world requirements and conceptual thinking about the real world.

Future work involves placing the IS requirements framework in the context of the wider IS development process. The central idea of mediation will be applied to information modelling (e.g. object-oriented analysis), sociotechnical analysis (impact on organizational work) and the design of computer-based technical artefacts (Avison et al., 1996). The IS framework is being tested and refined through application in further action research projects that will consider issues, such as culture and ethics (Wood-Harper et al., 1996) and the identification/analysis of stakeholders using different metaphors (Morgan, 1993) and multiple perspectives (Linstone, 1985, 1989).

## Biography

Richard Vidgen is a lecturer in Information Systems in the Department of Computation at UMIST (University of Manchester Institute of Science & Technology). He holds a first degree in Computer Science & Accounting and an MSc in Accounting, both of which were awarded by the University of Manchester. On leaving university he developed and supported financial applications software for MSA Inc. (now Dunn & Bradstreet Software). This was followed by a number of years working as a freelance consultant engaged in the design and implementation of information systems in the financial sector. In 1992 he was appointed to a lectureship at the University of Salford, where he completed a PhD in the area of Information System Quality. Current research interests include IS development methods, requirements analysis and process modelling, and group-working technologies.
