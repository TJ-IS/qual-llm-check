# Studying system development methodologies: an examination of research methods

Judy L. Wynekoop & Nancy L. Russo\*

Division of Accounting and Information Systems, University of Texas at San Antonio, San Antonio, TX 78249–0632, USA, email: jwynekoo@lonestar.jpl.utsa.edu, and \*Department of Operations Management and Information Systems, Northern Illinois University, DeKalb, IL 60115, USA, email: nrusso@niu.edu

Abstract. Changes in organizational structures, competitive environments, technologies and economies are altering the types and complexity of computer-based information systems. For methodologies, tools and techniques to evolve to meet these changing needs, research to assess current system development practices would be expected. However, there is little evidence that the evaluation of methodologies and improvement of the systems development process are keeping pace with technological and organizational changes. This paper analyses existing research on system development methodologies and discusses the research needed to provide the information scholars and practitioners will need for the evaluation, selection and development of methodologies in a changing environment.

Keywords: Information systems, methods, methodologies, system development

## INTRODUCTION

Systems are being developed in an increasingly complex business and technological environment. Technological advances and trends, such as client–server architectures, graphical user interfaces and distributed systems, will accelerate as we approach the next millennium. Changes in the business environment to accommodate increasing domestic and global competition and changing economies will continue to pressure information systems (IS) staff to deliver systems ‘yesterday’ to exploit business opportunities. Technologies, such as fourth-generation languages, application generators and local area networks, have changed the way systems can be developed, facilitating rapid, non-linear and more user-centred development (Keyes, 1992; Heichler & Cafasso, 1995) These systems are less likely to require large-scale, long-term development projects, and more likely to be smaller, short-term, incremental projects (Baskerville et al., 1992).

Based on such changes in the development environment, one would expect to see fundamental changes taking place in system development methodologies (SDMs). One would expect such changes to be based on evaluations and assessments of how well current SDMs have worked and how systems are developed today. However, there is little evidence to indicate this has been occurring. Although many diverse SDMs exist, and more continue to be developed, there is no agreement that they are useful in today's environment, nor is there evidence that they have ever been useful (Lyytinen, 1987, 1989; Glass, 1995).

SDM theory and research lag behind practice. SDM research has emphasized the development of new SDMs and frameworks for the selection and comparison of SDMs, while addressing the use or evaluation of methodologies in practice in only a limited fashion. The paucity of research in organizational contexts involving the development of actual systems by real developers has been noted (e.g. Curtis, 1986; Westrup, 1993; Fitzgerald, 1994a). During this decade, researchers have begun to pay more attention to the study of SDMs in practice (e.g. Conrath, 1991); however, this progress has been slow.

The current SDM research paradigm, its weaknesses and devices that might speed its shift are the focus of this paper. Critical research areas that must be examined to provide information to guide the development of SDMs to meet the needs of today's systems and development environments are identified. A framework is used as a means of summarizing and evaluating existing SDM research. The paper concludes with suggestions for altering the SDM research paradigm to fill the gaps between research and practice identified in the analysis.

## METHODOLOGY RESEARCH TAXONOMY

A fundamental problem of methodology research has been the inconsistent definitions of ‘methodology’ and the failure to define what an author is actually studying. A methodology is a systematic approach to conducting at least one complete phase (e.g. requirements analysis, design) of system development, consisting of a set of guidelines, activities, techniques and tools, based on a particular philosophy of system development and the target system (see Wynekoop & Russo, 1995 for definitions of related terms). In the following discussion of SDM research, a study is included if its author indicates the object of study is methodologies, although discrepancies between the definition of SDMs above and that used in the research are noted. Studies of the system development process that do not explicitly address SDMs are not included (e.g. Boland, 1979; Curtis et al., 1988), although the importance of such work is acknowledged.

Although one could explicate numerous research issues as yet inadequately addressed by SDM research, the overriding question is ‘What do practitioners need in current and future SDMs?’. To answer that, the issues in Table 1 (derived from Basili et al., 1986; Westrup, 1993; Wynekoop & Russo, 1995) must be addressed. The answers to these questions are essential for understanding system development and the role of SDMs in the development process today. These three issues, discussed in the next section, provide one dimension of the framework used to categorize and evaluate existing SDM research.

## Research purpose

## SDM use

This category includes studies, which explicitly address a specific aspect of the application of an SDM in practice. Examples would be a field study examining the types of methodologies used, the frequency with which they are used and the context in which they are used (e.g. Fitzgerald, 1996), or a practitioner's description of one organization's experience using a methodology (e.g. Menard, 1980).

## SDM selection, development and adaptation

The research in this category is aimed at identifying the criteria used by organizations for selecting between the hundreds of commercially available SDMs; the process by which organizations develop or adapt SDMs; and the reasons for methodology adaptation (e.g. Saarinen, 1990; Sauer & Lau, 1994).

## Evaluation

Evaluation is the systematic study of an SDM to determine its usefulness, effect or impact. Evaluations might be restricted to a particular phase (e.g. Lubars et al., 1992) or specific techniques included in the methodology (e.g. Jones & Arnett, 1993). Conceptual evaluations done without actually applying the SDM are not included here, but are included in the next category, since no evaluation of the SDM in use is done.

A fourth category — understand/describe — has been added to the three critical research issues in the framework. This category includes writings that define or describe SDMs, such as a discussion of an SDM's features. Arguably, all SDM research strives to understand or describe an aspect of an SDM or its application. However, some studies explicitly address a specific aspect of the application of an SDM in practice, while others focus on the description or understanding of the SDM itself. Frameworks to categorize, compare or evaluate methodologies, and their conceptual evaluation, are in this latter category, as are conceptual discussions of issues relating to SDMs.

Classifying SDM research based on the purpose of the research is useful in clearly identifying the areas with much research activity and the areas that have been neglected. The second dimension of our SDM research framework is the methods that researchers have used to study SDMs. Research methods, discussed next, are important to our understanding of the past SDM research direction and a future course.

## Research methods

It has been noted that the use of multiple research methods is necessary to adequately understand an activity, process or object. When a subject area is not well understood, qualitative methods, such as case studies and ethnographic research, may be relied on to build theory and testable hypotheses. Theory may be tested using such methods as replicated case studies, field research and experiments (Jick, 1979; Lee, 1991). On the other hand, it has been argued that in-depth understanding of a phenomenon may only be gained through interpretive research, studying it in context from participants' perspectives (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1995). We do not enter the positivist vs. interpretive debate here, but assume that both approaches are useful.

In order to classify the research methods used in studying SDMs, a broad set of research methods was identified. Nine are summarized in Table 2 (adapted from Wynekoop & Conger, 1989; Galliers, 1991), including both positivist and interpretive approaches. (For a thorough discussion of research methods and philosophies, see Wynekoop & Conger, 1989; Galliers, 1991; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991.) The research methods included in the framework are: laboratory research, surveys, field inquiries, normative writings, case studies, interpretive research, descriptive research, action research and practice descriptions. Basic and applied research are omitted from the framework because the development of new methodologies is beyond the scope of this paper. In the following discussion, the term ‘field research’ is used to refer to all SDM research conducted in natural settings, from both positivist and interpretive traditions.

Based on these two dimensions of SDM research — research purpose and research method — a two-dimensional taxonomy for classifying SDM research has been developed. The research issues in Table 1 provide the three research purpose categories of use, selection and adaptation, and evaluation. These three categories are supplemented with an additional category entitled understand/describe.

The nine research methods from Table 2 make up the second dimension of the classification framework. Using these two dimensions — research method and purpose — areas of inquiry that have not been adequately explored and the types of research approaches that have been used in these areas can be identified to guide future research efforts. The classification of SDM research is discussed in the following section.

## CLASSIFICATION OF SDM RESEARCH

Over a hundred (123) research papers are classified in Table 3 by research method and purpose. This group represents prominent publications in the field but is not assumed to be an exhaustive sample of research in the area. The sample was arrived at by identifying papers addressing SDMs in Information Systems Research, Communications of the ACM, MIS Quarterly, Data Base and Management Science. These journals have been identified in the past as the key North American journals for IS publication (e.g. Culnan, 1986; Straub et al., 1994). Journals were systematically searched back to 1967 (or as far back as the journal existed), which corresponds to the early years of ‘software engineering’ that motivated the development of SDMs (Schach, 1996). Additionally, books, conference proceedings, trade publications, journals from outside North America and additional publications were searched to the extent possible with existing resources.

Most papers clearly fell into a single category. When a study primarily addressed one purpose or primarily used one research method, it was classified by the dominant one. However, research addressing multiple purposes or using multiple methods equally were included in all relevant categories. Furthermore, we assumed that the common denominator of all SDM research is to understand or describe SDMs. Therefore, if the research addressed any of the other three research purposes, it was not included in the ‘understand or describe’ category, but we stipulate that it would be there as well.

Normative research dominates, accounting for over half of the work that was classified. Most of the normative work is in the understand/describe category. Over a third of these are frameworks for the selection, comparison or evaluation of SDMs. These were not included under the select or evaluate purposes, since they do not address the questions of how well or when SDMs really work or how SDMs are selected, but rather are conceptual discussions of how SDMs should be selected or evaluations on paper of whether or not an SDM has features that the author identifies as important. Empirical evaluations of SDMs or field studies of the criteria for methodology selection are, however, classified in the evaluate and selection categories respectively.

Studies classified with a purpose of understand/describe or as normative or descriptive research are not discussed further since, although important in expanding our understanding of SDMs in general, they do not substantively address the critical research issues identified in Table 1. The following discussion focuses on the bolded portion of Table 3, which contains empirical studies of SDM selection and adaptation, evaluation and use.

## Empirical research

Almost half of the 58 studies classified in the highlighted section were undertaken to evaluate SDMs or parts of SDMs. Few studies were undertaken to identify how SDMs are selected or adapted or how they are used. There is little interpretive research and action research, and few practice descriptions or case studies. Most of the classified studies are positivist field research or surveys addressing the evaluation and use of SDMs.

## SDM selection, development and adaptation

Surveys have indicated that whether organizations develop their own methodology or buy a commercial one, SDMs are usually adapted (Jenkins et al., 1984; Hardy et al., 1995). However, little is known about the process by which these SDMs are selected, developed or adapted. Only nine of the studies reviewed addressed any of these issues.

There do not appear to be any universal reasons for selecting an SDM (Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996). The existing studies of SDM selection and adaptation reveal that some companies select and adapt SDMs in an ad hoc fashion, usually in response to SDM inadequacies or new tools, and in ways that as yet cannot be explained by existing frameworks or theory. Individuals acquire the skills necessary to adapt SDMs primarily from experience. In short, little is known about the motivations or processes for SDM selection, adaptation or development, or whether current practices are satisfactory.

SDM adaptation, selection and development is longitudinal and the reasons for, and methods of, selecting, developing and adapting methodologies are determined largely by context. This implies that field research is key to understanding this process. Practitioners' descriptions of the selection or adaptation processes in their organizations would offer some insight, particularly if a critical mass of such descriptions could be accumulated. Understanding SDM selection, development and adaptation is necessary to determine such issues as critical SDM characteristics and the effectiveness of adapted SDMs.

## SDM use

IS research generally assumes that SDMs are used. Yet there has been little research confirming this. From the research on SDM use, it appears that most organizations have an SDM, and it is more often custom than commercial (Hardy et al., 1995). However, there is evidence that almost half of all projects do not use an SDM (Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996). Although most studies indicate that respondents believe SDM use is valuable at times, there is little or no consensus on when an SDM should be used. SDMs are sometimes viewed as valuable and sometimes as a hindrance. In short, little is known about the use of SDMs.

Almost half of the studies classified in the SDM use category are surveys. Surveys provide a snapshot of SDM use at one point in time, and their value depends on the validity of the instrument itself and knowledge of the respondent. Most surveys report type or amount of SDM usage obtained via a checklist. Some compare SDMs with techniques or process models, thus confusing the results (e.g. Necco et al., 1987). Most are single-respondent surveys, presenting the viewpoint of one person (often the senior IS executive) who may or may not be knowledgeable about SDM use (Page-Jones, 1991). For example, 25% of the 112 IS managers responding to a survey evaluating SDM impact on maintenance described their SDM as 'unknown' (Dekleva, 1992). Surveys mainly identify SDM distribution, not how SDMs are used.

Additionally, it cannot be assumed that methodologies are followed or used consistently across all projects (Pressman, 1992). It is not sufficient to ask if a firm has a methodology, as most surveys do, but rather, one must address the extent and nature of usage on individual projects (Wynekoop et al., 1995). Clearly, the lack of field research in this area precludes knowledge of how SDMs are used. For instance, it is not known why, although most organizations have an SDM, only half of all projects use one. It is not known when an SDM is viewed as a help and when as a hindrance. Insight into these areas could be offered by practitioners describing SDM use in their organization. Without knowing how SDMs are used, necessary improvements or changes cannot be identified to keep pace with changing needs.

To accomplish this, there is a need not only for better surveys (e.g. those using multiple respondents and identifying the extent and nature of use), but also for field research examining how specific SDMs are used in practice.

## SDM evaluation

As noted above, we would expect existing SDMs to be evaluated, then possibly re-engineered to correct shortcomings. Although over half of the studies classified purport to evaluate methodologies, not all actually do. Six (20%) of these studies either evaluated prototyping and the SDLC or compared the SDLC to methodologies, CASE tools or techniques (Alavi, 1984; Boehm et al., 1984; Mahmood, 1987; Palvia & Nosek, 1990; Dekleva, 1992; Kiebeck et al., 1992). It is difficult to determine exactly which SDMs, techniques or tools are employed within a particular process model, such as the SDLC. Therefore, the results of these studies cannot be used to evaluate specific SDMs. Of the remaining studies, three (10%) evaluated methodologies used by students or the author (Capper, 1984; Floyd, 1986; Sharple & Cohen, 1993) and one was the methodology developers' evaluation of their methodology on a narrative case (Aksit & Bergmans, 1992). The remaining 17 studies evaluated SDMs or parts of SDMs (e.g. techniques) based on survey, field or experimental data.

Thus, two-thirds of the studies classified as evaluating SDMs actually evaluated identifiable SDMs or techniques in practice or in laboratory settings. The generalizability of the other 10 studies must be questioned because of the subjects and contexts or the failure to identify a specific SDM or technique being evaluated.

It should be noted that most studies are not complete evaluations of an entire methodology. For instance, although Vessey & Conger (1994) evaluated structured analysis, Jackson System Design (JSD) and Booch object-oriented design, they examined only how novice software developers use those methodologies. SADT, JSD and others were evaluated using a survey (Beck & Perkins, 1983), but context was not considered and the authors noted confusion among the respondents. Thus, certain features or applications of some SDMs have been evaluated — but rarely have complete SDMs been assessed. The two most complete evaluations have been done on SSADM and Multiview, which are not widely used outside the UK.

Longitudinal field research evaluating SDM usage in actual contexts is necessary. Without a database of evaluations of SDMs, there is no basis on which to improve the development process or adapt it to changing conditions.

## What is known

It is revealing to consider which SDMs were included in the studies in the bolded area of Table 3. Papers are coded in the References section according to the SDM(s) addressed, and summarized graphically in Fig. 1. The ‘other’ category includes SDMs addressed in fewer than five papers, including custom-developed SDMs and amethodological development. The ‘unspecified’ category includes papers that failed to identify the SDM under study. ‘SDLC’ and ‘prototyping’ are included since the authors of these studies claim methodology research. However, the unspecified link between these approaches and specific SDMs is problematic, a concern since these areas are among the most studied SDMs.

Studies are classified as addressing an SDM if that SDM is included in any way, e.g. if a survey includes a list of SDMs in which respondents check off those used, the study is considered to address that SDM. Most research addresses SDMs developed in house or an SDM not studied by many others (the ‘other’ category). Techniques, or parts of SDMs were also frequently addressed. However, with the exception of structured analysis and design, specific SDMs were not addressed by more than 10 studies.

In order to determine how complete the understanding of a specific SDM is, we examined research on a new and rapidly evolving SDM class, object-oriented (OO) methods. One would expect to find current research identifying how or why OO methods are selected, how they are used and how well they work in order to provide a basis for the improvement of OO methods.

Of the 58 studies addressing the research questions in Table 1, only seven address OO methods. One is an evaluation of an OO methodology by its creators (Aksit & Bergmans, 1992) and three are surveys that asked organizations to check SDMs used, with OO selected by some (Hardy et al., 1995; Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996; Eva & Guilford, 1996). The surveys indicated that OO has low market penetration and that the use of OO does not increase the amount of information collected during requirements gathering, but increases confidence in the information obtained. A field study found that OO specifications are considered hard for clients to understand and are often adapted to make them clearer (Lubars et al., 1992). Novices have more trouble learning Booch OO methods than process- or data-oriented ones (Vessey & Conger, 1994). Training in OO for all, including clients, is important. There are serious problems in object specification in the Shlaer–Mellor method (Fayad et al., 1993).

## DISCUSSION

The preceding analysis of existing SDM research demonstrated a reliance on normative research, largely focusing on the publication of conceptual papers and a paucity of empirical research addressing the use or efficacy of SDMs in practice. Most of the empirical research takes a positivist approach. There is little evidence that existing SDMs are thoroughly evaluated before they are modified or new ones developed. In this section, we consider why this situation exists and how it might be changed.

It is easier to say that the use and effectiveness of SDMs should be thoroughly researched than it is to do it. But when is an SDM considered ‘effective’? How is success measured? (Is it user satisfaction with the resulting product? Developer satisfaction with the development process? Design complexity or system maintainability? Improved system quality or developer productivity? All of these or something else?)

Furthermore, the use and effect of an SDM cannot be separated from the context in which it is applied. For instance, individual differences account for most of the differences in the development process and resulting product. This goes beyond training and experience — some developers are ‘exceptional’ (e.g. Brooks, 1987; Curtis et al., 1988; Glass, 1995). The same SDM may be brilliant when used by an exceptional designer but make no difference (or cause problems) when used by others. An outstanding developer might adapt, select or use an SDM differently than would others. Can we assume when evaluating an SDM in an organization that individual differences will ‘randomize out’ or should we test for them?

Project management, development and target hardware, and resources (e.g. staff, time, tools) would also impact on the performance of an SDM and the way it is selected, used or adapted. It has also been noted that an SDM is beneficial when used in an organization with a pre-existing ability to produce high-quality software (Humphrey, 1989; Loy, 1993).

Unfortunately, studying the use, adaptation and evaluation of every SDM in every possible context would be a Herculean undertaking — most likely impossible. Thus, an exhaustive, systematic, scientific study of SDMs is not feasible.

Few would disagree that the issues in Table 1 must be addressed — it is dangerous to use an unevaluated SDM to build systems or as the basis for new SDMs (Lyytinen, 1987). However, perhaps we must acknowledge that the nature of the beast prohibits examination according to traditional research paradigms. Clearly, the new research paradigm must break out of the positivist box. Owing to the interaction of context and SDM use and success, interpretive methods are desirable. However, the examination of every SDM in every context is no more possible using interpretive approaches than positivist ones.

In the remainder of this section, we propose one method to begin addressing this problem. We do not claim that it is the best solution, but present it to provoke a dialogue among scholars and practitioners about how to study SDMs. We start from the premise that perhaps satisficing is necessary. Rather than giving up and declaring the study of SDMs in use as too difficult to attempt in a rigorous, scientific fashion, we can broaden our perspective of what constitutes valid information in an applied field of study.

Ideally, systematic, empirical research (whether positivist or interpretive) should be conducted in as many contexts as possible on the most used SDMs. However, because of the combinatorial explosion of contexts and SDMs, we need not only to increase the pool of those conducting research, but also to redefine acceptable research. The IS research paradigm has shifted to recognize the legitimacy of the interpretive tradition (Walsham, 1995). We propose another shift to include practice descriptions as an accepted supplement to rigorous research, included in IS journals and conferences. In many areas of systems development, practice has led theory (Glass, 1995). Does it not then make sense to learn not only by studying practice, but from practitioners themselves? That is, to accept practice descriptions as legitimate sources of information on SDM use and evaluation. Although not vigorous or systematic, they provide insight into the use of an SDM in one context, albeit from a biased perspective. Although not individually generalizable, a large enough database of practice descriptions would be revealing. We would expect that analysis of such a database could identify emerging patterns of behaviour and outcomes, which would provide both research and practitioners with a deeper understanding of SDM use.

How can we encourage practitioners to become involved? Obviously, they must see a direct benefit from their participation. By working closely with organizations when conducting research in the field, researchers may be able to use the results of their research to have a positive impact on the organizations studied, rather than using the information only for scholarly publication (Hitchman, 1996). It may be that a database of practitioner experiences and research results could function as a clearing house for information on SDMs — descriptions, evaluations and ‘tales from the trenches’. Acquiring a database of practice descriptions and making them available to researchers and practitioners would provide a large data set to use for analysis.

Such an approach is of course not without its problems. One concern with any participant report (such as practice descriptions) would be the accuracy of the information. This would be difficult, if not impossible, to monitor. However, would the accuracy be any more suspect than that obtained in surveys (Hufnagel & Conca, 1994)?

How could practice descriptions be disseminated? One potential outlet is the World Wide Web. Currently, such descriptions appear primarily in trade publications and ‘cross-over’ journals combining practitioner and academic papers (e.g. Information Systems Management). Few are found in IS scholarly journals because practice descriptions rarely make it through the refereeing process — both for lack of a priori intention to do research and for the bias towards discussing only successful practices (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1991). Perhaps we must learn to live with the former problem. However, the latter is important. As much, if not more, can be learned from failed practices as from successes. However, practitioners seem unwilling to share failures. Indeed, the practice descriptions we found were all positive, with only one even discussing major problems (Menard, 1980; Mendes, 1980; Fayad et al., 1993). Furthermore, owing to competitive pressures, many practitioners may be unwilling to share information at all.

Returning to the introduction of this paper — the systems environment is becoming increasingly complex. Access to and sharing this type of information may become more critical as organizations realize that methods that used to work, do not work today; and that what works today will not work tomorrow. The lack of effective client–server SDMs is an example (Heichler & Cafasso, 1995).

Within the past few years, researchers have increasingly studied SDMs in real contexts. However, this research paradigm shift is occurring all too slowly. The emphasis remains on conceptual papers. For example, the British Computer Society's Specialist Group on Information Systems Methodologies has encouraged research in real contexts. However, in its most recent conference, Lessons Learned from the Use of Methodologies (Jayartna & Fitzgerald, 1996), half of the papers are still normative. One practice description was included (Tollow, 1996). A greater effort should be made to include practitioners in such conferences.

In order to determine the nature of SDMs needed now and in the future, the questions in Table 1 must be answered, using a variety of research methods. Although it would be impossible to evaluate every existing SDM empirically, some attempt must be made to identify how systems are currently being developed and whether or not existing methodologies work. Understanding the current development environment is necessary to guide the development and application of methodologies in the future.

Judy L. Wynekoop is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at the University of Texas at San Antonio. She received her PhD in Information Systems from Georgia State University in 1991. Her research and publications focus on the system development process, creativity in software development and the use of information technology in medicine.

Nancy L. Russo is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at Northern Illinois University. She received her PhD in Management Information Systems from Georgia State University in 1993. In addition to on-going studies of the use and effectiveness of system development methods, her research has addressed IT innovation, productivity and quality metrics and IS education issues.
