# The use of RepGrids to gather interview data about information systems analysts

M. Gordon Hunter

St. Francis Xavier University, Antigonish, Nova Scotia, B2G 2W5, Canada, ghunter@stfx.ca

Abstract. In view of suggestions for the application of more qualitative and interpretive approaches to information systems research, this article describes the use of RepGrids to gather interview data about information systems analysts. In the light of the objectives of a specific research project, various techniques are considered. Analysis of the techniques in relation to specific research project objectives leads to the adoption of a form of Kelly's RepGrid technique for the specific project described here. A detailed description is included about how RepGrids were applied in this instance. It is shown that RepGrids are appropriate for gathering qualitative interview data. The technique provides structure to a 'one-to-one' interview. But, at the same time, RepGrids allow sufficient flexibility for the research participants to be able to express their own interpretation about a particular topic. Further, because the content of the data is determined by the research participant researcher bias is reduced. The article concludes by suggesting that the RepGrid technique supports the generation of rich in-depth data and is a reliable research technique for gathering qualitative data about information systems analysts.

Keywords: Qualitative research, RepGrid interview technique, systems analysts

## INTRODUCTION

Currently, there exists a growing interest among the academic community of information systems (IS) researchers in qualitative research techniques. In 1991, the annual International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) initiated a series of panel discussions regarding various aspects of qualitative research. The discussion topics have included analytic induction and hermeneutics (Orlikowski, 1991), action research and ethnography (Lee, 1992) and interviewing and evaluation (Davies, 1993). These workshops represent an expanding forum for IS researchers who are considering or actively applying qualitative research techniques when conducting investigations. While qualitative research provides some interesting methodological approaches, it is important to ensure that the specific technique adopted is one which reflects a consideration for the specific objectives of the project. Galliers and Land (1987) have proposed that IS research ‘... methods must take account of the nature of the subject and the complexity of the real world.’ (Galliers & Land, 1987:901). Their taxonomy, included here in Table 1, is divided into ‘traditional’ and ‘newer’ approaches to IS research. Galliers and Land (1987) recommend that IS researchers should not use the methods of traditional research blindly and suggest that the choice of research approach should relate to the research objectives. It is interesting to note that Galliers and Land (1987) associate ‘observations’ with traditional approaches. This association seems to provide a description of the traditional uninvolved researcher in a research–subject relationship. On the other hand, ‘interpretations’ are associated with the newer approaches and seem to reflect the current emphasis of qualitative IS researchers interacting with research participants. For instance, rather than sampling many sources and drawing statistical inferences, newer approaches tend to investigate fewer sources in more depth. This association with newer approaches suggests the type of research originally described by new paradigm research which proposed ‘... an approach to inquiry which is a systematic, rigorous search for truth, but which does not kill off all that it touches: we are looking for a way of inquiry which can be loosely called “objectively subjective” ... a synthesis of naive inquiry and orthodox research ...’ (Reason & Rowan, 1981, p. xiii).

About a decade ago, Fitzgerald et al. (1985) presented opinions about IS research. Among a list of concerns was the following comment ‘The data collection and analysis techniques of the scientific method are not adequate on their own in areas involving human activity.’ (Fitzgerald et al., 1985, p. 5). The authors suggested ‘... we currently should be generating ideas, theories, and hypotheses, rather than simply testing them, and that anything which restricts or constrains this process is inappropriate.’ (Fitzgerald et al., 1985, p. 6). Further, Klein and Lyytinen (1985) have suggested that IS as an academic discipline ‘... will remain a doubtful science as long as it continues to strive to develop its stock of knowledge primarily through the practice of the so-called scientific method.’ (Klein & Lyytinen, 1985, p. 133).

More recently, Hirschheim (1992) has contended ‘... that information systems epistemology draws heavily from the social sciences because information systems are, fundamentally, social rather than technical systems.’ (Hirschheim, 1992, p. 28). Further, Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) and Lending and Wetherbe (1992) investigated the type of research published by MIS journals. The results of both projects, presented briefly in the following paragraphs, suggest that the majority of published research continues to be that which adopts a more traditional approach.

Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) examined 155 IS research articles published from 1983 to 1988 in three IS journals (Communications of ACM, Management Science and MIS Quarterly), as well as the Proceedings of the International Conference on Information Systems. The research findings suggest that published articles over the investigation period reflected a continued reliance on the more traditional research approaches. Fully 92.3% of the articles, when mapped onto the Galliers and Land (1987) taxonomy, relate to the more traditional modes of empirical approaches to research.

Lending and Wetherbe (1992) also investigated the type of research published by MIS journals. Their research represents an update of a similar investigation conducted by Vogel and Wetherbe (1984). When compared with the Galliers and Land (1987) taxonomy, the 1984 data indicates a 85:15 split between traditional and newer approaches, whereas the 1992 data shows a 72:28 split. Thus, a higher proportion of published research relates to the proposals by Galliers and Land (1987) regarding qualitative research techniques and further supports the idea that more IS researchers are becoming interested in and are applying more qualitative techniques.

This article proposes the use of the RepGrid technique from personal construct theory as a response to adopting newer qualitative approaches to IS research. While the technique has existed for some time, its major application has been in the areas of problem construction, market research and knowledge acquisition. To begin with, various alternative research techniques are reviewed in relation to the objectives of a specific research project. Then the adopted technique, RepGrids, is introduced and described relative to its use in the research project. Finally, conclusions are presented, which discuss the advantages and disadvantages of the use of RepGrids in general and their proposed use in future IS research.

## ALTERNATIVE RESEARCH TECHNIQUES

A recent research project (Hunter, 1992, 1993, 1994) attempted to determine an understanding of how various research participants (commonly known as stakeholders) perceived what constitutes the qualities of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst. Also, the project was replicated (Hunter & Beck, 1996) in Singapore which provided a cross-cultural comparison with the original work conducted by Hunter in Canada. In relation to this overall research goal, there were a number of further considerations, which would have to be addressed in the selection of a specific research technique. The first consideration was to allow the definition of ‘excellent’ to emerge from the data gathered from the research participants. Second, the research participants should base their comments on their own interpretations of their experiences, because it was considered important to gather interpretations about what is done, or has been in the past. Third, the adopted technique should support a structured yet flexible research approach. While structure supports subsequent data analysis, the technique should not inhibit the exploration of ideas, which emerge during the data-gathering process. Fourth, the adopted technique should influence research participants as little as possible. The questions should allow for a response based upon the participant’s views of the topic. Also, while completely eliminating researcher bias is not really possible, every attempt should be made to reduce it. Thus, a general qualitative research approach was adopted. The concepts of such approaches as the development of grounded theory according to Glaser and Strauss (1967) and the role of Reason and Rowan’s (1981) new paradigm research served to guide the further considerations of which research technique to adopt.

## Expert panel

This method requires gathering data from a group of ‘experts’. One of the first decisions required here is who are the ‘experts’? With regard to the current research, it was decided that rather than obtaining input from ‘experts’, there were various groups of stakeholders whose input was considered important. In the original research project, these stakeholder groups were referred to as audiences and consisted of three types of functional users and two types of information systems professionals. Audience members, then, become the ‘experts’. This approach is similar to ‘focus groups’ used in market research. That is, researchers determine who may provide a valuable and relevant interpretation on a particular subject and then pursue the gathering of these interpretations in some manner.

## Critical incidents

This method requires the participant to describe a specific incident relating to the research topic. A concern exists in relation to the initial reference point of the participant. That is, using this method to gather data, the participant will initially focus on the incident. While participants may be able to identify certain incidents, they may not be able to analyse and respond with a description of the skill components involved in the specific incident with a sufficient level of detail to provide data that can be used to address the research objectives. Further, the incident may not be related to personnel and may be based on hardware or software. The critical incident technique tends to give the researchers perhaps only one quality, whereas the participant may have much to say about a number of different qualities. Thus, critical incidents may lead to the provision of only one quality, while other techniques could lead to the generation of a number of qualities.

This elimination process left one technique, the RepGrid, which is explained in more detail in the following section.

## THE ADOPTED RESEARCH TECHNIQUE

The theory of personal constructs was first proposed by Kelly (1955, 1963), a psychologist, based on his work in attempting to help individuals analyse their own interpersonal relationships. He regarded individuals as ‘scientists’ and believed that in order for them to gain control of their environment, it was necessary to be able to interpret and predict (i.e. construe). Thus, Kelly (1955, 1963) proposed, individuals, based upon their past experience, would devise a system of personal constructs that would assist them in dealing with current or anticipated situations. He referred to this system as a personal construct system. A technique developed by Kelly (1955, 1963) to study personal construct systems is the role construct repertory test (RepGrid). While the RepGrid technique was originally devised to assist in counselling his clients, Kelly’s technique has subsequently been applied in a number of other areas. Some articles discuss the use of the RepGrid technique in general or for specific problem construction and market research (e.g. Bannister & Mair, 1968; Eden & Wheaton, 1980; Shaw, 1980; Fransella, 1981; Corsini & Marsella, 1983; Eden & Jones, 1984). RepGrids have also been used for various aspects of knowledge acquisition in relation to the development of expert systems (Botten et al., 1989; Latta & Swigger, 1992; Phythian & King, 1992). In the research reported in this article, RepGrids have been used specifically to identify the qualities of what constitutes an interpretation of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst. Among a number of different ways of using RepGrids, this article describes one approach that is considered appropriate for the specific research objectives.

Stewart and Stewart (1981) suggest ‘At its simplest, Grids provide a way of doing research into problems — almost any problems — in a more precise, less biased way than any other research method.’ (Stewart & Stewart, 1981, p. vii). These authors also suggest that the RepGrid technique ‘... enables one to interview someone in detail, extracting a good deal of information ... and to do this in such a way that the input from the observer is reduced to zero.’ (Stewart & Stewart, 1981, p. 5). Further, Pervin (1989) states ‘There have been studies of the reliability of the Rep Test and the evidence to date suggests that the responses of individuals to the role title list and constructs used are reasonably stable over time.’ (Pervin, 1989, p. 271). Thus, RepGrids, as used in this research project, have been proved to be a reliable technique for gathering qualitative data.

The specific project in which RepGrids were applied consisted of interviewing a total of 53 research participants at two field work locations. One-to-one interviews were conducted, which maintained confidentiality and allowed the researcher to pursue research participant comments in a fair degree of detail. It should be noted that this type of interview process tends to lead to a relatively small sample size, because it is a time-consuming technique. However, it was anticipated that the sample size would be sufficient within the context of the adopted research approach and in consideration of the availability of research participants.

The second step in the interview process consisted of asking each research participant to identify up to six systems analysts with whom he/she had interacted in the current or a previous organization. This number (6) would provide sufficient variability in the subsequent step, triadic elicitation of constructs. It was suggested that identified systems analysts should be individuals about whom the research participants was able to form an opinion regarding the use of skills in a working environment. To this list of identified systems analysts were added ‘ideal’ and ‘incompetent’. The research participant was encouraged to use these titles as representative of his/her concept of an ideal or incompetent individual who was currently working as a systems analyst. These titles would be used during the interview as comparison ‘anchors’ in relation to the six actual systems analysts. As Stewart and Stewart (1981) suggest regarding choosing elements, if the researcher wants to know what the research participant’s views are about effective subjects, ‘… obviously you will ask him to construe elements that include effective [subjects], but to know how he differentiates between effective and less effective ones you must include some less effective ones in your element list.’ (Stewart & Stewart, 1981, p. 30). The names of the actual systems analysts plus the two added elements were transferred to individual recipe cards.

The third interview step relates to the triadic elicitation of constructs. With one name or title per card, the research participant was presented with three of the eight cards. The three cards are generally known as a 'triad' in the literature. So that each of the eight cards has an equal chance of being included in a triad, the three cards to be included are chosen randomly. For each triad, the research participant was asked to identify, with regard to the skills of an 'excellent' systems analyst, how two elements of the triad were alike, yet different from the third. The research participant was encouraged to separate the two cards physically from the third and to verbalize the decision process. These descriptive comments were noted regarding the differentiation within each triad. Examples of elicited constructs are shown in Table 3 under the 'Constructs' column. This method of 'triading' promotes a discussion of similarity and contrast, which is how Kelly (1955, 1963) viewed all constructs. Kelly (1955, 1963) believed that understanding the nature of a construct requires knowledge of both the similarity and the contrast regarding a triad of elements. The word or phrase used to describe both the similarity and the contrast is determined solely by the research participant. Taken together, the similarity and contrast represent a bipolar description ('dichotomous construct') relating to one component of the investigation.

So, at this stage of the interview, the research participant would have three cards in front of him/her. The other five cards have been set aside for the moment. Two of the cards will be beside each other, representing the research participant's interpretation of an important skill that is shared by the two systems analysts' names on the cards. The third card is physically separate, representing a level of difference, as interpreted by the research participant, for the skill. For instance, the third construct, as indicated in Table 3 is good user rapport — no user rapport. Thus, the research participant would have associated two cards with good user rapport and the third card with no user rapport. Note that one card could have represented good user rapport and two cards could have represented no user rapport. It is important that the research participant identifies the skill and decides on its name, as well as the similar and different elements.

In order to understand what a research participant means by a particular construct, it is necessary to attempt to understand the content of the construct. Thus, the more data one gathers about a specific construct, the better the chance of understanding what is meant by the research participant. The concept of ‘laddering’ (Stewart & Stewart, 1981) supports the generation of the research participant’s comments regarding the elicited construct. A series of ‘how’ questions prompts the research participant to provide more detail and thus more clearly define what he/she means by the use of the more general construct. Detailed interview notes (step 3a) were taken in support of the RepGrid construct. Figure 2 represents the notes that support one of the seven constructs from Table 3.

The notes in Figure 2 represent the comments relative to the research participant's further elaboration of the meaning that he/she has attached to the construct. For instance, the research participant has said that good user rapport is established when the systems analyst and research participant have ‘... good relationship on all subjects...’ and the systems analyst ensures the ‘... user feels more comfortable...’. The further interview notes explain the research participant’s interpretation of how this was accomplished by the systems analyst. The lower part of Figure 2 presents further comments from the research participant representing an interpretation of what constitutes no user rapport and is based upon the research participant’s experience with the contrast side of the elicited construct.

Continuing with the interview (step 3b), the research participant was then asked to rate each systems analyst and the two added elements in relation to the elicited construct. The research participant was encouraged to place all eight cards beside each other physically representing relative variation of the elements with regard to the specific construct. Stewart and Stewart (1981) suggest that this technique helps research participants to decide which elements are similar and which are different relative to a specific construct. The research participant was then asked to assign a number to each card based upon a nine-point rating scale, where 1 represented the positive end of the construct and 9 represented the negative end.

Stewart and Stewart (1981) suggest acceptable scales range from two points to nine points. A nine-point scale was chosen for this research project for the following reasons. First, the scale is composed of an odd number, thus allowing a midpoint, which provides research participants with a reference point when rating systems analysts. Second, a nine-point scale provides sufficient flexibility for rating the eight elements associated with this research project. That is, with a nine-point scale the research participant could, if desired, rate each systems analyst separately, while with any smaller scale the research participant would be forced to give equal ratings to some systems analysts. These numbers were entered into the RepGrid as representative of the participant's differentiation of the systems analysts. The results of this rating process are shown in Table 3 as the numbers in the cells of the RepGrid.

The interview process (step 3), which includes construct elicitation, generating detailed interview notes (via laddering) and rating each element against the elicited construct, was repeated until it became obvious that the research participant was not able to offer further input on the subject. An average interview lasted approximately 90 minutes.

At the end of each interview (step 4), when the RepGrid was completed, a preliminary confirmation was obtained regarding which actual systems analysts were considered, by the research participant to be most like the research participant's interpretation of 'ideal' and 'incompetent'. This process entailed the visual comparison of columns in the complete RepGrid. This technique is similar to what Stewart and Stewart (1981) refer to as 'visual focusing', which attempts to identify those elements or constructs that are the most similar or the most different. In all interviews, the researcher's preliminary RepGrid interpretation and the research participant's interpretation were the same. This comparison seemed to be an appropriate method to confirm, at the end of an interview, that what had been recorded fairly represented what the research participant had stated during the interview. The interview was brought to a close by addressing any further comments offered by the research participant and responding to any questions about the overall process.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

An example of a completed RepGrid is shown in Table 3 and represents the data from one interview with a research participant from the systems analyst audience. Seven constructs were elicited during the interview. Recall that elements 1–6 represent specific systems analysts with whom the research participant has worked; and elements 7 and 8 represent the anchors of 'ideal' and 'incompetent' respectively. The numbers in the cells of the RepGrid represent the research participant's rating of each systems analyst with each construct on a nine-point scale.

The detailed interview notes supporting one RepGrid construct are shown in Figure 2. These interview notes were taken in support of the ‘good user rapport — no user rapport’ construct shown in Table 3. The detailed statements represent the research participant’s comments about what is meant by this particular construct, or in response to further questioning about the construct by the researcher. Note that the data in Figure 2 represents supporting comments for only one of the seven constructs. Thus, for this interview, there exists seven sets of detailed interview notes in support of the seven elicited constructs.

Extensive interview notes were taken, which provided descriptive support for each construct in the RepGrid. A total of 2727 individual constructs, representing all interview notes, were documented for 53 participants from the two fieldwork locations. This vast amount of rich qualitative data was subsequently analysed with the assistance of COPE (Cognitive Policy Evaluation) passive content analysis software (see Eden & Ackermann, 1989). Various themes and subthemes were identified as emerging from the data and were applied via a decision support system shell to hiring and training decisions by the two locations involved in the original fieldwork (Hunter, 1992, 1993, 1994).

What seems to be happening with this data may be explained as follows. First, certain ‘universals’ are emerging from the data. Common themes have been identified, which relate to how research participants interpret which skills and personal characteristics contribute to an interpretation of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst. Second, ‘locals’ have emerged from the data reflecting the value of the universal themes. In general, user-oriented stakeholders interpreted content-related aspects as relatively more important, while systems analysts and their supervisors interpreted process aspects as relatively more important. Based upon these research results, it should be possible to provide feedback to stakeholders about how they perceive ‘excellent’ systems analysts. These comments may provide a better understanding of what constitutes an ‘excellent’ systems analyst and, through subsequent action research, may be applied in areas, such as systems analyst recruitment and selection, assignment, training and project team risk assessment.

The RepGrid technique may be used in many different ways. It is accepted that each application of a RepGrid requires both the documentation of relevant elements and the elicitation of appropriate constructs, in relation to the domain of discourse (subject of investigation). However, beyond this there exists a vast array of RepGrid application methods. For instance, each RepGrid may be unique. That is, each RepGrid application could develop unique elements and constructs. This method was used in this current research project. On the other hand, common RepGrids may be used. That is, a number of research participants could be asked to complete a RepGrid containing the same elements and/or constructs. This commonality supports subsequent analysis of the data using such techniques as principal component analysis (PCA), which determines measure of relative similarity of the elements or constructs. PCA may also be used to document the hierarchy of the elicited construct systems by determining the series of superordinate constructs.

The above comments describe the use of RepGrids at the construct and element level. However, RepGrids may also be used to generate a large amount of rich, in-depth, qualitative and narrative data relating to a research participant's explanation of an elicited construct. The documentation of the research participant's explanations as interview notes forms the basis of the research data for this project. Detailed comments were recorded for each pole of the elicited construct. A system of hierarchies was determined by the researcher for each construct, which depicted the relationships within the interview notes between en elicited construct at the RepGrid level and a detailed action statement at the interview note level. The interview notes were obtained via the technique of 'laddering', whereby the researcher probes further regarding the research participant's detailed interpretations of a general comment.

The research participants determined their own list of elements and were able to decide upon their own constructs, as well as indicate their interpretation of the elicited constructs. Common RepGrids were not used because this would have forced the research participants to respond to previously identified (and therefore potentially biased) elements and constructs. While each RepGrid is therefore unique, common themes were identified via the subsequent analysis of the detailed interview notes. Thus, it is possible, using the technique described above, to be able to offer the research participant the fullest amount of freedom to comment upon a subject, yet still maintain a structured method to the data-gathering process.

Further, comments received from the research participants also support the contention of reliable results. First, the research participants were asked, at the end of each interview, to confirm a visual interpretation of the RepGrid by the researcher. In all interviews, the research participants concurred with the researcher's interpretation. Second, it was agreed by the company contacts from both fieldwork locations that the results could be applied in a beneficial way to each organization. Galliers and Land (1987, p. 901) ‘... the measure of the success of research in an applied topic such as information systems is whether our knowledge has been improved to the extent that this improved knowledge can be applied in practice.’ One organization used the results in conjunction with a decision support system to review a selection decision. The other organization used the results in conjunction with their version of the decision support system to develop training plans. Further, each organization plans to continue to use the results along with their own versions of the decision support systems.

## CONCLUSIONS

As indicated at the beginning of this article, there has been a call for the adoption of more qualitative research in IS, in order to understand this complex area better. While there has been some response, a review of published articles suggests that there still remains a lack of use of the newer approaches to conducting research. This article has described the application of a technique that supports one of the new approaches and addresses the call for more qualitative IS research.

The approach adopted for the research described in this article is considered significant regarding the general method of conducting an interview to gather research data. The data gathered, described as interview notes above, represent the relatively unbiased comments of the research participants. It is possible then to identify emerging themes and, in turn, to use the data also to define these same emerging themes. This method is supported by the underlying concepts expounded by grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

## Job description

Job descriptions for systems analyst positions might be restructured to reflect more appropriately the relationship between the skills of available systems analysts and the skills necessary to perform currently required tasks.

## Recruiting and selection

When choosing a specific systems analyst to hire, knowledge of important inherent personal characteristics of ‘excellent’ systems analysts would be helpful. Furthermore it would also be beneficial to be able to identify individuals who could develop trainable skills.

## Work allocation

An improved method of manpower resource allocation may be achieved by more closely matching the skills and personal characteristics of systems analysts with the task requirements to which they are to be assigned.

## Project team selection

For team-based projects, the combined profile of the systems analysts may creative a relatively highly rated composite ‘excellent’ systems analyst.

## Identifying training needs

It is possible to identify trainable skills that an individual systems analyst does not possess or, more likely, possesses at a level regarded as lower than necessary for the satisfactory performance of assigned duties.

## Career path

In order to provide the systems analyst with experience that would improve long-term performance, a specific career path could be planned. This experience may be gained through exposure to a combination of users, information systems or project teams.

## Project team risk assessment

Each member of an existing project team could be evaluated in a similar manner to provide information about aspects on which the project manager should concentrate.

## Performance appraisal

The results of this project may be used to relate more directly the review of performance to those activities performed by systems analysts. The themes identified may be used to describe behaviourly anchored rating scales and subsequently to provide the dimensions for specific feedback to systems analysts regarding their performance.

In conclusion, RepGrids generate a vast amount of rich qualitative data in a relatively unbiased manner. This technique can be used to investigate new ground or theory generation, as suggested by Glaser and Strauss (1967). RepGrids were used during the interview process because they bring structure to the interview, while allowing flexibility and reducing researcher bias. It was considered important in this project to determine the interpretations of the research participants. Thus, it was necessary to adopt a tool that emphasized gathering data, while allowing the research participant to determine the subject matter and content of the data. This aspect is one of the advantages of the RepGrid technique. According to Pervin (1985, p. 246)

'One of the remarkable features of the Rep test is its tremendous flexibility. By varying the role titles or instructions, one can determine a whole range of constructs and meanings.' Furthermore, because a number of research participants were interviewed, some amount of structure during the interview process would eventually assist the subsequent data analysis. Again, the RepGrid technique adds structure to an interview. In addition, this technique may be extended into other research areas where a researcher's objective is to gather unbiased qualitative data about a relatively unexplored topic. The next stage for this specific research is to migrate towards a more quantitative approach in order to address various research questions generated from the qualitative approach reported in this document.
