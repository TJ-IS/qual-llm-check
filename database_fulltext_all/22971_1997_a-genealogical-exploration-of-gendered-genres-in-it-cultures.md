# A genealogical exploration of gendered genres in IT cultures

Lynda Harvey

School of Computing Sciences, University of Technology, Sydney (UTS), P.O. Box 123, Broadway, Sydney, NSW 2007, Australia

Abstract. Information technology (IT) is socially constructed, occurring within what can be called an IT culture. In this paper, the argument is presented that IT culture is a masculine culture and that this denies the feminine voice. The paper looks at four formative cultures that have influenced the IT culture, these being the military, academia, engineering and industry. In looking at these, a genealogy is developed, using the voice of a post-modern ethnography. Five ethnographic field studies are revisited by the author to deconstruct the voice of IT culture, showing its masculine genre and the dominance that this has on a feminine voice. The implications for IT as knowledge in practice are discussed.

Keywords: Information technology, masculine, feminine, post-modern ethnography, genealogy

'None of us can choose to stay outside the computer culture. (Some of us will be forced outside and that is a very different matter.)' (Spender, 1995, p. 16)

## INTRODUCTION

Information technology is recognized as a socially constructed object of meaning (Zuboff, 1988; Orlikowski, 1991) from its creation (Floyd et al., 1991: Hughes et al., 1992) through to its adoption (Kling & Sacchi, 1982; Orlikowski, 1992) and subsequent management (Davies & Nielsen, 1992; Green & Guinery, 1994). Wacjman (1991, 1994) argues that technology's social construction is predominantly framed in a masculine culture. She makes reference to the ethnographic work of Kidder (1982), who tells of a story told to her of the development of a new computer that is predominantly cast in ‘masculine’ terms of warring and battling. Wacjman (1994. p.11) describes how Kidder found sexual metaphors in constant use when the development process was described to her:

'Sexual metaphors abound such that the excitement of working on the latest computer was likened to “somebody told those guys they would have 72 hours with the girl of their dreams.” It is surely no coincidence that the protagonists of the story are almost exclusively male.'

The gendered context of the world of computing can alienate women from entering the computing workforce. Turkle (1984) argues that the alienation arises from the values expressed by men in computing that revere ‘the preoccupation with winning and with subjecting oneself to increasingly violent tests (such as working non-stop for days on end)’ (Turkle, 1984, p.210). Wacjman also argues that ‘being in a relationship with the computer is also a substitute for, and a refuge from, the much more uncertain and complex relationships that characterize social life’ (Wacjman, 1994, p.12). Wacjman then concludes that the ‘workplace cultures of computing constitute a world from which women are profoundly alienated and from which they are anyway excluded’ (Wacjman, 1994, p.12).

The degree of the exclusion claimed by Wacjman is, of course, very much a question of stance, value and perception. Or, at least, that is what an interpretation of a value-centred argument may lead us to believe. The problem is that the workplace for information technology does show apparent gender separations that favour the male gender (Truman & Baroudi, 1994). The question that is explored in this paper is concerned with the extent to which that masculine culture can arguably be found in the formative contexts of information technology professionals.

In this paper, I explore the notion of a ‘masculine culture’ in relation to four formative contexts of information technology knowledge in practice. These are the military, academia, engineering and industry. These contexts are explored from a genealogical approach as presented by Foucault (1972, 1977a, 1979). Foucault’s approach looks back from the present, reconstructing history to explore possible alternative interpretations of political structures that disempower certain societal groups. In adopting this approach, I am accepting the knowledge values of a post-modern era (Lyotard, 1984), which view value-centred reconstructions as the most plausible approach to challenging the relationships that form society.

I adopt a post-modern ethnography stance (Tyler, 1986), which involves my re-reading events in my life and reflecting upon the possible contexts that could link to that reading. My intention is not to uncover truth but, rather, to create images from which the readers can judge my contribution by judging the plausibility of their evoked images. My approach is more akin to an artist creating a story than to a scientist finding a fact (Clifford & Marcus, 1986). However, the creation of my story has to be recognized for its grounding in my portrayed experiences and my interpretations of others' research. It does not stand alone in the world of fantasy but, rather, webs itself through the fantasy of reconstruction by the reinterpretation of other tales.

In presenting this research, in almost literally re-searching a topic, I have to reconstruct and present to you, the reader, for consideration, my interpretive stance. In order to do this, I will explore my own self-image, as far as I assume it is relevant. I will then explore what I see as a problem for me and for those around me; that problem is the central theme of this paper — the masculine culture of information technology. Next, I will explore what the problem means by critiquing the formative contexts of IT. I then conclude, in so far as a conclusion can be arbitrarily drawn, in a post-modern tale.

My stance in this paper is to critique a history of IT knowledge in practice that is concerned with the excremental nature of its culture (Kroker & Cook, 1988). This is in contrast to the functionalist view of history, which portrays the acceptable story of influences on change. The notion of excremental in looking at formative contexts stands in direct contrast to an incremental story of positive, progressive change. The excremental story is post-modern and the incremental is a modernist tale.

A critique of the excremental deals with that which is rendered invisible in modernist accounts, as it is excluded through being labelled as irrelevant or non-functional. An example is in the treatment of graffiti as an eyesore rather than as an indicator of the relationship between expression, art and culture. In critiquing the excremental, I am presenting a view of IT knowledge formation that explores the ‘unclean’ and the ‘unacceptable’. It is often viewed as irrelevant because it is not viewed as contributory or functional. My stance in this paper is to critique the excremental in pursuit of evocative images that reconstruct IT history from a political perspective. That perspective is overtly concerned with the acts of dominance portrayed through gender relations. Here, this is neutralized under the term of ‘masculine culture’. Other language portrayed in this paper has not been neutralized and may be offensive to some readers. I believe that I am portraying experiences and not simply sensationalizing events. The language is necessary for that portrayal.

I accept that the experiences I portray may be indicative of ‘atypical’ cultures. They may, for example, be peculiarly late twentieth-century Australian. I cannot know the relevance of this and so urge researchers in other nations and situations to conduct similar fieldwork and critiques to explore that possibility.

## MY RESEARCH APPROACH

The approach I have adopted to develop the story told in this paper is, of necessity, highly subjective. By this I do not mean simply that it is my interpretation and I shall present it in the voice of the first person. Rather, I place myself in this story. The genealogy of knowledge that I seek to present includes my personal genealogy in relation to the interpretation. This approach allows me to use experience as an acceptable mode of interrogation when questioning the relationship between masculine culture and information technology.

In taking a genealogical approach to the research presented here, I am retracing a history as a story of events, examined through revisiting experiences from field studies. My history becomes a product of the present as the material from the field studies is subjected to new critiques. The central challenge I am placing on my reconstruction is to see whether the issue that I assume can be evoked and augmented; that issue is the interpretation that a masculine culture, in the form of a distinctly masculine voice, has textualized the formative contexts of information technology as a body of knowledge. This includes the notion of practice as knowledge as I turn to experiences in practical events more than to research writings to create my genealogy. The genealogical approach has been assumed from my readings of the work of Foucault (in particular Foucault, 1977a, 1979). My work on this paper has also been informed by one particularly relevant discussion of the use of both ethnography and genealogy in information systems research (Preston, 1991).

The history I am approaching is both my personal history and a selected account of the history of information technology knowledge in practice. The areas I am visiting are structured into four, simply to enhance communication rather than in making a claim for their separation. The four areas are chosen for their general acceptance as areas of influence in IT knowledge in practice. My revisiting of personal experiences in field studies is then selectively structured to examine these four areas. My aim is to re-examine my assumptions about the concept of masculine cultures in relation to IT through the re-examination of the texts of my field studies.

My re-examination covers an 11-year personal history, with one anecdote from 21 years ago that was chosen because of its particular impact on my reflections. By placing myself so firmly in the picture, I am strengthening the view that cultures are not merely observed, recorded and analysed (Boland & O'Leary (1991); they are experienced, reconstructed, told and re-reconstructed through reading. The relationship between the author, the reader(s) and the text(s) is continuously changing (Geertz, 1988).

## THE FIELD STUDIES

The field studies revisited in this paper cover a time span of 11 years. They were all conducted by myself as the sole ethnographer. In each of them I had a specific role as an employee of the organization or, as in the case of the British Army, as an employee of a closely related organization, i.e. a military college. The original investigations were not all to do with gender issues. They were, however, all conducted with IT professionals. When the original investigations were not concerned with gender, it was during the revisiting of the field study texts generated (transcripts of interviews and diaries containing field notes) that the discourses relating to gender emerged. With the gender-specific investigations, the revisiting of those texts also led to further relevant discourses emerging. The field studies are as follows:

1985–87 Military The British Army, UK 12 months in duration (1985–86) plus revisit interviews over a 2-month period in 1987. The study was specifically looking at problems with an Army-wide IT strategy and resulted in the finding of significant human resource management issues that were based in their social construction of different models of the ‘family’ and their images of Army wives.

1993–94 Academic A computing science department in an Australian university, 8 months in duration. The study was to investigate computing labs as a genderized learning context.

1995 Industry — first study A business process reengineering (BPR) event in an Australian insurance firm, under the leadership and ownership of the Director of Information Technology and the General Manager, Technology, 5 months in duration. The study was to investigate the organizational issues with the design and implementation of the proposed BPR.

1995–96 Industry — second study An IT consultancy firm, Australia, 4 months in duration. The study was to investigate female consultants' gender issues.

1996 Engineering A railway engineering firm, Australia, 2 months in duration. I was employed as an IT and information management specialist, working under the direction of the Information Steering Committee. The study was to investigate how documents were managed as information commodities.

## MY VOICE

It is important that the voice of this text is critically constructed (Atkinson, 1990). In order to attempt this, first I need to explore the self image that I am recreating in the process of writing this paper (Linstead, 1993). I take ownership of a female self-image and in constructing ‘self’, I am necessarily constructing an ‘other’ (Cooper, 1983). In the case of gendered separation, the construction of a female implies that the ‘other’ constructed is male. There have been debates that have problematized the notion of the male (e.g. Hearn, 1993, 1994; Buchbinder, 1994; Horrocks, 1994; Connell, 1995) and have questioned the construction of the female so predominant in the feminist literature (Butler, 1990), showing that the acceptance of the sexual metaphors of masculine versus feminine is being challenged (Haste, 1993). Yet the social construction of the expectations of being female are still prevalent in work contexts (Linstead, 1995), and images of the female as victim of pornography still prevail (Collinson, 1988). Even though the notion of femininity versus masculinity can be deconstructed and debated, the linguistic reality of the separation is apparent and prevalent. In presenting this paper, I am referring to that linguistic construction of reality and am constructing a world in which there is a difference between being a female and being a male and acting accordingly.

## 'MASCULINE' CULTURE

At the start of this research, the whole concept of a masculine culture seemed alien to me. I continued to challenge what I could mean by such a term. Eventually, the texts from my field studies started to provide an answer. The insightful work of Spender (1980) has provided me with initial considerations, and Hacker (1990) has focused those on technology.

The revisiting of the field texts has led me to a number of assumptions. My first assumption is that a masculine culture is in some way different from a feminine culture. (In using the term ‘feminine’, I am using it as a contrast to the term ‘masculine’. I accept that the term feminine is rejected by many feminist writers but find that I cannot construct another term which portrays the contrast equally as well and communicates to a field of study which is, in general, not well versed in the arguments in the feminist literature. I hope that the use of the term, as with the term ‘masculine’ is not offensive.) My next assumption is that a masculine culture has a segregating influence on the female gender. The nature of that segregation has been my focus.

I am taking a hermeneutic view of culture (Boland, 1985; Boland & O'Leary, 1991; Harvey & Myers, 1995). This view treats culture as texts that can be represented through a shared voice, or genre. In critiquing the notion of a masculine culture, I am looking for a masculine genre or voice; this is often difficult to interpret. I shall lay down the basis by which I interpret masculinity in genre using the post-modern concept that a strong genre should be evocative, creating images for the reader that can be empathized with, so creating shared interpretations (Tyler, 1986).

A ‘masculine’ voice should evoke the image of an archetypal masculine speaker or writer. This is likely to be archetypal in that it is an extreme, e.g. macho, concerned with overpowering the weak and with controlling others and situations. The deliverer of the voice can appear as either male or female, even though it seems to have the characteristics of being masculine in that it is macho. The writer character is essentially dominant, controlling through being condescending, using derisory tones. The language in the example does not necessarily speak to me of ‘male’ but of ‘macho’. It is archetypically ‘masculine’ in that it excludes a ‘feminine’ voice.

In revisiting my field texts, I have sought less ambiguous examples of a masculine voice. From the field texts, it seems that there are a number of ways that I can interpret men adopting masculine voices when interacting with women. These seem to be used, inadvertently or intentionally, to deny the female voice by excluding or disassociating the female from the interaction. They act as a controlling device, disempowering a feminine voice.

My interpretations of my texts have led me to a view that the masculine voice excludes through degrees of impact, with the worst of those impacts leading to dominance that is often sexual in nature. The degrees of impact range from polite colloquialisms ('little lady') through to references to limited models of the female ('mother figure') to more explicit exclusions, such as reference to genitalia and sexual acts ('fuck', 'wank', 'nuts', 'balls'), including covert references through distortions of sexual terms ('fuckwit', 'dickhead', 'dickfor (brains)'), onto the even more explicit references to sexual acts (flirting, porn, sexual advances, oral sex). My field study texts find expressions of each of these in many contexts that can be described as formative contexts for IT knowledge in practice. The dominance is also found in sociopolitical structures that define the expected behaviour characteristics of roles in certain male–female relationships, e.g. wives and female consultants. My field notes provide the material for discussing these relationships when critiquing the four formative contexts of IT.

The masculine–feminine political structures are not static. History is a mode that explores societal changes. One effect of change is backlash. In my texts, I find that the genitalia-speak has shown some interesting backlash effects. The use of explicit sexual terms, such as ‘fuck’, have been considered in IT cultures to be inappropriate for female use, or even for use in the presence of women. In one event presented from my military field study, this was particularly noticeable. It is also overtly used to punish a woman in one industry field study. Both of these are discussed later.

In that same industry study, I found backlash (Faludi, 1991) and the counter-use of genitalia-speak to include as well as to exclude genders from/in interactions. In this study, the female consultants in an IT consulting firm would regularly meet for coffee, purposefully excluding male colleagues:

'No don't ask him. We don't want men there. It's just a girls get together.' (taken from my field notes during an industry study of an IT consulting firm, Australia, September, 1995)

During these coffee breaks, they would use genitalia-speak frequently in conversation e.g. ‘prick’, ‘dickhead’, ‘wanker’, ‘fuckwit’ and ‘fuck/fucking’. It seems that the women were ‘playing’ with this exclusory term in the purposefully exclusive and safe context of an all-female professional group. This play allows the women to reclaim territory by opting into the masculine voice, as opposed to being victims of its use as a text that is exclusory for women.

These terms were not used when male colleagues inadvertently joined the women. The coffee breaks were generally more formal and shorter on those occasions. There was one interesting exception to this. One male colleague had to spend a great deal of time at the consultancy office as he was developing and implementing a quality system. This consultant always interacted in a very friendly manner with the female consultants, but seemingly never by flirting. Later in the study, when he had been working at the office continuously for over 3 months, he listened in on a conversation about hair cuts and hair colourings and started to ask questions. Inquisitively, he declared that he was ‘fascinated as it is not something I normally have anything to do with. My wife comes home with her hair done and I say “that’s nice” and that’s all I really know about it’. On that same occasion, the female colleagues present started to talk about waxing, including tales of full-body waxing, which they had heard male models had had done to their bodies. This one male colleague was visibly shocked but continued to be curious as the women disclosed more information on beauty treatments, many with gruesome detail. When the women next went to their coffee break, they invited him along. They started to use the genitalia-speak in front of him and he just joined in. He did not seem to be using it to exclude the female voice but to include himself in their conversations. The masculine voice of genitalia-speak was being counter-used by the women to control entry into their discourse by a man. At another of these later coffee breaks, one woman started to discuss the negative aspects of men. Another woman commented that the male colleague was present so maybe these things should not be said. The response was ‘Oh, Brian’s all right, he’s one of us, he’s an honorary woman now.’ (Throughout the paper, all personal names have been changed.) The conversations continued with the exploration of negative aspects of men and the continual use of genitalia-speak.

It is possible to interpret this backlash event as the women adopting a masculine culture of machoism, exploring and expressing this as genitalia-speak and then playing the masculine culture games of dominance on the male colleague. This may have reconstructed him as a victim who then, in order to remain in that discourse, had to adopt the masculine discourse of genitalia-speak. Paradoxically, he was then accepted as a woman, but one with a masculine voice, thus denying him a possible feminine voice. If this interpretation has any worth, it demonstrates how destructive a masculine culture may be. It can deny the feminine in either the male or the female genders.

To summarize this section, I have put forward an argument that there is a recognizable archetype of a masculine voice. It is macho, dominating and controlling, often using sexual references and genitalia-speak. I have argued that there is a span of gender control, as used in language, that acts as a progressive exclusion of the woman as a freely operating individual. That span ranges from the use of patronizing terms to overtly using sexual references to dominate. The language and the sociopolitical male–female role relationships are what I take as being signifiers of a progressively masculine culture. I now present my discourse on the revisiting of my field texts to explore how the formative contexts of IT professionals have reflected the ingraining of this masculine culture.

## GENEALOGY OF THE FORMATIVE CONTEXTS OF IT

'Make no mistake about it, the Internet is male territory. Considering its roots are sunk deep in academia and the military–industrial complex, that's hardly surprising'. (Wylie 1995, p.3)

In this section, I present the four formative contexts by making reference to the relevant field studies. Selected texts from each of these field studies are presented, discussed and supported by further discussions from theoretical writings.

## The Military

Communicating with machines is a predominant aspect of everyday life. The predominance of technology inculcates into societal activities, leading to technological power relations affecting many aspects of everyday life. It is arguable that those who dominate technology can dominate reality.

Technology is substantiated through societal forms of knowledge control. Technologists become such professionals through differing forms of apprenticeships, earning titles and awards through ritualized training and initiation procedures. Each of these apprenticeships has everyday activities that allow for a disciplinary gaze to be used as a form of control (Foucault, 1977b). The disciplinary gaze regimentalizes the activities, allowing for the regimes of truth associated with technologized realities to be sustained; the regiments of truth protectors can fulfil their apprenticeship requirements as they are regimentalized into educational and professional environments, extending their professional regimentalizing into the play worlds of other technology users through various social group activities, with many of these using the medium of technology in their communication enactment. Those who sustain the regimentalizing of technologized dominance can sustain their regime of truth.

The argument of this paper is that the regiments of technologists are predominantly controlled by masculine ideals. It follows that the voices of the regiments are predominantly masculine. Women are, yet again, finding themselves in positions of disempowerment by not being able to join the regiments while using their feminine voices. The construction of women's silence (Spender, 1980) recurs in the institutionalized regimentation of technology knowledge and runs the risk of following through into the everyday worlds of women and men.

The notions of regimes, regiments and regimentalizing are all reflective of a military perspective. They are particularly relevant to the formative contexts of IT as a result of the significant historical links between IT and the military. Military computing was one of the first areas in which research and development in computing occurred, e.g. the US Air Force SAGE system (Bloomfield, 1986). It is an area that continues to shape the IT industry being a major source of IT research and development funding and accounting for a significant amount of computing consumption (Monk & Bissell, 1987; Robins & Webster, 1987). The indications are that there will be a strong military influence on IT cultures. Understanding the gendered voices in military cultures helps in interpreting their influence as formative cultures in IT knowledge and practices.

My military ethnography provides a basis for investigating those voices. In revisiting my military field notes, I find that the masculine voice is significant in two modes. The first is in the sociopolitical structures of gender-based role relationships. These are the relationships between army personnel and their wives. My ethnography found wives to be significant in the army because of the different forms of family that influenced their everyday lives. These forms are the corps as collegial family, the parents as family, the spouses as family and the Army as a family. Conflicts between the needs of each family group were central to the Army's problems at that time.

In reinterpreting texts, it is the notion of spouses as family that I have adopted as being relevant to my argument in this paper. The following extracts illustrate some of the perspectives on wives that constrain the feminine voice within the Army. My claims for the importance of this relationship to the Army is further supported by the ethnographic texts discussed in Jolly (1987).

Wives are really considered a burden to the Army. They are under a lot of stress because if they turn to drink, or break down, or can't cope, it's the husband's career that suffers. Any consideration of a promotion or posting will be spoken of as 'Oh no, we couldn't really give him a position of responsibility, because she might let him down and create a stir.' (Janie, Commanding Officer's wife, January, 1986)

'I still think you don't understand, Lynda. I love my wife, but if the Army told me that I had to take this gun and shoot her, I wouldn't ask questions. I would just do it. You have to obey, without challenge. Those who ask it may know far more than you and the safety of the nation may be at stake. If I got a phone call right know telling me to shoot you, I'd do it.' (Mark, Major in Bomb Disposal, posted to Northern Ireland March, 1986)

'The Army's no place for a woman. I'm getting out next month. I'm marrying my bloke. He's a squaddie too.' (Corporal Todd, driver, January, 1986)

Wives are central to the smooth running of the Army but are seen more as potential burdens and ultimately disposable. It is ironic that the female corporal felt she was leaving the Army by marrying her partner, who was also a corporal. The work of Jolly (1987) and my own ethnography indicate that she would simply be entering another female Army role, with many constraints.

A further way that the masculine voice is seen within my ethnography of the Army is in the use of genitalia-speak during social interactions. Paradoxically, it is the prevention of use that constrains the female presence, while abuse through sexual advances is acceptable. The following extract from my field notes shows this.

During a visit to an Army field camp, in the Mess bar, elderly Colonel: 'I'm not as old as I look. I'm only 53. I just look this way because I was in the Falklands War'. With another Colonel, there was a mimic fight and the other Colonel grabbed him by the collar and led him to the exit door. They were both laughing and he came back beside me at the bar. The only other woman there was the barmaid who said "I see you're wearing your Friday afternoon tie" (it was bright red). Apparently, he spends every Friday afternoon in the Mess bar, along with many other officers. Derek, the young Captain on the other side of me, told me that he was doing his PhD in organizational psychology, on a Defense scholarship. We seemed to have a lot in common and started chatting about the Army. The elderly Colonel butted in with “Most officers in Berlin have two wives you know, one you’re officially married to and one unofficially married to.” He laughed heartily when telling me this and said, “There’s still lots of life in the old dog, you know.” He was obviously flirting and I was rather embarrassed. At this point, Derek started to tell me a joke about a whorehouse in Berlin and an Irish visitor. When he was telling me, he used the words “fucking” and “fuck”. I didn’t mind as I assumed this was a normal Mess bar joke and normal language for the context. The elderly Colonel did not appreciate the joke. He got very angry, muttered something to another officer at his side and left the Mess bar, hurriedly and noisily. As it was only 1.45 p.m., I wondered why as this was his “Friday afternoon in the Mess bar” time. The bar went very quiet after that and I was escorted to another part of the field camp. I assume that it was unacceptable to tell such a tale, and use such language to a female visitor, even though it was a Mess bar.

The sociopolitical power games shown in the above text deny the woman the ability to hear vulgarity, even in a jocular frame, let alone use it. Yet dominance through sexual innuendo is allowable. The female is a sex object in that she can be confronted with sexual proposals but not part of the evocative sexual discourse of language games as seen in swearing and jokes.

The masculine culture of the military shows extremities in the reverence of the macho image. Individuals are trained to kill, control or die in mutilating or terrifying situations. The elderly Colonel was held in particularly high esteem at this Army field camp because he had actually been in the battlefield, a rarity at that time in the Army as the opportunities to go to war had been limited to the Falklands War (and possibly the terrorist ‘war’ of Northern Ireland). This appeared to make it acceptable for him to treat a female visitor as a potential sexual commodity.

The seeming ‘politeness’ of the military is sharply contrasted with the control exerted over wives and other female members or visitors. This is particularly ironic as the Army’s primary purpose was to defend the crown, i.e. the Queen of England.

The military exclusion of the feminine voice appears to be most powerful in discourse. As a formative context, it may well have a significant influence on the masculine culture of IT.

## Academia

'A university student who transmitted a computer message describing how he wanted to abduct, rape and torture a female classmate claims he was merely exercising his right to free speech.' (Atkinson, 1995), taken from Spender (1995), p.209.

Greenhill et al. (1996) discuss how masculine meanings ascribed to computing lead to women rejecting computing as a preferred path in university education. Spender (1995) discusses at length the educational issues with the lack of a voice for women in computing. Academia appears to be a problem area for the nurturing of a feminine voice. The masculine culture appears to dominate this formative context of IT.

My ethnography of the computing labs in an Australian university computing science department reveals many accounts of what can be interpreted as a dominating masculine culture. Parts of this ethnography are reported in Davies (1994) and are discussed by Spender (1995). The following extracts from my field notes illustrate the accounts of a masculine culture. The first is from notes taken late on a Saturday night, a time when young students usually prefer to socialize in human to human interactions, i.e. without the medium of the computer. The student discussed in this first extract seems to prefer the computer as discourse medium. The treatment of the assumed female partner in relation to sexual advances indicates how lacking in sexual constraint the student's behaviour is. This same student was painfully shy in face to face interactions, avoiding working with female students whenever possible.

On a split computer screen (showing writing from the sender and the receiver on the same screen), in a University computing department, in a student's computing lab, late on a Saturday night, a student's writing is expressing his desires for the object portrayed by the disembodied words on his screen. He is communicating with what he believes to be a female computer enthusiast in another city, in another state. On this late Saturday night they are exploring the daring topic of sexual pleasure as portrayed through oral sex. They are fondling each other's desires, making implicit promises, showing bravado of exploration, trying to convince each other of their extreme sexual capabilities. They have never physically met but through night after night of secret computer-based liaisons they have come to the point of deeply erotic interchanges. They have reached that point quickly, having little to say about each other as persons, linked through their intimate trust in the computer medium which they interact on. The male student is noted amongst his student body for being “a bit of a nerd”, someone who is more turned on by his machine than by interacting with people.

The educational contexts in which future computing scientists are nurtured show little respect for a feminine voice but an almost unfettered use of a masculine voice. The use of genitalia-speak is high and the resultant depersonalization of men is evident. The segregation of women from men in this context is seen in this depersonalization. As the following passage illustrates, male students use depersonalized names while female students use personal names. The male–female ratio on this course is 9:1 in favour of male students, which was typical of the course membership during my 5 years with the department. I am told informally that this is typical of most computing science courses in Australia. Around 45% of the students were present during the events described below.

'During the day, the computer labs are filled with students shouting across the rooms at each other, goading each other on with terms like “fuckwit”, “wanker”, “dickfor” being called out as terms of comradeship, seeming terms of endearment amongst the peer group. The student groups are almost exclusively male, with female students tending to be either quiet and contemplative or chatty and smiling. The female students do not use the same terms of comradeship for each other or for the male students. They tend to use their personal names. On the computer screens, some of the students are writing on newsgroups, communication channels for computer enthusiasts to talk to each other through electronic forms of writing. The writing tends to take the form of adoption of character identities or pseudonyms, as writers communicate to other similar groups around the world. Once in a while someone will shout out telling others to net into a group news item that is dealing with some form of discussion, often about continuing contributions to a joke category. The joke categories are most often explicitly sexual or about some form of killing or mutilation. War and sexuality are forms most frequently net-talked through the network newsgroups. The sexuality is almost always phallocentric, giving the image that the aggressive penis-centered activities are the key attributes of the hero figures. The age of the students is around that age group at which it is likely that many will still be virgins and others will be just starting to explore sexual relationships across the sexes. Sexual identities are emerging in these contexts.

Homosexuality is also discussed. It is only treated as a disparaging area of humour, with homosexuals considered objects worthy of being victimized, mutilated or destroyed. The image of females is very closely aligned to the image of homosexuals, both being objects of ridicule or potential abuse, waiting to be victims. The macho image of male dominates.'

The support staff on whom students rely share this tendency towards a strong masculine culture. This is disturbing considering that they exercise significant power with undergraduate privileges and female, as well as male, students often need to negotiate with them to gain those privileges.

'The technical staff who manage the computer labs often chastise the students for ‘playing’ on the machines yet also become involved in the same network interactions. They play their dominance out by using the machines for games that take up more computer power than those that students are allowed to play on. The hardware power of the computers becomes a key object of ownership to denote a place in the power hierarchies; the technical staff play this power through their rights of access. Of the nine technical staff serving this university section, only one is female, a part-time engineering student, and she is never expected to supervise the students labs. Female students have no choice but to conduct their work in these labs.'

The privileges, including access to play contexts, are expressed in terms of a masculine culture. One privilege is given as access to the multiuser dungeon (MUD) games. The play contexts of MUD games are renowned for machoism in role definitions of fantasy roles. The pseudomilitary nature of those play contexts is easily interpreted.

'The most common use of hardware access power used by the technical staff is the playing of multiuser dungeon games (MUDs). These games use a tremendous amount of processing power as they are connected to a world-wide network of players. A player has to develop his/her (usually his) own character with this taking considerable time. The character has certain strengths which have to be tested and developed. It is an imaginary world of imaginary personality development in which players can become champions in an alien and monster-inhabited world of magic and demons. Late on a Saturday night, the technical staff acting out their characters as champions of the universe by playing MUDs can be found at the other end of the computer lab from the student engaging in the explicit delights of oral sex with a disembodied “female” partner, by email interaction. Both of the computer users are male, both develop traditional macho self-images as they live out their fantasies by interacting with the screens.'

This is the regimental training ground for computer technologists. The scenarios mentioned may be found in many student computing labs around the world. If the international domain of cyberspace is any indication, they are recognizable as the norm rather than the oddity. This is the same training ground in which debates on pornography, child sexual abuse and sexual deviance of all forms are frequently conducted. Knowing these jokes (and accepting them) is a method of entry into the social world of the regiments of technologists. Trophies are taken in the form of ‘gifs’ (pictures) that are downloaded and portrayed at home on personal computers, usually within some file structure that makes it more difficult for parents to accidentally come across these ‘gifs’. The gifs are usually nude shots of females or of explicit sexual poses; they are the ‘playboy’ centrefold pictures of the technology regiments.

In this training ground, the dominance of the extreme of the violent, abusive male image prevails. Violence has been recognized in other work contexts as a metaphor associated with men in the workplace (Linstead, 1997). The question is ‘why?’ How has this regimental context come through into the academic environment of the technologist in training? It is not obvious that computer technologists are going to be gladiators needed to defend nations through brutal battles in which their lives are at stake, so why should that hero image be so prevalent and the aggressive, sexually dominant military image of the male be so dominating?

## Engineering

The work of Sally Hacker in looking at the professional development of engineers starts to give some clues (Hacker, posthumously edited by Smith & Turner, 1990). Computing is an area closely aligned with engineering and both are historically linked through their dependency on mathematics. Hacker discusses how the engineering profession is deeply influenced by its military origins and how much of the training ground for engineers is infused with genderized language games that are overtly verifying the dominance of the macho male image at the expense of the female voice. She describes how engineering was first conducted as part of military support, carefully making the distinction between chemical engineers, civil engineers and electrical engineers, with the latter being the most dominantly macho and also the most closely aligned with technology.

A great deal of the technology dominating the everyday reality of non-technologists is founded in electronic engineering, a form of electrical engineering more closely aligned to computing via the hardware aspects of the computer in the form of microchips. Many elements of the use of those chips are dependent upon software that is developed by computer technologists (who, incidentally, also become the developers of the related hardware).

My field notes from an ethnography in a railway engineering firm confirm the intensity of the masculine voice in the culture of engineering. Spender (1995) argues that any woman who displays an ability to defend herself verbally will be viewed as a bitch, or castrating. This was highly visual in one interaction that followed a very aggressive meeting in which genitalia-speak had been used regularly by the leader of that meeting.

'He attacked you because you're seen as a castrating female. The men here are real men, they're engineers and they are terrified of castrating females.' (A (male) client's 'apology' when a general manager had been abusive to me in a committee meeting in a railway engineering firm, September, 1996)

The individual who had been dominating in his attacks in that meeting had started the meeting by the use of the masculine voice in a joke. The informal discussions before the meeting formally began were around the problems of keeping leaves out of a swimming pool. The leader said:

'You need palm trees, but make sure that you get the ones without nuts, you know, the ones that aren't male.'

This same individual was renowned for using the term ‘fuck’ as almost every other word during conversations. My information-gathering interview with him was extremely difficult because of this. I was told that this was expected as he was ‘an engineer’ and this was common practice among engineers. Another colleague on the project was a principal officer and he used the same genitalia-speak intensity, as I note in my field diary:

‘Fuck and fucking is used as every other word by both Alan, a general manager, and Peter, a principal officer. I commented on this to my client. He just said that the reason is because they are both engineers and that this is very much a “boy’s world” culture. The organization is principally concerned with mechanical, electrical and structural engineering and does employ a very high percentage of qualified and skilled engineers. I was told by Peter that the project was being called “just a wank”. I was later told by my client that the CEO regularly says “fuck” and refers to any project which isn’t about trains or engines as “just a wank”.

My presence as a ‘powerful female’ created problems in this context, and this was used as the excuse for an abusive outburst in the final committee meeting. The outburst came from the General Manager (engineer). My client later informed me that he, the General Manager, apparently, did not find the situation disturbing, despite all other members of the committee making explicit moves to apologize to me for his behaviour. His comment was that I was a strong and powerful woman so he needed to get in the first attack. The use of the masculine voice to dominate is obvious. It was most disturbing to me that I felt I had not been particularly disturbed by the outburst. It seemed that I was inadvertently accepting the discourse of the masculine culture and even starting to adopt it for myself.

I found myself falling into the trap of adopting the masculine voice towards the end of the project:

‘In one of my final telephone conversations with my client we were discussing problems with the culture. He started saying “fucking” and “fuck” on a regular basis. He commented, “Look at me, I’m being taken into their culture”. He was trained in social work but had been in this essentially engineering firm for over 5 years. By the end of the project (only two months) I had to stop myself as I was saying “fucking” when privately describing them, without thinking.’

The dominance of the masculine culture in engineering is apparent. This is significant if the argument that engineering is a base discipline of IT is accepted. Hacker's arguments do support the view that the engineering discipline is the basis of all technology disciplines. This would make engineering a formative context for IT knowledge as it is a technology discipline (as well as an engineering discipline as seen in software engineering and business process reengineering). The links between engineering and the military (all forms of engineering are revered in military knowledge) and between engineering and academia (engineering as an academic discipline is a core discipline in universities and one which is significantly sustained through the input of top students and research funding). Industry also links closely to engineering, in particular, the IT industry. This formative context is now considered.

## Industry

'Of course I'll do better than you, you're a woman'. (Discussion with a colleague on career prospects, British Civil Service, 1975, not officially recorded but always vividly remembered by myself as a point in my life when my attitudes to sexism changed — I became less passively accepting of such constraints.)

Industry is an area in which sexual equality has been a relevant discourse for many decades. Equal employment opportunities (EEO) is a policy area that provides a legislative structure for managing interactions such as those mentioned in the passage above.

Despite this, the IT industry, at least in Australia, still seems to revere a masculine culture that protects gender inequities. My first study, of an IT area that was managing a major business process re-engineering (BPR) project in an insurance firm, showed covert masculinity. The making of derisory references to femininity as a disempowering strategy was used:

'I don't want to know if it's got problems. Just fix it. What do you think I'm paying you for, just to stand around and look pretty?'

This study was relatively mild in its demonstration of a masculine culture compared with a later study of an Australian IT consulting firm. In this later study, women were viewed directly as sexual objects, to be used to gain sales. One male senior consultant ‘comforted’ a junior female consultant who found a client’s behaviour repulsive in the following manner:

'Of course men will flirt with you. They pay for that privilege. Just keep smiling and keep the customer happy.'

The masculine culture was treating the woman as a form of economic commodity whose sexual attractiveness could be used to keep and gain business. Her treatment was akin to that seen in prostitution. The use of the female employee as an economic commodity can be compared with the role of wives in the Army study. This is seen in both the role restrictions, in which identity is found only through being a part of a husband (like only being part of a client's potential harem), and the disposability or sacrifice when a higher purpose is at stake.

The study with the IT consulting firm revealed many instances of overt and intense masculine culture. Only a few have been selected for brevity. Another female consultant, far more senior to the one mentioned above, spent some time with me telling confessional tales to illustrate the problems with the dominating masculine culture. One such tale was:

'I listened to the guy shouting “fucking” this and “fucking” that at me every day. In the end I just snapped and told him “why don’t you just fuck off”. The client firm (an IT development firm) requested that I was taken off the project as they considered that my language was inappropriate for a woman. I expected Jack (the most senior consultant in that office) to stick up for me. Instead, I nearly got fired.'

This situation was one in which the domination of the masculine voice seemed to deny any potential for the feminine voice. The earlier discussions of the backlash illustrate the intensity of this. Reclaiming territory by use of genitalia-speak is acceptable in the play context of a solely female peer group but completely unacceptable when conducted as a retaliation to abuse in a client's context. This consultant had obviously felt that her retaliation was justified, only to find that the firm viewed her as disposable if she did not simply play the victim and take the abusive behaviour of the client. The intensity of this constraint shows a powerful control mechanism. The masculine culture could dominate no matter what, even to the point of a form of linguistic rape. The genitalia-speak is not used as a sexual play but uses a sexual reference to dominate and render the female as victim.

In the wider context of the IT industry in that Australian city, other instances of the masculine culture in less intensive forms had previously been experienced by myself. They ranged from the use of female references as a form of a seemingly polite colloquialism:

‘Don’t worry, little lady, we’ll look after you.’ (Comment from the chair of a software quality committee, Australia, 1990)

This acted to exclude me from a masculine discourse and present me as a helpless female, diminutive and in need of attention. Without the use of a direct sexual reference, this ‘politeness’ limited my professional identity to that of female as defined by a masculine culture, diminutive, behaving in a lady like manner and in need of masculine care. The image of a frail and defenceless child holds in stark contrast with the image of a ‘castrating female’ used in the engineering study, but both show female stereotypes whose use serves to restrict my capacity for action as a woman.

Another incident minimized my presence in discourses by limiting me as an interactor to that of a model of a female archetype:

‘We need more women at the top. Men need a mother figure to help them cope.’ (Comment made to me by the chairman of a local professional IT interest group, Australia, 1995)

Here, the woman as leader is reduced to the maternal carer of men. The chances for equitable consideration when the interactions are limited to this form of relationship are lessened. Leader as person is replaced by the notion of leader as maternal being, there only to take care of men and not to act in the full capacity of a leader.

From the texts in my field notes, those in the IT industry show a strongly dominating masculine culture in practice. This seeks to constrain the feminine voice and protect gender differences at the expense of the female as a powerful entity in the area. This is consistent with the findings in the other contexts of the military, academia and engineering.

## DISCUSSING THE MESSAGES

As this paper has tried to demonstrate, the military, academia, engineering and industry are all arguably formative contexts for IT knowledge in practice. The consistency between them in relation to the expression of a dominant masculine culture has been shown through the revisiting of my field studies. The compounding effects of this consistency are particularly problematical. Breaking the dominance of the masculine culture is likely to be particularly difficult in IT because of the convergence of the messages. The messages are arriving from multiple directions making deconstruction and critique difficult. This difficulty is a significant problem for IT knowledge and practice. Deconstruction and critique provide the means for rendering as visible that which is denied or treated covertly. Visibility is needed to challenge the constraints and controls and seek to change the values of IT as a masculine culture.

In this paper, I have argued that the formative contexts of IT adopt a masculine culture. Using Foucault's notion of genealogy, arguments have been presented that support the notion of the military, academia, engineering and industry as formative contexts. Texts selected from the revisiting of my field studies illustrate the presence of a masculine culture and the dominance of that culture in denying the feminine voice.

My readings of the texts has illustrated for me that the notion of masculine culture is not necessarily an expression used solely by men. Genitalia-speak is used to dominate the feminine voice whether used by men or by women. It is used to either exclude or to allow for a rite of passage into the masculine culture. It is noticeable, however, that the rite of passage was allowed by a female group to a man, but when adopted by men was used to deny women the use of such speech. Unfortunately, language that is viewed as ‘ladylike’ still prevails and the ‘language of the boys room’ is still predominantly owned by men.

Looking across the four formative contexts, there were consistencies in the masculine culture's projected view of women. The woman as a victim is frequently both found and protected as a stereotype. This may well be one of the most dominant views in the masculine culture, as it is seen in other views, such as the view of the female as the sacrificial object, to be disposable when some form of higher order need is present. There is also a consistency seen in the way that the woman is only allowed an identity through her role interaction with a man; a woman as a wife, prostitute or mother/carer of men were all plausible interpretations from a reading of the texts.

My argument has not been that the use of genitalia-speak alone excludes a feminine voice. Instead, I have argued and demonstrated that the power to use genitalia-speak is predominantly allocated to men and denied women in IT cultures. It is this power of denial that renders the genre as authoritative and creates an exclusory context in which women's voices can be effectively silenced. That silence comes from the constructing of what women's voices are allowed to say and, alternatively, what they are excluded from expressing. This paper has demonstrated that the power of denial, exclusion and the creation of an ‘acceptable’ voice for women is not simply a creation of one context. It is developed from a number of disciplinary sources within IT work contexts. These sources are critical in the development of the IT work culture. Because the sources are so varied, the intensity of the effect on the culture is likely to be strong. This is why there cannot be a simple solution, such as for women to start using genitalia-speak freely. The contexts are unlikely to accept such acts from women who wish to be accepted in professional IT cultures. The fieldwork presented here supports this assumption. The solution has to be more subtle — one of constant exposure, challenge and critique, supported by evidence from the field; this is what this paper has tried to provide as a starting point to addressing the problem. Gendered language creates both genders, restricting the behaviour and creating expectations of behaviour for both men and women; this is the main reason why it needs to be deconstructed and reconstructed in more ethical and equitable genres, leading to equity of voice for both sexes in IT workplace cultures.

## Biography

Lynda Harvey is Professor of Information Systems at the University of Technology, Sydney (UTS), in Australia. She has previously published under the name of Lynda Davies. She holds a PhD in Systems and Information Management from The University of Lancaster in the UK. She is an active member of IFIP WG 8.2 and of ICIS. She teaches organizational aspects of information technology management to computing students and IT professionals. She recently returned to academia after 2 years in industry, working as a management consultant in Brisbane and Sydney, specializing in strategic IT management. She is an Associate Editor of Information Technology and People and on the Editorial Boards of five other scholarly journals. She has written numerous internationally recognized papers in the area of the management of information, systems and technology and is co-author of the book Information in Action. Her research interests are strategic information management, gender issues in the IT professions and the management of IT-based organizational change.
