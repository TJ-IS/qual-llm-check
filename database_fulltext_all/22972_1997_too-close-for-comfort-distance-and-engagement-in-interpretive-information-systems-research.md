# Too close for comfort? Distance and engagement in interpretive information systems research

Joe Nandhakumar & \*Matthew Jones

University of Southampton, Department of Accounting & Management Science, Highfield, Southampton SO17 1BJ, UK, email: joe@soton.ac.uk; and \*University of Cambridge, Management Studies Group, Department of Engineering, Trumpington Street, Cambridge CB2 1AG, UK, email: mrjl@eng.cam.ac.uk

Abstract. In this paper, we seek to explore the implications of the assumptions underlying interpretivism for the preferred relationship between the researcher and the research phenomenon. The growing interest in interpretive information systems research has drawn attention to the need to gain access to the interpretations of social actors. Various data-gathering methods are available to the researcher to achieve such access. These may be seen as located on a spectrum in terms of the degree of engagement between the researcher and the research subject. While engagement is not without its drawbacks from a research perspective, it may be argued that it enables good access to the sorts of data that interpretive researchers are seeking. Most of the research reported in the information systems (IS) field, however, has adopted relatively distant methods by which the researcher avoids intervention in the research context. Some of the characteristics of more engaged forms of research are illustrated through a discussion of a participant observation study of executive information systems development. This research has highlighted a number of issues that can be seen to be common to other data-gathering methods. Reasons for the neglect of engaged data-gathering methods are explored, and it is suggested that consideration of the issues it raises may contribute to more reflexive interpretive IS research practice.

Keywords: Research approaches, interpretivist research, data-gathering methods, participant observation

## INTRODUCTION

The growth of interest in interpretive research methods in the information systems (IS) field in recent years, and in particular the increasing use of in-depth case studies, has raised a number of issues relating to the way in which research is conducted, reported and used (Walsham,

1995). In this paper, we wish to focus on just one aspect of the research process — the relationship between the researcher and the phenomena he or she is studying, which, it may be argued, is central to the interpretive endeavour.

The paper is structured in five sections. In the first, we explore the underlying assumptions of interpretive methods regarding the nature of IS phenomena and how we may know them, particularly as these differ from the assumptions of the dominant ‘positivist’ research tradition (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). From this, a number of implications of these assumptions for the characteristics of suitable research methods are developed. In particular, it is suggested that interpretive methods require a close interaction between the researcher and the research phenomena. In the second section, we review the range of data-gathering methods available to IS researchers and consider how they differ in terms of the perceived relationship between the researcher and the research phenomena. (The term ‘data-gathering’ is used for convenience — some research traditions would suggest that data are created through the research process rather than existing independently of it; see, for example, Rock, 1979.) Evidence is presented that IS researchers have tended to make limited use of ‘engaged’ data-gathering methods through which researchers have extensive contact with the research phenomena. An illustration of the use of one such method, participant observation, in IS research is presented in the third section, while the fourth discusses some of the issues raised by such methods for research practice. The final section considers the implications of the analysis for interpretive IS research and, in particular, what it may mean for the preferred relationship between the researcher and the research phenomena.

## THE PHILOSOPHICAL ASSUMPTIONS OF INTERPRETIVE RESEARCH

Although identified by Orlikowski & Baroudi (1991) as comprising only 5% of IS research in the late 1980s, interest in and examples of interpretive research have subsequently grown significantly (Walsham, 1993; Lee, 1994; Walsham, 1995). Walsham (1995) sets out the philosophical assumptions of interpretivism. In terms of ontology, the nature of being, three broad positions may be identified (Archer, 1988). The first, ‘external realism’, proposes that reality exists independently of individuals, it is ‘objective’. This is the position adopted in positivist research. The second, ‘internal realism’, considers reality as an inter-subjective construction, shared between individuals, while the third, ‘subjective idealism’, sees reality as a personal construction of each individual. Interpretive researchers generally adopt one of the last two positions.

Positivism is properly an epistemological stance that proposes that the social world can be described in terms of law-like generalizations, of an identical form to those in the natural sciences, and that knowledge can therefore be acquired through the collection of value-free facts. Archer (1988) identifies two alternative positions, which Walsham (1995) suggests may be adopted by interpretive researchers. These are: anti-positivism, which proposes that facts and values cannot be separated; and normativism, which sees scientific knowledge as ideological, serving the interests of particular social groups.

## Implications for interpretive research

A number of implications flow from these philosophical positions. From an interpretivist perspective, understanding social phenomena always involves interpretation (whether individual or inter-subjective). As Giddens (1993) puts it, social science is ‘irretrievably hermeneutic’. It is not just the researcher, however, who is engaged in interpretation, the human actors involved in the social phenomenon under study are also interpreting their situation. Thus, as Geertz (1973) puts it, the data for interpretive research are ‘really our own constructions of other people’s constructions of what they and their compatriots are up to’. To complicate matters further, the concepts that are used to explain social actions can themselves become elements of the actors’ understandings of their own condition. Giddens (1984) calls this the ‘double hermeneutic’.

The assumption that actors' interpretations are subjective or inter-subjective also suggests that these interpretations will be shaped by the actor's experience in his or her particular context, rather than being the product of implacable external forces. Interpretivism therefore favours forms of research that enable the distinctive character of such contexts to be studied. Thus, Walsham (1995) links interpretivism with the ethnographic research tradition of anthropologists such as Geertz (1973), arguing that interpretive IS researchers should seek to develop rich understandings of the research context from which a ‘thick description’ of its characteristics and complexities may be achieved.

This does not, as is sometimes assumed however, lead to specific methodological prescriptions, for example that qualitative methods are the only ones suitable for interpretive research or, conversely, that quantitative methods should only be used in positivist research (Atkinson & Hammersley, 1994). Rather, it implies a reinterpretation of the status to be attributed to the various sorts of data produced by different methods. Thus, surveys and questionnaires may be valid interpretive research methods. The data that they yield are seen from an interpretive perspective, however, not as objective facts about the phenomenon under study, but as products of the respondents' interpretations of their situation (including their position as subjects of research). Hence, as Giddens (1991) argues, 'all social research,...no matter how mathematical or quantitative, presumes ethnography'.

If, therefore, as Rosen (1991) puts it, ‘to understand social process one must get inside the world of those generating it’, then we can begin to identify some criteria for the sorts of qualities of research methods that are likely to provide the best way to gain access to this world. The simplest way to do this would seem to be to get access to the actors themselves and to elicit their interpretations directly.

Even if researchers are able to gain direct access to the actors, however, their ability to obtain an understanding of actors' interpretations may be limited in a number of ways. First, the researcher cannot assume that he or she understands the actors' description of their behaviour and their interpretations in a particular social setting. For example, terms used by actors in a particular setting may appear to be identical to those used in everyday life but may have different meanings in that context (Taylor & Bogdan, 1984). Relying on actors verbal descriptions alone can therefore lead to misinterpretation of what is said. Similarly, social rules and norms identical to those in other settings may have different meanings in a particular setting.

Secondly, actors may provide a distorted account of their behaviour because their ideas as to what ought to happen in the situation may differ from what actually goes on (Taylor & Bogdan, 1984). This is not to say that there is a single ‘true’ view of what should happen in a situation or of what actually happens, but rather that actors may interpret their own behaviour in terms of dominant perceptions (Becker & Geer, 1957).

Thirdly, as Mitchell (1991) notes, secrecy is present in all social actions. Actors may thus seek to deliberately mislead the researcher (Van Maanen, 1979) or may not talk to them about certain issues and activities because they feel that discussing these might be impolite or too sensitive (Goffman, 1990). Van Maanen (1979) identifies three types of data that are often shielded by conscious deception: hidden failings, including disreputable interests or shameful errors; perceived character defects; and collective secrets, widely known but controversial practices engaged in by the actors. Such data, it is argued, may provide valuable insights on what is deemed crucial by the actors or the organization.

Finally, actors may be unable to give an account of their actions because they form part of social routines of which they are only tacitly aware. Giddens (1984), for example, distinguishes between practical knowledge from experience and discursive knowledge, actors' ability to explain their behaviour, and argues that actors know more than they can say. Altheide and Johnson (1994) also refer to the importance of research reflecting ‘largely unarticulated understanding that is often manifested in nods, silences, humour and naughty nuances’. Similarly, Goffman (1989) suggests that researchers need ‘to pick up on the minor grunts and groans as [social actors] respond to their situation’ and ‘to note their gestural, visual bodily response to what’s going on around them’.

One way by which, it may be argued, at least some of these limitations could be overcome is by extended interaction with the actors over time. Thus, it may be difficult for actors to sustain deception over a prolonged period. Ellis (1995), for example, suggests that ‘people aren’t able to make themselves act very different for very long, because they are limited by culture, custom, natural impluses, habits and longstanding patterns of interaction’. In addition, repeated experience may provide insight on local meanings, dominant perceptions or tacit knowledge. Extended engagement with actors and their life-world may also enable the unfolding of social processes to be observed.

If researchers are really to acquire ‘deep familiarity’ (Goffman, 1989) with the research context and its actors, however, this may also require more intensive, as well as more extended, interaction. Thus, through personal experience of the actors’ world, the researcher may be able to gain additional insight on their interpretations. Face-to-face contact, Lofland (1995) argues, is the ‘foundation of entering the experience of others’. This may also help to reduce or evade barriers to disclosure and provide immediate experience of local meanings, dominant perceptions or tacit knowledge and non-verbal communication. As Goffman (1989) describes it, for the researcher this means ‘subjecting yourself, your own body, and your own personality and your own social situation, to the set of contingencies that play upon a set of individuals, so that you can physically and ecologically penetrate their circle of response to their social situation’.

## DATA-GATHERING METHODS AND THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN THE RESEARCHER AND THE RESEARCH PHENOMENA

The assumptions of interpretivism may therefore be seen to lead to the suggestion that it is desirable for researchers to interact directly and intensively with their research ‘subjects’ over extended periods of time. The main phase of the research process in which such interaction may be expected to take place is that of data collection, and a range of different data-gathering methods are available to IS researchers at this stage. These may be seen to vary in the extent to which they offer opportunities for interaction between the researcher and the research phenomena: from relatively ‘distant’ methods, in which there is no direct contact between the researcher and the social actors in the research context, to those that involve intensive, and often extended, engagement between researchers and their ‘subjects’. This is illustrated in Figure 1, in which a number of the main methods are placed on a spectrum between maximum distance between researcher and ‘subject’ and maximum engagement.

Each of these methods will now be briefly reviewed to illustrate the nature of the relationship between the researcher and research phenomena that they involve.

The analysis of published data (e.g. Loh & Venkatraman, 1993) clearly lies at the ‘distant’ end of the spectrum. While such methods require no direct interaction between the researcher and the social actors, this does not mean that the data they use do not involve any interpretation. For example, data on company expenditure on computers must depend at some stage on decisions made by individuals as to what to classify as computer expenditure (does it include the costs of training, or of printer paper, or of the electricity to run the computers?). Even when the data are recorded automatically, for example the duration of calls to a customer helpline, there was at some time a decision that it was appropriate to capture these data and about how they should be recorded and presented and to whom. From an interpretive standpoint, however, they provide very ‘thin’ descriptions (to invert Geertz’s terminology) of the actors’ interpretations, typically providing only the most rudimentary characterization of the actors’ context.

Textual analysis (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994) was the original focus of hermeneutics, especially as applied to holy scripture (Boland, 1985). More recently, audio and video have been added to the range of potential media available for analysis. Again, there is no contact between the researcher and social actor, although in some cases additional information about them may be available from other sources to assist interpretation. The extent to which such analysis can (or indeed could) provide access to the author's interpretations depends upon assumptions about the relationship between the author and their text and the ability of readers to achieve a sympathetic engagement with the text.

Although the postal survey (e.g. Barki & Hartwick, 1994) requires no face-to-face interaction between the researcher and the research subject, it clearly involves greater intervention by the researcher than the analysis of published data or texts. Survey respondents, for example, will, of necessity, be aware of the research and will need to devote time and thought to the preparation of their answers. While careful questionnaire design can mean that responses are tightly fitted to the researcher's prior interpretation of the research issues, well-designed surveys will also include open questions (Moser & Kalton, 1985) that can enable some insight into actors' interpretations when less fettered by the need to offer a precoded response.

Interviews (e.g. Lacity & Willcocks, 1995) are an important method in IS research. The degree of engagement between the researcher and the research ‘subject’ will vary depending on whether the interviews are: structured, when there is a set series of questions to be asked; semi-structured, when a number of pre-decided, general topics are raised but the interviewee is also given the opportunity to direct the discussion; or unstructured, when there is no pre-set agenda. Interviews also permit face-to-face contact with the social actors, thus allowing the researcher to pick up on, if not always to record, non-verbal communication. The lower the degree of structuring of the interview, the more chance the researcher may have to explore the respondent’s answers and to assess their trustworthiness and non-standard use of terms.

Observation does not necessarily imply any greater engagement with the research subject than interviews. Indeed, some authors, such as Friedrichs and Ludtke (1975), suggest that it is both possible and desirable to systematize observation through the application of standard protocols to produce more ‘exact’ and reliable data. Gold (1958) also identified four possible roles for the observer in such research: complete observer, observer as participant, participant as observer and complete participant. For the purposes of this paper, however, we will simplify this to two roles: passive, roughly corresponding to Gold’s first role, and participant, covering the rest.

Passive, or non-participant, observation (e.g. Leidner & Jarvenpaa, 1993) therefore typically means that the researcher is present at the research site for some period of time during which data of various sorts, such as student behaviour in electronic classrooms, are recorded, without any overt intervention on the part of the researcher. This does not mean that the presence of the researchers does not affect the actors' behaviour, as will be discussed later, but that they deliberately abstain from participation in the context. Friedrichs and Ludtke (1975) suggest that experimental laboratory studies (e.g. Constant et al., 1994) may be seen as a form of controlled, standardized non-participant observation.

Participant observation (e.g. Pentland, 1992; Mouakket et al., 1994) is closely associated with the ethnographic tradition of social anthropology (Atkinson & Hammersley, 1994). It was classically defined by Kluckhohn (1940) as ‘conscious and systematic sharing, insofar as circumstances permit, in the life-activities and, on occasion, in the interests and affects of a group of persons. Its purpose is to obtain data about behaviour through direct contact and in terms of specific situations in which the distortion that results from the investigator's being an outside agent is reduced to a minimum'. In IS research, for example, this might mean working as an IS developer (as described below) or as a member of a strategy group (Jones, 1994). Such participation may be covert, as is often the case in some of the classic participant observation studies of illegal activities or marginal social groups in which the researcher's dual role is not made known to some or all of the actors, or overt, when their position is openly disclosed. Both forms of observation enable the researcher to gain personal experience of the research context under 'normal' conditions and, if sustained over a sufficient period, potentially to get behind the 'official picture', to go 'backstage' (Goffman, 1990) and to observe processes continuously over time.

Action research (e.g. Avison & Catchpole, 1988; Baskerville, 1993) differs from participant observation in that the researcher actively intervenes in the research context to try to achieve particular outcomes, rather than simply seeking to act as a ‘normal’ member of the context. The action researcher therefore has a personal commitment to the outcome of their intervention and is often applying a specific, proprietary approach, such as the sociotechnical approach or the soft systems methodology that they have been involved in developing.

Finally, we consider consultancy (Newman & Kozar, 1994), which Gummeson (1991) proposes as a legitimate mode of management research, as the most ‘engaged’ form of all. Here, the engagement of the actors is tested by their willingness to pay for the intervention, and the researcher’s effectiveness can be assessed by the actors’ willingness to offer them further contracts.

## Distant and engaged data-gathering methods

From this brief review, it would seem evident that the neat ordering of data-gathering methods shown in Figure 1 is something of a simplification. For example, it is not clear that textual analysis of a video recording is necessarily more distant than a postal survey or that an extended programme of interviews provides less opportunity for engagement than a single consultancy project. Nevertheless, it would seem possible to identify two broad groups of methods. At one end of the spectrum, there are those ‘distant’ methods, such as analysis of published statistics, textual analysis and postal surveys, through which there is no direct interaction between the researcher and the research phenomena. To these we might also add structured interviews and experiments in which the effects of interaction are strictly controlled. At the other end are ‘engaged’ methods, such as consultancy, action research and participant observation, through which the researcher is personally involved in the activities in the research context. To these we might add passive observation and unstructured interviews, which can provide opportunities for extensive exposure to the social actors’ life-world.

If we take the argument from the principles of interpretivism presented above, therefore, we might expect interpretive IS researchers to favour the use of engaged data-gathering methods. In order to assess whether this is indeed the case, an analysis was made of the research papers published in three IS journals: MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research and European

Journal of Information Systems between January 1993 and December 1996. The journals were chosen as representing ‘European’ as well as ‘mainstream’ research traditions (Walsham, 1995).

As Table 1 shows, it is clear that the majority of IS research reported in these journals is based on more distant data-gathering methods. Thus, nearly three-quarters of the reported research used analysis of published data, textual analysis, surveys, structured interviews or experiments, while just 16% used engaged methods.

## PARTICIPANT OBSERVATION OF EXECUTIVE INFORMATION SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT

This study involved one of the authors working full-time for a 6-month period as a salaried member of the executive information systems development ‘team’ based in the headquarters of a large manufacturing company ('LMC Europe'). This team was responsible for developing new reports (referred to as 'projects') to be included in the proprietary executive information systems (EIS) and for maintenance, software and hardware support, documentation and security.

Initial access was facilitated by the fact that the researcher had previously worked for the team and had been given leave by LMC to enable him to carry out the study and that he would be an additional resource for the team leader whose three systems analysts and a trainee were facing high workloads as EIS use spread rapidly among LMC Europe's senior executives. The researcher's role was thus that of an overt participant. Because of sensitivity about publicity relating to this strategic IS, however, it was necessary for the researcher to sign a confidentiality agreement specifying that the company should not be identified and that all reports should be cleared by the team leader.

During the observation period, the researcher was fully involved in the EIS team's activities, being given charge of development and maintenance of a number of EIS projects. He was also given the same facilities as the other team members, including access to all documents and support staff involved in data provision, technical support and systems security. As a result, the researcher was rapidly accepted as a team member and participation soon extended not just to the formal work activities of the team but also to their informal social activities.

This insider status meant that the researcher gained access to information that would not otherwise have been divulged. For example, the EIS team sought to spread a story that the suspension of one of the EIS reports by the data providers' division was to hide adverse performance figures in that division resulting from a major strike. It subsequently transpired, however, that the suspension was largely due to the inability of the EIS to support the changing needs of the data providers' division. The EIS team had seen this as bad for their image in the company and had therefore encouraged an alternative story that cast the blame for the suspension elsewhere. It was not possible, however, to sustain the deception with a team member for any length of time.

Similarly, many of the practices of the EIS team were confidential and could not have been easily discovered without being involved in development work. For example, the EIS team did not talk about systems security to anyone outside the department because tight security was essential to safeguard the confidential reports and to win the support of data providers. In practice, however, security was compromised by many issues, such as the inability of the software to provide the necessary password security and the reluctance of executives to use many levels of passwords. Team members therefore covered this issue up to project an image of a secure EIS. For example, they followed an elaborate password allocation procedure to satisfy other interested parties, such as data providers and users, that the EIS complied with company practices. These informal practices were not concealed from the researcher but rather they involved him in them. By comparison, they usually gave a well-rehearsed version of the 'EIS story' to outside enquirers.

The personal relationship with the other team members also provided insights into their motivation and perspectives. For example, when the team leader asked one of the analysts to find out executives' needs from their secretaries, the analyst complained that the secretaries would not talk to EIS team members and treated them like maintenance workers. One of the other analysts, however, said that he had not found this reaction. This may have reflected the fact that the first analyst had made his way up from the shop floor, while the other was a graduate who had been recruited from the finance division.

Sitting at a desk alongside the other EIS team members, the researcher was able to experience the work atmosphere at first hand. For example, during a period when problems arose with one project, the EIS manager held a number of meetings with his office door closed, which was interpreted by other members as a sign that something was wrong, such as someone was being made redundant or adverse data on departmental performance was being published. Actual door slamming was used by managers to convey this message to their staff. In another period, when external conditions had lead to restrictions on overseas travel and a decline in the company's sales, motivation in the team was noticeably lowered, with members fulfilling only the minimum requirements and spending a lot of time reading trade magazines and software manuals. There were also tensions within the team, giving rise to disputes about development standards.

As a stranger to the research context, however, the researcher was also able to notice and question aspects of the social routine of which the participants were hardly aware unless interruptions occurred. For example, expectations of timing and location of many activities, such as report updates and team meetings, were largely taken for granted by other team members. Similarly, the researcher was very aware of the strong organizational hierarchy of LMC that required that EIS team members should never speak to the executives who used their system or even be seen in their office if working on one of the terminals. This ‘stepping back’ from the research context was also facilitated by taking time each month to write up findings and discussing them with the second author.

During the study at LMC Europe, a log was maintained by the researcher to record activities of EIS team members, events, meetings, discussions and other observations, as shown in Figure 2. Initially an attempt was made to record activities on an hourly basis, but this proved impractical because of time constraints, and it was decided to switch to a daily summary, usually prepared after work. Occasionally, discussions were specifically arranged to enable the researcher to obtain other participants' accounts of their actions. Data were also sought from other key players, such as support staff. In addition, data on the historical context were gathered from the staff at LMC and from company documents.

## DISCUSSION

The LMC case may be used to highlight a number of issues in the use of engaged data-gathering methods. The most fundamental of these is clearly that of intervention in the research context. While this constitutes one of the primary raison d'être for most engaged data-gathering methods, from the perspective of practitioners of more distant data-gathering methods, it is likely to be seen as a challenge to one of the basic precepts of ‘scientific’ research, i.e. that the researcher should seek to avoid influencing the phenomena they are studying. Consultancy and action research even go one step further than this in seeking to achieve particular outcomes.

While sociologists of science, such as Collins & Pinch (1993), would argue that researchers', often unacknowledged commitment to particular perspectives influences their perception of their findings, such an overt interest in obtaining particular results would seem likely to be a significant barrier to the widespread adoption of these methods. In their defence, action researchers might claim that their lack of a (immediate) direct financial stake in the results enables them to be more dispassionate but, for consultancy, bias in interpreting the 'findings' may be a necessary feature of successful research, if clients are to be persuaded to repurchase the consultant's services in future.

Consultancy and action research also face the problem that, in the absence of the control procedures of experimental research, the researchers' deliberate interventions, rather than the actors' 'normal' social practices in the context, may be the predominant influence and focus of attention in actors' interpretations. The results may therefore tell us something about the researcher's intervention, but not about the actors' 'constructions of what they and their compatriots are [normally] up to'.

Participant observers seek to avoid this problem by trying to behave like any ordinary member of the research context. In the LMC study, this meant that Joe matched his work style to those of his colleagues and did not deliberately seek a particular role but allowed the team leader to assign activities according to his judgement of the researcher's abilities and the needs of the team. Moreover, although some conversations were deliberately set up to discuss particular events or perceptions, in general, the researcher did not draw attention to his additional role. Thus, even if colleagues could not escape the fact that they knew why the researcher had joined the team, this rarely intruded in any overt manner on their interactions. The researcher's access to ‘back region’ (Goffman, 1990) activities, such as the approach to security, may be seen as evidence of his acceptance as a ‘normal’ member of the team.

At the same time, however, it can not be claimed that Joe's presence had no effect on the other team members. Thus, Joe was expected to play a full part in the team's activities, otherwise he would not have been seen as a ‘normal’ member. He therefore had an unavoidable influence on aspects of the phenomena he was seeking to observe, not least through his contribution to the team's productivity. Moreover, his particular contribution was inevitably atypical in that it reflected his particular skills and experience, hence another researcher in the same context might have had a different effect.

Although it might appear that passive observers and interviewers do not intervene in the research context, their interaction with the research site is still likely to have some effect on the actors (Schwartz & Schwartz, 1955). Thus, even if social actors do not change their behaviour in the presence of the observer or if they do not give the interviewer the answers they think he or she wants to hear, awareness of being observed or the process of being interviewed may casue the actors to reflect upon their situation, with subsequent effects on their behaviour. Moreover, as the classic Hawthorne studies illustrate (Roethlisberger & Dixon, 1949), the attention of the researcher may in itself be a source of increased motivation. These effects will, of course, also be present with participant observation and may also occur with postal questionnaires, although in the latter case the researcher has no way of assessing this as they have no other access to the actors and their interpretations.

Finally, even ‘distant’ methods, such as textual analysis and published statistics, which do not involve intervention in the research context at the actual point of use, are not immune to such effects. For example, social actors may have been affected by the act of producing the text, by being audio or video recorded or by whatever process was used to capture the original data for publication. As with postal questionnaires, however, the researcher will be very limited in their ability to assess the nature of this influence. The effects of the researchers’ intervention in the research context are therefore not restricted to engaged data-gathering method but may be seen as an issue for all forms of IS research.

The distinctive character of engaged data-gathering methods is not just restricted to the issue of intervention, however, but affects all aspects of the research process from initial access to reporting of results. We will now consider each of these stages in turn.

## Access to the research context

Although access problems are, of course, not unheard of for interviewers and low (<75%) response rates are typical with postal surveys, these can represent the most difficult aspects of engaged data-gathering methods, especially for observation. Thus, while consultancy and action research may be able to promise particular results, the potential cost of observation to actors in the research context in terms of time, disruption and risk of exposure of confidential information may frequently outweigh the, often intangible and unpredictable, benefits. Unless, therefore, the researcher has skills that are valuable in the research context, as Joe had for LMC, then there may be little incentive for the potential subjects to give permission. And even if the researcher could contribute to the activities in the research context, access may still be problematic because of concerns over confidentiality and visibility of informal work practices. Access may therefore be negotiable only at the cost of strong confidentiality agreements that may restrict the research value of the study.

Covert observation may be one way to overcome access problems in some situations, but it can give rise to significant ethical dilemmas in the practice and reporting of such research (Punch, 1986). Moreover, there may be some organizational arenas, such as board-level decision-making, for which access, whether covert or overt, is likely to be extremely difficult to obtain. This is not to say that there are necessarily any IS research phenomena that are, in principle, inaccessible to observation (a company board could invite a researcher to study their deliberations) but rather that, in practice, other methods may be likely to be more feasible as a means of studying them.

Good access to a particular research site may also have drawbacks. For example, it may restrict the engaged researchers ability to gain access to other sites or the willingness of others to talk to them because of their perceived partiality. Thus, in the LMC study, Joe's identification with the EIS team may have affected how members of other divisions of the company perceived him. In this respect, more distant methods may enable the researcher to play the part of a neutral outsider and to gain access to a wider range of relevant contexts.

Similarly, the practical requirements of engaged research methods, in terms of the time needing to be spent at particular research sites, may also restrict the range of contexts accessible. Researchers using more distant methods may therefore be better placed to study phenomena occurring across many sites, while engagement will facilitate in-depth analysis of one, or at most a few, sites.

## Data about research phenomena

Having gained access to the research site, the next problem facing the interpretive researcher is to decide what constitutes data, given the richness of the potential phenomena made available through close engagement in a particular research context. In the LMC study, for example, the focus was on the development process, but what data were relevant to understanding the way in which the process occurred? Should this include detailed analysis of the technical capabilities of the proprietary EIS software? Should it include the relationships between the team members and, if so, how could these be characterized? Should it include the activities of team members and, if so, in what detail: minute-by-minute, hourly, daily? And what about other data, for example the problems experienced by one team member in selling his house which distracted him for a number of days or the rumours about executive reshuffles, that might affect the EIS sponsor? As this illustrates, the range of data that could potentially be collected is almost literally infinite, from the individual mouse movements of a developer designing an EIS screen to the global social changes that were altering the market for LMC's products.

Such a richness of potential data can lead, Trow (1957) warns, to a tendency for such research to focus on the ‘cultural esoterica’ of a specific setting, thereby failing to identify phenomena that recur across many different settings. A related problem, identified by Walsham (1995), is that engaged researchers may get so wrapped up in the detail of particular sites that they become unable to step back and critically reflect upon actors’ interpretations — failing to ‘see the wood for the trees’. On the other hand, Light (1979) argues that, precisely because of their access to the complexity and ‘deep structure’ of a research context, engaged data-gathering methods, such as participant observation, may facilitate a more holistic analysis. Of these tendencies, the one that will predominate in any particular research situation is open to question, but what is clear is that deciding what data to collect with engaged methods is likely to be a major task; moreover, if the researcher is following a grounded theory approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), it is likely to be a task for which there may be no a priori guidance.

This is also an issue, of course, for other data-gathering methods. With published statistics and textual analysis, the data are predefined. Interviews and, to some extent, postal questionnaires can provide access to unforeseen and uncodified data, but the immersion in the research context that engaged data-gathering methods involves presents a much greater richness of potential data. This does not mean, however, that researchers using these methods cannot be systematic in their approach to data collection. A distinctive source of data in engaged studies that is not available in more distant forms of research is the personal experience of the researcher. This may be a valuable source of insight on actors' behaviour as the LMC case sought to illustrate. It cannot be assumed, however, that the researcher's experience is identical to that of the actors. As Vidich (1955) argues 'his (sic) own experiences, though genuine, are at best vicarious approximations of those of his respondents; he never completely enters their world and by definition, if he did, he would assume the values, premises and standards of his subjects and thereby lose his usefulness to research except as another subject'. In anthropology this is identified as the danger of 'going native' (Rock, 1979).

The researchers' understanding is likely to be influenced not just by their own experience but also by interpretations offered by informants. The strength of this influence will be affected by the willingness of informants to offer their views and the researchers judgement of the informants' reliability. The researchers' accounts may therefore reflect the perspectives of those informants with whom they get on best. It is, of course, the case that similar selective interpretation will affect interviews and postal questionnaires, but there may be fewer sources of countervailing perspectives and the researcher's experience of the research context is likely to be absent or very limited.

It is also important to recognize that the data collected are likely to be shaped by the practical contingencies of the activities being studied, particularly with observational studies which seek to follow events over an extended period of time. For example, organizational restructurings, departures of key actors, budget cuts and other unforeseen events may disrupt data collection. What started as a study of process may therefore become one of change. In addition, while observation permits the detailed continuous monitoring of process compared with the more limited, episodic interaction of interviews, some potentially significant activities may occur too infrequently to be recorded during the period of observation. Similarly, significant activities may occur outside the context being observed, such as the executives' use of the EIS at LMC. Although the ramifications of these activities within the context may be observed, interviews and questionnaires may provide more direct access to them. Such methods, while apparently more distant, may also facilitate controlled intervention, for example through directed questioning, to enable exploration of issues that the participant observer in their desire to avoid unduly influencing social actors may be unable to address. The engaged researcher may thus find themselves the subject of others' agendas and may be limited in their access to potentially significant aspects of a research context.

## Recording data

Because the distinctive data gathered through engaged data-gathering methods are often derived from sensory experience and informal conversation, there can be significant problems in creating a record of such research. Thus, as Rock (1979) describes, the process of participant observation is ‘a phase of lived experience which cannot be summarised or publicised without radical distortion’. Unlike an interview, for example, in which it may be possible to produce a transcript of a recorded conversation, the data of participant observation are also often ephemeral and internal to the researcher. This raises particular issues of verification, as Bruyn (1966) discusses. Moreover, as Schwartz and Schwartz (1955) argue, even if observations can be recorded as they happen, there is an inevitable process of interpretation between registering and recording an event. Recording data is thus a process of retrospective sense-making that may be more or less attenuated by the practical contingencies of the time lapse between the occurrence of an event and its recording.

## Sustaining interaction

Engaged research takes time. Goffman (1989), for example, recommends a minimum of a year in the field to observe a range of unanticipated events and to acquire ‘deep familiarity’. As such events are, by definition, unpredictable and, as Jorgenson (1989) notes, events will continue to unfold whether the researcher is present or not, any chosen period of observation is likely to be somewhat arbitrary and will rarely have any absolute end.

For the researcher, however, a more important consideration may be avoiding premature termination of the research period because of their own, possibly inadvertent, actions. As Adler and Adler (1991) argue, ‘relations between field researchers and their subjects rest on a base of trust (which rests in turn on a base of liking)’. Maintaining this trust, they suggest, requires: skill in dealing with sensitive issues and confidentiality; finding a comfortable level and type of exchange; selecting a role that works within the environment; moving carefully among the various individuals and groups in the setting, but above all ‘personability’, being able to get on with the social actors. As Mitchell (1991) and Kleinmann and Copp (1993) discuss, this may sometimes be emotionally disquieting for the researcher when it requires them to relate to informants to whom they might not otherwise be sympathetic.

Another important implication of the sustained and intensive interaction that engaged data-gathering methods involves is that the number of sites that can be studied is likely to be limited. While the principles of interpretivism suggest that conceptual generalization may be possible from a small number of cases (Walsham, 1995), the detailed focus on one or at most a few sites that is typical of most engaged methods means that it is likely to be difficult to generalize without evidence from other sources. The time requirements for engaged data-gathering methods would also seem likely to restrict the number of researchers who are able to practise these methods.

## Reporting engaged research

Walsham (1995) suggests that the participant observer faces ‘twin dangers of over-modesty or self-aggrandisement’ in reporting their own contribution to the research phenomena. While it is certainly true that the preparation of participant observer reports is far from easy, the problem Walsham identifies is neither unique to engaged data-gathering methods nor, necessarily, the most difficult the researcher faces. Thus, the temptation to self-aggrandisement would seem likely to be considerably stronger with action research and even more so with consultancy, when the researcher has a commitment to his/her intervention. On the other hand, interview reports may be seen as over-modest, rarely discussing the way in which the responses may have been shaped by the interviewer’s questioning, but reporting these as statements of the interviewees’ views. Therefore, a sensible guideline would be that the contribution of the researcher should be given no more nor less prominence, compared with other actors in the context, than their contributions to the actions being studied deserve. Achieving the appropriate balance is likely to be a particular dilemma of engaged data-gathering methods, but getting other team members to review reports would seem a useful corrective.

Altheide and Johnson (1994) propose an ‘ethnographic ethic’ that should govern reporting of observational research. Reports, they argue, should ‘show the hand’ of the researcher and not try to present the findings as the product of a disembodied and omniscient observer. Bochner (1994) goes further than this in arguing that reports should also reflect the way in which the researchers’ experience of themselves has been changed by the social actors as well as the ways in which the researcher may be changing the social actors’ experience. Other aspects of Altheide & Johnson’s ethic propose that reports should be ‘clear and precise in delineating the basis of their claims’, describing how the researchers were able to reach their understandings, and should acknowledge the ‘multiplicity of meanings and perspectives, and the rationality of these perspectives, by setting forth the context(s)’. This last should remind researchers to be aware of the dangers of relying on the accounts of individual informants.

From the experience of the LMC study, a bigger problem than false modesty or selective reporting is that of translating the incredible detail about the research phenomena to which observation gives access into a form that can reasonably be presented in academic articles or even books. As Lofland (1995) argues, social research ‘is an effort to see the universe in a grain of sand’. Becker (1958), for example, reports that his study gave rise to about 5000 single-spaced pages of description, Goffman (1989) suggests that a year of participant observation is likely to yield between 500 and 1000 pages of single-spaced typed notes, while the LMC log comprised 250 pages of heavily abbreviated notes. Condensing such material, or even small sections of it, into 2000 or 3000 words for inclusion in a standard academic paper inevitably involves massive simplification. Moreover, there are no obvious principles on what material should be presented. If the researcher has had access to the ‘full story’ behind an event (assuming for the current purpose that it is possible to talk of such a thing), then how do they decide what detail is relevant and what is irrelevant. As writers of fiction, such as Marcel Proust or Nicholson Baker, have shown, a moment's reverie may evoke innumerable associations that contribute to the actors' interpretations. Whatever is chosen will therefore inevitably reflect the researcher's own biases. And this problem is not obviated by other methods. Selection of excerpts from interview transcripts, for example, involves a similar process, while in postal questionnaires the selection has often been done in phrasing, or even posing, the question.

Reporting of the results of engaged research may also raise significant ethical issues reflecting the dilemma between the ethical duties of the researcher to disclose their sources of data (and also any constraints on their reporting) and their duties to protect the social actors' confidences. This situation may be further complicated by tacit or formal commitments to non-disclosure. The first of these may be powerfully reinforced by the affective pressures developed through close interaction with the actors. For example, should a researcher report behaviour that may reflect badly on his or her erstwhile colleagues? How do they report important phenomena that might compromise confidentiality agreements? Ellis (1994) provides an illuminating discussion of these issues in reflections on the repercussions of her publication of the results of a participant observation of a fishing community.

## Dual role

This dilemma relates to a broader issue for the engaged researcher, and in particular the participant observer, i.e. their dual role. On the one hand, they wish to experience the taken-for-granted world of the social actors and, on the other, they seek to be the continuously questioning researcher exposing its hidden assumptions. While, in practice, the roles are rarely quite as discrete as this suggests, it demands mental agility on the behalf of the researcher in regularly flipping between the roles, and social dexterity in avoiding drawing the attention of other actors to the change.

The dual role also gives rise to potential tensions in a participant observer's contribution to the research context. On the one hand, they don't wish to do any more than any normal actor and should be happy with a relatively stable environment in which routine practices can be more clearly discerned; on the other, they are likely to want to study interesting events and to be where the action (if any) is, maybe even prompting it, because that is where hidden assumptions may be most clearly exposed.

The engaged researcher's access and reflection on the research context may also provide them with insights not immediately evident to other actors. This is a valuable part of the research process but may be more problematic if, for example, it indicates fraud or the possibility of future redundancies. Should such information be disclosed to the actors? This is not to suggest that other actors may not be equally capable of such reflection, but rather that, in the absence of their research interest, the researcher might not notice such things either.

This points to the more general issue of the role of the researcher. In much positivist research this is seen as elucidating generalizable causal relationships that will permit reliable prescription. For many interpretive researchers, however, the primary aim is description, with the possible additional hope that conceptual generalization may yield ‘useful insights for related work in other contexts’ (Walsham, 1995). A third perspective, however, characteristic of the ‘critical’ perspective (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991) suggests that the aim of the researcher should be the emancipation of less privileged groups. This can be the justification for action research, although it should be acknowledged that, in practice, such research (and even more so consultancy) is more usually driven by less emancipatory ideals. Participant observation per se does not require the adoption of any one particular role. For example, Friedrichs & Ludtke (1975) propose that participant observation may lead to prescription. The emphasis of observational methods on the detailed analysis of specific contexts, however, suggests that they may be more typically associated with description.

## Personal involvement

Underlying many of these issues relating to engaged methods is a concern that the engagement of the researcher with the research site and, more specifically, with the social actors somehow compromises their ability to observe, reflect and report. It is certainly true that effective involvement in the research context is likely to lead to the development of personal relationships with colleagues that go beyond what would normally be considered typical of that between researcher and subject. This can also arise, however, over the course of a series of interviews with the same people, as Walsham and Sahay (1996) discuss. This is not just a matter of personal friendship, although this may be a factor, but also of a sense of social duty to repay the time and effort that the actors have contributed to the research process by sharing insights and information that the researcher has acquired. Other questions concerning the extent to which researchers identify emotionally, and perhaps in psychoanalytical terms, with the actors in the research context have also recently begun to receive attention (Hunt, 1989; Kleinman & Copp, 1993).

Practitioners of engaged data-gathering methods can, however, take steps to address this issue if not to escape it. In the LMC study, for example, specific mechanisms were set up to encourage critical reflection on the research process, including regular discussions of the findings with a non-participant. Even if temporal or spatial constraints make such arrangements difficult, the writing up of a log and the subsequent drafting of the case-study description provide opportunities for ‘stepping back’ from the data and for deliberate reflection on underlying assumptions.

## CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have sought to explore some of the implications of the assumptions underlying interpretivism for the preferred relationship between the researcher and the research phenomenon, and we have suggested that these may favour the use of more engaged data-gathering methods than is currently typically the case in IS research. This does not mean, however, that we would wish to suggest that all research should use these methods. The choice of method depends on what sort of phenomena researchers are studying. If they are trying to do research on large-scale processes over long time periods, such as changes in national industrial structure, then published statistics may be more useful than engaged data-gathering methods (although it may still be argued that such processes must be composed of small-scale activities on shorter time-scales, even if the general trend is not always easy to discern). Similarly, postal questionnaires may be appropriate for collecting data on relatively unambiguous phenomena at large numbers of sites, while interviews can enable sensitive exploration of actors' interpretations at a smaller number of sites. Moreover, in practice, of course, researchers often mix methods, drawing on the strengths of each as appropriate. Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991), for example, describe Orlikowski's interpretive method as using 'ethnographic techniques such as observation of participants, researchers interactions with and study of CASE tools, documentation review, social contact, and unstructured and semistructured interviews'. Similarly, Jorgenson (1989) suggests that participant observers may also use documents, recordings (audio, photographs), artefacts, life histories, casual conversations, in-depth informal and unstructured interviews as well as formally structured interviews and questionnaires.

Engaged methods are also not without their potential problems. As has been argued, however, many of these problems such as what constitutes the data, how do we report them, what is the relationship of the researcher to the actors in the research context, are common to other data-gathering methods, if less clearly highlighted by them. Consideration of these issues may therefore be of value to all types of IS research and may contribute to more reflexive interpretive research practice.

Finally, what seems curious in the IS context is the relative paucity of engaged studies. Why is it that these methods which seem to provide good access to the sort of data that interpretive IS researchers want, are so comparatively rarely used? The issues discussed in the previous section might suggest some answers.

Is it that these methods are too difficult? Setting up access is rarely easy; the research process itself takes a lot of time; there are vast amounts of data to analyse; and, in the end, there is no right or wrong answer. Other research methods, such as postal questionnaires, may indeed be easier and be likely to produce results more quickly. There may also be significant practical problems for academics in full-time positions in getting so intensively involved in a particular research site, and research students and full-time researchers may therefore continue to be their main practitioners. It would seem unfortunate, however, if personal convenience were to be the prime consideration in choosing IS research methods. If more engaged research methods can indeed provide better data from an interpretive perspective, then perhaps more researchers should be prepared to put in the necessary effort.

Is it that engaged researchers cannot be trusted to be sufficiently dispassionate, reflective, modest (or immodest) about their own contribution? Or is there some characteristic of those researchers who choose these methods that makes them less able than those adopting interviews or other more distanced methods to stand back from their work and assess their own contribution. Of course, the immersion in the research context that engaged research involves has its own pressures, not least of time, as the researchers seek to meet the expectations of their dual roles; in addition, the fascination with the detail of the particular context can become engrossing to the possible detriment of the ‘big picture’. But does this mean that researchers cannot overcome these problems? If this is considered to be a significant drawback of engaged data-gathering methods, and assuming that its practitioners are not a breed apart, it would seem to suggest a low opinion of the sensitivity and ability of IS researchers — which has serious implications for all methods of data collection, not just engaged methods.

Finally, is it that practitioners of engaged data-gathering methods get too involved with their research ‘subjects’. If so, is this because these methods raise messy ethical issues, which are arguably an inevitable part of all research and with which interpretive researchers would typically profess a concern? Is it because researchers might start to care about what happens to their ‘laboratory mice’, or even to recognize them as human beings? Or is it that such engagement challenges the superiority of the researcher as a knowledgeable objective expert who is able to see things as others cannot? As this paper has stressed, it is not that these are issues exclusive to engaged methods, or that they provide any easy solution to them, but by drawing attention to them they can provide an opportunity for critical reflection on our research philosophy and practice.

## Biographies

Joe Nandhakumar PhD is a lecturer in Information Systems in the Department of Management at the University of Southampton, UK. He received his PhD in Information Systems from the University of Cambridge in 1994. His research interests include social and organizational issues of implementing information systems and particularly executive information systems and groupware. Prior to joining the university, he worked for several years in software development and accounting projects in multinational companies in Europe.

Matthew Jones PhD is a lecturer in Information Management in the Department of Engineering and the Judge Institute of Management Studies at the University of Cambridge, UK. He previously held post-doctoral positions at the universities of Reading and Cambridge, where he was involved in the development of computer-based models for use in public policy decision-making. His current research interests are concerned with the social and organizational aspects of the design and use of information systems and the relationship between technology and social and organizational change.
