# A senior executive end-user framework

Monica E. Seeley & David Targett

University of Bath, School of Management, Claverton Down, Bath BA2 7AY, UK, email:m.seeley@mailbox.ulcc.ac.uk

Abstract. Senior executives make decisions about their organization's information technology (IT) budgets that often involve substantial expenditure. Yet, in terms of their own competence in using IT, there is evidence to suggest that they may not be the best members of the organization to make such decisions. This paper reports on a study of 85 senior executives across 16 large organizations and the depth and breath of applications these executives use to support them at an individual level. The research design and methodologies were based on the principles of grounded theory. The primary source of data collection was in-depth interviews. A number of end-user frameworks have been developed by previous authors; however, most are more pertinent to middle and junior managers. Based on the analysis of the data from this study an end-user framework is proposed that is more appropriate for senior executives.

Keywords: Senior executives, end-user computing, personal computing, end-user frameworks, grounded theory

## INTRODUCTION

Although senior executives are responsible for investing significant resources in information technology (IT) for the organization, the extent to which they accept and use computers themselves, and hence the impact on how they operate, is debatable (Nelson, 1989; March & Sproull, 1990; Bird, 1991). Moreover, it is suggested that one of the main reasons why senior executives do not perceive a return on their IT investment is their lack of personal involvement with, and awareness of, IT and its role (Benjamin et al., 1984; Keen, 1991).

Some researchers advocate that senior executives should be concerned not with the use of the technology but rather with its deployment (Ackoff, 1967; Earl, 1989; Scott Morton, 1991). In contrast, however, Kanter (1992) suggests that the behaviour of the chief executive and the management team sends strong messages to the rest of the organization and creates the culture of what constitutes acceptable behaviour, whether dealing with either IT or other areas. This is not to suggest that executives need to use computers to make good decisions about how to deploy their organizations' IT resources, but rather their use or non-use will influence the IT culture and hence how acceptable it is for others to use it (Cheney & Dickenson, 1982; Ein-Dor & Segev, 1988; Rockart & DeLong, 1988; Keen, 1991; Schein, 1992).

There is an extensive body of literature on the use of computers by middle and junior managers and professionals, although few of the studies focus exclusively on senior executives and their needs and uses. As March and Sproull (1990) note, given the resources (time and money), most organizations invest in their IT systems, and particularly PCs, and the influence that senior executives have on the utilization of IT, it is not unreasonable to suggest that more is understood about the use these executives make of IT themselves. This paper reports research that investigates the use senior executives make of computers (and specifically PCs).

## DEFINITIONS AND TERMINOLOGY

## Senior executive

A major problem is to define the term ‘senior executive’ in a way that is unambiguous, leaves as little room as possible for subjective interpretation and enables the researcher to operationalize it (Herriot, 1993). Titles alone are not always a sufficient indication of executives’ seniority (Martin, 1986). Based on Anthony’s (1965) model of executive work and the experiences of previous researchers (such as Martin, 1986; Herriot, 1993), for the purpose of this study a senior executive is defined as ‘an executive who is concerned with the strategic direction of their organization’s business’ and:

\- is in a position to influence significantly the strategic decision-making processes for their function and/or the organization;

● has substantial control and authority over how resources are deployed;

\- is in a position to influence the strategic direction of the business of their function/organization;

\- may have other senior managers reporting to him or her.

## End-user

There are many definitions of the term end-user, which range from those that include MIS professionals to those that specifically exclude them (for example Rockart & Flannery, 1983; Yaverbaum, 1988; Cotterman & Kumar, 1989; Amoroso, 1992; Rainer & Harrison, 1993). For this study, Yaverbaum's (1988, p. 76) definition is adopted, namely 'any member of an organization who interacts with computer systems, but who is not employed as a programmer or systems analyst'. Those who use computer-generated output only in paper form as opposed to accessing it directly on-line are not regarded as users.

## END-USER COMPUTING (EUC)

## Frameworks

There are several definitions and frameworks of end-user computing (for example Rockart & Flannery, 1983; Yaverbaum, 1988; Cotterman & Kumar, 1989; Amoroso, 1992; Kraemer et al., 1993). Yet, as Rainer and Harrison (1993, p. 1187) note, ‘the large body of research on EUC lacks a common, universally accepted EUC construct that has been defined, operationalised, tested and validated’.

Cotterman and Kumar (1989) develop a three-dimensional ‘user cube’ based on the user’s operation of the computer, their development of applications and their control over the available MIS resources. They feel that, although the dimension of control is missing from previous definitions, it is appropriate to end-user computing in the late 1980s. Amoroso’s (1992) ‘end-user characteristics matrix’ comprises 10 single-dimension ‘user characteristics’ such as training and the nature of the applications developed. Despite this, his framework is vague in parts, especially with respect to the software applications used. Cotterman and Kumar (1989) and Amoroso (1992) are able to map the frameworks of previous researchers on to their frameworks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of end-users.

Rainer and Harrison (1993) develop a five-level end-user framework as follows:

1 ‘Beginners’ – passive users of menu-driven systems only looking at pre-prepared information and can make no changes to the systems without help.

2 ‘Intermediate users’ – input information and create their own report from the systems available information.

3 ‘Advanced users’ – develop applications for their own use, using tools like spreadsheets and database packages.

4 ‘End-user computer facilitator’ – who although not being MIS professionals have some responsibility for acting as the organization’s MIS professional.

5 ‘End-user computer infrastructure person’ – an MIS professional.

Rainer and Harrison (1993) validated their constructs on 776 university employees, but suggested that their framework needs to be further validated across other business sectors and personnel, especially managers. Despite its limited use, it does provide a basis and set of constructs that can meet users' depth and breadth of competence. It contains some specific guidelines about the type of software each type of user would be expected to use and their level of skill. Rainer and Harrison's (1993) and Amoroso's (1992) framework is used to guide the development of the semistructured interview questionnaire used for this study, and the range of expertise represented within the sample studied.

## Range of applications used by end-users

The range of end-user software has increased significantly over the past decade, although the extent to which it is used may not have increased significantly. What has changed is the accessibility of these packages to the end-user first in terms of their availability on PCs and, second, friendlier user interfaces have enabled those who wish to develop their own applications. Several researchers mention the specific type of software packages used by end-users in general (for example Lee, 1986; Martin, 1988; Igbaria et al., 1989; Mason & Wilcox, 1991; Eindor & Segev, 1992; King et al., 1992; McKinnon & Bruns, 1992). The range includes word processing, spreadsheets, graphics and presentations, project management, diary management, specialist statistical packages, databases (both the software to build one's own application and external specialist databases) and email. The tasks for which the packages are used included communications, planning and forecasting, budgeting, problem analysis and report writing.

Spreadsheets are the most popular, being used by more users in preference to other applications even when other software might be more appropriate, for example databases and graphics packages (Lee, 1986; Igbaria et al., 1989; Mason & Wilcox, 1991). Mason and Wilcox (1991) find that the main reasons relate to the initial investment made in learning to use them. Managers are reluctant to invest more time to learn another application when they can do what they want with a spreadsheet, regardless of whether or not it is the most efficient way to accomplish the task.

(1992); Vandenbosch and Higgins (1995) and Allison (1996) for EIS and ESS, and Martin (1988) for DSS]. ## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## Theoretical background

The study is designed to look both at the quantitative aspects, such as hard facts about the specific tasks for which executives use PCs and the type of applications they use, and the qualitative aspects, such as the processes they go through to develop their expertise and the context within which they work. Just as any study on the nature of managerial work needs to take account of the context within which the executive works, so too should any study of their use of the computer (as stressed by previous researchers including Mason & Mitroff, 1973; Boland & Hirschheim, 1987; Galliers & Land, 1987; Lyytinen, 1987).

A critical issue for these researchers is to find a way of understanding the executive use of computers from the executives' perspective. A fundamental characteristic of the qualitative approach is 'its express commitment to viewing events, actions, norms, values, etc., from the perspective of the people who are being studied' (Bryman, 1992, p. 61). An interpretivist style of research derived from the principles of grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Strauss & Corbin, 1990) provides the opportunity to explore the unknown and develop theories and frameworks that might otherwise remain opaque to the purely quantitative researcher who relies solely on an empirical positivist approach. Walsham (1995) identifies three uses of theory within an interpretivist case study, as an initial guide to the design and data collection phase, as part of the iterative process of data collection and as part of the final product of the research, as exemplified by Orlikowski (1993). This research adopts this approach.

## Case studies versus interviews

A number of researchers cite the need to use case studies more extensively in IS (Benbasat et al., 1987; Galliers & Land, 1987; Lee, 1989). One of the major strengths of the case study technique (Benbasat et al., 1987; Galliers & Land, 1987; Lee, 1989; Yin, 1989; Bryman, 1992) is the richness of the data that can be collected using a variety of techniques. The main disadvantage is that one is often restricted to a very few organizations and hence a small sample size. Although much may be learnt about a specific set of executives in specific organizations, generalization of the results can be limited (Bryman, 1992).

Access and continuity too can be a problem in general (Bryman, 1988) and in particular to senior executives (Mintzberg, 1973; Martin, 1986; McKinnon & Burns, 1992). Interviewing has the advantage of enabling a larger sample from different organizations to be studied and hence more generalization, while at the same time still allowing for exploration to take place. The issue of access is still a problem, but a major advantage of the interview method is that one is asking for less time commitment, which, given the busy working schedules of senior executives, is an advantage. Although interviewing is fraught with problems (Bryman, 1988, 1992; Burgess, 1993), on balance it was felt to be the most appropriate for this study.

A large sample comprising a good spread of users across organizations and functions was chosen because it should allow more generalization of the results [as Martin (1988) and McKinnon and Bruns (1992) found], and enable any organizational and functional effects to be assumed away.

## Capturing user's depth, breadth and frequency of use

Previous authors (Stewart, 1967; Mintzberg, 1973) comment that executives can be poor at estimating the time they spend on an activity and the way in which they undertake that activity. In the specific case of systems use, researchers often find a mismatch between self-reported frequency of use and objective use as measured from computer records (Straub et al., 1995). To try to obtain more accurate measures of use, for example from either direct observation or computer-generated logs of systems use, would not have been appropriate for this study and may have been seen as both an intrusion of privacy and a manifestation of mistrust. Other researchers dealing with senior executives' use of computers adopt self-report measures for similar reasons (Martin, 1988; Boone, 1991; King et al., 1992; McKinnon & Bruns, 1992).

The results of the data analysis are therefore based on a final sample size of 85 senior executives drawn from 16 organizations, as shown in Table 2. ## Length of time as a user and frequency of use

Half (50.1%) have been using computers in some form or another for over 10 years, whereas 23.5% have used one for between 5 and 10 years. Of the seven who currently did not use one in the office, three have never used a computer during their entire working life. The other four have used a computer at some point, albeit either as an integral part of a previous job, for example research scientist, or for social purposes, but not as a management tool.

The question of frequency of use was open-ended; a considerable variety of responses were obtained, from the broad ‘occasionally’ to the more precise response of ‘about 30–45 min per day’. Some who did not have any type of portable PC talked about using PCs only when they were in the office, which in most cases was not every day. Others felt frequency was more about instances of use and queried what one counted as use, as exemplified by this quote from executive F1:

‘… it’s not about hours, it’s about instances of usage. I (may) get 15 communications that come to me in a day with attachments. . . . Do I count the usage as sitting there reading off the screen? I doubt that at the absolute maximum I would spend two hours in a day . . .'

## Purpose of use and number of computers

Nearly half (43.5%) have two computers, usually a combination of either two desktops (one at home and one in the office) or a desktop and laptop or hand-held device such as a personal organizer. Of the nine executives who do not use a PC at work, three use one at home for work-related tasks.

## Level of expertise

Although the range of software used and frequency of use provide some picture of the executives' expertise with the computer, they give only a superficial understanding of executives' level of expertise; for example, if one compares two executives who use only one package, is it realistic to regard the level of competence of the one whose only use is email as equal to that of the one whose only use is a spreadsheet, especially if the latter has developed his or her own spreadsheet? Rainer and Harrison's (1993) end-user framework was used to guide sponsors when selecting executives for interviewing, as discussed previously. Using the data analysis procedures described previously, four categories of user are identified from the executives in this study, as shown in Table 6.

Initially, executives were assigned to a category of user based on the sponsor's description. Once all the interviews had been analysed, this assignment was then reviewed, and in about $10\%$ of the cases the executive was reassigned to a different user group. In the following sections the characteristics of each user group is described.

## Novice user

Novice users typically use only email and/or a traditional mainframe system or, when it is available, an EIS. They probably read and respond to their email when they are in their office and may access it remotely. Their frequency of use may be daily and they may well log in and out several times a day. 'wherever I am (within the organization) I log in somewhere . . . Most of them (emails) I do. . . . Just occasionally if I'm doing some dictation and I'll say, oh, can you send that out. . . . 'I am okay most of the time on the standard system I have slightly more difficulty with an attachment but I grope my way through.'

## The intermediate user

Typically, the intermediate user uses up to four packages, which include spreadsheets and/or some form of word processing, as well as email when it is available. More often they predominantly use just one or two packages, regardless of their suitability. For example, although they might prepare their own presentations, this is usually carried out using either word processing or spreadsheet software as opposed to specialist graphics software that an advanced user would use. They may prepare long documents and hand them over to their secretaries for completion and distribution. The depth of their expertise with the software they use is less than that of the more experienced users, and in some cases might be described as superficial. Executive D1 says:

‘…I think that for every package that I’ve ever worked on, I probably only know 20% of the package. There’s 80% locked away inside that I’ve never needed to know, therefore I’ve not bothered. Whereas if I was better I would try and find out how to get the extra benefit.’

The software they use often depends on the purpose of use and what they first learned to use. For example, one executive who is the deputy head of regulations for a public utility has to spend much of his time writing reports and predominantly uses word processing. For others, for example executive D7, the spreadsheet is the dominant software used to ‘store and manipulate data which I receive’, and word processing is ‘the next step’.

Frequency of use may be daily, especially when there is some form of email. However, in most cases, software other than email is used on a less regular basis, and, like novices, executives often look to colleagues for help. Some see using PCs as a hassle, as exemplified by B5:

‘One of the difficulties that I’ve always found with computers is this printer interface, it seems to be almost black art. It isn’t always as straightforward as you would think, it always seems to be quite a hassle.’

Others are more inclined to talk enthusiastically about their use of PCs, often obtaining some enjoyment from what they do, for example executive M7 says,

‘It’s fun . . . I find it creative. . . . It’s quite stimulating.’

## The advanced user

At the next level of competence there are those who make more regular and extensive use of computers and use a wider range than email, spreadsheets, word processing and traditional MIS systems. A rough dividing line would seem to be around the use of graphics and presentation software. Advanced users emphatically use these, whereas the intermediate users may not. Advanced users may also be more inclined to access external commercial databases than intermediate users.

Executive D10 (group personnel director) is typical of an advanced user. He produces all his own job offer letters by ‘cannibalizing existing documents’, for example contracts that he has on his PC. He feels he is now slightly more computer literate than his secretary, which he says is ‘embarrassing’. He still hand writes memos and dictates from time to time as he feels this can be quicker ‘given my level of keyboard skills’, although he is trying to improve these using a computer-based self-teach program. He constructed his own directors’ salaries databases and manpower planning models.

This group of end-users also talk about the fun and excitement they obtain from their use of PCs and are usually looking for new ways in which it can support them. Executive M1 says:

'(I'm) an enthusiastic user, simply to see what new technology can bring for you, what benefits can it bring for you, rather than sit back and say, well, I'm sure it's all very good and someone will serve it up on a plate for you. I'm willing to try out new things . . .'

As users, this group is generally able to support themselves but will call on selected colleagues when they are stuck. Executive M1 says:

‘… (I ask) people who respond quickly, who know what they are talking about, and I hate people who seem to be two pages ahead of me in the Windows handbook…’.

## Expert users

At the top of the scale there is a user who is in many ways quite different from the other users. The top two levels of users in Rainer and Harrison's (1993) framework are the ‘end-user facilitator’ and ‘end-user computer infrastructure person’. These terms do not seem appropriate for a senior executive, and the term ‘expert’ is suggested for executives at the top of the scale in terms of their competence with the computer. The way in which the expert users talk can be highlighted by these extracts from E2 and C1.

E2's main use is the corporate database, which he wrote (using a relational database application) to organize and analyse sales, reaction to enquiries and progress of projects.

‘We have all this information and that’s the best way of trying to interpret those sorts of results and draw conclusions from them.’

He finishes a document once he has started it, including any attachments.

‘… it’s often easier to send a fax or memo by typing it yourself rather than asking the secretary. That’s just simply time saving, and I have little templates of these things and I have to do minimal work, just type in a little and there’s the memo done.’

E2 does a lot of public speaking and ‘… feels uncomfortable with public speaking if somebody else has prepared the presentation for me.’ Whenever he has time he produces his own presentations using graphics software, and this is something he ‘enjoys’ ‘… it’s no more difficult than (writing memos). When you’re doing it, it looks a particular way and you think how that’s going to go and you just make the changes until you get something that you think is going to flow in a sensible way.’

Other applications he uses include: external databases, journey planning software, project and critical path analysis, reminders, to do lists and a diary and personal contact database on a palmtop. This is an executive who also repairs his own PC using specialist software utilities.

A second expert user, C1, talked about always configuring new software to his personal style, ‘… so it’s mine. In-coming email is a gorgeous red.’ Like E2, he uses a wide range of software to meet most of his own needs. Sometimes he sketches (using graphics software) what he thinks the next marketing campaign should be, ‘I’m forever getting on our marketing department’s nerves (but) they have a much better idea than if I had done it by hand’.

When it comes to support, the experts are usually totally self-sufficient and sometimes even provide support for others. Executive L4 says:

'I'm used to looking after myself on these things. . . . Occasionally I will ask someone I know who uses the program . . . These machines are not very difficult these days . . . they are amazingly forgiving compared to how they used to be.'

Like the advanced users, these expert users talked about seeing and borrowing ideas (as per D10) and nearly always spoke about PCs being fun to use. In the words of executive L4:

'...they're quite fun, and I quite like it. ... I mean I've always thought it slightly magic....

These things are so clever and I can't help as I use it to think, how does it do that? . . . You can harness this enormous calculating power. This uncanny memory which I don't have.'

Some experts had built their own computers. In the interviews, the more experienced users (advanced users and experts), perhaps not unexpectedly, came across as much more positive and excited about their use of PCs. For the more experienced user, using PCs was ‘intuitive’ and they found it hard to understand why others found PCs so difficult. Some were scornful of their non-user peers, for example executive M1:

‘… if they (the non-users) have budget accountability, they are a failure if they can’t work a basic spreadsheet. It’s just arrant nonsense for somebody to say I’m manager of 10 million pound project spend, and I’ve got a rubber and a pencil, and a trusty calculator. They ought not to be unleashed on that sum of money, that degree of control, if they can’t work in a reasonably modern fashion.’

## DISCUSSION

## Level of expertise – a proposed end-user framework for executives

Initially, it was thought that frequency of use and breadth (range) of software might give some indication of user type; novice users might make only occasional use of computers, in contrast to expert users, who would be expected to use theirs daily. With the proliferation of email, frequency of use must be looked at in terms of how frequently users use their PC for applications other than email, and possibly for what tasks they use their email. The range of packages on its own is not detailed enough information, especially when one of those packages is email. Some users may use several packages but at only a superficial level, whereas others may use a few packages and be quite expert at them.

## Executive end-user cube

Cotterman and Kumar's (1989) end-user cube describes users in terms of their control over the MIS resource, involvement in the development of applications and operation of a system. From this research a user cube based on frequency, range (breadth) of software and depth of use is proposed, as shown in Figure 3. At the far extreme, each group of users corresponds to one of the nodes. Clearly, most activity takes place on plane B, and users can be placed at points along the various axes. For example, F1, who makes multiple applications of his email, would be characterized as operating along the line 1,0,0–1,1,0, that is somewhere between being an absolute novice and a true intermediate user. M1 is probably operating somewhere between an advanced user (1,0,1) and the true expert at (1,1,1), whereas D1 is somewhere along the 1,1,0–1,0,1 line, in that, as he says, he is only using a small percentage of the functionality of the software he uses. The cube provides a model to categorize users in terms of their current expertise (depth and breadth of software used) and frequency of use and hence, as appropriate, the level to which they might further develop their expertise, and what skills and investment in time would be required to move up to this level.

This study shows that Rainer and Harrison's (1993) framework can be used broadly to describe other groups of workers in other businesses and nationalities, and in particular senior executives from large organizations, with the following two main modifications.

First, in the case of novice users, use should include interactively handling their own email, at least in terms of producing short replies. ## CONCLUSIONS

This study revealed that executives are making more extensive personal use of computers than has been noted previously: they are using a wider range of software and are more competent than previously noted, although there is evidence that the range of software used is related to age. Younger executives make use of a wider range of software than their older counterparts. The chi-square value of 11.52 indicates that this relationship is significant (P < 0.10). Based on the previous work of Cotterman and Kumar (1989) and Rainer and Harrison (1993), an ‘executive end-user cube’ was developed, as shown in Figure 3, which accommodates each of these broad groups of executive end-users.

## Limitations of this research

The main weaknesses of this study are as follows. First, interviewee bias – in some cases undoubtedly the researchers were being told what the executive felt they wanted to hear. Second, executives are poor estimators of how they spend their time and how proficient they are. Third, within the field of IS there has been some debate about the reliability and validity of self-reporting measures of computer use as opposed to electronic measures. However, as there seems to be some degree of consistency and continuity over what the executives reported, this was not regarded as a serious problem.

## Contributions of this study

This study has made the following contributions to the field of both management and IS research. The existing knowledge base of end-user computing, and specifically that of the executive as an end-user, has been extended. This research provides a further example of how interpretive and qualitative research methodologies can be used successfully within the IS field. An end-user framework that has practical applications for senior executives in a wide range of organizations has been developed (Seeley & Mordue, 1996). Finally, it has highlighted some areas for further research. For example, how does their use of change over time; what factors promote and conversely inhibit executive's personal use of computers; and how appropriate are the current end-user computing models to the executive end-user?
