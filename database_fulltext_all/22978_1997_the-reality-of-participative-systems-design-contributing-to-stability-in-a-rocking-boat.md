# The reality of participative systems design: contributing to stability in a rocking boat

Enid Mumford

Manchester Business School, email: em@enid.u.net.com

Abstract. This paper suggests that participative design will only be accepted by industry if it can be shown to contribute both to greater efficiency and to more satisfied and productive employees. It compares the introduction of socio-technical systems into US, Japanese, Scandinavian and British industry and suggests that this approach was used with most enthusiasm in those countries that had a shortage of labour.

Keywords: Participation, socio-technical, business process re-engineering, work organization

## IS THERE A NEED FOR PARTICIPATIVE DESIGN?

The question raised by this paper is ‘why isn’t participative systems design more popular?’. It gives practical form to the philosophy of humanism by providing employees with an opportunity to influence the work systems that surround them. It also provides them with an improved work environment in which they are encouraged to increase their skills, make judgements and take decisions. It should therefore be good for democracy and good for efficiency. By reducing frustrations and grievances, it should also increase organizational equilibrium and control. This could be a very important gain in today’s turbulent world. One of the most pressing problems for today’s managers is providing their companies with a degree of stability in times of rapid, continuing and multiple change.

Zygmunt Baumann (1994) contends that one of today's great divides is that between order and chaos, in particular the divide separating the regular, predictable and controllable from the contingent, erratic, unpredictable and hard to control.

Baumann argues that the world is less solid than it used to be. It has lost its unity and continuity. He says:

We now know that all things, no matter how solid they may seem, are not immune to change. Things burst into attention without warning and then sink into oblivion. Time is cut into episodes, each one with a beginning and an end but without a prehistory or a future. The world we live in appears to be marked by fragmentation, discontinuity and inconsequentiality.'

The hypothesis behind this paper is that participative design, if it is going to be used in industry, must be seen to reduce risk by contributing to organizational stability in ways that are recognized by management. It has to have a purpose and a benefit that pays dividends and increases profits. Few companies will introduce it on the purely ethical grounds that it is beneficial for employees. A reduction of risk and an increase in stability could be seen as worthwhile rewards.

## HOW PARTICIPATIVE DESIGN BEGAN

Participative design can take two forms – the participation of employees in the development of a new work system and the opportunity for them, as members of small groups, to make choices and take decisions when operating that system. Many approaches, including the author's own, will include both of these.

A seminal paper by Robert E. Cole examines the factors that led firms in the United States, Sweden and Japan to move towards small group work structures and worker participation during the period 1960–80 (Cole, 1985). In the United States the ‘human relations’ movement of the 1960s and 1970s stimulated a discussion on the need to humanize work. There was a belief that the small, restricted jobs that had emerged from Taylorism were demotivating employees and preventing them from realizing their intellectual potential. Companies could make better use of their labour forces. This debate involved primarily social scientists and some managers, for few American trade unions showed any interest in work restructuring. The trade unions wanted the clarity of defined jobs with known grades and pay levels to facilitate equitable pay bargaining. There were also no powerful incentives for managers to make major changes. The US had a large pool of unemployed labour and there were few problems stemming from labour shortages.

Participative design in the United States was never widely accepted but restricted to a few far-sighted and well-documented firms (Davis & Cherns, 1975). There was little pressure for its general acceptance. The exception was the automobile industry, in which Japanese competition was rapidly increasing. Because of this external threat, General Motors and the United Automobile Workers Union jointly set up a number of experimental groups based on multiskilling and participative decision taking.

The situation was very different in Sweden. Here, relations between management and trade unions were co-operative, with the unions having considerable power. But Sweden, along with other Scandinavian countries, also had severe labour shortages. Few people were prepared to undertake low-level routine work, and increasing standards of education were making the situation even more difficult.

In the late 1960s, numerous efforts were made to develop new work structures with an emphasis on autonomous, self-managing groups. Norsk Hydro was a pioneer in Norway, and in Sweden Volvo was one of the firms most affected by the labour shortage. The Volvo experiments with a ‘docking’ system in which groups of workers assembled an entire car are well known (Lindholm & Norstedt, 1975; Berggren, 1992). Swedish mangers were influenced by the ideas of the Tavistock Institute in London and by the experiments in industrial democracy that had been taking place in Norway. There was an International Quality of Working Life Committee, of which the author was a member, chaired by Einar Thorsrud, a leading Norwegian academic. Both academics and trade unions were encouraging this approach with the unions, led by Kristen Nygaard another Norwegian pioneer, providing training courses and textbooks so that workers could play a more influential role when decisions about work and technology were being taken (Bjerknes et al., 1987).

In the 1960s, the Swedish Employers' Confederation took a high-level decision to support small group activities on the shop floor. This policy was also supported by the trade unions and, as a result, many examples of participative work groups appeared and flourished. Although a democratic and humanistic ideology supported these initiatives, the principal reason for the rapid spread of multiskilling and small group structures was the management desire to overcome the labour shortage (Jenkins, 1975).

These initiatives began to fall away in the 1970s. Sweden was now experiencing economic problems, and the trade unions were pressing for an extension of worker control, which managers did not welcome.

Japan too was influenced towards small groups and employee participation by severe labour shortages. Here, as in Sweden, there was a tight labour market and rapidly rising educational standards. Work restructuring made industrial employment more attractive to well-educated young men, for women were rarely employed. Unlike the Swedes, the Japanese, although very committed to good human relations, were not striving towards greater industrial democracy (Locke, 1996).

Robert Cole points out that the movements towards greater participation took different forms in all three countries. In Sweden and Japan, the principal reason was to reduce the increasing risks of a tight labour market. In Sweden, this was reinforced by a democratic ideology, and in Japan by a belief in the importance of good human relations, of which communication with every level of the enterprise played an important part. However, management was everywhere in charge and took the decision to change, although in Sweden academics and trade unions participated in the discussions. Trade unions had little influence in Japan and were not interested in these kinds of reforms in the US. In all three countries another inducement for management was the possibility of higher performance and improved economic results (Cole, 1985).

Cole draws a number of conclusions from these examples. The first is that the change in all three countries was initiated by management to solve management problems, with participative design seen as contributing to a solution. The future of companies was being threatened by the instability of a vanishing labour force. Trade unions rarely took the initiative (although there were some examples of this in Sweden, where one of the author's projects was financed by a union), as unions usually found that they did not have the necessary resources to give practical assistance to their members.

Cole points out that, if a national business leadership becomes committed to a particular solution to a problem, and if the solution is uncontested by other groups, it will spread rapidly. Business process re-engineering is an excellent example of this trend.

PERSONAL EXPERIENCE IN THE 1970s

The author's own experience can provide some understanding of what happened in the UK. Her first contact with participative design took place in the early 1970s. She was then asked by a Manchester company, Turners Asbestos Cement (TAC), to provide some assistance with a move from batch to on-line data processing, which it was proposing to implement in its sales department (Mumford, 1981). The systems group there wished to reduce the risk of failure by ensuring that a new system would be acceptable to staff.

At that time, the author had a method for defining and measuring job satisfaction, and she thought it would be helpful to discover the frustrations staff were experiencing with the current organization of work (Mumford, 1995). She went round the department talking to staff about their problems and at the same time asking them to complete a questionnaire. When the questionnaires were returned, she analysed these and gave the results to the systems group. They, in turn, designed a system that they believed would meet both technical needs and human needs and they called a meeting to describe their solution.

This led to one of the most influential and insightful moments in the author's career. The sales clerks listened politely to the systems analysts' proposed solution, but at the end there was silence. Then a senior clerk rose to his feet, thanked the systems group for their interesting contribution and said that the clerks too had been thinking about how their work might be restructured. He offered to describe this to the meeting. The offer was accepted and he gave a clear account of how the department might be reorganized around the new computer system. He suggested that the existing system of giving some clerks the boring task of inputting routine data while others had the interesting task of solving quite difficult sales problems should be abandoned. In its place there should be a skill enhancement approach, with all staff taking responsibility for data input and problem solving. As new clerks gained in knowledge and experience, they would handle increasingly difficult problems until all staff could deal with any task the sales office required. He pointed out that all the clerks would welcome such a system.

The systems group recognized the strength of the clerks' proposal, and it was their organizational reforms that were implemented. These proved to be a success, and the department became a showcase for TAC, with the manager frequently taking visitors round his new, model department.

The author learnt a most valuable lesson from this experience: one that greatly influenced her subsequent career. This is that most groups, given the opportunity, can make major improvements to their own work situations if they are motivated and allowed to do so.

This learning experience was reinforced for the author by her next participative design experience, which was to assist the regeneration of a purchase invoice department in Rolls Royce Aero Engine Division in Derby (Mumford & Henshall, 1979). Here, too, a more advanced on-line computer system was being introduced, but this time into a department where morale was low, the labour force was old and inefficient, and working there was avoided by other employees because of its poor reputation. The systems designer in charge of this project was well aware of all these problems and believed that a participative design approach, together with new technology, might contribute to solving them. In terms of the author's view of how participative design should be carried out, this turned out to be a model project.

All groups in the company that were involved in the project gave it their active support. The clerks chose a number of their members to form a user design group that the departmental manager agreed could work on its own provided the members kept him informed of what was taking place. A senior clerk was elected chairman and the author acted as facilitator. A steering committee was also created and included the senior manager responsible for purchasing activities, the IT director, the personnel director and the senior trade union official, who had a full-time responsibility for union affairs.

The author's then embryonic ETHICS methodology was used as a tool for helping the design group to think through its work problems and needs. This gave some structure to discussions and also helped speed up the design process. All employees were involved in two stages of ETHICS: the identification of work problems and the analysis of job satisfaction needs. An important task for the design group was always keeping their departmental constituents informed of how the design process was developing.

Everything worked smoothly and according to plan. The design group decided to prepare three organizational alternatives: a job enrichment programme to enhance the existing situation; a new system based entirely on autonomous groups, each responsible for a group of customers; and a less extreme approach that was half-way between the other two. They first described these to the Steering Group, whose response was that any of them would be an improvement on the existing system. They then asked the clerks as a group to choose the one they preferred. This final decision was taken at a meeting of all department staff, chaired by the trade union official. Each option was described by the clerks, discussed and then voted on. The final choice was the half-way one, based on small multiskilled groups, but with one group responsible for routine work. This was created because a group of clerks did not want to become multiskilled. They preferred the security of routine work.

It is worth mentioning here that, in the author's experience, groups lower down the organizational hierarchy, such as clerks, will usually, although not invariably, opt for a form of work redesign that leads to the creation of multiskilled jobs. They do this because these provide a degree of challenge and opportunities for decision taking. Involving users in design processes provides them with an opportunity to enrich their work in this way if they wish to do this.

Two years after the system was implemented the author went to check on the results. She found that most of the clerks had become multiskilled, the department had increased both its efficiency and its level of morale and employees from other departments were now anxious to work there. Everyone was pleased with the results. But there was one unexpected consequence: the department had lost some of its best staff. Other managers were so impressed by the knowledge and versatility generated by the new form of organization that they were offering purchase invoice clerks higher grades to move to their departments. The departmental manager had to find some strategies to prevent this.

The author has always regarded the Rolls Royce experience as an example of a good participative design project. All groups, from the trade union to senior management, co-operated in helpful and appropriate ways. The clerks were able to achieve what they regarded as an improved work structure and were assisted in this by both the senior management and the trade union. It seemed to be a ‘win win’ situation in which every interest group had gained something. Despite this apparent success, it was perhaps a sign of the times that the author’s approach was described by an American academic as a subversive method for achieving management interests while, in reality, working against the interests of oppressed clerks.

Other participative design projects in the 1970s took a similar form. These were located in a number of banks, divisions of ICI, a brewery and a number of civil service departments (Mumford, 1981). With the exception of one project in ICI plastics division, all were successfully implemented. In the ICI case, the division was closed down before change was introduced.

There were a number of reasons for this degree of success. First, the initiative to try participative design came, in most cases, from the IT department, whose analysts were anxious to avoid risk and secure stability and approval by being seen as creating and implementing successful systems. At the time, the departmental managers in these firms knew little about computers and their effects and were quite happy for their staff to be exerting an influence on the final outcome. The clerks, although sometimes not entirely trusting the motives of management, were pleased to be able to influence the new systems as they believed this increased their future security. Also, many of the senior managers knew about the Scandinavian experiments and were happy to try similar exercises.

Another important factor was that these early systems were not threatening. Most did not require any major staff reductions, and firms were able to guarantee that job loss would occur only through natural wastage. From the author's point of view, they were not difficult to manage. Participation encouraged good relations and co-operation between the interested parties. The users were not frightened of possible redundancy or of undesirable changes to their jobs. The major factor with a question mark over it was usually whether the new technical system would work effectively once introduced.

But in the UK, as in the United States, management tended to regard these successful projects as interesting one-offs, and there was no great enthusiasm or motivation to spread the approach widely though their companies.

## INCREASING COMPLEXITY AND UNCERTAINTY

At the beginning of the 1980s the participative design situation changed dramatically for the author (Mumford & MacDonald, 1989). She was invited to the United States by the Digital Equipment Corporation to give some seminars on participative design to its staff. Present at one of these was Bruce MacDonald, whom Digital had recently hired as project manager for one of its first ventures into artificial intelligence. For many years Digital, like many computer manufacturers, had had a configuring problem. Large computers are extremely complex machines with a considerable variety of cables and components. It is quite common for some of these to be missed out at the assembly stage, and customers then receive new systems that do not work. These omissions occurred in the manufacturing plants, and an expert system called XCON (expert configurer), developed by Carnegie Mellon University, had recently been installed. This provided a graphic output displaying the components that were required and how these fitted together. Although this assisted the assembly process, it did not remove all the configuring errors as many of these occurred in the sales offices. Sales people had to provide customers with machine configurations for two purposes: first, to give them an accurate account of how much their new system would cost and, second, to send an accurate order to manufacturing stating exactly what the customer required.

The configuring problem was worsened by the replacement of salespeople with engineering knowledge by a group whose knowledge was primarily business. Digital believed that a knowledge of a customer's business would increase sales. Sales may have gone up as a result of this policy, but so did customer complaints. Customers would refuse to pay extra for items that had been omitted from the original specification and there were law suits for breaches of contract. The result was a considerable loss of money for Digital and a deterioration in customer relations.

To address this problem, the Digital engineering department decided to build a second expert system to act as an aide-mémoire for the sales force. This was called XSEL (expert seller). It was decided that a prototype would be commissioned from Carnegie Mellon and then Digital would take over the building of the system. As there was little expertise available on expert systems at this time, the engineering manager decided that he would look for someone with good management skills to control the project. He selected Bruce MacDonald, an ex-Boston headmaster who knew little about computers but had a wealth of management knowledge through his experience in running a large school.

Bruce moved into the job with some apprehension. He knew that the Digital sales force was a very powerful group, dedicated to selling. He also knew that they were intolerant of untested new tools and were likely to throw new software into a wastepaper basket if it was not immediately efficient and successful.

Bruce attended one of the author's participative design workshops and decided that a participative approach might be the answer and could reduce the risks of failure. If the sales force played a major part in the development of the software for XSEL, they were likely to produce something they would consider useful. They would also become identified with the product and see it as belonging to them, not to the engineering department.

This was the route that Bruce proposed to take, although there were immediate problems in starting on it. First, the Digital sales force was a very large group working in offices scattered throughout the world. Even if attention was first directed to the United States, this would still be a considerable group of people, numbering many hundreds. How could he form a design group from so many? Fortunately, Digital had an excellent email system, and so Bruce communicated with all the sales offices asking for volunteers. He hoped not to receive too many applications as he wanted to keep the design group as small as possible. He also had to manage a group of knowledge engineers who would actually build the software to the salespeople's specifications. This software would take the form of rules specifying the relationships between different components. In its final stage, XSEL had more than 15 000 rules.

This project provided an excellent example of the problems of creating large innovative systems in big companies. This was in contrast to the relative simplicity of the small UK participative design projects in the 1970s. Bruce was now required to deal with major complexity and rapid unexpected change. Most of this was due to political rather than technical factors. His role had three critical components. The first was keeping up the morale of the design group members and the knowledge engineers over a long period while ensuring that the project made steady progress. The second was protecting both of these groups from the many unexpected and destabilizing events that occurred in Digital in the 4 years it took to build the system. The third was ensuring that, throughout this period, all members of the sales force knew what was happening and were able to express an opinion. This was more a logistics than a political problem. Digital's email system had an important role in disseminating information after each design group meeting and asking for opinions and suggestions when decisions had been being taken.

The participation strategy worked well and, in the United States, the design team stayed together until well after the implementation of XSEL. But Bruce had continually to monitor and manage the boundary between the design and knowledge engineering groups and the rest of the company and protect them from major disturbances. These included the development of competing, although non-expert, systems by other groups, a change from a decentralized to a more centralized organizational structure by Digital, the possibility of a move from customer-based to more standardized computers and a continuing inability to find a sponsor for XSEL in senior sales management. XSEL had always been financed as a development project by the engineering department. Although the involvement of the sales people in the building of XSEL made them quite enthusiastic about the system, this enthusiasm never included the vice-president of sales, a post that rotated with some frequency in Digital. The occupants knew little about XSEL except that they would eventually have to pay for it. Their goal was selling computers, and it was someone else's problem to ensure these were in good working order when sold. The were not prepared to devote time, attention and money to a new initiative called artificial intelligence.

XSEL was eventually introduced into the US sales offices with some initial success and, according to Digital, its accuracy did save a great deal of money. It was never accepted in Europe, partly because the European sales offices had played no part in its design and had developed manual methods for handling the configuring problem. Even in America, its use gradually died out after a number of years. Sales people found that the old inaccurate methods of mental configuring were quicker than waiting for XSEL to plod its way through its 15 000 rules. XSEL fell into disuse because it did not meet the need of the sales force for fast information and because it was not relevant to the sales people's overriding goal of ‘selling machines’.

By using a participative approach Bruce hoped that he would prevent the rejection of XSEL by a conservative sales force. In this he was correct, because the design group did enjoy examining their configuring problems and developing software to overcome these. He also reinforced the spirit of co-operation and enhanced the performance of XSEL by giving the new system to the sales offices to test out as it moved through different stages of its development. But, while avoiding a major risk, he did experience, and have to cope with, a series of unanticipated and mostly unavoidable risks during the development stage. However, there was also a positive consequence. The success of the participative approach persuaded Digital to make user participation a regular feature of all innovative projects.

Other projects in the 1980s, although not as large as that undertaken at Digital, involved groups at a higher level than clerks, for example the CNNA (Centre for Academic Awards), set up by the UK government to ensure that new universities achieved sufficiently high standards, involved all its staff in a project to identify information needs for university degree monitoring and assessment.

## THE RISE AND FALL OF BUSINESS PROCESS RE-ENGINEERING

At the beginning of the 1990s, multiskilling and teamworking suddenly got a large boost in publicity. At this time, industry, and particularly American industry, was becoming very nervous about changes that were taking place in its market environment. Large firms such as Boeing and McDonnell Douglas were joining together in an effort to become global duopolies; others were negotiating cross-border mergers. In parallel with this increase in size and power was an opposite trend, that of downsizing, with many large companies shedding labour and also outsourcing some of their functions. These two apparently contradictory strategies were taking place in parallel. Economists suggested that companies that could establish a global monopoly would do so, whereas everyone else had to cut costs to survive.

Hamish Macrae has argued in The Times that changes in the size of companies are occurring because of an increase in the scale of risks (McRae, 1996). A second factor is the need for firms to maintain a critical mass of clever people within the company. A third reason, in his view, is marketing. Customers today are bombarded with so much information that it is difficult for them to make choices. Any company that can provide a variety of products and services then has an edge over the others. It can provide ‘one-stop shopping’. Competitive advantage comes from retaining this core while establishing effective relations with subcontractors.

A group of Boston consultants, Michael Hammer, James Champy and Tom Davenport, recognized that these changes were taking place and suggested that they could be facilitated by using a new approach called business process re-engineering (BPR) (Hammer & Champy, 1993). This required companies to regard their activities as sets of processes directed at getting an activity, or a product, through a number of linked stages, for example from the receipt of an order to the provision of a particular product or service. The Boston recommendation was that this restructuring, which wherever possible should be based on streamlined process teams containing a wide variety of expertise, should be accompanied by the introduction of sophisticated new technology. Although increased efficiency was the primary objective, this reorganization, when associated with advanced information technology, would enable considerable staff saving to take place both at middle management level and lower down the company. The Boston consultants added a threat to their recommendations for change. They said that firms that did not take this drastic approach risked bankruptcy.

American firms and, somewhat later, British firms reacted with a mixture of fear and enthusiasm and embarked on what today are called ‘slash and burn’ exercises. Old methods of work were abandoned and new ones introduced, while at the same time large numbers of staff were made redundant. For the first few years everything seemed to be going well. Managers described their companies as ‘lean and healthy’ and looked forward to a rise in their share price.

Sociotechnical consultants were dismayed that their long-standing strategy to persuade industry to make more use of collaborative team methods of working had apparently been hijacked by the business process re-engineers.

But, increasingly, evidence appeared showing that many companies were experiencing serious problems, especially with employee morale. Surveys carried out by management consultants and journal editors showed how opinion was changing from initial euphoria to increasing doubts about what was taking place (Mumford, 1997). The coup de grâce came at the beginning of 1996, when one of the three BPR pioneers, Tom Davenport, wrote an article in a journal called Fast Company. This was entitled ‘The Fad that Forgot People’, and it described how the writer had become increasingly disillusioned with the ways companies were using BPR (Davenport, 1996). He began the article in the following way:

're-engineering didn't start out as a code for mindless bloodshed. It wasn't supposed to be the last gasp of Industrial Age Management.'

Criticizing the association of re-engineering with cost reduction through lay-offs, he described how the re-engineering genie quickly turned ugly. He said it had become:

‘so ugly that today, to most people in the United States, re-engineering has become a word that stands for restructuring, layoffs, and too often failed change programmes . . . The rock that re-engineering foundered on is simple: people. Re-engineering treated the people inside companies as if they were just so many bits and bytes, interchangeable parts to be re-engineered.’

At the end of the article he emphasized:

'In late 1995, re-engineering isn't dead; it is effectively over... Companies can use many different approaches to do process improvement without ever embarking on a high risk engineering project. ... When the Next Big Thing in management hits try to remember the lessons in re-engineering. Don't abandon all your ongoing approaches in favour of the handsome newcomer.'

At the end of 1996, it seemed that Michael Hammer had also seen the problems. An article in the Wall Street Journal, headed ‘Reengineering Gurus Take Steps to Remodel Their Stalling Vehicles’, described how his training courses now include ‘people’ issues (White, 1996). And there was talk of abandoning the term re-engineering and replacing it with a softer description, such as ‘business transformation’ or ‘organizational agility’. In the Wall Street Journal, Hammer admitted to his blind spot, saying:

'I wasn't smart enough . . . I was reflecting my engineering background and was insufficiently appreciative of the human dimension. I've learned that's critical.'

## RETHINKING THE FUTURE

The sociotechnical group in the United States seized on this debacle as a new opportunity to market what it had to offer. The members had 50 years' experience in designing for organizations and for people, and it seemed that the economic climate had made companies seriously interested in redesign. Sociotechnical design no longer concentrated on the human development objective of trying to make work more interesting for frustrated employees. It was directed at rethinking the structure of the enterprise to make it more responsive to its environment, more attractive to customers and more effective at selling its products. This seems to be where we are now. But what does a sociotechnical strategy today require? Here are some questions that need to be answered (Mumford, 1996).

First, what is industry going to be like in the next century? Today's pundits are anticipating that firms will have a small core of highly paid permanent specialists, a larger group of less skilled staff on short-term contracts, many functions outsourced to subcontractors and customers who want product variety and a fast response to their needs (Beck, 1992; Handy, 1994). This scenario, which although the received wisdom of the moment may turn out to be wrong, presents difficulties for sociotechnical designers. In the 1960s and 1970s their principal role was seen as on the shop floor, helping operatives to have more interesting and challenging jobs. Their role today will require them to work at all levels of the firm. They will be helping the valued specialized employees not to achieve more challenge, but to experience less stress and avoid burnout. With temporary staff, the aims will be to help them achieve greater job security through acquiring skills that enable them to move around freely in the labour market. This will mean a focus on education and training.

This will certainly be the pattern in some firms, but how universal will it be? British companies have always experienced skill shortages, and there seems little chance of this situation changing in the future. In addition, outsourcing is not always an ideal solution: firms may lose control of important functions and have difficulty in maintaining standards, whereas employees hired from subcontractors will almost certainly prove expensive and could owe little loyalty to their temporary employers. Yet the importance of these relationships requires that they are built on a high level of trust. Also, a constantly shifting group of insecure employees is not likely to produce high motivation and morale. Research has shown that the best performance occurs when employees, as in Japan, have what is called ‘bounded freedom’. Staff are treated as intelligent and responsible and are provided with excellent working conditions, although there are certain rules and standards to which they are expected to conform (Bunker et al., 1996).

A more likely scenario is that there will be a variety of organizational forms and structures, including bureaucratic structures similar to those of today. These will be companies that have stable markets and that are not required to be constantly innovating. At the other extreme will be the highly flexible or ‘agile’ company operating in a highly competitive and rapidly changing market situation. This company may experience increases or reductions in size as market conditions alter and so can profit from the flexibility of having a considerable number of employees on short-term contract. It may also have co-operative relationships with other companies that it can make operational or let lapse according to the market situation. A third organizational form is likely to be the large international conglomerate that has a monopoly or a large slice of world market share. These firms will be few and powerful and their ability to influence market situations will mean that they will only change when they feel disposed to do so.

## PROBLEMS TO BE OVERCOME

What are the problems of a participative approach when systems are being designed and implemented in these new structures? The greatest difficulty has always been one of culture. Whereas Scandinavian companies have always been attracted by shared problem solving and equality of contribution, this has not been the case in the UK and USA. Here, strong hierarchical structures and fixed roles have caused managers to be reluctant to give lower level employees a greater degree of power, which is what participation implies. At senior level the same problem takes a different form. Managers do not regard playing a role in the design and implementation of a new system as their responsibility. There are technical staff available who should do this. These old attitudes are difficult to break down.

New solutions that create new problems are also working against participation. All the projects in which the author was involved allowed users to assume a considerable degree of responsibility for the design of the organizational context that would support and mesh with any technical solution.

Today we have available software that offers high technology together with what is called ‘best practice’ in the organization of work. This seems to be another ‘quick and easy’ solution to attract managers. It suggests that there is now no need to think carefully about work structure options or take account of the pressures and demands of the local environment. All that is necessary is to buy the package. Once again we see another attractive and well-marketed example of the ‘instant solution’. Yet there is little evidence that universal panaceas ever work.

Another constraining factor on participation is the increasing need for a high level of security to prevent the theft of vital company information from networked systems. This need can introduce a new set of procedures and controls that can reduce employee freedom and place restrictions on organizational restructuring options. Multiskilled work, the predicted pattern of the future, does not necessarily mean a lessening of controls. Fear of information theft and espionage may make controls tighter.

Despite these difficulties, the future of sociotechnical design seems bright. High-level multi-skilled groups are valuable commodities that can exert a great deal of power. In the XSEL project, Digital management was afraid that if the sale force were not involved in the design of the expert system they would refuse to use it. As a result, participation was seen as essential and did result in an acceptable and accepted system, even although in the long term it fell into disuse.

Today's skilled workers must be involved in the design of new systems that will affect them; they will insist on this. The big question is ‘what about the rest – the employees that are easily replaceable?’. The vast numbers of telephone- and computer-based selling and information-providing workers are examples here.

As yet, we do not know what the future will hold, and this requires sociotechnical supporters to start thinking along a number of broad fronts. In some situations, they will still require their old skills of helping employers to take account of technical, organizational and people factors, and to provide stimulating developmental work situations for their employees as well as humanistic technology and participative forms of organization. But they will also have to assist firms to reduce the new stress factors of overwork, too much responsibility and the constant pressure of changing demands that require a fast response. There is yet another group that they must try and assist. These are the growing numbers of unemployed people. They too require programmes that give them new skills and the prospects of continuing employment and a lifestyle that has a degree of security. There is also a move to suggest that the sociotechnical approach should address ecological issues. Governments should be encouraged and assisted to investigate fully and take account of people issues when new technologies and industries are being introduced.

There seems to be a vital role for sociotechnical design and sociotechnical systems in the world of the future. There is also a group available with many years of experience in designing from a sociotechnical perspective and with a humanistic value system. The problem is getting this role recognized and accepted by governments and by industry. History suggests that acceptance will not readily come about through appeals to ethics and values. Morality is often regarded as too expensive in today's world. Supporters of a sociotechnical approach must be able to demonstrate that its use leads to efficiency, profitability and a high-morale labour force. We then get the best of all possible worlds: an ethical approach and an economically successful society.

## Biography

Enid Mumford is an Emeritus Professor of the University of Manchester and a Council member of the British Tavi-stock Institute. She is the author of 26 books and many papers. Her current interests are in developing tools and methods to assist the management of technical and organizational change, and in complex problem-solving. Her most recently published books are Effective Systems Design and Requirements Analysis: the ETHICS method and Systems Design: Ethical Tools for Ethical Change. These were published in 1995 and 1996 by Macmillan. She is currently writing a book on complex problem-solving, using drugs and cyber crime as examples.
