# Trying to adopt systems development methodologies – a case-based exploration of business users' interests

Chris Sauer & Colleen Lau\*

Fujitsu Centre for Managing Information Technology in Organizations, Australian Graduate School of Management, University of New South Wales, Sydney, NSW 2052, Australia, email: C.Sauer@unsw.edu.au, and \*25 Hardy Rd, Ashfield, Perth, WA 6054, Australia

Abstract. Information systems methodologies are an important component of the IS infrastructure and a primary device for organizing systems development work. Evidence suggests that methodology adoption and use are problematic. This research seeks to generate insight into business users' interest in adoption through detailed examination of a case. A framework is developed for organizing relevant research findings. The field research methods are described and details of the case reported. The case highlights the role of business managers in methodology adoption and the influence of business pressures originating in the strategic environment. Analysis shows the organizing framework to require extension to include a more direct role for business decision makers. It is argued that previous research has obscured the legitimate concern of business with systems development methodologies. As business increasingly asserts its interest in and control over IS, it will be necessary to give greater consideration to the needs of business in the selection and adoption of methodologies.

Keywords: Information systems, methodologies, business, users, systems development, organizational form, SSADM

## INTRODUCTION

Systems development methodologies continue to be an important component of the information systems (IS) infrastructure. They contribute to the organization, discipline and control of work in IS departments; in particular, they shape the systems development and maintenance process. They are treated as a technical innovation chiefly of interest to IS managers and developers (when IS managers and developers have shared professional interests, they are referred to jointly in this paper as IS professionals). Methodology designers and vendors continually update their products so as to overcome earlier deficiencies, exploit new process technologies such as computer-aided software engineering (CASE) and accommodate new infrastructural technolologies such as client–server. The recent fashion for business process re-engineering has stimulated further developments and modifications. These extensions and developments continue to emphasize the technical character of methodologies.

The virtues of methodologies have long gone unquestioned. What they offer has seemed so unarguably desirable that to reject them outright has been tantamount to being politically incorrect. This assumption of desirability has been reflected in research that has emphasized methodology design and construction, the comparison of technical features (for example Olle et al., 1982, 1983, 1986, 1991; Maddison et al., 1983; Avison & Fitzgerald, 1993) and the selection of appropriate methodologies (Naumann et al., 1980; Davis, 1982; Burns & Dennis, 1985). The implicit assumption has been that if a methodology is well designed and is selected carefully then it will achieve what is claimed for it in terms of a better end-product, a better development process and a standardized process (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1993).

Emerging research has begun to question these assumptions (Fitzgerald, 1996). The evidence that many organizations do not use methodologies is growing (Bubenko, 1986; Friedman, 1989; Kozar, 1989; Necco, 1989; Keyes, 1992). When use does occur, it is often partial (Jenkins et al., 1984). Some researchers question how useful methodologies are (Wynekoop & Russo, 1993). Yet only a minority of methodology designers have bothered to review their own experiences (Checkland & Scholes, 1990). And when they have done so, their evidence inevitably finesse some problems of adoption because, as the methodology's designers, they start as skilled, experienced and committed users compared with the more normal case in which adopters are faced with implementing the methodology with limited preparation. Evidence from researchers without a vested interest in specific methodologies suggests that adoption and use is problematic to the point of being a project in itself (Necco et al., 1987; Downs et al., 1988; Baskerville et al., 1992; McMaster & Kautz, 1994; Wastell, 1996).

Contrary to the assumptions of those designers who see methodologies as an obviously desirable technology, empirical evidence reveals that methodologies are not adopted on their intrinsic technical merits alone but are in practice affected by organizational and behavioural factors (Zmud, 1982; Leonard-Barton, 1987; Baskerville et al., 1992; McMaster & Kautz, 1994). Baskerville and colleagues (1992) challenge the conventional wisdom on both the usability and the desirability of methodologies. They argue that methodologies cannot be used effectively because they violate the needs of post-modern forms of organization whose distinctive character is to be adapted to continual change. Whatever the merits of their argument, there are two reasons why it does not justify our ignoring the issue of methodology adoption. First, the post-modern organizational form will not apply uniformly in all sectors and all industries immediately. For many organizations change will remain modest for some years to come. Second, methodologies apparently do work to the satisfaction of many IS managers (Beck & Perkins, 1983). Thus, methodologies can be expected to continue to have relevance for the foreseeable future, and therefore research that advances our understanding of methodology adoption will be valuable.

This paper looks at the difficulties that organizations may encounter in adopting a methodology in which ‘adoption’ refers to the process that is directed to the acceptance and institutionalization of an already selected methodology. In the absence of a validated model of methodology adoption, this paper organizes a variety of relevant studies into a framework whose elements may contribute explanations for individual instances of methodology adoption. The research reported here explores the framework in the context of a case study of a government agency trying to adopt the methodology SSADM and argues the value of extending explanatory analysis to give greater emphasis to the strategic business environment and the drivers influencing business managers' preferences and behaviours.

The paper argues that existing explanations are partial in respect of the case described because prior studies have obscured the concern of business with the systems development process. It argues further that business managers have a legitimate interest in methodology adoption. This develops the insights of Baskerville and colleagues (1992) and places methodology adoption squarely in the context of the business as opposed to more traditional types of explanation that focus on the systems development task, the individual developer or a limited conception of the organizational context. In an era in which companies are seeking to gain greater alignment of the IS function with business organization and business strategy so as to exploit the business potential of IS (Brancheau et al., 1996) and in which business managers are taking greater responsibility for control of IS (Rockart, 1988), it is appropriate to recognize the legitimate role of business in decisions relating to the systems development process including methodology adoption.

This paper starts by examining existing research relevant to explaining the outcome of methodology adoption and classifying it into an organizing framework according to whether the adoption decisions of IS developers or IS managers are explained, and the different levels at which explanation is made. The case research methods are outlined and the case described. The case is analysed in terms of the role of business drivers. It is used to explore how well this kind of explanation is handled by the organizing framework. The discussion is used to highlight the tension between short- and long-term priorities in respect of systems development, and to discuss the changing balance of between them.

## RESEARCH RELEVANT TO METHODOLOGY ADOPTION

It is difficult to determine a priori how the outcomes of a particular methodology adoption process should be explained because there is no well-established theory specific to systems development methodologies. Rather, we have to look at two different sets of research. First, there are those studies that bear very closely on the success or failure of adoption (Zmud, 1982; Leonard-Barton, 1987; Kozar, 1989; Baskerville et al., 1992; McMaster & Kautz, 1994; Wastell, 1996) but which are limited either by the very narrow range of explanatory factors they study or by the smallness of their sample, often a single case. Second, there is other research that is potentially relevant because either, like the systems development selection literature (Naumann et al., 1980; Davis, 1982; Burns & Dennis, 1985; Episkopou & Wood-Harper, 1986; El-Louadi et al., 1991), it relates indirectly to the adoption of systems development methodologies or, like diffusion of innovations theory (Rogers, 1995), resistance theory (Child et al., 1987; Hirschheim & Newman, 1988) or labour process theory (Kraft, 1977; Greenbaum, 1979; Sauer, 1986), it relates to the adoption or rejection of innovations but not necessarily to methodologies. The purpose of this section is to survey and organize the findings from both sets of research so as to identify what kinds of explanation might apply to our particular case.

## Diffusion of innovations theory

The most widely known theory of individuals' innovation adoption decisions is Rogers' (1995) diffusion of innovations (DOI) theory. The theory was initially used to explain individual consumers' decisions to adopt product innovations. DOI's emphasis has been upon five product characteristics – compatibility, complexity, relative advantage, trialability and observability. Prescott and Conger's (1995) review of DOI studies of technological innovations that have impacts internal to the IS unit, such as methodologies, found a lack of consistent results for the five product characteristics.

## Stakeholder attitudes and preferences

Leonard-Barton's (1987) field research on the methodology adoption decisions of individual developers explored several types of factor, including task, individual and organizational factors. She found that the most important influences are client, systems developer and supervisors, attitudes and preferences, recent training, systems developer experience, availability of consulting and acquaintance with a methodology champion. Kozar (1989) reports a number of similar findings, although he found system developer experience to be negatively related to adoption.

## Resistance theory

Research on individuals' resistance to change bears on methodology adoption, although it has not been applied directly to this issue. Reasons often cited for individuals rejecting change are employee uncertainty about the new order and whether they will be able to cope; threatened self-interests; differing assessments of the value of the change; innate conservatism; and feelings of personal loss (Child et al., 1987; Barney & Griffin, 1992). The substantial IS literature on implementation resistance identifies these same individual-based causes of resistance plus others such as lack of involvement (Hirschheim & Newman, 1988). One study of CASE adoption also showed that both unrealistic expectations and the amount and veracity of communication lead to resistance (Wynekoop et al., 1992).

## Labour process theory

Labour process analysis of systems development has also been used to explain the outcome of the introduction of systems development process innovations. Although Braverman's (1974) original argument that management-promoted change typically deskills workers implied worker compliance, subsequent commentators have noted that in fact workers frequently resist innovations (Coombs, 1978), and subsequent studies demonstrate this (Wilkinson, 1983). This line of argument has been applied explicitly to systems development in respect of structured programming methods (Kraft, 1977; Greenbaum, 1979) and programming environments (Sauer, 1986). It is natural to extend the argument to encompass methodologies. Accordingly, labour process analysis explains the rejection of a methodology by developers as the result of methodology formalizing analysis and design work, thereby deskilling IS professionals and increasing management control. The effectiveness of developer resistance can then be seen as a function of the relative power of developers and their managers. Although Friedman (1989) cautions against uncritical acceptance of labour process explanations of innovations in the systems development process on the grounds that disciplined methods are rarely strictly applied as a means of imposing direct control, his argument does not deny the possible relevance of labour process analysis for specific cases.

## Social defence

A very different study of developers' reasons for adoption offers psychoanalytical explanation in terms of social defence against developer anxiety (Wastell, 1996). Using this argument, methodologies are a kind of security blanket for IS professionals. As such, social defence provides more by way of an explanation of why IS professionals accept methodologies than why they do not.

## Task fit models

Normative research on the selection of methodologies targets the organizational (IS management) decision to adopt. It highlights task fit, i.e. the compatibility of the methodology to the development task at hand. Such research is relevant because, to the extent that it specifies conditions that determine an appropriate choice of methodology, it implies a model of necessary conditions for successful adoption and use. The core constructs for differentiating tasks are project complexity and uncertainty (Naumann et al., 1980; Davis, 1982; Burns & Dennis, 1985). Uncertainty and complexity have also been mapped on to systems type (El-Louadi et al., 1991). Thus, prototyping is prescribed for low-complexity tasks; life cycle products are prescribed for low-uncertainty–high-complexity tasks such as transaction processing systems; and mixed methodologies are prescribed for high-complexity–high-uncertainty tasks such as executive information systems.

The appeal of the task fit approach has been considerable, prompting a number of pieces offering practical advice (Benyon & Skidmore, 1987; Jayaratna, 1988; Lyytinen, 1989; McLeod, 1992), and even attempts to build contingency methodologies (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990) or meta-methodologies (Kumar & Welke, 1992). Yourdon (1986) used task fit to provide an industry-wide explanation of the abandonment of structured methodology by organizations, noting the inappropriateness of the techniques vis-à-vis emerging alternatives and their inability to cope with complex real-time systems. Survey research has provided some validation for the contingency approach based on task fit (Saarinen, 1990; Palvia & Nosek, 1993), but such surveys have also revealed that many organizations adopt methodologies counter to normative recommendations.

## Multivariate fit

In their work on how IS managers should select a methodology, Episkopou and Wood-Harper (1986) propose a multivariate fit model according to which a development approach (methodology) should be chosen according to its fit with the problem (task) and its context (organization), with the problem-solvers or developers, and with the problem-owners or users (individuals). Although this recognizes that there are more relevant variables than just the task, their model is still very task oriented. For example, users are conceptualized only in terms of how well they will be able to perform as methodology users for the purposes of requirements elicitation.

## Post-modern organizational form and strategic environment

Baskerville and colleagues (1992) have developed an argument that the rejection of systems methodologies is to be expected because change is a critical determinant of organizational form and methodologies are unusable in organizations adapted to coping with persistent environmental change. Unlike most other studies, their argument emphasizes factors affecting the whole business organization, specifically the strategic environment and the business's responses in respect of its overall organizational form, but it confines its application of these factors to post-modern organizational forms. Fitzgerald (1996) also notes the importance of the changing nature of the business environment for methodologies but does not elaborate.

## Miscellaneous factors

A wide range of factors have been thrown up in different studies. Those which affect the individual developers' attitudes and preferences include a methodology champion (Leonard-Barton, 1987; Prescott & Conger, 1995) and centralization and formalization of the IS function (Zmud, 1982). McMaster and Kautz's (1994) idiographic study of an organization level (IS management) decision to abandon a methodology generated a range of mostly organizational factors focusing almost exclusively on the IS department. Factors they identify as influential include unfavourable project antecedents, unclear objectives, lack of management support and commitment, ill-defined roles, conservative culture, lack of fit with systems development practice, lack of commitment from users, insufficient training, inadequate feedback during adoption and lack of developer involvement in the decision to adopt.

## Summary

In the absence of a single dominant or compelling model of methodology adoption, it is possible that any of a variety of elements from the existing models may prove explanatorily useful in one particular case. As these different models are not discrete, it is helpful to draw together our survey of the research into a single organizing framework so as to make explicit what range of outcomes the research attempts to explain and what kinds of explanation it offers.

The research surveyed has focused on two types of adoption outcome: IS developers' adoption decisions and IS managers' adoption decisions. The kinds of explanation are pitched at three levels: methodology/task, individual and organizational. The methodology/task level influences relate largely to the appropriateness of the methodology to the task. The individual level influences relate to attitudes and preferences. The organizational level influences comprise a variety of factors. Table 1 summarizes our survey. The different explanatory factors discussed are listed in the table in italics under each model.

## CASE STUDY – THE STATE PROPERTY AGENCY

The case concerns the adoption of the methodology, Structured Systems Analysis and Design Method (SSADM – see CCTA, 1990), by the Information Services Division (ISD) of an Australian state government agency, the State Property Agency (SPA). The initial intention of the study was to explore some of the difficulties of using a methodology, but as soon as the researchers gained access to the site it became clear that SSADM was not fully implemented so the focus of the study was switched to the lack of success in its adoption. In this section, we briefly explain the research method before describing the situation at the SPA.

## Research method

The field research was conducted between February and October 1992. During that period one of the authors had free access to the ISD personnel at the SPA and good access to users. She was able to conduct unstructured interviews that were taped, to observe, to attend meetings, to conduct a small survey of developer attitudes and to interact informally with ISD staff. Feedback suggests that she was able to blend into the background very effectively and her status as a student probably contributed to the openness of the staff with whom she dealt (Gordon et al., 1973). Validity of the analysis has been checked by presenting the research findings to the SPA sponsor on separate occasions. A report was presented to and accepted by the sponsor at the end of the main period of field study. A follow-up visit was made to the site 2 years later.

The purpose of the data collection process was to acquire enough understanding of the conditions and events at the SPA to permit exploratory testing of the elements of the organizing framework (Lee, 1989) and to be able to develop an alternative explanation should that be warranted. In the event, explanation required an extension to the framework because the decisions of neither IS managers nor IS developers could be demonstrated to have determined the final adoption outcome. Unlike Markus (1983, 1994), we are therefore unable to use the case analysis to reject specific theories. Instead, we use it to explore the limited way that existing models would conceptualize the important business influences in the SPA case and draw our conclusions from those limitations.

## Case description

## Business background

The SPA was established in October 1984 to bring together previously dispersed responsibilities for procuring, building, managing and maintaining the state government's properties, such as office buildings, schools, workshops and warehouses. In its early years, the SPA was funded by grant from the state government's consolidated revenue. This was not a happy arrangement for the SPA's clients (specifically the heads of schools, warehouses, government departments, and other agencies and authorities occupying state government buildings), who were given nominal budgets over which they had little control. The SPA largely determined how these budgets were spent. The clients had no effective means by which to monitor their expenditure because the SPA's invoices were inaccurate or uninformative. Moreover, they felt they were receiving poor service. The SPA's applications development manager spelled out the reasons for client dissatisfaction, 'Part of it was the slowness, part of it was not getting the job done properly, part of it was the way they were being treated by the SPA people, part of it was not getting the right information on their invoices, part of it was that they didn't know how much it was going to cost before they asked for the work to be done. It just came up on an invoice and they weren't quite sure if what appeared was accurate. In many cases the wrong information actually appeared on the invoices.'

The changing climate of both business and public administration in the late 1980s and early 1990s created pressures for the SPA to change. The importance of customer focus became commonplace. So too did the idea that government should be a source of policy and expenditure decisions without being a direct provider of services. The agency experienced these pressures from a number of sources – direct from the government, from departmental clients, and from community interest groups such as schools' Parents and Citizens (P&C) Associations. This raised the fear within the SPA that if it did not adapt it might cease to exist.

## IS background

Information systems support for the SPA business functions was initially provided through contracts to external software houses and consultancies. Starting in 1984, the SPA's information systems work was carried out within its Finance Division with the assistance of consultants who introduced the methodology, Method/1. In 1986, the SPA used a different consultancy and a separate supplier of contract systems staff. Outnumbering the SPA's own information systems staff, the contractors required that they be allowed to use their own methodology, a variant of Business Systems Planning (BSP) (IBM, 1981).

In 1988, the SPA set up its own Information Services Division (ISD) with a typical portfolio of applications for a government department including many commercial systems. The systems ISD inherited were not robust and proved costly to maintain. Users in the SPA's business divisions (business users) found they had limited access to ISD resources for new developments and tended to hold ISD in low regard. Comments included:

‘we don’t like [ISD] . . . there’s a perception from me that they don’t understand my problem’

'kept on the 5th floor and in cotton wool... [and] paid overtime'.

ISD in turn had a stereotypically negative view of its users. Talking of a typical project, the applications development manager describes a familiar story:

'When we first started trying to implement it our users didn't have a really good idea of exactly what they wanted, and they wanted a system that was extremely complex, and they wanted a lot of options, and it took a lot of effort to do it, and then we discovered they weren't using most of the options, they weren't really required, it was too complex for them to use themselves, and so it's subsequently been simplified.'

ISD management wanted a methodology as a discipline for developing systems. It chose SSADM because it saw it as more complete than other methodologies and because it had the reputation of being particularly appropriate to public sector systems development. In mid-1991, project management guidelines (PMG) were issued indicating how SSADM was to be incorporated into the systems development process. According to the PMG, SSADM was preferred but not mandatory. Moreover, if selected, it was not obligatory that it be used in its entirety or that its detail be strictly observed. The quality assurance administrator was given responsibility for helping tailor SSADM to the specific needs of projects.

## IS developers' response

Individual developers' skills and experience of methodology use were varied. Over half had both a working knowledge of a methodology and some practical experience. Only a small minority admitted to little or no knowledge of them. Some training was provided initially – the majority view was that it was better on theory than practice. Consultant support was available, although not always well regarded by ISD staff. A CASE tool was made available, but there was only one copy and it was available on a machine that was not integrated into the main development system. Some developers were dissatisfied with the provision of telephone support for the CASE tool.

Survey results showed that system developers were generally in favour of or accepting of methodologies in the abstract. Interviews suggested that they were more inclined to have reservations in the SPA context, although outright opposition was rare. Even a long-term contractor who stood to lose control of his system, and hence his assurance of employment, if SSADM led to its being documented, acknowledged that the methodology had some virtues.

During the period of the field study, there was a continuing, vigorous, informal debate within ISD as to the merits of methodologies in general and SSADM in particular for the SPA's situation. Benefits in terms of improved quality, greater structure and more discipline were widely accepted:

‘the quality is what suffers most if a methodical approach is not used’

‘one of the main reasons for having any standard development/documentation tools like

SSADM is that once the system/project enters the production life cycle, additions/modifications to the system should be easier to apply as the system is documented in a standard fashion and the maintenance person can quickly grasp how the system works'

‘the success of SSADM in any organization is the strictness to [sic] which it is implemented’

‘the more structured effort that goes into the analysis/design phases, the more likely it is that the user will be satisfied and the less maintenance will have to be done’

The case against SSADM was typically articulated in terms of increased effort and time to produce a system:

'SSADM gives the impression of slow development – an analysis paralysis in the way it is used at the SPA.'

‘SSADM will increase overall costs of systems development. SSADM will increase the amount of work required in developing systems.’

‘SSADM breaks the work up into smaller pieces which are no easier to work with.’

Several developers who expressed reservations about SSADM at the SPA advocated a combination of the methodology and prototyping. A typical comment was:

'SSADM complemented with prototyping and appropriate user (customer) focus should help as long as we don't get too involved in a purely theoretical approach. Our focus as systems developers is to produce a timely product, not a product that revolves around methodology design. You need the right mix of methodology and prototyping.'

The contract developer summed up the debate:

'SSADM will increase overall costs of systems development (for me personally). SSADM will increase the amount of work required in developing systems. The increase is justified – better product documentation. The increase is not justified – for simple systems.'

## Business users' responses

When questioned, users proved less favourably disposed towards the methodology than either ISD developers or ISD managers. The following comments are indicative:

‘they [ISD] give themselves too much time’

‘the way IS people are using SSADM are [sic] slowing things down instead of speeding things up’

‘the trouble with the method . . . as soon as you say “let’s run it down the methodology” people immediately assume that it’s going to take 12 months and that it is going to involve a main-frame solution’

‘the Invoicing team [which used SSADM] have a reputation for taking a long, long time and producing very little’

'I don't care what the code looks like [as long as the product] performs to criteria of response times'

'[ISD] try to build a bells and whistles product'

‘the analysis has to be a lot more cut down’

## Role of business managers

ISD attempted to improve relations with its users by giving users control of projects within the ISD project management structure. The project manager was typically a manager from the application business area. A project leader would then be selected from ISD and made responsible to the project manager. The project manager, having direct responsibility for the successful implementation of the system, reported to a project steering committee chaired by a divisional head or senior SPA manager with a special interest in the project. One of the project manager's responsibilities was to decide whether SSADM would be used. The result was that in their role as project managers, influential users actively discouraged use of SSADM and encouraged alternative approaches which promised shorter lead-times on system delivery.

## Use of SSADM

Of seven major projects in progress in 1992, Invoicing was the only one to try to use SSADM extensively. It got as far as the requirements analysis stage. Another project had used parts of the methodology in a fast track development that had relied on evolutionary prototyping. Two had used it for initial project definition but no further. Three had made no use of it. This very limited use is reflected in comments from ISD project staff included:

'no we didn't use SSADM forms at all'

‘we’re currently in feasibility stages, haven’t used SSADM yet’

‘using techniques, but not the methodology’

‘we haven’t really decided to use it or not’

In early 1992, Invoicing, the SSADM flagship project, was halted to give priority to work on a more urgent project, Faults Invoicing. The new project was important for several reasons. It was a large-volume system producing 300 000 invoices per year, with significant associated complexity. Recent changes in the state public service had resulted in the SPA being required to fund its own activities through recharging its client departments for its services instead of being funded by government grant. This meant that users were now driven by the need to complete critical revenue-generating activities quickly. Faults Invoicing was given priority because repair of faults was a particularly regular source of revenue. SSADM was not to be used.

The quality assurance administrator's comment in mid-1992 that ‘there hasn’t been a project completed using SSADM’ remained true. Since that time ISD has pursued its goal of improving the discipline and quality of systems development by introducing function point analysis, but has not used SSADM as a full-blown methodology. Rather, it selects techniques appropriate to specific projects. According to a follow-up interview (2 years later) with the manager responsible for the original adoption decision, relations between ISD and its business users have improved.

## ANALYSIS

The case description shows that IS developers had a mixed response to SSADM, and IS managers chose not to persist, but it was business managers in their role as project managers who formally decided against the methodology. Prior adoption studies have either not encountered or not recognized cases in which business managers directly determine the outcome. Consequently, the existing organizing framework is deficient in not including this possibility within its scope. This analysis seeks to do two things. First, it provides an explanation of the rejection of SSADM with reference to the factors influencing business managers. Second, it examines the adequacy of the organizing framework in respect of these factors so as to explore how it would have handled them had the business managers not had a direct decision-making role.

## What were the influences on business managers' decisions?

Business managers' views on what they wanted from a methodology were influenced by the changes in the SPA's strategic environment. These included the political drive towards greater commercialization of business practice in state government agencies, pressure from the SPA's client agencies, and indirect pressure from interest groups such as schools' P&C groups. Changed funding processes created new organizational conditions under which the SPA had either to provide a satisfactory service to its customers or to risk organizational decline and demise. Thus, the SPA's strategic shift to increased customer focus was a key to the type of performance business managers demanded of ISD. To support this strategic reorientation, new and redeveloped systems were needed urgently in key applications, particularly those relating to revenue collection such as Invoicing and Faults Invoicing.

Business users viewed SSADM as likely to hinder rather than help ISD's provision of the systems they needed in the time frame in which they needed them. The users wanted more timely, business-focused systems; witness the emphasis in their comments on delay and failure to do what was wanted. This was different from IS management's objective, which was to bring the development and maintenance processes under tighter control, as evidenced by the IS emphasis on quality, discipline and structured process. It did not require much exposure to SSADM and the kind of development process associated with it for users rapidly to recognize that it would not help meet their business need for timely systems – ‘as soon as you say “let's run it down the methodology”, people immediately assume it's going to take 12 months’. This was not mere ignorance or narrow-minded negativity on the part of business users. IS professionals did not challenge the validity of the business's perceptions. Their debate did not question whether SSADM would deliver more timely systems – the general understanding was that it would not. The issue then did not relate to the performance characteristics of SSADM, about which there was agreement, but rather it was a matter of priorities for the systems development process.

What makes the SPA an unusual case in the research literature is that business managers were able to impose their priorities directly on the adoption process. The change in the project management structure was a response by ISD both to the new demands made by business users and to the history of user dissatisfaction. Although it served both to improve ISD's business focus and at the same time co-opt senior user managers, the cost for IS management was that it delivered power over the systems development process to users. Thus, the lack of fit between performance characteristics of SSADM and business managers' priorities for systems development resulted in SSADM's rejection.

An alternative interpretation suggests that the business managers' decisions can be explained in terms of resistance theory. Two points argue against this. First, it was precisely because the business managers were oriented to change in their business operations that they were pressing so hard for more rapid delivery of systems. Second, they did not have a satisfactory relationship with ISD. They were not seeking the continuation of existing levels of performance from ISD. They wanted improvements that necessarily implied some change. Nevertheless, whether or not there was an element of resistance, the fundamental point remains that business pressures for timely systems were not an irrational reason for their choosing against SSADM.

## How might the organizing framework have applied to this case?

As noted earlier, prior research has not included business managers' decisions within its scope, so the organizing framework is not directly applicable to this case. Even if we factor out the role of business managers, there are two difficulties in trying to use the case to thoroughly test the models included in the framework. First, there was no clear-cut decision on the part of either IS managers or developers in respect of SSADM. IS management supported the methodology but did not attempt to fight for its continued use. IS management did not itself reject SSADM, it merely went along with its rejection. Had it not done so, its attempt to improve relations with business users through the changed project management structure would have been undermined. We cannot therefore infer that IS management decided against SSADM rather its acceptance of rejection was part of a wider initiative. It is clear from the kind of debate among IS developers that they had mixed responses. Thus, we also cannot treat IS developers as though they made a clear-cut decision against SSADM. Thus, as no consensus emerged among IS professionals before adoption of SSADM stalled, we cannot readily attempt an explanation of their responses.

The second difficulty is that applying the different explanatory models in the framework to the case generates a range of influences, the strengths of which are variable and sometimes uncertain. Table 2 summarizes the variable support the data lend to the models in the framework. Moreover, the absence of support for any part of the framework does not invalidate it – it merely means it may not be applicable in all cases. The combined effect of these difficulties is that we cannot use the SPA case as a definitive test of models in the framework.

However, we can use the case to probe certain specific aspects of the framework so as to gain insight into previous research. Our analysis so far has emphasized the importance of business priorities deriving from the new strategy and changed strategic environment. The question we can explore is how the framework would treat these influences had IS professionals been the project managers. Would the difference in priorities over the systems development process surface equally readily?

We have already noted that the framework makes no explicit provision for business users as methodology adoption decision makers. At the methodology/task level there has not previously been any consideration of business interests. Analysis at this level has been dominated by concern with whether the methodology is appropriate to developing a particular type of system, not whether it can be developed to satisfy specified constraints for systems development performance.

At the individual level the framework introduces business users and their interests in three guises. First, in the multivariate fit model (Episkopou & Wood-Harper, 1986) they appear as individuals whose cognitive characteristics, skills and experience are relevant only in so far as they enable them to interact with the methodology and specify system requirements. Multivariate fit assumes that users will co-operate so long as they have the attributes that will enable them to do so. Business users are not treated within this model as having a legitimate interest in the effects using the methodology would have on systems development performance. This model cannot therefore provide insight into why business users at the SPA were negative towards SSADM.

Second, users appear in the framework in the form of user preferences. They thus appear as an independent variable whose preferences are an external and in effect arbitrary influence on developers' acceptance and use of the methodology (Leonard-Barton, 1987). If we applied this to the SPA, we would see that the users were opposed to SSADM without seeing why. Even if we recognized that business had a different set of priorities that emphasized timeliness, we would still be inclined to see this as ill-informed about the longer term consequences of rapid development and hence irrational. It is only by penetrating as far as the business drivers influencing users' preference that we see it as a legitimate and rational preference in response to a changed organizational strategy and environment.

Third, business users also appear in the framework as a relevant source of commitment. When its absence is described as lack of commitment (McMaster & Kautz, 1994), there is an implicit suggestion that this is unco-operative bordering on bloody-minded. At the SPA, the users did lack commitment, but such an analysis does not encourage us to understand why. The suggestion that they were plain unco-operative would only serve to entrench the existing divide between IS and business users.

At the organizational level, the framework recognises the possibility that the changing strategic environment might influence methodology adoption by IS managers. Although the changing environment was important in that it led to new funding processes and a new strategy, attention merely to the fact of change rather than its content does not explain business managers' behaviour at the SPA. Likewise, asking whether the organizational form was postmodern does not reveal the business drivers. Rather, to the extent that strategy and funding processes are components of organizational form, asking about the elements of organizational form would help reveal the business drivers.

Thus, by exploring how models in the framework would have treated business concerns in the SPA case had business managers not been project managers, we see that where users are considered, either as methodology users in requirement elicitation or as merely a source of positive or negative influence, they are not recognized as a source of information about business priorities in respect of systems development performance. Consequently, they provide little insight into the conflicting priorities at the SPA. On the other hand, the organizational form and strategic environment can reveal the nature of the business drivers and hence clarify business managers' priorities. Thus, the SPA case suggests that we should develop a more expansive conception of organizational influences to encompass the elements of organizational form including business strategy, organizational structure, core technologies, roles, skills management processes and the strategic environment.

## DISCUSSION

The SPA case not only highlights the difference between IS professionals' and business managers' priorities, it also helps us to see the legitimacy of the dispute. IS professionals' concern for a quality development process is always legitimate when systems are likely to exist in the operational and maintenance phase for a number of years. At the SPA, ISD had faced the problem of maintaining systems of varying quality – some undocumented – that had been built without consistent use of the same methodology. The effect of this on ISD's ability to provide a satisfactory service to business users was apparent to IS professionals. They did not challenge SSADM's potential to bring benefits to ISD. Business managers, by contrast, were directly affected by the new strategic environment and the threat it posed for organizational survival. The use of a methodology whose principal benefits were derived in the long term and whose costs involved a slower development process was precluded by the imperative for organizational survival. This clearly legitimated the opposition of business managers to SSADM.

There are other grounds for business managers to have a legitimate interest in the systems development process beyond the extreme case of organizational survival. The concern of business for early system delivery is increasingly apparent throughout industry. As time-based competition becomes ever more intense (Keen, 1988), companies incur heavier and heavier opportunity costs for systems delays. In addition, if the common perception that the rate of business change is increasing is correct, any extension of project duration, even to improve long-term quality, will introduce risk because it increases the probability that business conditions and hence user requirements will change before completion. The changing balance of priorities toward short-term outcomes from systems development may or may not ultimately prove to serve the best interests of organizations. However, although it is appropriate for IS professionals to ensure that business managers understand the long-term consequences for IS of their short-term focus, persisting in the face of business opposition with a systems development process whose benefits are biased to the long term will only breed conflict between business users and IS with the likely result that the business will not co-operate in the use of the chosen methodology.

The argument for the legitimacy of business interest in methodologies extends beyond the timeliness versus technical quality tension. Organizations are driven by their business strategy. Business managers seeking to implement that strategy are justified in trying to influence the systems development process to the extent that it contributes to or interferes with the success of the strategy. It is readily conceivable, for example, that business managers seeking low-cost systems might be in conflict with IS professionals trying to improve the level of system functionality they deliver.

The management of the systems development process and the selection and adoption of methodologies has been seen as largely a technical and hence internal issue for the IS function. IS managers and developers have therefore been the only relevant decision-makers in relation to methodology adoption. It is not surprising, therefore, that this is mirrored in the organizing framework. Nor is it surprising that, where they are included in the framework, business users are treated either as non-IS methodology users, or as independent and arbitrary providers or withholders of potentially supportive co-operation. Thus, the framework recognizes that business users may be a source of influence on methodology adoption, but in restricting that influence to a marginal and indirect role through the final decisions of IS professionals it fails to recognize that where the influence is negative its source may be a legitimate difference of opinion about systems development that should be taken into account in adoption decisions. We can only speculate whether a significant but largely hidden reason for the research findings of non-use or partial use of methodologies in so many organizations might be the pressures from business sources.

## CONCLUSIONS

The organizing framework for previous research (Table 1) has shown that the adoption of systems development methodologies has mostly been seen as a technical problem for IS developers and managers. It has been seen as affected by a relatively narrow set of contextual issues such as individual preferences and attitudes and specific organizational factors that typically do not reflect business needs. This article builds on and extends our understanding of the relationship between business drivers, the systems development process, and systems development methodologies. In doing so, it demonstrates the legitimacy and appropriateness of business users' interests and priorities in respect of the influence of methodology on systems development performance. Acknowledging the legitimacy of business's interest requires both researchers and IS professionals to start thinking differently about the theory and practice of systems development.

The argument in this paper does not suggest that the models in the existing organizing framework should be abandoned. Rather, IS researchers should shift their emphasis from being IS centred to recognizing business users' interest and acknowledging the tensions over systems development priorities that may result. Future research studies will need to extend their scope in two ways. First, they will need to take account of the possible role of business managers as a decision-maker in methodology adoption. Second, regardless of the role of business manager, it will be necessary to incorporate in such studies their interests in the systems development process. This will involve conceptualizing adoption so as to bring business interests closer to the centre of the analysis, and conceptualizing users as sources of relevant information about business' requirements of the systems development process. Not only will this expand our framework and provide for a more complete analysis of methodology adoption, but, in addition, shifting the focus of the analysis will prevent misrepresentation of business resistance as arbitrary or ignorant.

Practitioners need to recognize that it is no longer a matter of choosing the technically best methodology for the development task, training and preparing the developers, and then trying to influence apparently capricious business users to behave co-operatively in respect of the new methodology. Nor can it be a matter of persuading business to accept the consequential costs or degradation of development performance associated with a methodology they would prefer was not used. Instead, adoption becomes a problem in whose solution business users should be involved if an acceptable methodology is to be found and used. And if IS professionals do not take the initiative in this, the trend for greater business involvement in the management of the IS function (Rockart, 1988; Sauer Yetton and Associates 1997) suggests that business managers will. Two changes are required. Practical guides for methodology selection, such as the task and multivariate fit models, must be extended to recognize the priorities of business. A consultative and decision process must be put in place to ensure that tensions over priorities can be resolved so that adoption can be successful.

Unfortunately, involvement of business managers will not guarantee a simple resolution of the tensions. The SPA's project management structure brought swift decisions against SSADM adoption. This resolution in favour of timely development could be justified even though it overlooked the long-term costs associated with lesser technical quality because there was a threat to organizational survival. In other situations, there may be no such decisive argument, in which case a structure which gives power over methodology adoption to business managers without there being a significant counterbalance may risk dysfunctional resolutions. However, it is important for IS professionals to remember that both this and earlier studies show that adoption is put at risk by processes that result in the choice of a methodology that business users do not support. Thus, both from this pragmatic perspective and because of the business importance of IS methodologies, IS professionals should give their full attention to the interest of business in the choice of a systems development methodology.
