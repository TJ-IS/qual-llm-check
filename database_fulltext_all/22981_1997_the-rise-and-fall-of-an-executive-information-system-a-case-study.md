# The rise and fall of an executive information system: a case study

Neil McBride

Department of Information Systems, De Montfort University, Leicester LE1 9BH, UK, email: nkm@dmu.ac.uk

Abstract. The progress of an executive information system project within a manufacturing organization over a period of 9 years is described. The case study illustrates the importance of the interaction between the business environment, the organizational environment and the perceptions and interpretations of events and facts by stakeholders on the success or failure of an information system. It shows the importance of context in the development and implementation of an executive information system and the dynamic nature of the influence of social, economic and technical factors. The reasons for the initial success and the subsequent failure of the EIS within the company are explored from a contingency perspective.

Keywords: Executive information systems, contingency theory, implementation

## INTRODUCTION

Information systems (IS) are social systems. Their success or failure is inextricably linked with the dynamics of the organization within which they exist. No study that concerns itself with how to develop a successful IS and how to avoid failures can reach many reasonable conclusions unless it addresses issues of context and culture.

It is generally accepted that IS failures are rarely a result of solely technical problems (Sauer, 1993; Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996). Recent studies of the Stock Exchange Taurus system (Currie, 1995), the London Ambulance system (Beynon-Davis, 1995), the Confirm system (Oz, 1994) and others (Sauer, 1993; Mitev, 1996) have demonstrated the complex interaction of technical, social, cultural and political elements that result in a failed IS. Equally, IS success depends on more than technical competence.

The cultural and political environment is difficult to study as it depends not only on the tangible organizational structure but also on the tacit knowledge and the perceptions of the participants. Furthermore, the cultural and political environment of an organization is not static; it is in a state of constant flux and dynamic change.

Although the use of surveys can draw out information concerning the tangible aspects of organizational culture such as the nature of the management structure, perceptions of the success or failure of information systems and numerical information about the spread and use of IS (Glover et al., 1992; Kelly Rainer et al., 1992; Young & Watson, 1995; Whyte & Bytheway, 1995), the study of the complex social nature of the evolution of IS within an organization is often better served by case studies. Case studies based on a number of interviews with key participants over an extended period of time can highlight the dynamic and on-going effects of organizational culture and context on the development of an IS.

This paper charts the progression of an executive information system (EIS) within an organization over a period of 9 years. Based on interviews with two key participants undertaken over a period of 3 years, this case study follows the rise and fall of an EIS that, at one point in its life, was considered a great success and subsequently came to be viewed as a failure.

## INTERPRETIVE APPROACHES TO THE STUDY OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS SUCCESS AND FAILURE

Studies have suggested that the success or failure of an IS cannot be explained purely in technical terms. Nor can it be defined by the presence or absence of static factors. The roots of successful IS lie in the social and organizational context (Nandhakumar, 1996; Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996). Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski (1995) suggest that the roots of project failure lie deep within the organizational and social experience of the project. They found that neither economic nor technical factors contributed significantly to IS development project abandonment. Although efforts to identify measures of IS success (DeLone & McLean, 1992; Whyte & Bytheway, 1996) have been of some use, it has been more difficult to identify general factors that explain why certain IS become successful. Young and Watson (1995) could not definitively identify any correlated factors that were diagnostic of EIS acceptance.

The reason for the failure to identify general success and failure factors lies in the importance of organizational context in determining the progress of an IS implementation. Each organizational context is unique and distinctively affects the IS development. The same system and technology that is successful in one organization may fail miserably in another, outwardly similar, organization.

Davis et al. (1992) developed an interpretive approach to explain the failure of an administrative system within a university faculty. They developed a two-dimensional matrix that linked the technical dimension with the social dimension. The use of the framework drew out some important points concerning the culture of the faculty, the nature of social barriers and the characters of the people involved in the development. However, the division between technical and social is to some extent artificial. The technical and social are linked in such a way that efforts to separate them will sacrifice some of the richness of the interpretation.

In a study of the implementation of an EIS (Jones & Nandhakumar, 1993; Nandhakumar, 1996), the factors approach was criticized. It was argued that the influence of factors is dynamic and not static, that causal connections between factors are not simple, and the factors are not independent of one another. Nandhakumar (1996) developed a layered view of contextual factors, in which factors affecting EIS development can be examined at the individual, team, IT department, company and external business environment level. Hackney and McBride (1993) similarly identified three layers of context influencing IT within hospitals.

Poulymenakou and Holmes (1996) argued that IS failure should be viewed from a contingency point of view. IS failure depends very much on the situation. The circumstances surrounding a successful IS implementation or a failure will be uncertain or fortuitous. From evidence in the literature, Poulymenakou and Holmes identified contingent variables that they divided into macrovariables or organizational variables and microvariables or project-specific variables. This is a valuable approach in that it combines technical and social factors and enables their interaction to be explored.

## EIS AS A SUBJECT FOR THE STUDY OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS SUCCESS AND FAILURE

EISs were initially developed to meet the distinct information needs of executives (Rockart & Treacy, 1982). In many organizations, their use has spread to managers at various levels of the organization (Friend, 1990; Wheeler et al., 1993). They are generally bespoke developments that extract information from operational data sources and present it in a way that is meaningful to executive users. They are characterized by the ability to move freely between a high-level view of data and a detailed view ('drill-down'), a concentration on data relating to key performance indicators and critical success factors, and the ability to highlight exceptions and variance automatically (Watson et al., 1991; Millet & Mawhinney, 1992; Young & Watson, 1995).

The properties of EISs make them suitable candidates for the study of IS success and failure. EISs attempt to reflect the activities of the whole enterprise. Unlike many operational systems, they are not supporting one particular process, but aim to bring together data from all parts of the organization to form a whole picture. They are aimed at executives and higher management. Therefore, their influence on decision-making is likely to be significant. Also, their use is non-mandatory, unlike operational systems, whose use is mandatory for the running of business processes. As a result of this, they are more dependent on executive support and the presence of champions (Cottrell & Rapley, 1991; DeLong & Rockart, 1992) for their diffusion within the organization. Therefore, the risk of failure may be higher with an EIS implementation than with key operational systems that must be made to work. Furthermore, we would suggest that the interactions among participants will more directly affect the success of EISs as technical and managerial champions must find ways of enrolling others into EIS use (Latour, 1987; Bloomfield et al., 1992). It is therefore likely that the relationship between the organizational culture and the IS will be more apparent with an EIS than, say, an order processing system.

The dynamic nature of EIS development, their dependence on organizational context and their high profile within organizations make the study of their life cycle within the organization rewarding and revealing. The case study that follows illustrates several themes concerning the influence of the organization on the development of an information system. After a description of the case study, it is interpreted using a contingency framework.

## THE COMMUNICATION COMPANY EIS

The Communication Company is a major defence contractor, internationally active in its marketplace. Since 1987, the company's operating environment has changed radically. The end of the Cold War brought with it a shrinking defence market. The advent of competitive bidding brought long-term cost-plus contracts with the UK government to an end. The increasing influence of global communications, electronic data interchange (EDI) and new technologies demanded a response from the company. Between 1987 and 1993, the company was division-based. Divisions were spread across several sites and business functions were centrally controlled. Computing services were provided by the Information Technology Services (ITS) Department.

In 1987, a need to improve IS was recognized. Having heard of executive information systems, the directors believed that an EIS was required. They felt that survival within a turbulent market required the monitoring of critical success factors (CSFs). The finance director at the Weston site was asked to look into Communication Company's information requirements. In 1988, he commissioned a study from ITS. The result was a document that specified a complete office automation system with a price tag of £5 000 000. The directors rejected this as being too expensive. The managing director at this time was seen as a ‘numbers man’, interested in CSFs. He asked for an EIS to be considered. With his support, an EIS development system was selected. The first effort by ITS to design an EIS using the system was not successful. The finance director considered that ITS was using the software to replicate spreadsheets. ITS saw the request for an EIS as just another request for a reporting suite. They had failed to understand what the finance director required.

In 1987, an MIS team was set up at the Weston site. This small group of IT developers reported to the commercial manager. It provided ‘computer systems to support the business’, arguably filling the gaps that ITS left because the business did not have the resources or the motivation. In 1990, the finance director recruited this MIS team to develop an EIS. The MIS team then reported to the finance director, a situation that caused some concern in the business. For the next 12 months the team concentrated on a budget module. They experimented with colours and different ways of navigation and built several prototypes. The budget module was available for use early in 1992.

In early 1994, the team project manager was able to identify some of the benefits. Overtime working was cut down, and data quality and ownership were improved. However, data entry became more difficult. There was some resistance in accounts to using the budget module because of the extra work involved. Staff continued to use the old spreadsheets, but the finance director insisted that budgets be produced using the new EIS system.

The budget module was followed by a marketing module. This was supported by the marketing manager, who used the system on-line in meetings to demonstrate its effectiveness as an alternative to paper-based reports.

The success of the EIS was limited to a small set of users, mostly at Weston. It became clear to the finance director that the expansion of its use to all directors and all sites would require substantial further investment. The finance director was aware that it would be difficult to put together a financial case. The benefits of the EIS were intangible. There would be no short-term payback. The finance director decided that he would have to sell the idea of the EIS to the board of directors. Instead of producing paper justifications of the EIS project, he would show them. Demonstrations were arranged for the board of directors and the managing director. These were very encouraging. The managing director insisted on its use at other sites. One site started a large implementation of the EIS. As it spread to other Communication Company sites, there were plans to make it company-wide by networking it from the central data centre.

In the summer of 1993, the managing director left. With his departure, there was no real champion of EIS left at the centre. Local resistance increased. When other sites found that they could ignore the EIS without incurring criticism from head office, they abandoned it. Cost was one reason cited. In the absence of central funding, local units had to absorb the costs of EIS.

In April 1994, a new managing director arrived. His emphasis was on marketing and looking for new business. He replaced the division-based structure with a site-based structure. Sites now used their own networking protocols. Where EIS was being used at other sites, problems occurred on the network that were sometimes blamed on the EIS. Network staff at these sites would not support communications problems involving the EIS. Further development of the EIS did not occur and it began to decline.

In May 1994, the finance director left. As the most experienced, keen and influential user of EIS, his departure was a major blow. The MIS team now reported to an engineering technical manager. Resources were redeployed on engineering projects to the detriment of EIS. The MIS team continued to seek sponsors for the EIS. A case for investment in the EIS was prepared with the help of ITS, but was turned down in favour of an engineering data management system.

By September 1994, the EIS project was in limbo. Use of EIS at other sites had ceased. A third change of managing director occurred and the new management team did not have time to consider EIS. Mid-1994 was spent preparing for and obtaining ISO9001 certification and little effort went into the EIS. Interest in the EIS was further reduced by a move to MS Windows in 1994. The entire EIS development had been DOS based. Users thought that EIS requirements could be met by an Access database or Excel spreadsheets. The need for an expensive EIS was questioned. By April 1996 the on-line use of EIS had ceased.

Company-wide IT standards were being prepared and a policy of systems purchase rather than internal development was in place. IT decisions were now being taken at the centre. There was less room for the bottom-up initiatives which the EIS had represented.

At the Communication Company headquarters a new resource planning system was being defined that would replace the current finance system. It was planned to use a relational database with an EIS front end.

## ANALYSIS

The Communication Company case study illustrates the dynamic nature of an EIS development, set within the context of a rapidly changing organization. The need for an EIS is born out of the volatile business environment within which the organization exists. Equally, the demise of the EIS came about in part because of the frequent changes in personnel and the evolving goals of the company. The following section considers the contingent variables associated with IS failure (Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996) in the light of the Communication Company case study.

## Macrocontingent variables

## Culture

The culture at the Communication Company changed significantly throughout the lifetime of the EIS. In response to a changing business environment, a more flexible business culture emerged that could deal with a larger number of small projects and a need for a more market-oriented way of working. This cultural change gave rise to a greater interest in IT and the developing interest in EIS expressed by the directors.

Within the Communication Company, the presence of subcultures gave rise to conditions that encouraged the development of the EIS. The culture of ITS was that of a traditional IT department, focused on mainframe operational systems. ITS failed to understand the cultural needs of the directors and the finance director. ITS remained culturally separated from EIS development throughout its lifetime.

Although culture can affect the take-up and success of an IS, it is equally true that the IS can result in culture change. At the height of its success, the EIS was used by the finance director and the marketing manager to catalyse culture change. The culture at Weston became more information aware and responsive to the need for business change as a result of the promotion of the EIS. Productivity gains were identified as the culture became more oriented to electronic communication. These cultural changes remained after the fall of the EIS. The case study suggests that culture is a valid contingent variable affecting the success and failure of the EIS.

## Planning

Strategic planning did not feature as a contingent variable in the Communication Company EIS development. The development of the EIS was not a strategic decision. It was initiated by the directors' perception that an EIS would be helpful. This was based on an identified need for better information and a technical awareness that EIS existed. This case study suggests that planning should be replaced by two contingent variables: technical awareness and a micro-contingent variable – strength of organizational need.

## Technical awareness

Technical awareness across the organization can help promote the success of an IS and help in the identification of technical problems that might render the IS project non-viable. If management and users are aware of the strengths and limitations of the technology, realistic expectations can be set and problems caused by over-optimism in the technology or underuse of the technology's capabilities can be avoided.

## Accountability

Poulymenakou and Holmes (1996) explain that accountability depends on perceptions of ownership of the project. In the absence of clear accountability, failure of an IS project is more likely. Accountability for the EIS project lay firstly with the managing director. Ownership, and therefore accountability, was transferred to the finance director when ITS failed to deliver and remained with him after the managing director's departure in 1993. The departure of the finance director reduced accountability. Accountability for some of the EIS was taken up by the marketing director, but after his dismissal no senior manager was accountable for the progress of the EIS. Although enthusiasm for the EIS project remained within the MIS team, other projects took priority and no one owned the EIS.

## Irrationality

Apparently irrational behaviour within the project life cycle can occur in many ways. The clearest evidence of irrationality within a failing project is escalation, when more and more resources are poured into an ailing project based on a faulty perception of the chances of success (Newman & Sabherwal, 1996). It should be noted that apparent irrationality may not be irrational given the individual's perception of the situation. A stakeholder's theory-in-use (Davis et al., 1992) may make the escalation of a project perfectly rational.

Escalation phenomena were not associated with the Communication Company EIS. The financial director at Weston sought to extend the influence of the project by enrolling the managing director and the board of directors.

## Evaluation

Lack of evaluation of an IS project may increase the likelihood of failure and reduce the possibility of lessons being learnt from the project. In this case study, no formal evaluation took place. There was a perception at Weston that the project had resulted in some benefits. This perception was reinforced by the views of the project manager and finance director.

## Organizational structure

During the lifetime of the EIS, the organizational structure of the Communication Company evolved. A division-based structure ensured centralized control and encouraged the spread of the EIS once it was approved by the managing director. The move to a site-based structure increased local autonomy and allowed sites to set their own standards and control their own budgets. This effectively produced new barriers to the EIS, which now needed to gain local acceptance. Local resistance was now more effective, reducing the influence of the EIS, as sites were not subject to centralized authority. In late 1996, the pendulum began to swing towards more central control under a new managing director. This was expressed in the establishing of company-wide IT standards and a renewed interest in the concept of an EIS.

## Business environment

The changing business environment had a significant effect on the development of EIS. The need for better information for budgeting, marketing and manpower resulted in calls for an EIS. The appearance of new technology coincided with the appearance of new business needs catalysed by the changing business environment.

The demise of the EIS may have been affected by the changing priorities of the managers, who were responding to further changes in the business environment. IS failure may result from the IS not adapting to its environment technically, socially or economically. A responsive IS must be capable of rapid evolution in a changing environment.

## Microcontingent variables

## Methods and methodologies

Poulymenakou and Holmes (1996) suggest that the method of system development may contribute to the success or failure of an IS. Methods may well contribute to failure by focusing on technical standards without addressing social or ethical issues. It should be noted that the method selected often reflects the culture of the organization and the culture of the IT department within the organization. In the case of the Communication Company, ITS failed to appreciate the importance of interface and presentation in the development of the EIS. It may be significant that the MIS team focused on the user interface and applied a prototyping approach. This approach may have matched the needs of the users and promoted the organizational fit of the system. Thus, the method used for development was an important contingent variable affecting the initial success of the EIS. The subsequent failure of the EIS may not have been influenced by method.

## Resistance to change

The Communication Company EIS hit barriers resulting from resistance to change at various points in its lifetime. Administrative staff in accounts, network staff at other sites and the new directors in 1994 all resisted EIS.

As the company at this time was subject to great organizational change, it may have been felt that a further burden of technological change was inappropriate and premature. The introduction of new IT before organizational change has occurred and before the organization is ready to take it on board may increase the possibility of failure. Poulymenakou and Holmes (1996) note that resistance to change is not always negative. As a response to IS, it may indicate that organizational transformations are incomplete and that the organizational environment is not ready for the IS. In the case of the Communication Company, it may be suggested that, although the management environment at Weston was suitable for the use of the EIS, the general management environment throughout the company was not and efforts by the managing director in 1992 to extend its use across the company were premature.

## Power and politics

Significant aspects of the success and failure of the EIS may be attributed to shifts in power within the Communication Company. The managing director was influential in initiating the process of EIS development. Through his control of resources he authorized the initial feasibility study and enrolled the finance director at Weston. In addition, the finance director had the power to promote the EIS. However, he was dependent on the managing director for the promotion of the EIS beyond Weston. When the managing director's interest waned, the finance director sought to gain his support by selling a particular interpretation of the benefits of EIS. The departure of the managing director and the shift to site-based operation reduced the extent of the indirect power the finance director could exert outside Weston.

## Strength of organizational need

The strength of organizational need for the project may affect the success of the project as it influences the organizational commitment to the project. The level of commitment to the EIS project varied as the perception of the organizational need for it varied. This perception of organizational need was, to a large extent, set by the managing director and the finance director at Weston. In their absence, the organization took a different view of the importance of the EIS.

## Technology shift

During the lifetime of the EIS, information technology trends moved away from mainframes to PCs and from MS-DOS to Windows-based visual applications. Although the Communication Company was slow to take up new technology, it was nevertheless the case that moves towards Windows in 1994 left the EIS behind. Technology shift is a significant contingent variable affecting the success or failure of an IS. An IT project can fail because the technology is superseded and no longer meets the user's expectations. Alternatively, it can fail because the technology is too advanced and is being implemented without the skills, culture or technology-awareness needed. The technology shift made it difficult for the MIS team to sustain an investment case for the upgrading of EIS. However, the technology is affected by the interpretation put on it by stakeholders within the organization. The use of existing, proven technology may be interpreted either as providing a firm foundation for an IS or as locking the IS in the past. Teething problems with new technology can be interpreted as reasons for avoiding its use, or as minor hurdles to be overcome or circumvented. The success of new IT will depend on its social interpretation within the company. This in turn depends on who has the power to make that interpretation and the extent to which that interpretation of the technology becomes common currency throughout the organization.

## CONCLUSIONS

The identification and description of contingent variables enables us to discuss the issues involved in the success or failure of IS within an organization. From a practical point of view, the identification and analysis of contingent variables as part of a post-implementation analysis may help the organization to improve its management practice and make organizational changes.

This case study illustrates the close and dynamic relationship between an IS and its organizational environment. Information systems evolve within organizations as environmental forces act on them. IS that fail to adapt to changing environmental conditions die and are replaced by new systems. The business environment places selective pressures on the organizational environment that in turn selects appropriate information systems.

The EIS at the Communications Company grew within an environmental niche that arose out of a complex interaction between the business environment, the organizational environment and the people within the organization. Changes within the organization subsequently rendered the environment hostile to the EIS. The EIS was affected by its changing links with organizational structure and people, the changing responses of people within the organization to the environment around them, and the changing individual and collective mindsets and understanding of those people.

## Biography

Neil McBride is a Principal Lecturer in the Department of Information Systems at De Montfort University. He was previously a senior consultant with a large management consultancy, involved in the design and implementation of systems for hospitals, the evaluation of information systems and SSADM-based projects. His current research interests include information systems strategy in the public sector, the business use of the Internet and IT investment appraisal. His previous research has been published in the International Journal of Public Sector Management and the European Management Journal.
