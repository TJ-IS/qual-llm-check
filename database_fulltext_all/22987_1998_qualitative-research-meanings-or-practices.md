# Qualitative research: meanings or practices?

D. Silverman

Professor of Sociology, Goldsmiths' College, University of London, UK, email: D. Silverman@gold.ac.uk

Abstract. With information systems (IS), as in other social sciences, the critique of quantitative research can lead to an oversimplified opposition between ‘positivism’ and ‘interpretivism’. This is one reason why qualitative IS research sometimes unnecessarily limits itself to the study of participants’ meanings. A simple tabulation of published qualitative research is used to reveal the predominance of interview-based studies in both sociology and IS. This is followed by a brief account of the limits of both quantitative studies of ‘objective’ variables and qualitative studies of ‘subjective meanings’. The emerging case for IS research on organizational practices is set out, coupled with an account of Suchman’s (1987) study of how workers make use of a computer-based system attached to a photocopier. The paper concludes with a call for an end to paradigm wars in organizational studies.

Keywords: Methodology, interviews, ethnography, organizations

## INTRODUCTION

I am a sociologist whose areas of research have included the sociology of organizations (Silverman, 1970), health and illness (Silverman, 1987) and counselling for HIV infection and AIDS (Silverman, 1996). I have a continuing interest in the potential of qualitative research to reveal how humans interact, particularly in organizational settings (Silverman, 1993, 1997).

In this paper, as a disciplinary ‘outsider’, I offer some views about the quality of a sample of recent IS research studies. These views are placed in the context of the following set of preferences:

1 Qualitative research is best viewed not as a set of free-standing techniques but as based on some analytically defined perspective.

2 From my perspective, the particular strength of qualitative research, for both researchers and practitioners, is its ability to focus on actual practice in situ, looking at how organizations are routinely enacted.

3 The apparent identification of non-quantitative social science with the open-ended interview needs to be re-examined. I reject the fashionable identification of qualitative method with an analysis of how people ‘see things’, preferring to focus instead on how people ‘do things’.

4 Qualitative researchers ought to question the conventional wisdom that their kind of research can only be ‘exploratory’ or ‘anecdotal’. Case study methods can be applied to large datasets, and standard issues of ‘reliability’ can be addressed by systematic transcription of data.

I will illustrate these preferences through concrete examples of organizational research.

## THE CASE FOR QUALITATIVE IS RESEARCH

King (1996) has noted how research on computer-aided systems has been dominated by survey methods; for instance, such systems are routinely evaluated by questionnaires that seek to elicit managers' and professionals' perceptions of the usefulness of systems' tools.

Properly designed questionnaires appear to deliver valid and reliable quantified information. However, respondents' answers to survey questions are inevitably abstracted from the day-to-day business of actually operating the system. Thus, there may be a world of difference between the contingencies of working with a given piece of IT and responding to a questionnaire about one's ‘attitudes’ towards the system.

As King notes, this means that social surveys can decontextualize perceptions of IS. As he puts it:

‘the surveys do not reveal the context in which the tools are used . . . which . . . influences the respondents’ perceptions’ (King, 1996, p. 174).

For King, such surveys depend upon what he calls a ‘positivistic’ model, which assumes that ‘tools are discrete entities’. By contrast, he argues that ‘interpretive research’ can give ‘a deeper understanding of the underlying process of organization change [in the context of] an information system’ (King, 1996, p. 174). King goes on to illustrate his argument by reporting a longitudinal, qualitative case study that, he claims, reveals organizational processes.

Of course, it has long been recognized that purely quantitative research may neglect the social and cultural construction of the ‘variables’ studied. C. Wright Mills (1959) attacked the atheoretical character of much quantitative research, which he called ‘abstracted empiricism’. Blumer (1968) noted how the attempt to establish correlations between variables depended upon a lack of attention as to how these variables are defined by the people being studied. Finally, Cicourel (1964) drew attention to how the choice of a purely mathematical logic can neglect the common-sense reasoning used by both participants and researchers. Instead of attending to the social construction of meaning, quantitative research, it is claimed, uses a set of ad hoc procedures to define, count and analyse its variables.

More recently, Kirk and Miller (1986) have followed Cicourel's questioning of the theoretical assumptions of some quantitative researchers. A much measured concept such as ‘attitude’ is, they suggest, far from simple. ‘Attitudes’ do not simply attach to the inside of people’s heads, and researching them depends on making a whole series of analytical assumptions. They conclude:

'The survey researcher who discusses is not wrong to do so. Rather, the researcher is wrong if he or she fails to acknowledge the theoretical basis on which it is meaningful to make measurements of such entities and to do so with survey questions addressed to a probability sample of voters' (1986, p. 15).

For such qualitative critics, quantitative research uses a set of ad hoc procedures to define, count and analyse its variables (Blumer, 1968; Cicourel, 1964; Silverman, 1975). Understandably, most qualitative research has preferred, instead, to describe and illuminate the meaningful social world.

Such critiques have led to attempts, such as King's, to characterize two very different versions of social science research: positivism (which seeks to test correlations between variables) and interpretive social science (which is more concerned with observation and description and, at best, generating hypotheses). In particular, I suggest that Table 2 contains the following difficulties:

\- The possible confusion of appropriate methodologies with political issues ('negotiating goals' with 'the whole client group' is, no doubt, highly democratic but, in itself, has nothing to do with the validity of the research).

\- ‘Context-dependent insights’ sound attractive but, by definition, are probably not be generalizable.

\- ‘Context’ itself is by no means a straightforward concept. For instance, there is no guarantee that a researcher’s sense of context will correspond to that actually invoked by participants (see Schegloff, 1991).

\- The use of data triangulation, advocated by King (1996, p. 178), is a highly contested research method (see Silverman, 1993, pp. 156–8).

\- Action research, as described by King, draws on a hazy theoretical base related to what he calls ‘phenomenology’ or ‘hermeneutics’. Yet the latter is, primarily, a literary endeavour, whereas phenomenology is either a philosophy with no relation to empirical research or involves the rigorous study of everyday practices (Schutz, 1962) not the recording of the researcher’s own ‘experiences’ as Wood-Harper recommends. As I shall argue later, this lack of theoretical specificity of action research, which King himself calls ‘a hybrid approach’ (1996, p. 177), can result in a muddle.

## THE NORMATIVE COMPONENTS OF THE QUALITATIVE/QUANTITATIVE DICHOTOMY

Perhaps in response to the dominance of quantitative IS research, some qualitative IS researchers such as King (1996) seem to assume a fixed preference or predefined evaluation of what is a good (i.e. qualitative) and bad (i.e. quantitative) research methodology. Such normative assumptions have, of course, been around for many years. This is illustrated in Table 3, which is drawn from the words used by speakers at a conference on research methods in the late 1970s.

Table 3 shows how imprecise, evaluative considerations come into play when researchers describe qualitative and quantitative methods. Such normative critiques of quantitative research ('positivism') lay very shaky foundations for a school of qualitative or interpretive social science.

In the first place, the critics had established a ‘straw man’ to represent quantitative research. Quantitative researchers are rarely ‘dopes’. Thus, for instance, epidemiologists and criminologists are only too aware of the problematic character of what gets recorded as, say, ‘cause of death’ or a ‘criminal offence’ (see Hindess, 1973). Equally, good quantitative researchers are highly conscious of the problems involved in interpreting statistical correlations in relation to what the variables involved ‘mean’ to the participants (see Marsh, 1982, ch. 5). In these circumstances, it might be better to avoid the term critique and instead talk about some complaints made about some quantitative research.

Second, it is inaccurate to assume that quantitative and qualitative research are polar opposites. In Interpreting Qualitative Data (henceforth IQD), I sought to undermine the assumption that these two paradigms are incommensurable or, indeed, that they offer any worthwhile description of the major alternative directions of sociological research. For, of course, there are no principled grounds to be either qualitative or quantitative in approach. It all depends upon what you are trying to do. Thus, the choice between different research methods can depend upon quite pragmatic matters; for instance, if you want to discover how people intend to vote, then a quantitative method, such as a social survey, may seem the most appropriate choice. On the other hand, if you are concerned with exploring people's wider perceptions or everyday behaviour, then qualitative methods may be favoured. Indeed, often one will want to combine both approaches.

For example, I recently tested a hypothesis by using simple tabulations on approximately 50 examples of HIV counselling ‘advice packages’, whose form can readily be identified and counted (Silverman, 1996, pp. 124–30). This means that, if we want to understand the logic behind qualitative research, we need to recognize its points of continuity, as well as difference, with more quantitative or ‘positivistic’ studies.

Third, just as quantitative researchers would resist the charge that they are all ‘positivists’ (Marsh, 1982), there is no agreed doctrine underlying all qualitative social research. Instead, there are many ‘isms’ that appear to lie behind qualitative methods; for example, interactionism, feminism, post-modernism and ethnomethodology (see Lincoln & Denzin, 1994). This means that qualitative research can cover a vast range of research styles and can be used in studies without any conscious analytic perspective.

As I argue in IQD, this makes any attempt to characterize qualitative research as a whole open to severe criticism. For instance, although Hammersley (1992, p. 160) attempts to identify qualitative research with hypothesis generation, more and more non-quantitative work happily tests prior hypotheses (for example Strong, 1979; Silverman, 1996, pp. 124-7). Again, although many ‘qualitative’ researchers would agree with Bryman (1988, p. 61) that their aim is to ‘see through the eyes’ of subjects, others would criticize such an aim as ‘subjectivist’ and even ‘journalistic’. For instance, it is worth noting that Cicourel (1964) criticizes the claims that open-ended, qualitative interviews offer direct access to ‘experience’. For Cicourel, common-sense reasoning enters into how both interviewees and researchers understand ‘experience’.

This raises an important but neglected point about the aims and conceptual models of qualitative research. In particular, how far does the pursuit of ‘human experience’ (King, 1996, p.190) provide a distinctive or even coherent target for such research? More precisely, should qualitative organizational research be identified with such a ‘subjectivist’ agenda often at the cost of the study of organizational behaviours?

## RESEARCHING ORGANIZATIONAL 'MEANINGS'

Curiously, the pursuit of organizational ‘meanings’ or ‘experiences’ implies a division of labour that quantitative organizational researchers have eagerly propounded. They maintain that, whereas quantitative researchers are concerned with organizational ‘structures’, interpretive social science focuses on how individual organizational members construct ‘meanings’ (see Blau & Schoenherr, 1971).

Like King, many recent qualitative researchers have been happy to put on the mantle offered by their quantitative colleagues. For instance, note the attention given to ‘meanings’ in a recent qualitative organizational text:

'Organizations and their environments ... are enacted daily and socially constructed ... meanings are ... created ... deconstructed, negotiated and elaborated' (Czarniawska-Joerges, 1992, p. 33).

Although Czarniawska-Joerges wants us to study the way that ‘organizations are built on the foundations of everyday life’ (1992, p. 120), her attention to everyday practices sits uneasily with her call for ‘an interpretation of organizational processes from the standpoint of the actors involved’ (1992, p. 4).

To be fair, she does recognize that actors' ‘standpoints’ do not speak for themselves but have to be ‘collected and retold by a researcher’ (Czarniawska-Joerges, 1992, p. 4). But, although recognizing the context-boundedness of accounts (pp. 123, 136), she still wants to treat actors' accounts as depicting organizational realities. ‘Actor’s accounts’, she writes, ‘are representative … (they) reveal a “reality outside” as seen by the speakers’ (pp. 136–7).

Consonant with her interest in ‘meanings’, she is drawn to the interview method, despite the fact that, in her own terms, they are ‘provoked accounts’ (p. 199) and ‘representations’ (p. 201). Indeed, she dismisses Crozier’s (1964, p. 94) rejection of interview methods grounded, in part, on a justified distrust of stories provoked by a researcher.

Similarly, the argument that interviews tell us only about what people say in interviews is, she argues, ‘true, but exaggerated’ (p. 198). This leads her to suggest various remedies to avoid such troubles, for example avoiding hypothetical questions, asking about what people actually do and why (p. 201), and using triangulated accounts to tell ‘a story of a phenomenon’ (p. 204).

Unfortunately, such remedies only provide practical solutions. They cannot resolve the analytical problem about the status of interview accounts (see Silverman, 1993, pp. 90–114).

Like Czarniawska-Joerges, John Hassard (1993) assumes an intimate link between organizational theory and research. However, Hassard does not assume that we always have to choose a single research paradigm. His research on the British fire service shows how each of four paradigms can reveal different, but equally important, features of organizational life. As summarized in Table 4, Hassard shows how each paradigm can be used to generate a different topic and research method.

The recognition that paradigms shape our choice of research topics and our preferred methodology is not new. What is unusual about Hassard's series of studies is that he applies this insight at all stages of his research design. The outcome is a highly skilful demonstration of the interpenetration of theory and method. Rather than dwell in the unreflective space of a single paradigm, Hassard thinks through and applies the logics of multiple paradigms. Thus, by slicing the organizational ‘cake’ in several ways, he produces a body of complementary sets of data.

Faced with such an ambitious set of studies, I hesitate to criticize. Nonetheless, for the present purpose of exposition, some critical observations are in order. First, we may note that, like Czarniawska-Joerges and King, Hassard ends up by using open-ended interviews for his interpretivist paradigm.

Of course, Hassard shows that he is aware that other methods are available for non-functionalist research. For instance, one of his studies is based upon recorded talk during training seminars. This should allow analysis of what organizational members do (rather than what they say about what they think and do).

Unfortunately, his research on such ‘naturally occurring’ data is strangely inconclusive and contradictory. This is because, inexplicably, he positions the analysis of such recorded talk in a paradigm that purports to address the meanings of individuals ('radical humanism'). Thus, Hassard misses the opportunity to use his tape-recorded data to analyse the socially organized structure of a naturally occurring organizational ‘event’. By doing so, he appears, like King, to equate ‘interpretivism’ with the ‘subjectivist’ pursuit of individual meanings.

As we have seen, both Czarniawska-Joerges and Hassard provide inventive ways of proceeding that recognize the broader links between research and theoretical models. Both reject the empiricist tradition in which empirical indicators are found and turned into measures. In turn, both show the value of analytically based organizational research.

However, these gains are balanced by losses. The apparent identification of non-quantitative social science with the open-ended interview generates a whole series of problems. Methodologically, it underrates the value of data gathered from naturally occurring situations. Analytically, it appears to accept that the only alternative to treating organizational structures as 'real' even 'natural' facts is to focus on an individual's private experiences of organizational life. It thus leaves vacant the vast terrain of socially organized practices that cannot be reduced to either 'objectivist' organizational structures or 'subjectivist' individual meanings. Yet, as we shall now see, the open-ended interview seems to have become the preferred research method for qualitative research in a whole range of areas and disciplines.

Of course, we should not make too much of findings based on such a tiny and perhaps unrepresentative data set. Nonetheless, Table 7 is not strikingly out of line with the preference for interview-based qualitative research found in the articles published in the more sociological journals.

I have discussed elsewhere the possible cultural roots of this phenomenon in the context of an ‘interview society’ (Atkinson & Silverman, 1997). At present, I am more concerned with its methodological impact.

As already noted, qualitative researchers' preference for interview studies ironically respects a division of labour preferred by quantitative researchers. According to this division, although quantitative research focuses on objective structures, it falls to qualitative researchers to give ‘insight’ into people’s subjective states.

As I have pointed out elsewhere (Silverman, 1996, pp. 24–25), the unfortunate consequence of this division of labour is that both approaches neglect a great deal about how people interact. Put more strongly, both kinds of research are fundamentally concerned with the environment around the phenomenon rather than the phenomenon itself.

In quantitative studies of ‘objective’ social structures (for example how organizational size impacts upon behaviour) and qualitative studies of people’s ‘subjective’ orientations (for example how people report that they ‘experience’ new technologies), we may be deflected away from the phenomenon of how people actually interact with these new technologies). Instead, we are inexorably deflected towards what follows and precedes it (causes and consequences in the ‘objectivist’ approach) or to how people respond to it (the ‘subjectivist’ approach). This can be illustrated in two simple diagrams (see Figures 1 and 2).

In both approaches, the phenomenon with which ostensibly we are concerned disappears. In ‘objectivism’, it is defined out of existence (by fiat, as Cicourel, 1964 puts it). Equally, what I have called ‘subjectivism’ is so romantically attached to the authentic rush of human experience that it merely reproduces tales of a subjective world without bringing us any closer to the local organization of the phenomena concerned.

## NON-SUBJECTIVIST IS CASE STUDIES

Two recent papers by Cavaye (1996) and Ngwenyama & Klein (1994) have begun to set out an agenda for IS research that rejects any simple identification of the qualitative case study with the open-ended interview.

Cavaye (1996, p. 229) points out that the case study method has only two necessary components:

1 It does not seek explicitly to control or manipulate variables.

2 It studies a phenomenon in its natural context.

This allows Cavaye to elaborate on the range of logics underlying case study research

\- from attempts to discover new hypotheses to the testing of existing hypotheses;

● from single to multiple cases; and

● from purely qualitative case studies to combined quantitative and qualitative studies.

As she refreshingly points out, the logic chosen depends on what you are trying to do rather than upon ideological commitments to, say, an interpretivist paradigm (Cavaye, 1996, p. 239).

However, Cavaye is equally blunt that her pragmatic outlook does not mean that anything goes. In particular, case study research is never theory free, nor does its reluctance to manipulate ‘variables’ mean that it rejects scientific scepticism or the attempt to use reliable measures.

Ngwenyama and Klein (1994) complement Cavaye's pragmatism with a powerful critique of the possibly unreflective preference for the open-ended interview. They note how current theory in knowledge system development (KSD) is based on research that asks experts and novices to ‘think aloud’ or to give voice to their ‘social interpretations’. As they point out, such research:

'has given primacy to facts, rules and their relationships which can be elicited from the mind of the knowledge worker' (Ngwenyama & Klein, 1994, p. 130, my emphasis).

The problem with such interview-based research is that ‘experts are unable to articulate all their expertise’ (Ngwenyama & Klein, 1994, p. 131). If we really want to study ‘the expertise upon which knowledge workers rely’ (p. 129) and, thereby, to develop ‘a theory of expertise’ (p. 130), we must turn away from asking questions and examine experts’ behaviour in situ. In this way, we are better able to access how implicit background knowledge is used in particular contexts (p. 136) as Bodker and Kensing’s (1994) study of design in an organizational context shows.

Ngwenyama and Klein suggest a programme for IS research with clear theoretical implications:

● a theory of everyday knowledge and its associated concepts;

\- a methodology which offers access to the naturally occurring contexts in which that knowledge is deployed.

Like Ngwenyama and Klein (1994), Cavaye wishes to move qualitative IS research away from its dependence upon the simplicities of the so-called interpretivist model. As yet, such a programme seems to be relatively unfulfilled within specifically IS research. To indicate a promising direction that future research might take, I will cite one sociological study of how people actually interact with new technologies.

## COMMUNICATING WITH A PHOTOCOPIER

Lucy Suchman (1987) was concerned with a computer-based system attached to a photocopier and intended to instruct the user in the photocopier's operation. Her study, which is cited, without details, by Ngwenyama and Klein (1994), is based on videos. These videos were then transcribed using precisely the systematic techniques for the collection and recording of data that Cavaye (1996) takes to be properly characteristic of what she calls the 'field study'.

Table 8. Field study

Like Ngwenyama and Klein (1994), Suchman focuses on how rules actually function in human-computer interaction. She draws upon Gladwin's (1964) account of the navigation methods of a South-East Asian tribe – the Trukese – with no ‘rational’ Western theory of navigation. Instead, the Trukese navigate via various ad hoc methods (for example responding to the wind, waves, the stars, the clouds, etc.).

Suchman asks how real is the contrast between Western and Trukese methods of navigation? Theories and plans do not determine the actions of either Western or Trukese navigators. Rather, Western navigators invoke a plan when asked to account for their navigation that, inevitably, depends on ad hoc methods (for example accounting for disasters such as the Exxon Valdez oil spill off Alaska).

This creates a problem in artificial intelligence systems that are:

'built on a planning model of human action. The model treats a plan as something located in the actor's head, which directs his or her behaviour' (Suchman, 1987, p. 3).

As Suchman notes, plans neither determine action nor fully reconstruct it. Thus, she argues that:

'artefacts built on the planning model confuse plans with situated actions' and proposes 'a view of plans as formulations of antecedent conditions and consequences that account for actions in a plausible way' (Suchman, 1987, p. 3).

Conversely, she suggests, the successful navigation of the Trukese shows that:

'the coherence of situated action is tied in essential ways not to . . . conventional rules but to local interactions contingent on the actor's particular circumstances' (Suchman, 1987, pp. 27–28).

This implies that, in designing computers that can interact with humans, the system of communication:

‘must incorporate both a sensitivity to local circumstances and resources for the remedy of troubles in understanding that inevitably arise’ (p. 28).

This will mean that:

‘Instead of looking for a structure that is invariant across situations, we look for the processes whereby particular, uniquely constituted circumstances are systematically interpreted so as to render meaning shared and action accountably rational’ (p. 67).

There is a methodological basis behind Suchman's focus on ‘processes (of) . . . systematic( ) interpretation’ that is worth noting. Although I have not reproduced her data here, her analysis is concerned with the sequential organization of verbal and non-verbal interaction. Suchman's data derive from videos of four sessions, each of more than an hour, involving first-time users of this ‘expert system’. In each session, two novices worked together in pairs. She is particularly concerned with how interactional ‘troubles’ arise and are resolved.

In Suchman's study the computer used in the photocopier:

‘project(s) the course of the user’s actions as the enactment of a plan for doing the job, and then use(s) the presumed plan as the relevant context for the action’s interpretation’ (p. 99, my emphasis).

However, the problem is that ‘plans’ have a different status for computers and users:

'While the (design) plan directly determines the system's behaviour, the user is required to find the plan, as the prescriptive and descriptive significance of a series of procedural instructions' (p. 101, my emphasis).

This is shown in Suchman's model of how the computer is supposed to ‘instruct’ a user (see Table 9).

Despite this rational model, much of the user's behaviour is unavailable to the system; for instance, ‘the actual work of locating referents and interpreting action descriptions’ (p. 107). This means that if an instruction is misunderstood by the user, the error will go unnoticed.

Predictably, Suchman's study reveals many conflicts between the design assumptions (DA) built into the machine and user assumptions (UA). Some examples of this are set out in Table 10.

As Table 10 shows, a faulted action can go unnoticed at the point where it occurs. This is because:

'what is available to the system is only the action's effect and that effect satisfies the requirements for the next instruction' (p. 167).

As a consequence, although from the point of view of the design users have achieved precisely what they want, this is not how users actually perceive their situation. Because of these kinds of conflicts between the assumptions of designers and users, Suchman concludes that users often fail to get what they want from the photocopier:

'Due to the constraints on the machine's access to the situation of the user's inquiry, breaches in understanding that for face-to-face interaction would be trivial in terms of detection and repair become ‘fatal’ for human-machine communication' (p. 170).

Like many studies concerned with the mechanics of organizational interaction, Suchman's findings are both analytically and practically rich. Among the practical implications of her study, we may note that:

1 It reveals the character of practical decision making in a way relevant to the design of expert systems.

2 It suggests the constructive role of users' troubles in system design, i.e. troubles arise not by departing from a plan but in the situated contingencies of action. She notes how such systems may not seek to eliminate user errors but 'to make them accessible to the student, and therefore instructive' (p. 184).

Analytically, from an IS point of view, Suchman's work is important because of its focus on the precise mechanics of interaction (see also Heath and Luff's, 1992 paper on London Underground traffic control rooms). In particular, Suchman begins by using everyday interaction as a baseline and then seeing how far human-computer interaction departs from it. This means that she avoids the common sense assumption that there is a stable organizational or institutional order, separate from everyday interaction, that participants can reveal in interviews.

Suchman's focus on workers' everyday common sense practices derives from a sociological theory called ‘ethnomethodology’. Following Garfinkel (1967), ethnomethodology attempts to understand ‘folk’ (ethno) methods (methodology) for organizing the world. It locates these methods in the skills ('artful practices') through which people come to develop an understanding of each other and of social situations.

These skills are not available in participants' interview accounts – the very staple of most ‘interpretivist’ research studies. As Maynard (1989) has suggested, if we reduce social life to the definitions of the participants, we tend to lose sight of social phenomena. Instead, the point is to narrow the focus to what people are doing. As Maynard puts it:

"The question that ethnographers have traditionally asked – “How do participants see things?” – has meant in practice the presumption that reality lies outside the words spoken in a particular time and place. The . . . (alternative) question – “How do participants do things?” – suggests that the microsocial order can be appreciated more fully by studying how speech and other face-to-face behaviours constitute reality within actual mundane situations' (Maynard, 1989, p. 144).

Maynard's alternative question derives from Garfinkel's ethnomethodological approach. This shows us that the simplistic opposition between positivist and interpretivist models of social research is an unhelpful basis for qualitative or case study research.

## AN END TO PARADIGM WARS?

In organizational studies, there are signs of a long overdue retreat from paradigmatic thinking. Thus, Ackroyd (1992) writes of the ‘sterile academicism’ of the paradigmatic approach given its central emphasis on ‘the production and restatement of general theory, and (its) acceptance that differences between theoretical positions are irreconcilable and fundamental’ (p. 112). Similarly, Hughes (1992), citing Burrell and Morgan’s (1979) paradigm approach, argues that the proliferation of alternative frameworks ‘imprisoned in a set of ontologically delimited paradigms which were essentially incommensurable, plunged the field into relative chaos’ (p. 296).

There are signs that organization studies are now emerging from the paradigm prison that Hughes criticizes. This emergence carries a number of implications that are in line with the methodological arguments of this paper. I list these implications below.

## Giving up the argument with 'science'

While paradigms ruled, it was very difficult for qualitative organizational researchers to discuss the ‘objective’ character of their knowledge. After all, standard issues of validity and reliability were seen to be tarred with the unacceptable brush of positivism. However, the attack on paradigmatic thinking has led to a healthy critique of relativism and incommensurability (see Reed, 1993, pp. 179–181). At last, qualitative researchers can debate and criticize the conventional wisdom that their kind of research can only be ‘exploratory’ or ‘anecdotal’ because of the lack of ‘massive datasets’ (Aldrich, 1992, p. 37) and therefore of statistical validity (Gummesson, 1988, p. 78). In contrast to essentialist views of the nature of scientific method, Parker’s (1993, p. 208) recent support for a Popperian position on refutability is highly refreshing.

## Defending case study methods

Once organization research ceases to be concerned with old debates about ‘science’, the path is freed for the vigorous development of case study methods. As Gummesson (1988, p. 79) points out, the aim of case studies is not ‘a superficial establishment of correlation’ but ‘to reach a fundamental understanding of . . . structure and process’. Moreover, as revealed by Heritage and Greatbatch’s (1986) study of hundreds of cases of audience response to political speeches, there is no reason why case study methods cannot be applied to massive data sets and standard issues of ‘reliability’ be addressed by systematic transcription of data.

## Using naturally occurring data

Once we no longer assume that a single paradigm lays out all the basic features of organizations, space is created to explore how organizations are routinely enacted. For instance, Schwartzman (1993), an anthropologist, remarks on ‘a turn towards routines’ that redirects our interest away from ‘organizations’ and towards ‘organizing’ (p. 36).

In support of this approach, Schwartzmann cites Weick's (1979) focus ‘on the organizing processes out of which a sense of organization unfolds and is enacted’ (Schwartzmann, 1993, p.36). Following Weick, Schwartzmann criticizes researchers who have failed to examine ‘everyday routines’ in organizational life and make them ‘taken for granted’ or ‘dismiss(ing) them as unimportant’ (p. 38). She goes on:

'The approach described here is motivated by an appreciation of the idea that the world does not appear to us as formalized concepts (such as structure or culture, or hierarchy or value), but only in particular routines and gatherings composed of specific actors (or agents) attempting to press their claims on one another and trying to make sense of what is happening to them' (p. 39).

Schwartzmann concludes by showing the kind of naturally occurring data that may bear on organizational ‘routines’. For instance, she suggests that we might look at organizational meetings as ‘practical accomplishments’ which contain ‘innumerable possibilities for displaying as well as finding out about one’s status in an organization’ (p. 43). Here, unlike many interview studies, we need not treat organizational members’ accounts as true or false indicators of organizational realities but as narratives with specific functions (for example shaping and sustaining images of the organization) (p. 44 passim).

## CONCLUSION

Schwartzmann's work links up with C. Wright Mills' (1940) classic sociological paper on 'vocabularies of motive'. Mills reminds us that nothing lies 'behind' people's accounts of their own or of other's motives. Thus, when 'motive-talk' happens, the appropriate analytic questions are: When does motive talk get done? What motives are available in the situation? What work does motive talk do?

Following Mills, a substantial number of studies have focused on organizational activities rather than reported ‘experiences’ and therefore treated ‘motive’ as significant only in so far as it is appealed to by organization members in their actual activities (for instance in completing personnel files or discussing management decisions). A key early text is Bittner (1965).

As shown in an earlier paper (Silverman, 1975), such studies have demonstrated the practical rationality of actors in courts, in police work, in social work, in juries and in selection interviews and promotion panels. More recently, Drew and Heritage (1992) have assembled a valuable set of research studies dealing with organizational interaction, including video-based research (Heath, 1997) and a study of the impact of the use of information technologies on the receipt of telephone calls to emergency services (Zimmermann, 1992).

I believe that such studies carry two important messages for the IS community. First, following Suchman (1987), the design of new IT must take account of everyday rationalities and avoid treating people as what Garfinkel (1967) calls ‘cultural dopes’, preprogrammed by rules. Second, qualitative IS researchers, along with other social science disciplines, would do well to think a long time before rushing into yet another interview-based study. For, although everything depends on what you are trying to achieve, decontextualized accounts of ‘meanings’ are very limited guides to the complexities of human-computer interaction.

## Biography

David Silverman is Professor of Sociology at Goldsmiths' College, London University. His research interests are qualitative methods, conversation analysis and professional-client communication in medical settings. He is the author of around 60 journal articles and book chapters and 14 books. Among his books are: Communication and Medical Practice (Sage, 1987), Interpreting Qualitative Data (Sage, 1993), Discourses of Counselling: HIV Counselling as Social Interaction (Sage, 1997), Harvey Sacks (Polity Press, 1998) and Doing Qualitative Research (Sage, forthcoming). He is the editor of Qualitative Research: Theory, Method and Practice (Sage, 1997) and of a new Sage series called Introducing Qualitative Methods, which will offer a 'hands-on' introduction to qualitative methods. He is committed to a dialogue between social scientists and the rest of the community and he is an active member of the Forum on Medical Communication, Royal Society of Medicine, London. He has held workshops on his research for parents, doctors and counsellors in the UK, USA, Australia, Trinidad & Tobago and many Scandinavian countries.
