# Constructing user requirements: a social process for a social context

Donal J. Flynn & \*Mohammad Davarpanah Jazi

Department of Computation, UMIST, PO Box 88, Manchester M60 1QD, UK, email: flynn@umist.ac.uk; www.co.umist.ac.uk/\~djflynn and \*Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Isfahan University of Technology, Isfahan 84156, Iran, email: istechun@dci.iran.com

Abstract. We discuss our approach to constructing user requirements whereby users build requirements models themselves. The approach, termed user-led requirements construction (ULRC), is a social process that addresses a major problem: the user-developer culture gap. An important feature of the approach is that we have conducted, with users, an interpretivist empirical evaluation study of its constituent event flow diagram (EFD) modelling method and associated training. The ULRC has also been used in a live environment. The results are that users regard the EFD as effective for the requirements process, the EFD may be used for further studies to deepen our knowledge of the requirements process and the results contribute towards a set of public results for general evaluation and improvement.

Keywords: Empirical evaluation, interpretivist study, requirements model, requirements process, social process, user-led design

## INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

## Problems in requirements process

The requirements process is arguably the most important phase within systems development as errors made in this phase have a potentially high impact on the eventual information system. However, a major barrier to achieving high-quality requirements within the requirements process in organizations (Goguen & Linde, 1993; Wood et al., 1994) is the user-developer culture gap. Two characteristics of developers that are different sides of the same coin and that contribute towards this gap are: (1) a lack of domain, ‘business’ knowledge and (2) an ‘engineering’ viewpoint with a bias towards technical concerns. As traditional approaches to requirements are developer-led these characteristics influence the process significantly.

An example of a problem resulting from the first characteristic is that requirements are not fully understood or may never be discovered by developers (Curtis et al., 1988) as the level of communication between users and developers concerning the domain is low. For the second characteristic, problems are: (1) developers and development methods tend to assume that requirements are completely known at the beginning of the requirements process and never change (Dobson & Strens, 1994); (2) users cannot understand requirements models built by developers, due to differences in viewpoint between user and developer and the fact that models are expressed in computer-oriented modelling languages (Flynn & Warhurst, 1994); and (3) inadequate attention is paid to the social context within which the computer system will function, with the result that many systems eventually fail (Luff et al., 1993).

## Our approach

To address the ‘gap’, we are developing an approach to the requirements process, termed user-led requirements construction (ULRC), that provides the capability for users to build their own requirements models. Such an approach is aligned with the views of several commentators who take the view that the traditional user-developer relationship is problematic (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994), for example Kjaer and Madsen (1995), who state that the user has a rather passive role, which, according to Harker et al. (1993), needs to be changed to become more active.

There is an increasing view that it is social, not technical factors, that are important within systems development in general (Lyytinen, 1988; Walsham, 1993), and we wish to improve factors such as user involvement, commitment and ownership of the requirements process.

As well as addressing the problems of the culture gap, such a user-led approach may provide three significant advantages. First, a post-modern viewpoint (Drucker, 1991) is that users are the origin of the best ideas for improving their work. Second, we suggest that the neohumanist approach (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; 1994), aimed at improving users' working environment as well as that of their colleagues, is more likely to be adopted by users than developers. Third, there is a general belief that system development can be made more successful by allowing users to participate in the early stages (Keil & Carmel, 1995). Although this approach has been debated for many years, a challenge remains, according to Bubenko (1995), in proposing suitable methods.

We have also conducted an interpretivist, empirical evaluation study of a key part of the approach, the event flow diagram (EFD) modelling method and its associated user training. Such evaluation is important, as the fact that many methods and approaches are proposed, with very little empirical evidence in their support, poses a serious problem (Galliers, 1992). Ryan (1995) states that a common factor of much work in the requirements area is the ‘dearth of experimental evidence’.

The structure of the rest of the paper is as follows: first, the ULRC approach to the requirements process is described, based on the view that requirements are socially constructed by users; this is followed by a discussion of its constituent EFD modelling method, a user-oriented method taking social and technical aspects into account. Next is a discussion of the rationale for the main features of the ULRC and EFD. The empirical study whereby users have evaluated the EFD is then described, followed by a discussion of the main results; finally, we present the conclusions and future work.

## USER-LED CONSTRUCTION OF REQUIREMENTS (ULRC) APPROACH

## Overview

The ULRC approach is illustrated in Fig. 1 in the form of a spiral. We regard the overall process of systems development as one which introduces change into an organization, transforming it from a current to a future state.

Round 1 consists of user training, in which the developer transfers knowledge and skills to the user for building the requirements model. In round 2, the emphasis is on constructing a model of the current domain, which is used as the basis for constructing the requirements model in round 3, which is a model of the future, desired domain. In this context the domain is typically (part of) an organization, containing an information system, in its environment.

The main purpose of the requirements model is to serve as a basis for further development stages such as SSADM requirements specification (SSADM, 1990).

## Social process

The approach recognizes that the requirements process is a social process and that it is based on the principles of iteration, which may occur within and between rounds, and user involvement. We take the view that requirements are not objective artefacts, available at the start of the requirements process, to be ‘captured’ like butterflies, as observed by Dobson and Strens (1994). Rather, requirements are emergent: they are socially constructed by the interactions involving users and developers in the requirements process. The fact that we model social aspects of requirements emphasises the need for this approach.

It is not possible to prescribe a sequenced set of activities and the rounds and their constituent activities should be interpreted as a guide only. Figure 1 is an idealized representation with arrows representing a ‘direction of intent’ to build the requirements model incrementally, rather than a dynamic exchange of information or flow of control.

## User training – round 1

## Aim and activities

The aim of the first, user training, round is to transfer the required knowledge of the EFD method to users so that they can build their own requirements models, and Fig. 1 shows that three activities are involved. First, the developer obtains a general knowledge of a user's work by carrying out a tape-recorded interview with the user in the workplace (information gathering). Based on this, the developer produces an EFD model (model building) of an area of the user's work from which training exercises will be taken. The training activity that follows consists of five one-to-one sessions, described below, with user and developer and is performed individually for each user.

The initial interview above takes 1 hour of user time and the subsequent training takes about 3 hours. In addition, the user requires about 4–5 hours of their own time in which to complete assignments. Time periods between sessions should not be so long that users find it hard to recall their modelling skills. It takes about 60 hours of developer time to train a user.

## Sessions

In session I, lasting 45 min, the main aims are to explain the EFD concepts and diagrammatic symbols, using a standard training booklet that includes examples. At the end of the session, the user is given an assignment to complete for next time, consisting of two text-based case studies that help the user to acquire practical EFD modelling skills. In session II, the developer checks the user assignment, gives standard solutions and discusses any issues. Further EFD concepts are explained and the user is given a self-assessment sheet to complete, testing understanding of the material covered so far. This session lasts 1 hour.

In session III, the self-assessment is checked and the user is given a validation assignment, consisting of EFD models based on the user's work into which the developer has introduced errors; the user is asked to validate these and indicate the errors. This session takes half an hour. Session IV consists of the developer checking the completed validation assignment with the user. An assignment is then given that requires users to finish an incomplete EFD model based on the user's work and to build an EFD model from scratch; the developer jointly with the user select the area of work to be modelled. This takes half an hour. In session V, the developer checks the completed building assignment with the user, clarifying any issues. This takes about 20 minutes.

Basing assignments on a very familiar situation (user work) helps user understanding of the initially unfamiliar modelling language of the EFD. Acknowledging the importance of ethnographic principles, we interview users individually at their workplace rather than relying on ‘prescribed practices’ (Sommerville et al., 1993).

## Current model – round 2

## Current and future domains

Round 2 is concerned with the domain characteristics at the time the requirements process is carried out and builds a current model of a relevant part of the domain. The current model is a preparation for round 3, which builds a model of the future, desired domain. We distinguish the indicative (current) and optative (future) moods (Jackson & Zave, 1993) within the requirements process and allocate them to different rounds.

## Activities

Round 2 provides the model-building activity for users to build current models, based on their own view of the domain, that they perform, by virtue of round 1 training, without developer assistance. The model-debating activity consists of group sessions, facilitated by a developer (or a user), in which users discuss models and attempt to reach a state of mutual understanding based on them. Together, model building and model debating help to construct a language for expressing typically a single current view of work practice. Information gathering consists of users seeking information concerning work practice from their colleagues and other sources such as standards documents and organizational mission statements, as well as information sought by the facilitator to assist in this role.

## Future model – round 3

## Aim

In round, the aim is to model the future domain. The activities build on the current model from round 2 in which users have expressed their work practice; this provides a familiar basis to highlight problems and improvements and leads naturally to the construction of the future model in which different options for change may be explored.

## Activities

The information-gathering activity is when users seek information concerning requirements from their colleagues, developers and other sources; information is also sought concerning technology impact. Model building is when users build future models, incorporating their views of requirements, and model debating comprises group sessions, with a facilitator, in which users discuss the requirements expressed in their models and attempt to reach a state of mutual understanding for the future domain. Potts et al. (1995) suggest that much requirements activity is asynchronous, in that most time is spent not in meetings but individually developing requirements.

Model debating is highly political, as users are typically aware that decisions concerning the future domain that will be prescribed in a computer system have a pervasive effect (Akrich, 1992). This is also the time when trade-offs may be made between the technical and social implications of a model. A simple example is when production department users want a parts code that is meaningful, whereas accounts department users want a sequential numeric code. Favouring the view of production over accounts leads to more complex parts code processing.

We do not attempt to be prescriptive about the nature of model debating, as we believe that social, political and economic forces interact in ways different in every situation. However, a problem exploration method such as SSM (Checkland, 1981) or the KJ method (Takeda et al., 1993) may be introduced if the situation is favourable. For example, when obvious requirements options are not being discussed and when the power distribution would permit such a discussion (Jackson, 1992).

Disadvantages of our approach are that some implicit requirements, for example values, may still not be expressed (Goguen & Linde, 1993), and unofficial work practices may not be revealed (Sommerville et al., 1993). Ethnographic observation may be used for these, if suspected.

## Iteration between rounds 2 and 3

There is usually informal interaction between these two rounds as the process of building a current model may spontaneously generate proposals for solutions to problems; similarly, focusing on a future domain may bring about the realization that the model of the current domain needs clarification or expansion. However, the end of round 2 represents a point at which it is considered that enough knowledge and understanding has been gained of the current domain and the time has come to focus on the future domain.

## EFD METHOD

A key element of our approach is the EFD method, which provides concepts and a language for users to build the requirements model and we now describe the main features of the EFD.

## Concepts

## Event flow

The EFD is based on the concept of event flow, which we define informally as: the flow of information or material initiated by the occurrence of an event together with its related organizational and environmental components.

An event flow relates together those components that constitute the behaviour that is initiated when an event occurs. When building an EFD model of a domain, the event flow is the central concept used for grouping (composing) representations of domain components.

An event flow is a type of scenario (Kuwana & Herbsleb, 1993; Gough et al., 1995) or problem frame (Jackson, 1995) that corresponds to user perceptions of a task in their organization, providing a familiar concept for users when building EFD models. It enables interactions that constitute purposeful activities (Luff et al., 1993) to be modelled in terms of a network (Latour, 1991) of actors, such as humans and machines, performing social and computer activities. It is a holistic concept that is increasingly the focus of effort when rethinking organizational behaviour using concepts such as business process (Hammer, 1996).

## Departments and partitioning

Organizations are naturally structured into smaller parts termed, for example, departments or divisions. To partition an organization to reduce complexity, the EFD uses current or future structures and uses the term department to refer to them; this term does not imply a hierarchical organization and may be mapped to any other term in use in the organization. A second partitioning device is the grouping of organizational behaviour into event flows as described above.

Combining the two concepts of partitioning by department and behaviour grouping based on event flow reduces the complexity of a domain. Each event flow in each department, or in the organization as a whole, may be considered as a unit, separate from other event flows, although separate event flows have their relationships to other event flows and departments clearly shown.

## Constructs and symbols

An EFD model represents components related to a computer system such as automatic processes, objects, information/material flows and events that initiate processes, as well as components of a more social nature such as departments, manual processes, roles/actors, managers, teams and external bodies. ## Abstractions

A diagrammatic EFD model provides three levels of diagrammatic detail, useful for users with different views of the domain: departmental event flow diagram (DEFD), organizational event flow diagram (OEFD) and overall departmental flow diagram (ODFD). To make diagrams easier to read, a flow table containing more detail is presented separately from the main diagram. Referenced by flow sequence number, this contains, for each flow, a natural language description and a flow type, for example D (document) or V (vocal).

## DEFD

The DEFD is the lowest level of diagrammatic detail and concerns one department in the organization. There are a set of DEFDs for each department, each DEFD concerning one event flow in that department.

One type of event flow, termed a single department event flow, starts and finishes in one department with no relationship to other departments. A multiple department event flow spans several departments and an event flow will be modelled over several DEFDs, each DEFD showing that part of the event flow that takes place in that department, as well as previous and next departments.

Figure 2 consists of part of a case study with an event flow involving three departments, showing the behaviour that results when a customer order is received in an order-processing department. The event flow continues in the accounts and production (control) departments where it is modelled by two other DEFDs, not shown here.

## OEFD

There is one OEFD for each event flow in the domain. It is on a middle level of detail and provides a view higher than that of the department. Gough et al. (1995) found that this type of high-level scenario interaction is useful. For each event flow, processes are omitted, showing only departments, external bodies, events and, optionally, objects. The OEFD is used to estimate the complexity of each event flow and also provides overview facilities by allowing event flows that span different departments to be shown on the same diagram. Figure 3 shows a partial OEFD corresponding to the three DEFDs of the case study.

## ODFD

Only one ODFD exists for the whole domain and it constitutes the top level of detail, showing only departments, external bodies and flows between them. Separate event flows are not distinguished. The ODFD overviews the complexity of the whole model.

## EFD tool

An EFD tool has been developed using C and XVT in a Windows environment on both Sun and PC. Extensive syntax checking is carried out based on the EFD formal definition. One important advantage of the tool is that the user only builds the DEFDs as the tool can build the ODFD and OEFDs automatically from DEFDs.

## ULRC – round 1 (training)

Training enables users to develop effective modelling skills and allows them to acquire a common language in which to communicate their requirements models to others; such user-built models address the problems of the user-developer culture gap described in the introduction. In our view, this will lead to the models more closely reflecting user needs and involves a paradigm shift in thinking whereby users, rather than developers, are trusted to produce these models.

Since traditional views of the requirements process were formulated, we feel that social changes have occurred that ease the acceptance of this user-led approach. For example, users are more familiar with the design of computer systems and in the post-modern view of organizations (Clegg, 1990; Drucker, 1991) users are more highly educated, more autonomous and are often viewed as the best source of new ideas for improving work practice. Within the context of outsourcing, users may have to be ‘RE experts’ (Greenspan, 1995) and according to a research report (End users to take the lead in IT, Computing, 18 May 1995) this type of participation constitutes an increasing trend. Many organizations appoint IT co-ordinators among their users; such individuals might see a role such as requirements co-ordinator as an extension of this job.

Cost is a frequently perceived disadvantage of user participation, as the most valuable users are those most needed to participate; against this, however, should be set findings such as those from an empirical study by Rauterberg and Strohm (1994) that, over the longer term, user participation may lead to cost and time savings.

Some users may be unwilling to risk an increase in their workload; however, many users may be keen to learn about model building as they will acquire new skills and, perhaps more importantly, they may find themselves better placed than developers to adopt a neohumanist approach (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989) that seeks to improve their working environment as well as that of colleagues.

In general, training sends users the powerful message that their informed participation is valued, and serves to increase their process ownership and commitment (Santhanam & Sein, 1994).

## ULRC – round 2 (current model)

## Reasons for building a current model

Distinguishing between current and future domains is important as ‘... understanding/assessing the current system provides the business case to construct a new system’ (El Emam & Madhavji, 1995). Thus, the benefits expected from a future domain are more convincing if they are based on a thorough understanding of the current domain, emphasizing the improvements to be made that will result in the future domain; the implications of proposed changes are also made clearer.

In the requirements process it is important that a climate is created in which discussion of problems is encouraged and supported as this is not necessarily an everyday occurrence in organizations. Fitzgerald and Murphy (1994) view a current model as a means for discussion to be assisted and unarticulated problem areas to emerge. Finally, a review of work practice, necessitating a current model, is frequently initiated by a proposal for a new computer system (Fowler et al., 1996) to avoid the preservation of ‘dead wood’ as new technology often requires organizational change.

A current model is almost always required, for the above reasons. Even in the situation in which a set of work practices including a new computer system are to be introduced into an organization on a ‘greenfield’ site, there will be some existing work practices that will require integration.

## Benefits of users building their own models

Culture gap. The fact that users build their own models, without a developer ‘translating’ their intentions, addresses the problem of the culture gap. The types of problems that arise are first that users and developers use different languages for communicating about the domain, with the result that users do not understand the modelling languages of developers (Flynn & Warhurst, 1994). Second, users and developers do not share ontological views, as developers view the organization differently and do not understand the domain sufficiently well (Curtis et al., 1988). Finally, users may be unable or unwilling to articulate their expertise (Sommerville et al., 1993) in traditional interviews; producing a current model allows the user to express his or her work and provides a familiar basis to identify improvements.

Social within the technical. Another aspect of the gap is that developers engage in many activities that, although perceived as neutral and ‘technical’, are in fact social (Bloomfield, 1992) as they are founded upon assumptions (Klein & Hirschheim, 1987) concerning, for example, the nature of knowledge, the structure of organizations and the existence or importance of power and control. We give three examples of this social within the technical phenomenon.

First, developers often favour the epistemological view that domain knowledge is objective, unchanging and precise. This influences the way in which they conduct the requirements process, assuming that requirements exist a priori, can be formally specified, will not change throughout the process and can be captured by a precise method with sequenced steps. Second, modelling techniques, such as top-down decomposition or object-oriented design, may be used that assume that users perceive organizations as being structured similarly to these techniques, thus imposing inappropriate categories on user descriptions (Goguen & Linde, 1993).

A final example emphasizes user sensitivity to the sociopolitical nature of the process whereby a mutual understanding of the current model is built, whereas developers may assume that such political considerations are unimportant or non-existent, regarding the process as a ‘technical’ problem to be solved by inconsistency resolution. Users are more motivated to invest effort in seeking a consensus and are more aware of the distribution of power and its key role in controlling and legitimating organizational views.

## Mutual understanding of current work practice

The model debating activity is needed for users to arrive at a mutual understanding of current work practice. This understanding does not have an a priori existence, but instead must be socially constructed as individuals have different viewpoints and values concerning the work environment and their role within it. Such an understanding is required as current computer systems do not generally support inconsistent viewpoints.

This is probably most clearly shown by the fact that descriptions of work practice vary considerably. Managers may have idealized or partial views concerning the practices of their staff, and users who have recently joined an organization may have significantly different objectives for their work compared with existing users for whom, perhaps, current work was designed. Contradictions between documented practices and practices as they are actually carried out are commonplace. In addition, different users may have only partial views concerning domain work practices; for example, when one organizational section is to be merged into another, this may require the work in the ‘external’ section to be understood, discussed and possibly ‘re-viewed’. There are also differences due to the terminology and the level of detail used by different users. The fact that we model social aspects heightens these differences. The shared EFD language assists the model debating activity.

## ULRC – round 3 (future model)

## Expressing requirements

The difficulties that users find in articulating their work, briefly alluded to in the discussion of the current model, also apply to expressing their requirements, and they may be due to embarrassment or political factors in the organization. For example, social requirements, such as who should perform a process may be implicit, are likely to be embedded in power structures or in organizational/individual values (Dobson & Strens, 1994) and may not be articulated.

There are also related problems, such as the ‘say-do’ problem of users being unable to describe work and the fact that user accounts of work given in interview often differ from actual practice (Goguen & Linde, 1993). Allowing users to express their requirements as work practice descriptions by building the future model addresses these problems and provides the opportunity for their knowledge and experience to be used in improving their work environment.

## Social and technological 'fit'

Another problem concerns social requirements, that is those requirements concerned with the social context of the new computer system. These are often viewed in terms of the 'fit' desired between the new system and work practice and are typically neglected in the requirements process. However, the problem is that there is no 'external' knowledge that can predict the impact of a given technology on a social system or vice versa. Further, as implied earlier, there is a view that technology is not an objective but a social artefact as it is built by practices based on social assumptions; technology is capable of different effects and is susceptible to different meanings depending on how it is used and who uses it (Bickerton & Siddiqi, 1993; Luff et al., 1993).

Taking this into account, our approach is to allow users to use their ‘internal’ knowledge to devise work practices incorporating the desired ‘fit’, based on their organizational experience and on developer advice as to likely impact. The EFD allows this by modelling social aspects of the organization. Bringing this broad range of knowledge and experience to bear is also likely to widen narrow notions of technical ‘efficiency’ that developers have traditionally held concerning system objectives.

## Advantage for developers

A side-effect of work practice descriptions is that they are typically sufficiently detailed so as to reduce ambiguity about user intentions for the future domain, addressing a frequent complaint from developers concerning user imprecision.

## Event flow diagram

## Aims

The EFD builds on and improves existing representational methods rather than instituting a radical paradigm shift (DeFoe, 1994). The main aims in the design of the EFD were to develop a modelling method suitable for users that: (1) enables users to build models that correspond to their views of their organization; (2) uses constructs that represent familiar organizational components and that are expressed in a language that is not computer oriented; and (3) models social as well as technical (computer-oriented) aspects.

## Genesis

The genesis of the EFD is as follows. Research in the object-oriented (OO) area, for example by Fichman and Kemerer (1992), indicated that users found difficulty using objects as a grouping concept; we therefore decided to follow a process-oriented direction and to improve the user orientation of traditional process modelling methods such as data flow diagrams and the process dependency diagram of Information Engineering (IE), and more recent workflow modelling methods such as the workflow reference model discussed in, for example, Alonso et al. (1995). According to Earl and Khan (1994), ‘Processes are real and show how the business works’.

The most significant differences between the EFD and traditional methods are the event flow and partitioning concepts. The event flow concept was developed from practical experience in process analysis with users in organizations, which indicated that groupings of events and their related processes corresponded to the way in which users dynamically viewed their organization, in contrast to the view embodied by DFDs and IE, fundamentally structured by functional decomposition, a criticism made in general by Jackson (1995) and Bloomfield (1992). For partitioning purposes, to reduce organizational complexity, we took the view that organizational structures, such as departments or sections, were more natural than abstractions devised by developers.

Although many workflow modelling methods group processes similarly to the EFD, often using the concept of business process, they frequently omit the event construct and do not use organizational structure as a partitioning device; in addition, these methods are often oriented to the design level, using computer-oriented notations, and are thus unsuitable for users on the requirements level.

## Languages

Some requirements methods use natural language to express the requirements model (Salek et al., 1994). This has advantages of user understandability but its major disadvantages are ambiguity and lack of abstraction capability. At the other extreme, different types of formal representation are used by many authors (Yu, 1993). From the developer's point of view, a formal model is easy to define, maintain and process by the machine with precise reasoning support. However, if the model needs to be understood by the user, then the user needs to be familiar with the language used. This usually causes users a problem (Jackson & Zave, 1993).

It is necessary to be completely clear about the purposes of a requirements model: is it aimed, implicitly or explicitly, at the user, the developer or both? The EFD is suitable for users as its concepts and constructs directly correspond to familiar organizational components, and we describe later an empirical study with users that has evaluated its effectiveness. The EFD addresses the problem of the neglect of social issues (Hughes et al., 1995) by representing work practice as a whole, including social as well as computer system aspects, and showing their interaction.

In addition, there is a mathematical foundation for the EFD in which, first, the semantics and the rules that should be satisfied by a correct EFD model are defined in terms of a first-order predicate calculus, and, second, an abstract syntax is defined based on Backus-Naur form (BNF) conventions to represent the correct structure of a model. This satisfies developer needs for model reasoning, view presentation and downwards mapping.

## The Elephant's Child, Rudyard Kipling

The EFD is concerned with the What, How, Where and Who, and it is important to be clear about the ‘serving-men’ that we intentionally omit from the EFD. We briefly discuss three issues: unrestricted decomposition, temporal factors and organizational goals.

Unrestricted decomposition (typically process decomposition), identified as a key issue by El Emam and Madhavji (1995), concerns the detailed How and is absent as an abstraction mechanism for two reasons: (1) we find from experience that departmental and event flow partitioning are adequate for coping with model complexity; and (2) the requirements process is, by nature, bottom-up (Takeda et al., 1993) and decomposition may produce unnecessary levels of detail (Jackson, 1995), allowing requirements to resemble low-level designs. We also discussed earlier how decomposition can allow unfamiliar abstractions to be built (Dobson & Strens, 1994; Hughes et al., 1995) by being based on inappropriate social assumptions.

Temporal factors concern the When and may be applied, for example, to event flows or event flow interaction. We decided that temporal factors that could be reasoned about would increase the complexity of the EFD significantly and should be considered at a later phase in the research. Kuwana and Herbsleb (1993) found When questions made up only 4% of questions asked in requirements/design meetings.

Organizational goals constitute the Why and encapsulate the motives and goals that underlie and justify current or future organizational work practice. We shall discuss this in the conclusions under future work.

## Social assumptions underlying EFD concepts and constructs

EFD concepts and constructs have been devised to represent familiar components within user views of organizations and use familiar component names, to avoid imposing artificial views or terms on users in model building. The exception to this is the name event flow, which is chosen to give the concept a unique identity.

## EMPIRICAL EVALUATION OF THE EFD

## Interpretivist approach

It is unfortunately the case, as discussed in Galliers (1992), that many methods proposed for information systems development lack empirical evaluation, whereas Byrd et al. (1992) consider the level of evaluation research to be ‘distressing and even shocking’. One consequence of this is that methods are proposed with no indications of their effectiveness in, for example, practical situations.

We carried out an EFD evaluation study based on the interpretivist approach (Walsham, 1993), which emphasizes the relativistic nature of the social world, viewed as being constructed through the subjective experiences of individuals, and that focuses on understanding the ways in which an individual creates, modifies and interprets their world. This approach, in contrast to the positivist approach (Burrell & Morgan, 1979; Potts & Newstetter, 1997), is appropriate in those situations in which ‘there is no objective reality to be discovered by researchers and replicated by others’ (Walsham, 1993), and is thus suited to our area of investigation, characterized by the absence of objective facts relevant to method evaluation by users and the existence of unknown and possibly significant influences on their evaluation.

The case study research strategy of the field study type (Cavaye, 1996) was used as we wanted the EFD to be evaluated by users in their everyday organizational environment. The study was planned as a multiple case study (El Emam & Madhavji, 1995) and there were two main parts: (1) gathering of data from study participants, resulting in 16 cases; and (2) analysis of case data into the results.

## Aims and method

The aims of the study were (1) to carry out a practical evaluation of the EFD method by users to determine its effectiveness within the requirements process; and (2) to evaluate the training process used to transfer EFD knowledge and skills to the users. A practical evaluation was necessary as we wanted users to base their evaluation on situations close to actual experience in the requirements process. The study stages were:

1 Interviewing. Users were interviewed by a team member so that an EFD model of their work could be built for use in subsequent stages.

2 EFD transfer. The users were taught the required knowledge of the EFD by a team member and a self-assessment was given to the users at the end of the stage.

3 Validation. To examine the ability of the users to validate EFD models, they were given an erroneous model of some parts of their work to check and were also required to provide a corrected model.

4 Building. Users modelled some parts of their work. This was to investigate the feasibility of the users to build an EFD model of their own work without help from the developer.

5 Evaluation. A semistructured questionnaire was given to users that asked about the EFD and the training. After collection, questionnaires were analysed to produce the results.

These stages were conducted in seven weekly one-to-one sessions between team member and user, at or near the user's workplace in their organization, with a total duration of six hours. Stages were tape recorded. Stages 2–4, comprising the EFD training, were accompanied by a training booklet that included examples based on the user's work, as well as assignments, given to users to complete in their own time. Training session detail has been described earlier when discussing round 1 of the ULRC.

## Influences on participants

## Guards introduced

As the interpretivist approach emphasises subjective individual experiences we briefly discuss two types of study influences (Sadler & Kitchenham, 1996) that may distort these experiences and some of the guards we introduced to minimize their effects.

Learning curve. To guard against the problem of participants' EFD knowledge being inadequate for evaluation, we planned a thorough EFD training phase. We also used examples from each individual user's work practice as the basis for the majority of user assignments, aiming to avoid the novelty effect whereby the difficulty of modelling an unfamiliar case study might affect the user's evaluation of the EFD.

Doctor effect. A characteristic of the interpretivist approach is that the researchers are not objective observers but are involved in the research situation. The doctor effect arises when the expectations or values of the evaluators distort participants' evaluation, for example evaluators may be too cynical or enthusiastic. We guarded against this effect in two ways. First, the evaluation team were instructed to approach their work from the viewpoint that they were engaged in a research exercise in which criticism of the EFD method was as welcome as praise; they were told that the overall goal was to improve the EFD based on practical use and that they were to expect a spectrum of views. They were asked to convey this attitude to the participants. Second, EFD training was systematized for all participants and carried out by different members of the evaluation team, within the same organization, to spread any evaluator influence during the key training phase.

## Researcher background

We now discuss researcher background as this may suggest bias to the reader (Calloway & Ariav, 1995). The evaluation team comprised the authors and two research students in information systems. The authors had carried out previous informal, empirical studies with the EFD and (other) users. This work had led them to form an opinion that the scope of the EFD constructs was adequate for requirements modelling, but they did not know how easy or difficult it would be for users to build their own models; they also did not know how successful the training would be. Both authors may be considered to be stakeholders in the future of the EFD. The other two team members were MSc students in information systems, supervised by DJF. At the beginning of the study their opinions regarding the EFD were privately estimated by DJF to be neutral (third member) and sceptical (fourth member).

## Participant background

Sixteen users from six organizations participated in this study. The organizations were: Manchester City Council, Oldham Metropolitan Borough Council, Manchester Libraries and Theatre department, UMIST Bursar's department, Employment Service (Didsbury Job Centre) and Christie Hospital, Withington. Their areas of work were administrative and transaction processing, with nine participants on the managerial or supervisory levels and seven on the operational level; none were IT staff. Seven had experience in the diagrammatic modelling of work procedures. Four were slightly familiar with diagrammatic methods such as DFD and flowcharts and two of them also had some programming experience.

## STUDY RESULTS

## Overview

The questionnaire consisted of 17 open, qualitative questions to gather participant views on modelling, human and organizational issues. In addition, eight quantitative questions asked for a weight to be given to an aspect of the EFD method or the training: the weights ranging from 1 (very unfavourable) to 6 (very favourable). We considered that a response value of 3 or less would indicate that the participant considered that particular aspect a problem.

The questionnaire was structured into sections using categories that we felt relevant to evaluation, based on those in the method evaluation literature (Yadav et al., 1988; Maiden & Sutcliffe, 1993; El Emam & Madhavji, 1995). These categories, consisting of understandability, completeness, communication and level of detail, were explained in the questionnaire to introduce concepts whose meaning might be shared by the participants and that would guide us in the data analysis. ## Evaluation of EFD training

Training was rated highly with a modal value of 6 and participants also made several suggestions for improvement.

## Training material

Some of the participants complained about the small number of solved examples provided in the training material. A suggestion was:

'It would have been useful spending more time getting used to the idea of mapping out a process, e.g. making a cup of tea . . . as the process is familiar to users, thus they can quickly understand the concept and get to grasp with the symbols within a familiar context.'

## Building

Some participants spent more than the anticipated time carrying out the building assignment due to the need to look at their notes frequently. There were several comments that building diagrams from scratch (the second assignment) seemed very difficult at first glance, but after some thinking and defining possible work situations it was straightforward. For example, one participant reported that:

‘Any problems I had were due to lack of time in building up my expertise.’

Another point was the difficulty of drawing diagrams manually. Several participants suggested that a hand-held stencil containing symbols might be useful and one used a drawing tool on his PC.

## Time schedule

Several participants commented that the weekly gap between sessions was too long as they could forget previous material. The problem was aggravated if participants took one or two weeks' leave during the study.

## Evaluation of EFD method

To preserve the context within which participant responses were made, important in an interpretivist study, we present these results under the relevant questionnaire categories.

## Understandability

Under this category, we asked how easy/difficult it was for the participant to understand an EFD, and we found that participants felt able to express modifications to the method. For example, the comment below, echoed by others, led to a decision to simplify process and object shading:

'I found the shading of change processes and computerised objects a little confusing. A more straightforward approach for me would have been to shade both computerised processes and objects.'

Some participants had problems modelling objects, confusing them with flows. We found that the term object was ambiguous for many participants; several mentioned that it would be better to remove objects from the diagrams or make them optional. In addition, a comment reported below in Level of detail brought us round to the view that the term is more suited to the developer rather than to the user, and we shall investigate the use of alternatives.

## Completeness

Under this heading we asked whether the EFD had the ability to completely model all necessary organizational aspects, and one participant drew attention to the fact that flow logic is binary only and that multipart logic may be useful. Two participants stated that the EFD could provide a time construct to model how long it takes for a process or an event flow to complete. A comment under the final question asking for method improvements was:

'1. Introducing some concept of timescale. 2. Introducing a symbol for ‘delays’ as these are important when trying to introduce more efficient ways of working.'

However, we had deliberately omitted a time construct that could be reasoned with on the basis that it would significantly complicate the EFD; our current view is that temporal issues are a candidate for investigation but require careful thought. A comment on the middle level diagram (OEFD) made under Level of detail was:

‘it would need to show details of performance to be of use to our management.’

Thus a potentially useful addition to the EFD could be, for example, measures of resources consumed, such as time, money, materials or personnel.

## Level of detail

Participants were asked to rate the EFD for its level of detail and appropriateness for different levels of users in the organization. For the middle-level diagram (OEFD), a participant who had modelling experience stated:

‘it is a good way to understand a fairly lengthy process in a short period of time and easier to communicate the process to others.’

## Communication

Here we aimed to find out the effectiveness of the EFD at exchanging knowledge and ideas between users and between users and developers. One participant observed:

‘I think that with anything new there’s a “breaking in” process. The diagrams look confusing at first but at a second glance they are fairly easy to read.’

Validation. To take part effectively in the interactive process of constructing requirements, users need to be able to understand the models of other users to find discrepancies and identify views different to their own. The weights given to validation were in line with our experience throughout the study as most of the participants had no major problem analysing diagrams, spotting errors and expressing their changes. However, users are only human as:

'It was necessary to concentrate quite hard to ensure no mistakes had been made or to correct any errors. Consequently, errors may be missed.'

Building. We wanted to check that users could build a model that correctly represented their view of the organization to themselves and to their colleagues. A common point was that more training and practice would be useful, for example:

‘starting from scratch was a problem. The diagrams are fairly easy to read but to build they are time consuming (probably because I need more practice).'

## Correspondence of EFD view to participant view

It was important to check that the EFD view, based on the two concepts of partitioning by department and modelling tasks by event flow, corresponded to the participants' view. One participant stated:

'I can see that there would be benefits in staff throughout the organisation having EFDs (at an appropriate level) for their own area of work and responsibility. Managers would gain a clear overview and be able to track the progression of work through the departments or sections under their control.'

## Comparison with other methods

Participants who were familiar with other modelling techniques expressed some comments based on their experience. For example:

'I have used flow chart type diagrams to describe the procedures covered by these models . . . These diagrams can involve a lot of descriptive texts and a number of options which can make them look complex. EFDs seem to be simpler to follow.'

Another participant who was on a managerial level and had modelling experience with data flow diagrams stated:

‘data flow diagrams are not as easy to view as EFDs and require considerably more attention to grasp.’

However, different views were given by two participants from the same organization who used English text manuals describing the organization that they found more useful than the EFD. One stated:

‘... (I) used written manuals and descriptive instructions which I have found much more useful than EFD.’

They mentioned that there should be more explanation for the activities than just the symbols, their names and the related roles/actors. As the EFD tool was not discussed with the participants, they were not aware of the facilities in the tool to accommodate extra descriptions. This may have gone some way to meet their criticism. It may be relevant that participants from this organization complained that the study was conducted at the end of the financial year when their workload was at its peak.

## Wider uses of requirements models

Some other uses of the EFD, in addition to requirements modelling, were listed in a table and participants were asked to rate them as major or minor. Several proposed uses suggested that a requirements model has advantages beyond the systems development process. For example:

'I feel the process could be used within an organization about to undertake change. It gives all staff from senior management to the newest recruit a picture of (i) what the organization does (ii) who does what and (iii) where each individual fits into the whole.'

Several participants mentioned benefits such as provision of monitoring and system administration procedures, reviewing methods of working and rewriting procedures with a view to introducing more efficient methods. Other uses suggested were training manuals for computer packages and a basis for service level agreements. Another comment was:

'Within a section, only one member of staff regularly undertakes a particular process or procedure. Difficulties arise when another person has to cover that work due to leave or illness. EFDs would constitute a useful reference manual in these situations.'

## Summary and discussion of results

## EFD method and training feedback

Participant views provided feedback for potentially useful changes to the EFD, indicating problems in the method, the training or in the difficulty of modelling with little or no previous experience. Within the context of an interpretivist study the EFD designers needed to make judgements on these matters. Likely EFD method changes are: diagrammatic symbol shading; multiple as well as binary flow logic; an option to show processes encapsulated in each department; and a more user-oriented replacement for the term object. In future we may consider adding temporal and resource features to the EFD.

Concerning the EFD training we may: (1) supply more solved examples in the training material for building, including a familiar ‘cup of tea’ example; (2) provide a stencil to assist building; (3) increase the time estimate for exercises as it is important for user self-confidence that these are realistic; and (4) concentrate training into a shorter time period to allow easier recall.

## Feedback for future studies

EFD generalizations. The quantitative and qualitative responses confirmed our view, formed during the study, that these users were able to perform validation and building well. There were no modal ratings below 4 for the quantitative questions, which we had decided would indicate a problem. The training was highly evaluated and there were positive comparisons to previously used methods (for example flowcharts and DFDs). Several participants commented that a requirements model could be used for many purposes other than systems development, also reported in Calloway and Ariav (1995). We therefore generate the following generalizations:

Generalization 1. Within the requirements process, the EFD is a method for building requirements models that are understandable by users.

Generalization 2. After training, users can build EFD models themselves to their own satisfaction.

Generalization 3. EFD training is an effective learning process for allowing users to build their own EFD models.

Applicability to further studies. Walsham states, ‘For interpretive evaluation exercises, the broad purpose is to deepen understanding and to generate motivation and commitment,’ (Walsham, 1993, p. 181) and he quotes Orlikowski and Baroudi on the issue of non-generalizability, who comment:

‘… every particular social relation is the product of generative forces or mechanisms operating at a more global level, and hence the interpretive analysis is an induction (guided and couched within a theoretical framework) from the concrete situation to the social totality beyond the individual case.’

Some of our results lend support to findings from other research, and, in general, the results motivate us to use the EFD as the basis for the design of further studies, in alternative settings, not only to obtain EFD feedback but also to deepen our knowledge of the requirements process. The results indicate that the EFD has the potential to allow users to participate fully in this process.

We would like to see these results form part of a set of results of similarly, publicly tested methods and theories for the benefit of a broad group of interested researchers and practitioners. Our strong opinion is that such empirical research is a sine qua non for method feedback and improvement, for demonstrating to a wider audience that a method has been found to be relevant and effective in a real-world situation. In this way (Walsham, 1993, p. 6):

'Theory can be compared, evaluated and improved by this form of public testing; the result is not the generation of “best” theory, but the creation of intersubjectively tested theoretical approaches, considered of value to a broader group than a single individual.'

## Similar work

In our study there was a relatively small number (16) of participants. However, this is common in similar studies that evaluate a method or methods in a practical manner, such as in Gough et al. (1995), Potts et al. (1995), Vessey and Conger (1994) and Calloway and Ariav (1995), in which the numbers of participants were 8, 10, 6 and 16 respectively. These studies included developers or IT staff as well as users among the participants and did not differentiate their results. No similar study is known to us that evaluates training as well as one, distinct, method with non-IT users in a practical manner in their organizational environment.

## Live use

An opportunity for observing the approach in live use arose, towards the end of the evaluation study, in the form of a consultancy project in a manufacturing firm. The ULRC approach was defined and was used for approximately one year. Experience in the requirements process in this live system development project is that, after training, the four users involved were able to build satisfactory EFD models by themselves, one of them using the EFD tool on his PC. The EFD scaled up to an industrial size problem with no difficulty.

## CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

## Summary

The ULRC approach is characterized by the fact that:

1 Users build models by themselves after a training process in the EFD method.

2 The EFD method provides a user-oriented modelling language that takes into account the social nature of organizations and is based on a scenario concept of event flow.

We believe that placing the emphasis on users to build their own requirements models is a promising approach, compared with traditional methods, for improving the quality of requirements. It is an approach that acknowledges the importance of social factors by addressing the problems of the user–developer culture gap and improving user ownership of the requirements process. It also may introduce a brea(d)th of relevant knowledge and experience into the process and provide users with a direct opportunity to improve their working environment.

3 The EFD and the training process have been empirically evaluated by users in an interpretivist study. User responses have provided improvements to both the EFD and the training process and have generated generalizations that indicate that the EFD is effective in the requirements process. The ULRC has been already been used in a live requirements environment and scales up to a real domain.

## ULRC and EFD development

With its emphasis on the social construction of requirements, the ULRC is likely to benefit from having its theoretical foundations explained more clearly, and we propose to develop such a grounding in terms of Giddens' structuration theory (Walsham & Han, 1990).

Concerning the EFD, modelling organizational goals (Feather, 1993; Yu, 1993) may be found useful during maintenance, for example when attempting to understand the rationale for a particular design feature, or during the requirements process, to explore in more detail problems whose solutions are being attempted. However, Kuwana and Herbsleb (1993) found that Why questions made up only 6% of questions asked in requirements/design meetings, and a problem about modelling such goals is that by nature they are high level and accordingly ambiguous.

This may be overcome by modelling goals in relationship to the work practice that is intended to achieve these goals and we are considering modifying the Checkland (1981) CATWOE root definition criteria and incorporating them in the definition of an event flow. Ideally, there will be a reference to a characteristic of the future domain that is detectable and that may be used as a basis for assessing system quality. Other possibilities for future development include the incorporation of temporal and resource features into the EFD.

## Empirical studies

Our study adopted the interpretivist approach, in contrast to the positivist approach, aimed at gathering a wide range of subjective user views on the EFD. We felt that the approach was successful in gathering these views and producing results, which, as well as the evaluation study method, may be combined with other studies, involving the EFD and other, similar methods, to build a public set of results for general evaluation and improvement. In this way, theory may be moved forward. To address the ‘doctor’ effect it may be necessary for such studies to be carried out by a team independent of the method designers, for example as part of an assessment similar to that of the CMM or SPICE. To have confidence in the applicability of proposed requirements methods (Ryan, 1995), we believe that it is very important that they be evaluated in a user environment.

For future studies we intend to evaluate the EFD as well as the ULRC with more users to obtain a wider range of views. We might standardize on a factor, for example users who have been actively involved in a requirements process.

In addition to studies resulting in method feedback, we also intend to design interpretivist studies aimed at deepening our knowledge of the requirements process; these will use the EFD, taking advantage of its user orientation indicated by our study results to allow users to become fully involved. Alternative data gathering and analysis techniques such as video and grounded theory may be used, as it is possible that, for the study reported here, some participants found it difficult to express their views in the form of questionnaire responses.

These studies will result in descriptive accounts of the requirements process and may be used in an inductive manner, to support findings reported elsewhere and suggest fresh insights. For example, supporting the findings of Calloway and Ariav (1995), participants in our study took the view that there were several uses for requirements models, not being limited to systems development, and appeared motivated to produce them. Another example, relating to an issue well understood in psychology, but perhaps not in the information systems evaluation area, concerns the variations between participants in their cognitive abilities. Specifically, some participants found a given assignment easy, whereas others found it the most difficult; such variations can obviously affect overall evaluations and underline the applicability of the interpretivist approach.

## Biographies

Donal J. Flynn has degrees in chemical physics, industrial sociology and computing from different UK universities. He has enjoyed a successful career in industry, working for a variety of companies in the UK and abroad, such as IBM(UK), National Westminster Bank, Rank Xerox and American Express in the UK, the Philips group in the Netherlands and Esso in Belgium. He has held roles such as programmer, systems analyst, database designer, project manager and consultant. At the Department of Computation in UMIST he is currently a senior lecturer and his research interests are in theoretical, empirical and industrial aspects of the requirements process.

Mohammad Davarpanah Jazi studied electrical and computer engineering at universities in Iran, the United States and the UK, obtaining a PhD in computing from UMIST in 1996. He has undertaken consultancy projects for systems development in several large companies and he is currently an Associate Professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Isfahan University of Technology, Iran. His research interests range from the requirements process to the formal specification of intelligent tool assistants.
