# A new perspective on the dynamics of information technology-enabled strategic change

R.L. Hsiao & R.J. Ormerod

Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK, email: orsro@wbs.warwick.ac.uk; or R.L.Hsiao@warwick.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper explores the dynamic nature of information technology (IT)-enabled strategic change by using empirical evidence from a variety of organizational contexts. The pilot research described here uses the MIT 1990s framework (Scott-Morton, 1991) as the underpinning theory to challenge the dominant approach to the management of strategic change. The preliminary findings provide new insights into how strategic change is pursued and offer a new perspective from which the propositions suggested by Scott-Morton (1991) and Yetton et al. (1994) can be reformulated. Four change patterns (archetypes) are identified that describe planned or emergent modes of change, driven by different change elements under various circumstances. These findings lead to a redesign of the MIT 1990s framework and suggest new ways of understanding how strategic change can be sustained. Further research is required to explore the link between the archetypes identified and the various methods of change.

Key words: Business transformation, dynamic perspective, IT-enabled change, strategic change.

## INTRODUCTION

Today's turbulent environment requires businesses to modify and extend their traditional approach to change. Businesses now have to undertake strategic change and transform themselves into adaptive enterprises in order to confront and respond to increasing complexity and uncertainty. One way of achieving this goal is to align information technology (IT) with business strategy. IT provides more innovative strategic choices so that businesses are able to manage effectively such critical factors as quality, lead time, cost, risk and their relationships with customers and suppliers (see the examples in Venkatraman, 1994). This task requires a synergy between strategy, organization, processes, technology and people in order to sustain the quality of ‘interdependence’ and thus achieve competitive advantage (Rockart & Short, 1991). For the purpose of discussion, the term ‘people’ will be used to indicate ‘individuals and roles' in the MIT 1990s framework. The traditional approach to strategy formulation is inadequate because of its static and linear nature. The study of the management of IT-enabled strategic change remains a relatively unexplored area, and the provision of new perspectives is essential if such complex change is to be understood.

The research reported here seeks to develop one such new perspective from a dynamic viewpoint. It attempts to investigate how organizations achieve operational and organizational effectiveness through the strategic use of information technology. Based on the previous research of Scott-Morton (1991) and Yetton et al. (1994), an explanation of the nature of the key change elements involved in the management of IT-enabled strategic change is sought. The research is both explanatory and exploratory, aiming to provide both an in-depth analysis of the strategic change path and the frameworks needed to characterize such change. Instead of suggesting prescriptive solutions, this research is intended to provide an understanding of the key elements that characterize strategic change and an exploration of the change patterns that lie behind the cases examined.

The paper begins with a broad review of current studies of strategic change, highlighting the theoretical boundaries of IT-enabled strategic change. Second, it evaluates the current dominant approach to strategic change and identifies the need for an integrative and dynamic perspective. Third, it discusses the research framework and methods that have been used. Fourth, it explains the empirical data acquired from five pilot cases that formed the basis for the theoretical synthesis. Finally, the paper summarizes the findings, discusses their implications, and points to the potential for future research.

## ‘IT-ENABLED’ STRATEGIC CHANGE

The study of strategic change resides mainly in four areas: strategic management, industrial engineering, organizational behaviour and psychology. The early studies of strategic change in the 1970s and 1980s were triggered by the experience of economic recession, globalization and the transformation to an information-based economy (Hinings & Greenwood, 1988, p. 4). The emphasis was on studying the grand strategy of a firm's behaviour in negotiating change in relation to the firm's external environment:

'strategic change is descriptive of [the] magnitude of alteration in ... the culture, structure, product market and geographical positioning of the firm, recognizing the second-order effects, or multiple consequences, of any such changes and, of course, the transparent linkage between firms and their sectoral, market and economic context' Pettigrew (1988, pp. 2–3).

The macro view observes how firms change and compete in order to sustain their long-term survival in the industrial, sectoral and market contexts, and often involves longitudinal investigation (see, for example, Johnson, 1987; Pettigrew, 1988; Hinings & Greenwood, 1988).

The later studies of strategic change shifted their focus to organizational behaviour and operational improvement in response to increasing competition (Blumenthal & Haspeslagh,

1994). The role of transformational leadership was identified as a key factor in enabling strategic change (Pettigrew, 1988). The emphasis then moved from a macro view to a micro view of the efficiency of the firms' processes and overall organizational effectiveness. Improvements were sought through quality programmes, restructuring, cultural change, learning and behavioural change.

Recent studies have emphasized the role of ‘IT-enabled’ strategic change in facilitating fundamental business transformation (Keen, 1991; Scott-Morton, 1991; Parker, 1996). The ‘IT-enabled’ strategic change approach merges with a range of issues from strategic management, organizational behaviour and operational improvement. However, in general the approach to ‘IT-enabled’ strategic change is still rooted in formal planning (the plan-do-see-check model) and strategic portfolio planning (for example Earl, 1988). The lack of alignment between the strategic IT and the strategic change literature suggests there is an opportunity to enhance the theory of strategic change with respect to the impact of information technology. For both academics and practitioners, there is a need for interdisciplinary study of IT-enabled strategic change and its implementation for the purpose of sustaining business transformation.

‘Information systems’, broadly defined, include the alignment of information technology, business strategy, management processes, people and structure embedded in organizations as a whole. However, for the purpose of the analysis, the term ‘information systems’ (which is replaced by ‘information technology’ in Scott-Morton’s terms) is defined as a separate element in order to differentiate it from business strategy, management processes, people and structure. This enables the analysis to identify the dynamics of the interaction between these five elements. Such an inquiry also helps in the understanding of the strategic alignment between IT and other organizational factors. In this research, IT refers to a broad range of solutions induced by information systems including, for instance, CIM (computer integrated manufacturing), EDI (electronic data exchange) and multimedia rather than traditional MIS (management information systems) alone.

## Mapping the terrain of strategic change

The current strategic change literature is summarized in Fig. 1. The macro view is concerned with a firm's strategic behaviour under the constraints arising from the industrial, sectoral and market levels, and aims to understand how firms link competitive performance to their abilities to adapt to major changes in their environment. There are two schools of thought: the first focuses on a firm's behaviour; and the second uses a change approach. The study of a firm's behaviour emphasizes how a firm seeks to cope with major external changes, and at the same time identifies the most important factors and solutions (for example Johnson, 1987; Hinings & Greenwood, 1988; Pettigrew, 1988). On the other hand, the ‘change strategy’ group observes strategic change through distinct models of change: ‘turnaround’, ‘sharpbender’ and ‘excellence’. The ‘turnaround’ literature (Slatter, 1987; Nelson & Clutterbuck, 1988) studies how firms recover or decline as a result of turbulence. The ‘sharpbender’ literature (Grinyer et al., 1988) studies firms that exhibit the foresight to predict major changes and take appropriate action before it is too late. The ‘excellence’ literature (Peters & Waterman, 1982) studies strategic change during a firm's excellent performance in specific periods, and seeks to understand the implications of their coping strategies.

The micro view studies strategic change by observing how firms rearrange their resources, streamline their operations, and enhance the quality of interdependence within their organization. Four major schools can be identified: resource deployment, decision making, organizational effectiveness and operational efficiency. The first school (resource deployment) stresses the reallocation of resources within a firm, including resource enhancement, expansion and migration (Prahalad & Hammel, 1990; Haspaslagh & Jemison, 1991; Slywotzky, 1996). The second school addresses the need to establish effective mechanisms in the decision-making process in order to facilitate change (see, for example, Dutton & Penner, 1993). The third microschool (operational efficiency) analyses strategic change through the rejuvenation of a firm's process by improvement and innovation. There are two distinctive approaches: the improvement perspective and the innovative perspective. The former adopts incremental change, aiming to rationalize existing processes through employee participation in order to achieve cohesion between workers and process performance. The focus is on improving overall product quality, lowering operating costs and reducing lead time. Total quality management and the Japanese concept of Kaizan (Imai, 1992) are two influential forms of this improvement approach. By contrast, the innovation perspective attempts to abandon the existing processes and engages in radical reform that is often referred to as 're-engineering' or 'redesign' (see

Hammer & Champy, 1993; Davenport, 1993; Davidson, 1993, among others). It seeks to establish new processes in a firm's value chain, and to outsource specific processes to other firms.

The fourth microschool is concerned with the improvement of organizational effectiveness. There are three main approaches within this school, reflecting their specific concerns with structural change, cultural change and learning-based change.

1 The structural change approach emphasizes the redesign of organizational structure in order to achieve synergy within the adopted strategy, contending that the effective restructuring of an organization will facilitate the formation of a flexible firm that can actively respond to changes (Heckscher & Donnellon, 1994; Lipnack & Stamps, 1994). The focus is on the quality of interdependence and hence on knowledge dissemination leading to innovation in processes and products.

2 The cultural change approach focuses on the transformation of the individual's psychological resistance (Lambert, 1993) and stresses how transformation can be achieved through leadership and education/training based on a top-down philosophy. It seeks to implant the required values into the organization (see the case examples in Gowler et al., 1993). Kanter (1983) adds that cultural change can also be achieved through instilling entrepreneurship into the organization through a bottom-up change philosophy.

3 The learning-based change approach emphasises the role of bottom-up organizational change. As Argyris and Schön (1978, p. 3) indicate, organizational learning involves the detection and correction of errors through two kinds of process (single-loop and double-loop learning) that seek to reveal the symptoms and underlying problems. The fundamental aim is to enable the knowledge workers in an organization to solve strategic as well as operational problems, thus reducing the traditional reliance on strategic managers. Organization learning theorists (Senge, 1990; Mills & Friesen, 1992; Schein, 1996) contend that the removal of communication barriers and the encouragement of empowerment through systems thinking can enable knowledge workers to perform the role of strategic managers.

The introduction of the notion of the strategic use of information has a significant impact on the practice of strategic change. In the intrafirm context, information technology facilitates the introduction of innovative processes in the value chain. It also facilitates new forms of structural configuration such as the network or virtual organization, transforms the style of leadership and authority, redefines the relationship between employers and employees, reshapes new career patterns for modern workers, and thus fundamentally transforms the culture of an organization. In terms of inter-firm relations, strategic alliances, made possible by information technology, help firms to manage the supply chain in order to reduce transactional costs, and to form a ‘metabusiness’ (Keen, 1991). This redesign of the business network changes fundamentally the nature of business practice. Firms can pursue entirely new ways of growing by transforming their business scope.

## Defining IT-enabled strategic change

Research into IT-enabled strategic change is still in its embryonic stage. Most of the concepts are borrowed from theories of strategic management, information management, industrial engineering and organizational behaviour. The discussion here attempts to categorize the main research areas according to three headings: process re-engineering, IT-enabled transformation and human-centred renewal. This categorization aims to reflect the literature survey according to the MIT 1990s framework. On this basis, the discussion explores the central concerns and problems emerging from the literature.

‘Process re-engineering’ stems from industrial engineering, dealing mainly with the ‘management processes’ element in the MIT 1990s framework. It focuses on process improvement in order to increase operational efficiency (see, for example, Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1993, among others). The key aim is to change processes innovatively in order to obtain improvements in lead time, quality and cost reduction. The new processes are often supported by empowered project teams organized around customers and facilitated by advanced IT. Although firms can frequently achieve significant advances in performance, they often find it difficult to sustain because of the resulting imbalance with other organizational factors.

Similarly, ‘IT-enabled transformation’, focuses on the alignment of information technology with the ‘strategy’ and ‘structure’ elements in the MIT 1990s framework. Information technology is used as an enabler to lever resource allocation (see Tapscott & Caston, 1993; Parker, 1996; Ward & Griffiths, 1996). This new way of allocating resources and redefining business scope causes the basic nature of modern businesses to change. This inevitably has a substantial impact on the social relationship within and between firms. Organizations need to find new approaches to create ‘trust’ within firms and coherence between firms in order to consolidate new forms of business (Handy, 1995).

‘Human-centred renewal’ highlights the impact of technology on the ‘people’ element in the MIT 1990s framework. The debate centres on behavioural change, as can be seen in three major studies by Zuboff (1988), Strassmann (1985) and Hoffman (1984). Zuboff (1988) uses an interpretative approach to study human experience under the impact of information technology, and advises firms to decentralize their power structures, disperse knowledge and responsibility throughout the organization, and empower employees at all levels. Firms should have a moral vision of the purpose of the firm and of the relationship between employers and management. Strassmann examines this issue from the individual, executive, organizational and societal perspectives, indicating that in order to achieve productivity and profitability through information technology, firms have to consider the four perspectives as a whole by linking structure and strategy with people. Hoffman further extends the argument to the issue of empowerment, emphasising the need to delegate responsibility and power to employees. The emphasis on cross-functional training for managers, users and IT specialists is aimed at building up consensus and acquiring the skills needed to achieve internal synergy. The balance between technology, organization and the human actors is a critical source of success in implementing IT-enabled strategic change.

## THE NEED FOR A DYNAMIC PERSPECTIVE

From the above review we conclude that there are five major weaknesses in the current theoretical positions: their partial viewpoints, their prescriptive solutions, their static frameworks, their linear models and their best practice recommendations. These weaknesses are considered in turn:

1 Partial viewpoints: each theoretical position takes a particular view of managing IT-enabled strategic change; for example, the perspective might be process re-engineering or cultural change. The tendency is to ignore other important change elements. The lack of a balanced perspective causes managers (or researchers) to be biased in their observation of change; for example Gould (1996) suggests that strategic change is mainly concerned with cultural change and he thus neglects the influence of technology.

2 Prescriptive solutions: the inclination is to give a set of packaged solutions that derive from the observation of exemplary firms. Although this may provide practitioners with guidelines for what should be managed in the process of strategic change (such as the need to set a clear vision, establish effective communication mechanisms and be aware of politics), it fails to recognize the complex dynamics of change. Managers following this approach attempt to adopt current fashionable practices (such as business process re-engineering) but may find that these are inapplicable in their own particular situation (see, for example, Quinn, 1980).

3 Static frameworks: a static framework is provided to illustrate the inter-relationship between the critical factors for the purpose of managing strategic change. An example of frameworks that demonstrate these features is the consulting approach of Goulillart and Kelly (1995).

4 Linear models: many approaches use a production model (involving input, process and output) to understand how strategic change can be managed. Such a step-by-step model may explain the general sequence of a strategic initiative, but fails to account for the intertwining of complex change elements in every phase of planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluation. Examples include the model of strategic agenda building suggested by Dutton and Penner (1993) and the rejuvenation model proposed by Badden-Fuller and Stopford (1992).

5 Best practice recommendations: here the aim is to summarize the lessons derived from the analysis of ‘successful’ firms in terms of an inventory of best practices, thus combining the individual best practices to give the best overall solution. In spite of the hindsight elegantly provided by such approaches, there is a danger that the individual best practices do not fit together. Firms adopting such recommendations try to excel in every respect and as a result often fail to recognize the need to allocate resources according to their core competencies. In Search of Excellence by Peters and Waterman (1982) is an example of this kind of perspective.

These theoretical positions continue to influence contemporary thinking in strategic change studies in the fields of strategic management, organizational behaviour, industrial engineering and IT-enabled change. They may be appropriate when dealing with structured problems such as manufacturing, marketing, service operation and project management, but they are inappropriate when addressing the complex changes that occur in a contingent and dynamic manner. IT-enabled strategic change involves the interplay of strategy, structure, people, management processes and technology that firms have to negotiate collectively to achieve advantage. It often deals with ‘complex strategic issues’ (Diffenbach, 1982) in which problems occur in an interconnected manner. Solutions initially implemented by the management tend to cause more problems if such an interdependent complexity is not discerned. In addition, this complex nature makes the change context-dependent and a single and all-embracing solution such as process re-engineering or organizational restructuring cannot be relied on. Therefore, there is a need to understand how organizations can manage change in a dynamic and integrative way so as to enable the negotiation of change from an existing state to a new state of equilibrium.

In conclusion, the current change literature tends to adopt a planning approach, using a ‘mechanistic application of a prescriptive set of clearly defined analytical frameworks’ (Morgan, 1986, p. 16). This results in the provision of fragmented solutions. It is less useful for firms that are seeking to achieve sustainable advantage and a fundamental transformation. Strategic change is dynamic and can be conflict based, paradoxical, unstructured and complex (Diffenbach, 1982; Beckhard & Harris, 1987; Pettigrew & Whipp, 1991). Moreover, technological change tends to increase the uncertainty in such a dynamic (Nolan & Croson, 1995; also quoted in Pettigrew’s chapter, 1988). A new approach to the interpretation and understanding of change is therefore needed (such as the approach of Morgan, 1986; Barr et al., 1992; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993). Strategic change needs alternative perspectives to enhance the appreciation of the dynamics of change. By gaining such an appreciation, managers will greatly improve their ability to make sense of today’s dynamic complexity.

## RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY

The research reported here uses the MIT 1990s framework (Scott-Morton, 1991) and the Fujitsu Centre (Australia) framework (Yetton et al., 1994) as the points of departure. The discussion that follows explains the design of the research questions and objectives, describes how a qualitative case study method has been used in this research, and gives a brief introduction to the five pilot cases.

## Research perspective

The MIT 1990s programme (Scott-Morton, 1991) was an important research effort designed to achieve conceptual integration between the different change factors. It identified five key elements of change: strategy, individuals and roles (human actors), structure, management processes, and technology. These elements are embedded in a framework for managing IT-enabled strategic change (see Fig. 2). Building on Scott-Morton's strategy-driven and process-centred approach, Yetton et al. (1994) propose a different perspective (technology-centred) supported by empirical evidence to illustrate an emergent mode of change (see Figure 2). Yetton and colleague's major contribution is to advance Scott-Morton's static view by mapping a dynamic path and incorporating the mode and rate of change into an explanatory framework.

There are three main issues that can be identified from the findings of these two research approaches.

1 Prescriptive solutions are insufficient to explain the dynamic and complex nature of change. For instance, strategy may be driven by changes in the other elements rather than being a driver of them. This requires a critical review of the main propositions and an analysis of the consequence of different dynamic paths within various contexts.

2 Although the two research works have identified the key elements of IT-enabled strategic change, the constituents of each remain to be identified.

3 The paths and frameworks offered by the MIT 1990s research programme are static in their nature. The MIT framework requires further elaboration in order to illustrate the distinctive relationships between the five change elements (Yetton et al., 1994, address this issue). Moreover, the MIT 1990s framework attempts to provide a conclusive answer through an aggregated change pattern, thus neglecting the fact that changes occur continuously over a period of time and can thus exhibit different patterns.

Thus, there is a clear need to study IT-enabled strategic change combining a longitudinal view of change paths, an in-depth study of the various constituents of the change elements, and the linkage of change patterns. The aim of this research is to attempt to produce enhanced propositions and a new interpretation of strategic change. The proposed framework seeks to provide a flexible perspective so as to interpret the contingent and dynamic nature of change through the study of the key elements. Unlike the static framework, which tends to generalize change in an all-embracing model, the new one seeks to understand each strategic change as a unique phenomenon reflecting particular circumstances. Such an interpretation will be illustrated through the empirical study of the interplay of the five change elements identified in the MIT 1990s framework.

## Research questions and objectives

Against the background sketched out above, this section describes the main research questions and objectives of the pilot case study. The research focuses on two principal aspects: the nature of the key ingredients that constitute IT-enabled change, and the manner in which they collectively contribute to change under different circumstances. The main research questions may thus be stated as follows:

1 What is the nature of the key elements of IT-enabled strategic change in terms of strategy, structure, management processes, technology and human resources?

2 How can organizations manage IT-enabled strategic change in an effort to coordinate these key elements?

The primary objectives of this research are to enhance the propositions provided by Scott-Morton and Yetton et al. to provide empirical evidence to complement the theories of IT-enabled strategic change, and to offer an alternative framework to improve our understanding of change at the strategic level. These objectives are defined as follows in order to formulate the research questions more precisely.

1 To provide an alternative perspective to enhance the propositions and frameworks provided by Scott-Morton (planned change: process-centred) and Yetton et al. (emergent change: technology-centred).

2 To explore the in-depth nature of the key elements of IT-enabled strategic change through the use of empirical evidence.

3 To investigate the different patterns of IT-enabled strategic change, and to explain how different paths negotiate the key elements in order to construct the transformation. The emphasis is on the understanding of strategic change in which IT plays a key facilitating role. It must be stressed that the purpose of this research is not to identify change patterns induced by IT. The patterns described here are used solely as a mechanism for understanding IT-enabled strategic change.

## Research method

The research uses a qualitative case study method to investigate the content and process of IT-enabled strategic change. The unit of analysis is the ‘change project’. A retrospective longitudinal analysis, rather than a snapshot, of the project is taken so that this investigation can obtain a ‘processual’ view (Pettigrew & Whipp, 1991). The semistructured interview is the main technique used for data collection in the pilot studies. Pattern mapping techniques are used for cross-case analysis. Although the firms studied had reservations about revealing all the relevant information, evidence from different informants and various literature sources has been gathered to achieve the best possible validity.

Five cases were selected because their features suggested that they would cast light on the type of change described in the MIT 1990s framework. They are referred to as the aeroengine, telecom, semiconductor, publishing and automobile cases. These five cases are all multinational enterprises: two of them are British and the other three are based in Taiwan. The criteria used to select the cases were:

1 The case firm must have used IT as a major enabler to implement strategic change, which goes beyond traditional automation and informatization.

2 The case firm must have a formal organization (medium or large) that operates globally.

3 The case firm must have sought strategic change rather than tactical operational improvement. This change may be incremental or radical, planned or emergent, and must be over at least a 7-year period.

4 The selection of case firms focuses on ‘sharpbenders’, i.e. those firms that have undertaken change to avoid imminent failure, and excludes the ‘excellence’ types of strategic change.

The five cases operate at different stages of strategic change as a result of their continuous change initiatives (see Table 1). In the semiconductor, publishing and automobile cases, new change programmes continue to be implemented. In the aerospace and telecom cases, the change programmes have been completed.

## THE INDIVIDUAL CASE ANALYSIS

In analysing the empirical data, various approaches have been used to decipher the change described by informants. We found that if a particular strategic change is mapped out in terms of one pattern per case, the pattern then fails to explain the complex nature of change. To incorporate different modes of change driven by various change elements such as technology, structure, human actors and processes, we decided to map out the change patterns over three key time phases: early, middle and late (on the basis of trial and error). There are consequently 15 patterns in total: five cases over three key time phases. Initially each change pattern seemed complete and unique. However, we became interested in the special role of strategy which, we observed, both initiates changes in the other elements and responds to these changes in a continuous way. It became evident to us that through a topological transformation (which, diagrammatically, repositions these elements and their connections so as to provide a simpler visualization) of these change patterns and by giving a special role to strategy, we could link the relationships between the change elements in the different cases and explain the components that constitute change. Finally, we sought to use the redesigned platform to provide a different perspective to that of the MIT and Fujitsu Centre frameworks. Thus, we believe we were able to achieve a better interpretation of the way in which strategic change takes place in an organization.

## The Telecom case

In this section we will use the Telecom case to illustrate how change patterns can be deciphered by examining strategic change over three key time phases. In the example shown in Fig. 3, we use the MIT framework to map out the path of each strategic change initiative (according to the approach of Yetton et al., 1994). Although the sequence of arrows in each path of change is linear, this is mainly to show how an organization within a particular time span collectively undertakes change from a strategic perspective. We can observe the dynamics of change patterns in terms of the mode of change (planned or emergent), the change path and the change driver (for instance, structure-oriented change).

![](/api/attachments/SBDWQ7SN/fulltext/images/d1fb737c4ed8a9f0960c134e1e09d8f21db5d5a6ed92370e1dbf0803c41c8860.jpg)  
Figure 3. An example of mapping change patterns: the Telecom case.

The example in Fig. 3 illustrates how the three change patterns occurred over time: structural change, cultural change and process re-engineering. In the early stage, change started with a major restructuring followed by the introduction of TQM (total quality management). Thus, change commenced with a change in structure, followed by a human resource training programme leading to process improvement activities. Consequently, it had an impact on the introduction of new technology. In the middle stage, the need for building consensus emerged as a result of the prior introduction of technology. The telecom firm then decided to initiate a cultural change programme, readjusted its structure, integrated new technology with the existing systems, and redesigned processes in order to align with technology. It must be noted that the firm did not deliberately plan this change but was stimulated to do so by the need to resolve some problems caused by the previous structural change. Finally, in the late stage, the firm realised that current practice could no longer be used if it wanted to be competitive in the global market. It thus launched a process re-engineering initiative: it replaced some parts of its technology, added a task force team to the current structure to promote the re-engineering programme and, as a result, redefined the roles and responsibilities of its employees.

It must be noted that such a longitudinal study does not focus on one specific change incident. The emphasis is on how IT is used in different stages of strategic change and why different change elements are triggered by IT (or trigger the use of IT). The benefit of viewing the change in three stages is that it avoids the oversimplification that results from aggregating the sequence of change into one change path as suggested by Yetton et al. (1994). To analyse change in terms of a more detailed change process allows researchers to capture the transition of the different change paths initiated in various stages. Thus, the analysis allows strategic change to be illustrated through the interplay of change elements over time.

## CROSS-CASE SYNTHESIS AND KEY FINDINGS

The discussion in this section is focused on three needs: to identify the key constituents of each change element; to explore the distinctive relationships in the MIT framework; and to analyse these change patterns. A tabulation (Table 2) of the various key issues identified from the five cases is provided in order to reveal the main constituents for each change element and thus to explore those constituents' nature in turn.

In Table 2 we list the key themes that we found in practice to be necessary in order to characterize each change element. These can be used to identify the main emphasis of each change element when firms implement strategic change. In the ‘strategy’ element, the emphasis is on aligning business scope with core competence and deciding to what extent firms use a third party to facilitate change in the various stages. In the ‘structure’ element, the focus is on readjusting organizational architecture and managing the quality of interdepartmental coordination. As far as the ‘process’ element is concerned, the aim is to investigate which core process in the value chain firms wish to improve or innovate, and to view the implementation of change as a portfolio of processes and product changes. For ‘individuals and roles’, the concern is to coordinate the integration of cultural change, organizational learning, and the redefinition of jobs and responsibilities. Finally, for the ‘technology’ element, attention is centred on the management of the IT portfolio in order to maximize its potential and to choose the best way of sourcing technology services.

However, in order to explore the inter-relationship of each change element and to explain the platform of the MIT framework, it was necessary to re-examine the interconnections between the five change elements to see if there were any significant relationships between them. We found distinctive relationships in four pairings of the change elements. 3 inner infrastructure: the strategic fit between management processes and human actors; and

These findings enabled us to reconstruct the MIT framework as shown in Fig. 4, and thus helped us to understand the importance of aligning the four change elements with the ‘strategy’ element. The alignment is supported by the integration of technological and organizational coherence as well as the fit of inner and outer infrastructure.

Initially we had plotted each change path on the MIT framework, resulting in the kind of diagram shown in Fig. 3. A multiplicity of patterns thus emerged. In order to decipher the patterns across the cases over time, we now decided to transform the change patterns topologically onto the new framework in Fig. 4. An important breakthrough was achieved when we were able to appreciate the role that strategy played in each strategic change initiative: it was either planned or emergent throughout each change process. The result of using the new framework was startling in the clarity achieved. When the transformed patterns were displayed together in Fig. 5, across cases over time (the three time phases), we noticed that there were only four patterns that repeated themselves. We decided to refer to these as the four ‘archetypes’, adapting the definition of ‘archetype’ from Hinings and Greenwood (1988, p. 191) as ‘a combination of structural coherence and underpinning interpretative scheme’. It is this interpretative scheme that we use to explain the complex phenomenon of strategic change.

An examination of these patterns reveals several implications for the interpretation and understanding of strategic change. First, the empirical evidence suggests that for each change path there are three major components that form a complete pattern of strategic change: driver, lever and impact (also refer to the discussion in Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993). Each change path is constructed by a ‘driver’ (for example technology), and then the change is followed by leveraging through two ‘levers’ (for example process and individuals and roles that flow from the introduction of technology change). Strategic change then ends with a significant influence on an ‘impacted’ change element (in this case, structure) (see Fig. 6).

The findings suggests that firms, because of to their particular situation, often initiate strategic change through a ‘driver’. For instance, in the Telecom case an overall business strategy is identified in the early stage that sought to downsize the organization before it moved forward to further consolidation through a change in the people aspect. In this case, ‘structure’ is the driver. Moreover, as the strategic change proceeded, it required the firm to adjust other organizational factors (i.e. the two levers – ‘people’ and ‘management processes’) so as to achieve an impact on ‘technology’. The completion of such a strategic change path helped the firm achieve specific advantages in the early stage. For example, the downsizing resulted in a cultural change programme and TQM project for process improvement, and finally, led to the introduction of technology (a database integration project) to facilitate the change initiative. However, as changes are introduced from the external environment, firms then have to initiate a new business strategy and, intentionally or unintentionally, engage in another change path. This finding indicates that when a strategic change is attempted, it will inevitably impinge on other elements following a certain logical sequence.

Second, there are two modes of change that intertwine in the process of strategic change: the planned and emergent modes. It needs to be noted that the strategy element refers neither to formal business strategy nor to technology strategy as such. For example, a firm may begin with a planned strategy to introduce certain technologies, but later it may realize the need to change management processes. By redesigning management processes, it may then recognize the need to launch a cultural change programme to empower its employees. However, this then requires a readjustment of current organizational structure to cohere with the cultural change programme. Although the intention of introducing technology is deliberately planned, the eventual change strategy is emergent. With strategy placed at the centre of the framework, we can depict its relation to other elements as either planned or emergent (see Fig. 7).

The findings suggest that, although firms formulate specific business strategies, they may not be aware of their change path. A more specific change approach may emerge as a consequence of corresponding changes in the firm's context or problems triggered by the previous change action. For example, in the telecom case a cultural change programme was implemented because of the previous introduction of IT. In the middle stage, when the change path was completed, the firm then recognized the emergence of another change strategy. This triggered a new business strategy to implement a ‘planned’ re-engineering programme. It should be noted that the planned or emergent change refers to the mode of change strategy (how firms go about change) rather than the business strategy (what business objectives are set). Business strategy is derived from these two modes of change interchangeably according to the situation. In both the planned and emergent modes, as the empirical evidence suggests, business strategy serves as a convergent force to push, or to be pulled by, the other organizational factors. For the planned mode of change, the change in the people, management processes, technology and structure follows the guidance of business strategy. In the Telecom example, in the late stage, the business strategy seeks to promote efficiency in a radical manner in order to revamp the firm's competitiveness. The implementation of change in each element is guided by the efficiency-oriented business strategy.

On the other hand, with the emergent mode of change, the business strategy (although it is stated in the annual report) is formed gradually through the process of change implementation. As a result, a new business strategy becomes explicit when firms recognize a strategic opportunity through the completion of the change path. For example, in the middle stage, the Telecom firm starts with a ‘planned’ cultural change programme. As it completes the change path that follows the sequence of change in structure, technology and management processes, the new business strategy (which sought to enhance service efficiency) became clearer to the management team over time. As a result, the mode of change is emergent, arising out of the process of the cultural change programme. A key feature of this emergent mode of change is the role which uncertainty plays in the strategy formation process. The firm often initiates a planned strategy for specific actions, then, because of to the uncertainty of its situation, it has to muddle through these actions and gradually formulate an integrated strategy for subsequent guidance.

Third, four distinctive archetypes can be identified from the cross-case analysis. These are the result of the contingent causes arising from the internal and external contexts, and they may be identified as: process re-engineering, structural reconfiguration, human renewal and IT transformation, each indicating a strategic change driven by different change elements. These four types of change are designated in Fig. 8 and may be explained as follows.

Archetype 1: process re-engineering. This refers to both process improvement and process innovation and is often leveraged through the use of information technology. For change undertaken in this way, the change path tends to negotiate through technology and structure, ending with a cultural change initiative that impacts on human actors. This type of change has a specific plan at the outset, and follows a traditional strategy formulation method (i.e. set vision, formulate goals, implement actions and evaluate results in order to provide feedback for a ‘revision’).

Archetype 2: structural reconfiguration. This is concerned with a strategic change, starting with a restructuring initiative, modifying the organizational architecture in order to react to change, and trying to involve employees in the change activity. As a result of these collective efforts, firms identify important areas for the redesign of management processes, and attempt to implement new technology in order to make the process change effective. This type of change also tends to be deliberately planned when it commences.

![](/api/attachments/SBDWQ7SN/fulltext/images/58291d92c39baec42bf4d81567b7918a3ff967780bb96bcb62e1db4ff2e39ef6.jpg)  
Figure 8. Four change archetypes identified in this research.

Archetype 3: human renewal. This refers to cultural change at the beginning of the change process which then leads to a restructuring of organizational architecture and the enhancement of the quality of interdepartmental coordination. Later, when the firm responds to bottom-up suggestions, the need to implement technology in order to achieve significant improvement in the management processes is recognized. This type of strategic change follows an emergent mode of change.

Archetype 4: IT transformation. In this type of change, the firm often begins by experimenting with certain (information) technology in management processes. The firm then recognizes that such change needs to incorporate the transformation of human actors and culture if it is to be effective. This type of change is emergent.

Fourth, the simplicity achieved derives from the empirical patterns and is not a result of the structure imposed on them. There are 120 possible paths through the MIT framework $(5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2)$ . By taking ‘strategy’ out of the paths, the possible number is reduced to 24. But each could be associated with a planned or an emergent mode of change. Thus, the total possible patterns in the framework is 48. We imposed no further restriction on the paths. The fact that the paths follow the perimeter in a clockwise or counter-clockwise direction and never cross the middle is an empirical outcome rather than a constraint imposed by the new framework. ## THE MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS

The above discussion leads to a new way of perceiving change and generates a new set of propositions (see Scott-Morton, 1991; Yetton et al., 1994 and refer to Fig. 9). First, strategic change is not a one-shot activity; it is driven by various organizational factors: management processes, people, technology or structure, whereas strategy serves as a convergent force to align these four elements. An integrated view is required to manage the interaction and transition of the four elements as a whole. Firms that use a single solution to implement change (for example process re-engineering alone) will not be able to direct change; instead, they will be controlled by the change.

Second, the role of strategy is influenced by the two modes of change: planned or emergent. These two change approaches do not exist alone but are created by the firm's intentional and unintentional change initiatives. The planned mode of change enables the firm to establish an explicit business strategy in order to guide different change actions. For example, an instance observed in the semiconductor firm indicated that the context required the firm to set up an efficient manufacturing system and thus needed to streamline the administration processes. The firm used MIS and readjusted its organization structure to achieve such a strategy. As a result, the change initiative is completed via a change programme on the people aspect to enhance motivation. The completion of the change path is pushed by strategy. On the other hand, some firms because of their uncertain situation may not be able at the outset to articulate a clear strategy to respond to change. However, they initiate a specific change action and muddle through. Gradually a strategy emerges from the muddling. In this case, strategy is pulled by the completion of the change path (this corresponds to the discussion in Mintzburg, 1978). The empirical evidence we have observed indicates that the process re-engineering and structural reconfiguration types of change tend to produce planned change, whereas information technology transformation and human renewal type of change tend to create emergent change.

![](/api/attachments/SBDWQ7SN/fulltext/images/6da45a3c676ab6b4eee19e535d37851376e8b1ffa58af61aca029616f1b2660f.jpg)  
Figure 9. The new framework and enhanced proposition for the study of IT-enabled strategic change.

Third, the emphasis of strategic change is on the management of the change path rather than on individual change activities. The success of strategic change lies in the management of the change path in which technology, people, management processes and structure are coordinated. Individual project success or failure during the implementation of change is less important than an appreciation of the chain reaction – comprehending where the change comes from and where the change may go. The linear view of strategic change (see, for example Nadler, 1981) limits one's power to understand change. One way to achieve a dynamic perspective is to conceptualize change as the interplay between organizational factors and to follow the chain reaction occurring in the change path.

Fourth, in order to achieve a contingent view of strategic change, there are four generic strategies suggested by the research: process re-engineering, structural reconfiguration, information technology transformation and human renewal. These can be used in a contingent manner according to circumstances. However, one must note that these four generic strategies serve as archetypes for the implementation of IT-enabled strategic change. In practice, change may not follow the exact path as indicated by our research. The important lesson derived from these four archetypes is that one should prepare for the management of strategic change in a dynamic and contingent way but not to follow them as prescriptive strategies.

Fifth, the research suggests four significant relationships in the redesigned framework shown in Figure 4. To implement strategic change and direct the change path successfully, firms need to maintain a sound balance within these relationships. These relationships can be summarized under two headings: strategic coherence and synergy. Strategic coherence involves technological and organizational coherence. Technological coherence seeks to maintain an appropriate fit between technology and management processes, ensuring their compatibility when fundamental change is required. Organizational coherence requires a symmetry between structure and people, bridging the imbalance created by the change. Strategic synergy is achieved through the alignment of another two relationships: synergy of internal and external infrastructure. The synergy of internal infrastructure refers to the integration of management processes and people, emphasizing the alignment of process change in relation to cultural change, redesign of job and responsibility, and the establishment of appropriate organizational learning. The synergy of external infrastructure, on the other hand, demands the integration of technology and structure, stressing the alignment of technological application with the overall organizational architecture design and the organizational interdependence among departments. These four relationships together construct a platform for the implementation of strategic change. Success in implementing a change path relies upon the degree of fit and the integration of the platform.

Although the new proposition that we suggest (see Fig. 9) opens a fresh dimension to the understanding of strategic change, there is a danger in seeking to apply it in a prescriptive way. It has to be appreciated that there may exist other patterns of change, and, moreover, different change paths may occur within the four archetypes suggested in this research. The key implication of strategic change for management is to recognize the consequences of the chain reaction involved in the change path. Furthermore, these four archetypes may exist with other combinations of the planned and emergent modes of change. It is not the purpose of these archetypes to suggest that the planned change mode is mandatory for process re-engineering and structural reconfiguration and the emergent change mode must be used for human renewal and information technology transformation. Rather, the aim is to promote an awareness of the use of the planned and emergent modes of change in the process of change implementation. The key lesson for firms is that these archetypes should be used as references and to recognize the stage of strategic change that they have revealed; thereby, firms can adjust their strategy to respond flexibly to the existing situation.

We suggest six practical guidelines that can be drawn from this research. Although they are presented in a linear fashion as a list, the purpose is to acknowledge the dynamic nature for the management of strategic change.

1 Examine the balance in the current platform for IT-enabled strategic change. Firms need to consider the current technological and organizational coherence and the synergy of the inner and outer infrastructure. The balance of coherence and synergy may indicate the appropriate next step in the change process.

2 Direct the chain reaction into a complete change path in order to achieve strategic advantage. Firms need to recognize the behaviour of driver, lever and impact in the implementation of strategic change. Although the changes in structure, management processes, people and technology may occur concurrently, firms can use the change path concept to guide the coordination of these various change activities. An important implication is that managers need to develop a sense of awareness of the consequences in the individual change elements.

3 Construct business strategy through the use of planned or emergent modes of change. If firms use a planned mode of change, they need to bring together the four change elements (technology, structure, people and management processes) under the guidance of business strategy. Alternatively, if firms undertake specific change action but are unable to articulate an overall convergent strategy, they may want to develop this strategy from the process of muddling through strategic change. In both cases, firms need to contemplate strategic change as an integrated implementation.

4 Deploy the four change strategies as reference archetypes for managing strategic change. Firms can use these four reference archetypes to diagnose their current change position. Once firms identify the change path in which they are currently involved and the previous change path they have gone through, they are in a better position to devise a strategy for integrating the current change or moving forward to another new change initiative. These reference archetypes can be useful in conceptualizing the transition of various change paths within firms.

5 Manage key change elements. In the implementation of strategic change, firms need to manage key variables that affect individual change elements. For strategy formulation, firms need to consider business scope, core competence and change management. For processes redesign, firms need to consider the overall re-engineering of value chain and corresponding product changes. For the people aspect, firms need to consider cultural change, the redesign of individual responsibility and the establishment of learning mechanisms. For restructuring, firms need to consider the change in architecture and the corresponding quality in the interdependence of departments. Finally, for the introduction of IT, the application portfolio, potential exploitation and sourcing governance need to be considered.

6 Avoid ‘success’ and best practice approaches to the implementation of strategic change. The primary implication of this research is to provide a new perspective for strategic change that is based on a dynamic and contingent view. The purpose of managing strategic change is not to apply a best practice formula that, if implemented step-by-step, will produce successful results. Firms need first to appreciate the complex and uncertain nature of strategic change and then navigate change by the flexible use of the reference archetypes for guidance.

## CONCLUSIONS

There are three major contributions of this research. First, it provides an alternative view of IT-enabled strategic change to that provided by the MIT 1990s framework (Scott-Morton, 1991)

and the follow-up research of Yetton et al. (1994). The findings confirm that the management of IT-enabled strategic change must not be confined to a planned approach (which centres on process) as suggested by Scott-Morton nor an emergent approach (which centres on technology) as suggested by Yetton et al. Rather, this issue needs to be examined from a dynamic viewpoint: the implementation of strategic change changes over time, as do the different drivers guiding the change. These drivers, with the help of various levers, will impact on a particular change element and achieve the strategic change at that particular stage. As the context alters, new strategic change initiatives will evolve through either a planned or emergent mode of change strategy, and will be driven by another change element. The management of strategic change needs to be considered in terms of different change patterns over time with contingent change elements. This provides a new perspective and proposition for the understanding of strategic change, which is illustrated in Fig. 9 (cf. Fig. 2).

Second, the findings identify the focus of managerial concern in each change element while conducting IT-enabled strategic change. In the strategy element, the concerns are the alignment of change strategy with business strategy, how they together guide the implementation of change, and the governance of a firm's change project. In the process element, the concerns are the focus of the value chain and the interplay of product and process change. In the technology element, the concerns are the planning of the IT portfolio, maximizing the potential of IT, and a decision about the most appropriate sourcing. In the human actors element, the concerns are the jobs and responsibilities of employees, cultural change and organizational learning. In the structural aspect, the concerns are the architecture and interdependencies of the firm.

Third, four distinctive change patterns (archetypes) are identified from this empirical study. The findings indicate that for each change pattern there are three basic components that govern the behaviour of change: one driver, two levers and one impact. The mode of change is planned when the strategic change begins with process re-engineering or structural change, and is emergent when change is initiated by the introduction of technology or cultural change. These findings have important implications for IT-enabled strategic change as these patterns illustrate how the combination of different dimensions of change (improvement and transformation, incremental and radical, planned and emergent) are intertwined in a limited set of distinct patterns. The findings also demonstrate a different perspective to the macro view according to which changes are assumed to be relatively static and limited to certain specific dimensions of change (see, for example, Hingings & Greenwood, 1988). The proposed change archetypes thus provide an integrated view of such constraints and suggest a revision of the MIT 1990s framework. The reconstruction of the platform illustrates four significant relationships: technology coherence, which implies a detailed specification for the technology needed to support the particular business processes; organizational coherence, bringing the integration of organizational structure and job design; inner infrastructure synergy, signifying the strategic fit between process and human actors (culture); and outer infrastructure synergy, signifying the strategic fit between technology and structure.

In a nutshell, the major contribution of this research is to suggest a dynamic understanding of IT-enabled strategic change through a recognition of the impact of the mode of change on different change patterns. This is achieved through a new way of viewing the nature of strategic change, the identification of the content of the key change elements, and the reconstruction of the MIT platform to accommodate the four basic change patterns.

Finally, some guidelines have been provided to help practitioners manage IT-enabled strategic change:

1 Examine the balance in the current platform for IT-enabled strategic change.

2 Direct the chain reaction into a complete change path in order to achieve strategic advantage.

3 Construct business strategy through the use of planned or emergent modes of change.

4 Deploy the four change archetypes as reference modes for managing strategic change.

5 Manage key change elements.

6 Avoid the ‘success’ and best practice approaches to the implementation of strategic change.

## Further improvements

We acknowledge that the empirical evidence for our conclusion is limited, and our case studies are lacking in depth. However, we were surprised at the clarity and force of the findings and felt it worthwhile to present our results at an early stage. We believe that the conclusions are sufficiently important to warrant wider discussion, more in-depth research and refinement of the concepts. The notion that there are only a limited set of change patterns is attractive. We intend to seek counter examples and examine other cases in the literature from our new perspective. Theoretical explanation will be sought for the absence of other potential change patterns, for instance where processes drive structure directly.

We will be conducting in-depth studies of a limited set of cases in which change processes have been completed, and will explore the link between the different archetypes and various method of change. We will continue to focus on IT-enabled strategic change and choose our cases accordingly.

## Research limitations

The first limitation is the strategic perspective to the research. As the research seeks to adopt a strategic viewpoint, it inevitably fails to address the behavioural aspects (for instance, personal resistance and coping strategies) and the operational aspects (as in process redesign implementation or the decision-making mechanism). Although these are important for the strategic change initiative, they are subsumed in the pattern portrayed by the strategic perspective of the research.

The second major issue is whether these change patterns, once appreciated, can help firms to implement a transformation. Businesses, after all, need prescriptive solutions to inform managers about what actions to take, and they need to translate these change patterns, as a diagnostic mechanism, into clear implementation guidelines. This research, on the one hand, has to acknowledge such a desire, but, on the other hand, it must offer a warning. For structured problems such as those of IT project management, it is possible to articulate a procedure and sequence of actions, and there may be some ‘critical factors’ that are identified with hindsight. However, for complex process of change, such as a sociopolitical transformation or a strategic change, in which contexts and conditions are constantly changing, prescriptive instruction is less useful. It is possible, as this research suggests, to attempt to understand the basic nature of the phenomenon, to appreciate the dynamic interplay of relationships and elements, and to identify the constant parts (underlying rules) of the change. Thus, we believe sensible and contingent reactions to the problems encountered can be formulated. Through such understanding, it may be possible to navigate through the continuous changes that lead to a further transformation. It is on the basis of this belief that the research described here sets out to achieve a dynamic understanding of strategic change and its inherent complexity, conflicts and paradoxes so as to appreciate the equilibrium of different paths of change. Armed with this new perspective, managers may be able to face IT-enabled strategic change with more confidence and develop a more informed response.

## Biographies

Richard J. Ormerod is Professor of Operational Research and Systems at Warwick Business School, University of Warwick. His research interests include information systems strategy development and consulting interventions.

Ruey-Lin Hsiao is a doctoral candidate at Warwick Business School, University of Warwick. His former experience includes being a manager in industry and a project officer, researcher and lecturer in the Institute for Information Industry (a quasi-government agency) in Taiwan. His current research interest is in the area of strategic IT management and strategic change linking with the use of system dynamics.
