# End-user computing and information systems methodologies

M. J. Taylor, E. P. Moynihan & \*A. T. Wood-Harper

School of Computing and Mathematical Sciences, Liverpool John Moores University, Byrom Street, Liverpool L3 3AF, UK, email: M.J.TAYLOR@LIVJM.AC.UK and \*Information Systems Research Centre, Salford University, Salford M5 4WT, UK

Abstract. End-user computing is a growing area within the information technology (IT) industry. The number and size of end-user-developed applications is steadily increasing, yet little attention is paid to the way in which such applications are developed, and their impact upon organizations. In this paper we examine the relationship between information systems methodologies and the end-user-computing environment. In particular, the potential benefits and disadvantages of the use of information systems methodologies for end-user-computing, and strategies by which such usage can be accomplished are discussed. This paper is partly the result of a research programme involving case studies in 34 UK organizations aimed at improving business systems.

Keywords: End-user-computing, information systems methodology

## INTRODUCTION

End-user-computing can be defined as ‘an information processing activity in which the person has direct personal control over all stages of the activity’ (Hackathorn, 1988).

End-user computing is a steadily growing area of the IT industry (Kettelhut, 1992; Blili et al., 1996). The proliferation of personal computers within organizations has meant that an increasing number of end users are now capable of potentially developing their own applications (Moynihan, 1993; Edberg & Bowman, 1996). Initially end-user computing was limited to manipulation of downloaded data using spreadsheets and other types of statistical packages (De Thomas et al., 1991; Barr & Foley, 1993). The development of user-friendly 4GLs (fourth-generation languages) meant that users then had the option of generating their own reports using 4GLs from extracts of corporate databases (Raymond, 1990; Jenson, 1993).

With the introduction of user friendly relational database packages (such as Microsoft Access and Borland Paradox) end users then had the capability to develop their own stand-alone database applications. The increase in distributed computing has meant that end-user-developed databases can now be linked together, or linked to corporate databases under a distributed database architecture (Lee et al., 1991).

Overall, the IT industry has seen a dramatic increase in the percentage of organizational data that is held by, and manipulated by, end users (OTR, 1995). However, despite the growth in end-user computing, little attention is paid to the way in which end-user applications are developed and maintained, and their impact upon organizations (Amoroso & Cheney, 1991). In this paper we examine the potential benefits and disadvantages of using information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects and discuss strategies for such usage.

## RESEARCH METHOD

The research reported in this paper was based upon case studies carried out in 34 UK organizations during 1994, 1995 and 1996. The organizations studied were from both the public and the private sectors of UK industry and covered a range of industrial activities. The size of the organizations researched ranged from seven to over 30 000 staff. The case study approach was used for this research because the researchers and others believe that: case studies allow the study to be performed in a natural setting, learning about the state of the art, and generating theories for practice; that the nature and complexity of the process can be studied; and that situations that are new and rapidly changing can be studied using the approach (Benbasat et al., 1987). Gummesson (1991) states that an important advantage with case study research is the opportunity for a holistic view of a process:

'The detailed observations entailed in the case study method enable us to study many different aspects, examine them in relation to each other, view the process within its total environment and also utilise the researchers capacity for understanding. Consequently case study research provides us with a greater opportunity than other available research methods to obtain a holistic view of a specific research project.'

Researchers have criticized the lack of empirical research on information systems in real organizational contexts (Hamilton & Ives, 1992).

Details of the 34 organizations studied are shown in Table 1. The questions applied to the case studies by the researchers were:

How is the development of end-user computing applications carried out?

How is the maintenance of end-user computing applications carried out?

How is the quality of end-user computing projects assured?

How are end-user computing projects supported/assisted by the IT department?

How are end-user computing activities co-ordinated within organizations?

What are the potential benefits of using information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects?

What are the potential disadvantages of using information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects?

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Previous research had noted that there were various areas of concern associated with the way in which end-user application development and maintenance projects were undertaken.

## Integrity of end-user-developed applications

Allen (1996) identified security and control weaknesses as the highest priority issues in end-user computing. Barr and Foley (1993) quote an example of a large drug company that used end-user-developed applications to support some of its core activities, that had no security, back-up or recovery procedures. Bigler (1995) noted that many end-user applications lack attention to control considerations such as data backup and recovery and systems security. In a survey of 33 user-developed applications in the USA, Sumner (1985) noted that fewer than 20% of users had any security, backup or recovery procedures. Kettelhut (1992) noted that many database package end users lack awareness of the problems associated with relational integrity, or with deletions, updates and insertions. As a result, end users tend to develop inadequate database applications, which can affect the entire organization, because decisions are based on the incorrect data in end-user-developed databases. Weldon and Plagman (1994) noted that data administration was a weak aspect of end-user computing activities.

## Management of end-user computing activities

Guimaraes (1996) and Huizingh (1993) noted the need to establish mechanisms for management and operational control of end-user computing activities. Chung et al. (1989) discussed the problems that can occur if the management of data from end-user-developed applications is not well co-ordinated, especially when such data are being transferred across functional areas and other subsystems. Jenson (1993) and Angell and Smithson (1991) commented upon the wasted end-user application development resources that are due to duplication of end-user data and processes, resulting from lack of managerial control over such development.

Nantz (1990) found that the lack of training received by the majority of end users in IT skills adversely affected the productivity of their application development activities. End users appeared to develop application systems by a random, trial and error approach, attempting to construct systems from scratch, rather than in any structured or consistent manner. Thus, end users wasted many hours effort in the trial and error process. Another factor found to affect end-user application development productivity was the number of missed opportunities for end-user developers to reuse systems or subsystems developed by other end-user developers. McGann (1990) noted that lack of use of CASE tools by end-user application developers also reduced the scope for increases in end-user application development productivity. Shah and Lawrence (1996) proposed that the provision of intelligent end-user development tools could provide significant business benefits.

## Maintenance of end-user-developed systems

Barr and Foley (1993) and McGann (1990) noted that the majority of end-user-developed systems were difficult even for the original developer to maintain. Zarley (1989) found that IT staff often had great difficulty in understanding and maintaining end-user-developed applications.

## Lack of standards in end-user computing activities

Kettlehut (1992) and Sumner (1987) noted that formal systems development practices were rarely followed in the end-user computing environment. Angell and Smithson (1991) found that lack of systems analysis and design skills by end users led to quality problems in the systems developed by them. Sumner and Klepper (1986) in a study of 33 user-developed applications noted that end-user development activities rarely follow the same development discipline as IT department projects. Jenson (1993) in a survey of 97 American organizations, concluded that the majority of organizations do not provide training for end users in application development methodologies. Barr and Foley (1993) noted that end-user-developed systems were evolved, rather than developed, by amendment and experimentation until the required functionality seemed to be obtained.

Barr and Foley (1993), Barr et al. (1994), Schultheis and Sumner (1994) and Pierson et al. (1990) concluded that there is a need to introduce quality assurance mechanisms into the field of end-user computing. Without such mechanisms there are real dangers that the validity of the data from end-user-developed applications that is used for decision making within organizations may be compromised (Yeager, 1990; Klepper & Sumner, 1990; Alavi et al., 1990). Jenne (1996) commented upon the need for internal audits of end-user-developed systems within organizations. Barr and Foley (1993) stressed the need to improve the quality of end-user developments by applying BS5750 and other related standards to end-user software development. Karten (1987) identified establishing guidelines for end-user application development as one of the 10 key challenges in managing end-user computing. Bento (1989) confirmed that end-user application developers needed development methodologies as much as suitable software tools for successful projects.

However, despite the concerns regarding end-user computing, Rockart and Flannery (1983) noted as far back as 1983, that end users have needs that are not satisfied through the traditional IT department. In particular, users expressed the view that the IT department often ‘held the business back’, (Angell & Smithson, 1991) by insisting on long timescales and high costs for projects. End-user computing is viewed by many end users as a means of freeing themselves from the ‘control’ of the IT department with regard to their business operations. In a study of 162 users in 30 different organizations, Huarng (1995) suggested that end-user development can be more effective than IT department development. From the IT manager’s perspective, however, direct end-user access to corporate databases considerably complicates the job of the IT department. Canning (1986) concluded that many IT mangers were not willing to encourage end users to access corporate databases directly and were hesitant to invest the necessary funds for this purpose.

## RESULTS

## The problems of end-user computing

This research, in the course of case studies in 34 UK organizations, encountered a number of problems areas in end-user computing activities:

## Duplication of effort

This research found that in the organizations studied unless specific mechanisms were established for promulgating awareness of similar end-user application developments, end-user developers appeared to rarely consult each other and simply develop their own systems in isolation. Although only 14 of the 34 organizations studied had a significant number of end-user developers or end-user-developed systems, only 6 of these 14 had any formal mechanism for coordinating end-user activities as shown in Table 1. As the number of end-user-developed applications continues to grow, there is clearly a growing cost for organizations in terms of unnecessary duplicated data and processes. This research suggested that unless end-users were aware of each others' systems, development activities and instances of duplication of both data and processes were bound to arise. This not only meant that the organization overall was operating less than efficiently, but also that data integrity problems could easily arise as duplicated data could easily become out of step when updates occurred. There was also the problem of integrating data from different end-user-developed systems to give more global information. Weldon and Plagman (1994) and Chung et al. (1989) noted the problems of integrating user-developed systems and other subsystems to effectively support organizational management and decision making. As end users tended to develop systems in their own way, there was little consistency between the developed systems. For example, in an insurance company, studied as part of this research, a number of underwriters have developed their own spreadsheet-based applications to assist them with rating reviews of insurance products. Unfortunately, because of the incompatibility of the developed systems, the overall departmental rating reviews ended up being performed manually using printed outputs from the different systems.

## Quality of end-user-developed systems

This research indicated that the majority of end users in the 34 organizations studied applied few if any quality assurance or quality control principles in the systems they developed. For example, the IT staff interviewed in the organizations researched confirmed that end users hardly ever produced formal test plans for the applications they developed. In particular, the IT staff interviewed in the majority of the organizations studied indicated that end users appeared to be unaware of the full implications of network/PC failure when designing applications, and unless advised by the IT department typically had insufficient backup and recovery procedures.

This research indicated that if they did any testing at all, end users in the 34 organizations studied appeared to test their own applications by means of a few ‘standard examples’, which meant that the ‘non-standard’ transactions, or unusual combinations of data or processes, would often not be adequately tested. Cale (1994) noted the lack of testing guidelines for end-user application developers. Barr and Foley (1993) had previously commented that testing of end-user-developed systems was superficial at best and completely non-existent in other cases.

In addition to the technical quality of end-user-developed systems, there was also the question of quality of use, both in terms of the developed applications and the development tools used. This research indicated that the quality of use of end-user development tools could potentially be improved through improved training in such tools. For example, in one manufacturing organization researched, some database end users tended to set up single table relational database applications simply because they were uncertain about how to join tables together. In terms of quality of use of the developed applications, the lack of co-ordination between end users in the majority of the 34 organizations studied meant that in several of the larger organizations researched, duplication of end-user-controlled data occurred, with the possible risk of data not always being up to date across the duplicated data stores.

## Lack of training for end-user developers

This research and others (Karten, 1991; Alavi et al., 1990) indicated that the vast majority of end users have little if any formal training in systems development techniques. Lee (1995) noted that end-user training in general was weak within organizations. This research indicated that typically end users may have at most attended a 2-day training course on the foundations of such tools. Table 1 shows which of the 34 organizations studied provided training in the use of development tools for their end users. De Thomas et al. (1991) had previously found that in the case of spreadsheet-based end-user application developments, the majority of end users had to learn the spreadsheet package unaided.

End users in the 34 organizations researched appeared to have had no training at all concerning the maintenance of the systems they developed. This was exemplified in a financial company researched regarding a project that involved the introduction of VAT on non-life insurance premiums. The end users in this organization had developed a number of applications containing insurance premium data. However, because of the ad hoc manner in which these spreadsheet applications had been written, it proved extremely difficult to modify them to incorporate the new VAT charge, and in some instances the applications had to be scrapped as they could simply not be modified in the time frame available.

## The potential benefits and disadvantages of information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects

In an attempt to reduce some of the problems associated with end-user computing, this research investigated how using information systems methodologies for such activities could potentially reduce the identified problem areas of end-user computing.

This research suggested that using information systems methodologies may reduce the problems associated with the maintenance of end-user-developed systems by ensuring that, first, systems are designed to cater for future enhancements by adopting a more structured/modular or object-oriented approach to assist future comprehension of the systems nature and operation; and, second, that impact analysis and coding of amendments is simplified by adhering to good programming practices, such as standardized data names and commenting of functions/data within the systems. Overall, the original design of the system can be made clearer by virtue of the design documentation generated by most information systems methodologies. The example of a maintenance project to implement the new VAT charge on non-life insurance premiums previously quoted was one in which the poor quality of the original design and coding lengthened the time required to implement the change. As the maintenance requirement came from HM Customs and Excise, this maintenance project was one in which the deadline was fixed, and therefore project delays were particularly unwelcome.

A formalized information systems methodology may help to ensure that duplication of data and processes in end-user-developed systems does not occur so frequently by using feasibility studies to identify possible reuse of other end-user-developed or IT-developed systems or systems components. Co-ordination and control of end-user data and processing can be achieved by a number of mechanisms, such as change control committees, and review and inspection procedures, to ensure that systems are developed to an appropriate standard of quality, and that such systems will be, when appropriate, compatible. The majority of mature information systems methodologies would include these or other similar control mechanisms. As shown in Table 1, few of the 34 organizations studied applied much co-ordination to end-user development within their own organizations.

Most information systems methodologies would indicate how to achieve appropriate network security/backup/recovery within either the design or the implementation stages of the methodology. The quality of end-user-developed systems can be enhanced through the use of the quality assurance mechanisms embedded in most mature information systems methodologies. For example the cross-checking that is possible through use of SSADM due to its three views of the system, namely process, data and the effects of time. Information systems methodologies can also help to ensure that the developed systems are complete in the sense that all aspects of the requirements are considered.

The main disadvantages of using information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects centred around the bureaucracy associated with such methodologies. For the majority of end users the time and effort required for producing the numerous items of documentation in order to follow their IT department's methodology would probably be too great an overhead for the size of projects undertaken. The IT staff interviewed indicated that there were few end-user projects that took more than 1 person-week to complete in any of the 34 organizations studied.

Although this paper promotes the use of information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects, it should be recognized that such usage needs to be ‘appropriate’, that is the most ‘beneficial’ aspects of a given information systems methodology should be used, without incurring that problems that excessive bureaucratization can bring. Mathiassen & Stage (1992) discuss the balance between formal and informal approaches to information systems projects based upon the complexity and uncertainty of a design situation. Fitzgerald (1994) commented that formalized information systems methodologies may not always be the most productive approach to carrying out information systems projects. Taylor et al. (1996) examined the use of information systems methodologies within small IT departments and concluded that even within IT departments, information systems methodologies unless ‘appropriately’ used may provide little benefit for smaller scale IT projects. ## Strategies for using information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects

Although none of the 34 organizations researched used an information systems methodology for end-user computing activities, during the course of this research the researchers identified a number of strategies that could potentially be used by organizations for using information systems methodologies for end-user computing projects.

1 End users should develop and maintain their own systems to the same standards as the IT department (Evans, 1989; Nantz, 1990). Liu (1989) recommended that end-user groups should be taught the information systems methodology and then assisted in the use of the information systems methodology by a ‘chief developer’ in the information centre. However, this research indicated that for the vast majority of end-user activities in the 34 organizations studied, the information systems methodology used by the IT department would be far too large and unwieldy. For example, spreadsheet developers would not really need to use data normalization or structured English, even although these techniques were part of the IT development methodology in a number of the organizations studied. In addition, the time and cost to follow all the steps in the IT department’s methodology would discourage most end-user developers.

2 End users should adopt a mandatory ‘cut-down’ version of the IT department’s information systems methodology. In the majority of the 34 organizations studied, this research indicated that end-user developments are usually much smaller in scale than IT department development projects, hence end users do not really require use of a ‘full-blown’ information systems methodology. For the majority of the end users in the 34 organizations researched, the time and cost of following their own IT department’s methodology would probably be too much of an overhead for their own development activities.

3 IT facilitators/advisors should guide users through an appropriate subset of the information systems methodology for each project. End users appeared to be developing an increasing range of types of systems, for example spreadsheet-based data processing applications; reporting suites from corporate databases using query languages; and database applications for small data volumes, as shown in Table 1. This research found that different facets of an information systems methodology applied themselves more usefully to some of these activities than others. For example, data normalization and entity modelling were considered useful by database end-user developers in one of the manufacturing organizations researched, but end-user spreadsheet developers within the same organization regarded them as being of little relevance. Thus, using ‘appropriate’ techniques within a given information systems methodology for a particular end-user development project appeared to be more effective than continually using the same set of techniques each time. Sumner and Klepper (1986) noted the need to develop different application development approaches for end-user development projects with different characteristics.

4 End users should use the IT department's information systems methodology in a contingency fashion, deciding for themselves which techniques within the methodology would be most appropriate for a given project. This is the approach advocated by MULTIVIEW (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990).

5 End users should develop their own specific information systems methodology. Amoroso (1988) had noted that the process of end-user development differs from the traditional systems development life cycle. This research indicated that for the majority of end-user activities a 'full-sized' information systems methodology would be too much of an overhead for the majority of projects. What was found to be potentially more appropriate was an information systems methodology that catered specifically for smaller scale projects. Karten (1987) suggested that a scaled down mainframe methodology could possibly provide a suitable approach for end-user application developments. MicroSSADM (Downs et al., 1988) is a version of SSADM that is specifically aimed at small projects, and thus may be suited to some organizations' end-user computing activities.

## CONCLUSIONS

In this paper we have examined the way in which end-user computing activities were carried out using case studies in 34 UK organizations and have discussed the potential benefits and disadvantages of using information systems methodologies for end-user application development and maintenance. Finally, this paper has outlined strategies by which organizations can attempt to reduce some of the problems associated with end-user computing by ‘appropriate’ application of information systems methodologies to such work.

Unless action is taken to effectively manage and control end-user developments, then the integrity of organizational data affected by such systems may well deteriorate (Schultheis & Sumner, 1994; Panko, 1996). As many organizations are beginning to use end-user-developed applications to support core organizational activities (Sumner & Klepper, 1987), this is an area of deep concern. Further research is required into this area to develop more detailed managerial strategies to reduce the potential for harm to organizations from uncontrolled end-user developments.

## Biographies

Mark Taylor is currently a senior lecturer at Liverpool John Moores University. In the course of his industrial career, he has worked in both the manufacturing and finance sectors as an analyst programmer, systems designer and systems analyst. He is a member of the British Computer Society and an active IT consultant.

Eddie Moynihan is currently a reader in information technology for business administration at Liverpool John Moores University. He spent several years as an analyst-programmer in manufacturing and commerce, and was a project manager for a systems house in charge of developing financial software packages. He was also a systems development manager with the Dunlop Group. He is the author of a book on business systems analysis and is a member of the British Computer Society and a Chartered Engineer.

Trevor Wood-Harper is currently Professor of Computer Science at Salford University and heads the research group in information systems methodology and evaluation. He is an active consultant using the Multiview methodology, and has published widely with more than 50 research articles and four books to his credit.
