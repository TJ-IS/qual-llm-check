# Process and reality in information systems benefit analysis

Chuleeporn Changchit, Kshiti D. Joshi\* & Albert L. Lederer

School of Management, Carol M. Gatton College of Business Administration, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, and \*Washington State University, College of Business and Economics, Pullman, WA 99164-4726, USA, email: lederer@ukcc.uky.edu http://www.uky.edu/\~lederer

Abstract. The identification of the potential benefits of a proposed information system project is a very challenging and important task. However, little research describes how this is actually carried out. The objective of this research was to answer the core question: how do organizations identify the expected benefits of proposed IS projects? To accomplish this objective, the authors studied 24 projects by interviewing 20 subjects from 13 organizations. Based on these interviews, a model of the benefit identification process was formulated showing it to be a set of loosely defined, overlapping, iterative activities. The study suggests that, despite great uncertainty about the potential benefits, proposers were compelled to use the benefit identification process to persuade the organization to be committed to the project. This effort to build commitment under great uncertainty may contribute to the overstatement or understatement of expected benefits. The study offers managers an understanding of the process and guidance for carrying it out. It also suggests that future researchers validate the model, use it to help identify best practices and test related hypotheses.

Keywords: Information systems benefit analysis, cost–benefit analysis, information systems planning, information systems management

## INTRODUCTION

The identification of the expected benefits of proposed information systems is a challenging task (Powell, 1992). The dynamic nature of information systems, the variety of technical options, the uncertainties of the projected pay-offs, the potential presence of intangible benefits, and the presence of multiple perspectives on the contribution of information systems (Symons, 1990) may be reasons for this. Moreover, because competitors are constantly trying to find ways to gain advantage via information technology, failure to invest appropriately in IT may lead to very unfavourable business consequences (McFarlan, 1984; Porter & Millar, 1985). A comprehensive search for benefits can contribute to the success or failure of an IT investment (Farbey et al., 1995).

According to one study, the amount of money invested in IT has now reached about half of all durable equipment spending (Loveman, 1991). However, a lack of consensus on the question of economic cost-effectiveness still pervades IT investment (Orenstein, 1986; Oman & Ayers, 1988; Sutherland, 1994). In fact, with the rise of IT investment in recent years, considerable uncertainty and concern about its productivity impact has ensued (Willcocks, 1996).

Moreover, investment in IT has usually not met expectations (AT Kearney, 1990; Willcocks, 1992a; Audit Commission, 1994). A major reason for this disappointing outcome may be poor evaluation of such investment (Wilson, 1991). This ‘productivity paradox’ — dramatically increasing investment with uncertain or limited impact — may be attributable to the difficulty in evaluating the outputs from IT (Loveman, 1988). On the other hand, it may be that the paradox arises because much of this investment is made without firm knowledge of the expected costs and benefits of information systems when they are proposed (Weill, 1992). Despite the prescribed practices of numerous popular information systems development methodologies (Checkland, 1981; 1989; Ashworth, 1988), this paradox continues to plague organizations (Brynjolfsson, 1993). Thus, inaccurate IS project selection decisions may be the source of the paradox (Bacon, 1992).

Because IS project selection includes predicting the expected benefits of a proposed system, predicting them is a key factor (Willcocks, 1992b). However, there is a shortage of information on how organizations actually do this (Willcocks & Lester, 1991) and a growing expression of the need for more in-depth study of the benefit of the identification process (Vizachero, 1986; Changchit et al., 1997). Top management is especially concerned about the process (Farbey et al., 1993). This research attempts to understand and explain the process. Doing so might begin to contribute to the development of a theory about the process and thus stimulate further empirical research. Such research might also eventually help managers understand why investment in IT does not always appear to have commensurate benefits.

## BACKGROUND

Although much has been written about the anticipated costs of proposed information systems, much less has appeared about their expected benefits (Lederer & Mirani, 1995). In fact, despite the importance of the identification of benefits of proposed information systems, virtually all of the literature on the subject has been subsumed within writings about the broader study of both IS costs and benefits.

## Prescriptive studies

Most of the previous studies have been prescriptive. They typically presented analytical models or methods for benefit appraisal and evaluation. For example, traditional cost–benefit analysis has been suggested for evaluating proposed information systems before their implementation (King & Schrems, 1978). Cost–benefit analysis has been seen as a very rigid tool that cannot be bent to the strategy of the organization. Moreover, with its emphasis on both the ‘hard’ benefits and the rate of return, it tends to favour the development of conservative systems with a high pay-off. Thus, a phased design methodology has been suggested to use cost–benefit analysis as an effective tool for complex IS projects (Keim & Janaro, 1982).

Other techniques have also been prescribed. Decision analysis has been suggested as an objective method to establish the value of IS via expected pay-offs (Schell, 1986). An approach using a ‘total factor productivity’ incorporates the analysis of activities necessary to the functioning of an organization by examining the effect of IT on productivity (Oman & Ayers, 1988). A cost-value method (Orli & Tom, 1987) and options-pricing theory (Cheung & Bagranoff, 1991) may be helpful for evaluating the benefits of proposed IS projects. Because a major portion of the value of a new IS stems not from its initial use but rather from projects that might be undertaken in future stages, the value of these later projects must be considered (Dos Santos, 1991).

Some approaches consider the intangible factors in IS selection decisions (Orli & Tom, 1987; Rivard & Kaiser, 1989; Cheung & Bagranoff, 1991). Incremental analysis, expected value, value analysis and a benefit profile are techniques for measuring the intangible benefits of IS investments.

Information economics has been suggested as a means of evaluating IS projects (Parker & Benson, 1987). It is used for measuring and justifying the value of IT based on business performance.

Finally, benefit identification is seen as a function of strategic information systems planning. Value chain analysis, competitive force analysis, or similar strategic planning techniques are means of identifying benefits in that context (Porter & Millar, 1985). Brainstorming is also used during strategic IS planning.

## Empirical studies

Some empirical studies have considered the identification of benefits. For example, a quasi-experimental study assessed the criteria and content of a feasibility study for IS development projects for industry and academia (Palvia & Palvia, 1988). The study identified criteria (e.g. the importance of operational factors) but offered little about the process by which managers identify benefits.

The criteria used by organizations in allocating strategic IS resources were also examined in a survey (Bacon, 1992). This study also concentrated on the decision criteria rather than the process for identifying benefits.

Willcocks and Lester (1991) used questionnaires with follow-up interviews to discover techniques for evaluating the costs and benefits of IS investments. The study uncovered a variety of evaluation practices with which managers were satisfied but did not explain how they initially conceived the benefits.

Structured interviews were conducted to identify current practice in the evaluation of IT benefits (Sutherland, 1994). This study used McFarlan's strategic grid (McFarlan, 1984) as a framework with which executives can measure IT benefits. It also explored associations between IT system types and effective evaluation practices but did not explain how a manager initially conceived the benefits.

Thus, in general, these empirical studies have focused on the evaluation of benefits and costs.

They have not examined the process by which the benefits had been identified. There remains a dearth of studies that describe the current practices in the benefit identification process.

Therefore, a study to explore the current practices in the benefit identification process was undertaken. Such study can facilitate an understanding of the nature, complexity and diversity of the benefit identification process. The objective of this research was to answer this core question: how do organizations identify the expected benefits of proposed IS projects?

## METHODOLOGY

This study was conducted using structured interviews in which a series of well-defined, open-ended questions was followed by extemporaneous, probing questions. Although investigation of each case was not as in-depth as in the conventional case study approach, many aspects of case research justify the structured interview method.

For instance, such qualitative research is appropriate for sticky, practice-based problems when the experiences of the actors are important and the context of action is critical (Bonoma, 1983). Identification of benefits is such a practice-based process. In fact, a lack of previous research would have made the development of a survey to capture the fuzzy nature of this process very difficult and thus made it necessary to conduct the research as a series of open-ended questions.

Qualitative research is suitable for areas in which research and theory are at its early formative stages (Roethlisberger, 1977; Benbasat et al., 1987). The study of identification of benefits is in its early stages. The purpose of the current research was to explore the benefit identification process rather than explain it. Qualitative research with structured interviews can generate a set of propositions or hypotheses that can be investigated later for confirmation or refutation. As an explanation building technique, qualitative research has been commonly cited as a basis for such proposition or hypothesis generation (Yin, 1989).

Qualitative research is useful to study a process in its natural setting and learn about the state of the art (Benbasat et al., 1987). The study of the benefit identification process in a natural setting would be necessary for learning about it.

Finally, qualitative research allows researchers to understand the nature and complexity of the process taking place by seeking answers to why and how questions (Benbasat et al., 1987). By answering such questions, current practices in identification of benefits could be understood. Seeking answers to these types of questions (e.g. how do organizations conduct the benefit identification process and why do they undertake certain activities?) was only possible if the investigation was carried out using a script with open-ended questions. In other words, structured interviews permit research to proceed without full stipulation of hypotheses at the beginning of a study; final understanding can result from a series of iterations to discover them (Yin, 1989).

For site selection, 15 companies were chosen from two business regional directories of all the companies in a large mid-western US city. Of the 15, 13 agreed to be interviewed. One declined because its holding company in another city did its assessment of benefits and another declined because its assessment of benefits had been carried out by employees no longer with the firm.

The criterion for site selection was diversity in the type of industry. Therefore, the sample included organizations from manufacturing and services generally with their headquarters in the city. Table 1 identifies the industries of the participants.

The researchers also attempted to have diversity in the positions of the subjects. They therefore wrote a letter to the selected senior executives whose generic titles appear in Table 2. The letter asked them to identify individuals who had evaluated the benefits of proposed information systems in their organization. It assured confidentiality to gain their cooperation.

Data were gathered through structured interviews. Subjects were interviewed for one hour on each project and were asked open-ended questions about the project and the identification of its expected benefits. They were also asked some demographic questions.

## DATA ANALYSIS

On the basis of similarities of purpose in the context of the interviews, the authors categorized answers to ‘What tasks and activities did you follow to identify the benefits?’ into four groups of activities — problem identification, mini-study of current business processes, mini-design of proposed business processes, and comparison of benefits of current and proposed processes. In other words, one group of activities appeared to help identify problems, another helped understand current business processes, and so on. Several responses fit more than one activity.

The authors used these responses along with the subjects' elucidation of them and their responses to the other questions to formulate a proposed model for identification of benefits. The model is descriptive and intended to characterize broadly the process of benefit identification.

The flow of the groups of activities in the model was based on their general chronological order. After considering various alternative versions, the authors conceived the final model because the groups of activities resembled a condensed system development life cycle. However, the model represents strictly an initial, feasibility-study phase of the life cycle. The authors use ‘mini-study’ to emphasize that the study is preliminary and that extensive systems analysis and design phases follow later in the life cycle. They also use it because the study described here is much smaller scale than the study in those phases. Figure 1 shows the model.

## Problem identification

Identification of benefits typically begins with activities aimed at identifying problems. Problem identification can include the proactive identification of opportunities and initiatives. This step appeared to be one of the most critical ones in determining the success or failure of the project. As a manager of client relations said, ‘It is very, very important in our business to understand fully the requirements of our customers (users). We conducted user interviews and had them identify for us what they needed now and what they would need in the future to do business.’ A general manager of information systems said, ‘The first step is to identify the problem.’ He added that ‘We talked to the people who were using the processes and tried to find out what their problems or biggest concerns were.’

Of the 24, 16 (67%) explicitly mentioned that they started the benefit identification process with problem identification. Table 7 provides a list of specific activities that were carried out to identify the problems. The activities are shown almost verbatim from the interviews. The numbers in Table 7 were derived by counting the projects that used each activity. The activities appear in descending order by frequency of occurrence. (Similar tables appear for each of the other groups of activities.)

The most frequent activity mentioned by the subjects was a discussion meeting. Such meetings were held to identify users' needs and their requirements for new processes. Then, the prioritization of the problems to determine the most critical would follow. Costs, timing, and other business issues were considered while prioritizing the problems.

## Mini-design of proposed business processes

Once the developers sufficiently study the current business processes and extensively elicit users' needs, the next step is to develop an initial, rough design of the proposed business processes. The term ‘mini’ assumes the same meaning as above. Of all of the activities gathered through the interviews, the largest number fall into this step.

Of the 24 projects, subjects indicated that 22 (92%) followed this step. Table 9 provides a list of all activities carried out here.

The most frequently mentioned activity in this group was discussion meetings. As one information systems manager said, 'We had meeting discussions with users. Sometimes, there had to be some changes such as to make the systems more accurate. The users could give us some ideas or opinions to improve the systems.' One major concern in this step was how to select the design with the best benefits. This was carried out in several ways. As a chief financial officer said, ‘We approached the companies which were similar to ours and which used the same computer systems as we did. We asked them what they did when they were faced with similar situations and what were the alternatives they found. We also called our software vendors and asked for alternatives.’ A finance project administrator told us that his organization ‘interviewed the key users. This helped us to identify what is needed in the system, what are the benefits we should be looking for.’ Some subjects also sought help from outside experts as indicated by a director of restaurant systems when he said, ‘We consulted an external consultant. They helped us identify the companies having best practices.’

It may be necessary to return to previous steps in order to collect more information that will help to design the processes better. As the manager of client relations said, 'In order to avoid a communication gap, it is very important to have ongoing monthly type review meetings. These meetings were very important, not only to hear what the users said, but also to articulate it back to them so that they had vivid knowledge of what they would be getting. Moreover, this project took a long time to complete and during that time, business processes and needs change. Therefore, these meetings help us to modify project plans to accommodate those changes.' Such remarks indicated a reconsideration of the problem identification and mini-study of current business processes. They thus formed the basis of the arrows from mini-design of proposed business processes to the previous two steps.

## Comparison of benefits of current and proposed processes

After the proposed processes were designed, the benefits of the new processes were evaluated in comparison with the current processes. Table 10 provides a list of all activities carried out to compare the benefits of current and proposed processes. Of the 24 projects, subjects directly indicated that 17 (71%) followed this step.

Work measurement was the most frequently mentioned activity. The merchandise marketing operations manager illustrated this by saying, ‘The work measurement technique was used, the judgement was made on the person we found the most average. The same person was used for the old and the new system.’ A financial systems and planning manager elucidated by saying that ‘We projected benefits of time savings. We did the analysis on how much time it took to do manual matching of the invoice, purchase order, and receiving report vs. how much time it would take to do this electronically . . . we used a stop watch.’ A general manager of information systems added that ‘. . . piloting the new system and comparing it with the existing one is a part of benefit analysis process’.

## DISCUSSION

The purpose of this research was to understand how organizations identify the benefits of proposed information systems. Interviews with business and information systems managers facilitated the development of a model describing four groups of activities that comprise benefit identification. In addition, the interviews suggested other interesting insights.

For example, although the study identified four main groups of activities, it also found $\approx$ 60 diverse, specific activities. Several of these appeared in more than one group (e.g. discussion meetings appeared in each group). Also, more than one of the four groups could occur concurrently. For example, the mini-study of current processes could begin before problem identification is complete.

Furthermore, identification of benefits is iterative. For example, after some mini-study of current business processes, further problem identification may take place.

In other words, benefit identification is not practised as a set of highly defined, mutually exclusive, strictly sequential activities. Instead, it consists of loosely defined, overlapping iterative ones.

In addition to the development of the descriptive model, two interesting themes emerged from the interviews: uncertainty in benefit identification and the importance of persuasion.

## Uncertainty

Participants repeatedly indicated the presence of uncertainty. For example, a manufacturing systems manager acknowledged that ‘lots of intuition and guess work is involved’ and spoke of measuring labour savings ‘intuitively’. An information systems applications software manager alluded to the importance of ‘past experience and judgement’. After describing the identification of benefits in his firm in vague terms, a chief financial officer agreed that the basis of the process was a ‘judgement call’. Consistent with that view, a general manager of information systems said, ‘We had to make the judgement based on the experiences at that time.’ A vice president of administration perhaps best summed up this mixture of intuition and judgement by saying:

‘... you just take a lot of people who know the business and they make their intuitive guess where the work process is today vs. what it will be. Sometime it’s right, sometime it’s wrong.’

Although not directly mentioning judgement and intuition, several participants corroborated the presence of uncertainty. For example, a manager of client relations said, ‘we don’t know everything about what the future is going to bring’ and spoke of the importance of ‘assumptions’ in benefit identification. A general manager of information systems acknowledged that inaccuracy in identification of benefits may occur because ‘other things change at the same time’. A vice president and chief financial officer mentioned that ‘most of the measurement is qualitative’, whereas a finance project administrator said that ‘benefits were more subjective than objective’.

A controller acknowledged that ‘we did not do any in-depth analysis for this project’, thus suggesting probable uncertainty about its benefits. A manufacturing systems manager summarized his uncertainty by emphatically observing ‘we don’t define benefits accurately’. Finally, an information systems applications software manager excused his organization’s uncertainty in identifying the expected benefits of a proposed project by saying that at least ‘it was a good learning experience’.

## Persuasion

The second interesting theme that emerged from the interviews was the importance of persuading managers and their subordinates to feel committed to the project. Beyond simply identifying benefits for project justification, benefit identification is used to generate their commitment. A vice president of administration illustrated this when he said, 'We had to convince [the users] and make them understand the best value of that system.' He added, 'More than identify the benefits, you also had them sign up so they had commitment to make it happen when you got the system in.'

A director of restaurant systems spoke of the importance of ‘involving users into the process’ of identifying benefits. A director of management information systems at the same firm used the phrase ‘through consensus’ to describe the benefit identification process.

A vice president of administration said, ‘we had to compromise about the expected benefits of the system’ — that is to reduce a predication about benefits — in order to gain user commitment. A merchandise marketing operations manager further described the process of building commitment by saying, ‘We overcame this [user] resistance by formally handing over the system to the user department.’

An IS manager reiterated this idea by saying it was important to ‘make users feel they have the ownership of the project’. A general manager of IS spoke of using ‘focus groups — about 10 persons — so that when the system is implemented, the users feel that they are part of system development. They are more likely to support the system.’

A vice president and CFO summarized the importance of user commitment by identifying the activities that helped most in evaluating the benefits of an IS project. He said, ‘User input — if the users do not embrace it, adopt it, and support it we do not have a system.’

## Persuasion under conditions of uncertainty

Subjects in this study appreciated the need to build organizational commitment to their proposed information systems. Their remarks often suggested that they needed persuasion for that purpose. In all likelihood, uncertainty about the eventual benefits of the proposed information systems makes such persuasion a more challenging task than it would otherwise be.

Uncertainty also allows IS proposers to be imprecise. That is, whether as unconscious advocates of information systems or perhaps even as conscious manipulators, uncertainty permits them to overstate or understate the expected benefits. On the other hand, complete certainty about expected benefits — based on indisputable facts — would prevent overstatement or understatement.

Indeed, the need to persuade may compel proposers to overstate or understate expected benefits. In the absence of fact, it may result in the approval of projects whose eventual benefits cannot match their predicted ones. The productivity paradox might thus to some degree hinge on project selection decisions based on excessively sanguine expectations for proposed information systems.

## Implications for managers

When managers identify the potential benefits of a proposed information system, they need to reduce the possibility of eventually rejecting one with substantial benefits and of accepting one without such benefits. They also need to carry out the benefit identification process without the excessive waste of time, effort and other resources. This research provides assistance to managers in meeting these goals.

First, it provides a model with four groups of activities typically followed in identifying benefits. For those managers who do not rigorously identify benefits, the model provides guidance. Following it more closely is probably more useful than following it less closely. The model thus can probably allow these managers to plan and control the benefits identification process better. It can also enable them to report their progress and findings to other managers and users more clearly during the process.

Second, the research provides managers with four checklists of activities (i.e. the tables for each group). They can use the checklists to find and incorporate new activities into their own benefit identification process. Doing so might help managers feel more comfortable that they are not missing activities found useful by other organizations. It might also improve the process.

Finally, this research suggests that despite great uncertainty in benefit identification, persuasion to build organizational commitment plays an important role in it. Perhaps managers should more diligently try to base project selection decisions on their best knowledge of the expected benefits of proposed information systems. Perhaps they should also more diligently attempt to ensure that proposers' efforts to persuade managers and other users to feel committed to projects do not influence selection decisions.

## Implications for researchers

This study offers several implications for researchers. First, it provides them a model to validate empirically. Researchers should confirm the existence of the four groups of activities with problem identification as a starting point and comparison of benefits of proposed and current processes as its conclusion. Longitudinal study of practising managers could guide such study. It could assess the extent to which each group is attempted and accomplished.

Researchers should also attempt to determine the activities that are most and least effective and efficient in facilitating benefit identification and ultimate project success. Perhaps under certain circumstances, particular activities are more effective or efficient. For example, perhaps the use of business managers (rather than computer experts) is more effective in leading discussion meetings about problem identification in business areas of the organization. Hence a contingency approach might prove useful.

This raises broader research questions about the skills required for participants in benefit identification. For example, researchers should investigate the relative importance of communications and negotiations skills in contrast to technical and managerial skills.

Researchers should also investigate the lack of use of some benefit identification techniques. For example, participants did not mention brainstorming. They also did not mention value chain analysis, competitive force analysis, or similar strategic planning means of identifying benefits (Porter & Millar, 1985). They did not mention any formal processes except joint application development (JAD). Are these formal processes seen as unnecessary, ineffective or inefficient?

Finally, this study prompted additional observations about benefit identification and thus suggested related propositions for researchers to test. Examples include:

P1: If users participate more in the identification of benefits for a project, then their commitment to it is greater.

P2: If the expected benefits of a proposed information system are more uncertain, then proposers make greater efforts at persuasion.

P3: If proposers make greater efforts at persuasion, then greater imbalance exists between expected and realized benefits.

## CONCLUSION

Perhaps the most important contribution of this study is the observation that in practice managers have not gone beyond the obvious. Either they do not appear to do more than what conventional methodologies have recommended, or they do not appear to use those recommendations as effectively as they need to. IS researchers, on the other hand, have not given them anything substantial for creative benefit identification. Perhaps poor project selection due to poor benefit identification is partly responsible for the apparently poor performance of IT investment. That is, poor identification of benefits may explain why the productivity paradox endures.
