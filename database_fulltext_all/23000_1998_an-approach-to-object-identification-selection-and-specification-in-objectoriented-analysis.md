# An approach to object identification, selection and specification in object-oriented analysis

Ying Liang, Daune West & Frank A. Stowell\*

Department of Computing and Information Systems, University of Paisley, Paisley PA1 2BE, UK, and \*Department of Computing and Information Science, De Montfort University, Milton Keynes, UK, email: lian-ci0@uk.ac.paisley

Abstract. When using current object-oriented methods in the development of computer-based information systems, problems frequently arise in the identification and specification of 'objects'. In this paper, these problems are discussed and an approach that draws upon interpretivist systems thinking is presented that, it is argued, may alleviate some of the problems. It is proposed that a method that has been developed specifically to aid the user and analyst to appreciate a perceived problem situation might provide a useful aid in the process of identifying objects from the situation which are meaningful to the user. [An attempt has been made to use terminology that is consistent with that used in object-oriented analysis. However, at times this terminology may be at variance with some of the interpretivist ideas put forward (e.g. the term 'user' is adopted, although the authors argue that the notion of 'client' is more appropriate to emphasize the increased involvement and responsibility of the 'user' in the approach advocated)]. The ability to achieve this is expected to facilitate the appropriate mapping of these meaningful objects into a technical specification through object-oriented methods and to encourage user acceptance of the resulting computer-based information system.

Keywords: appreciative inquiry method, object identification, object specification, problem appreciation, soft systems approach.

## INTRODUCTION

Booch (1991) describes object-oriented analysis (OOA) as ‘a method of analysis in which requirements are examined from the perspective of the classes and objects found in the vocabulary of the problem domain’. The identification of the ‘objects’ that make up classes is central to OOA. Previous investigation into the use of object-oriented methods for information system analysis (Liang, 1994, suggests that there are two activities that are fundamental to the useful application of object orientation in OOA, namely (1) the identification of candidate objects from the problem situation, and (2) the selection of those candidate objects that are likely to be significant to a computer-based information system and that are then developed and specified in an object model. The two activities, as shown in Figure 1, are included in a number of methods, such as Embley et al. (1992), Jacobson et al. (1992), Martin and Odell (1992) and Shlaer and Mellor (1988, 1992). The difficulty of identifying candidate objects and selecting meaningful objects is discussed in detail in the second section as an introduction to the argument for adopting an interpretivist system-based inquiry method as a precursor to OOA.

Norman (1990) states that the user's model (the user's view of the problem situation) is important in system analysis. Currently, object-oriented methods assume that objects within a problem situation will be identifiable and that the analyst's view will be consistent (as to usefulness and appropriateness of objects) with that of the user. Any discrepancies should be considered and then, traditionally, the analyst identifies and selects useful, or meaningful, objects from the situation. However, the process of gaining a full and rich understanding of the problem situation and appropriate objects falls outside the scope of current OOA methods. We would argue therefore that a way of gaining a wide appreciation of the situation under analysis (for the benefit of both user and analyst) could be included in the process of OOA in order to strengthen the approach.

Methods of problem appreciation are receiving considerable attention in the wider field of information systems analysis and practical ways of facilitating a rich appreciation of the problem situation for all concerned are being put forward (e.g. Stowell & West, 1994a; Stowell, 1995). Soft systems methodology (SSM) (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990) has been advocated as one practical approach to problem appreciation and information systems analysis and design. The potential contribution of SSM to the field of computer and software engineering has been recognized for some time. For example, Shaw and Gaines (1983) advocated adopting aspects of SSM to help provide a framework for knowledge engineering, whereas Holdsworth (1994, p. 69) has suggested that SSM has ‘introduced a useful way of developing and capturing a clear view of purpose and mission, when there are a number of competing viewpoints and when there is a need to find common ground’ in software engineering. The use of such an approach, or one that shares the same philosophical underpinnings, would not seem to be a particularly radical approach to addressing the problem of object identification and selection. However, the development of a practical way of moving from an SSM-type study to the application of some OO method is likely to cause some difficulties given the previous attempts to embed or graft SSM within/onto existing computer system analysis and design methods. [For a useful summary and discussion of work in this area see Mingers (1995).]

## PROBLEMS IN OBJECT IDENTIFICATION AND SELECTION

Previous work by Liang et al. 1993 can be used to illustrate the difficulty of moving from knowledge of a problem domain to an object model. The gap between what is known or understood about a situation and the representation of this situation using an object model is dependent upon the way in which user and analyst view the problem situation, as well as upon the structure and process placed upon the identification and selection of objects. Current OOA methods are deficient in that, although they recognize the need to move from understanding to representation they do not offer tools and guidance about how this movement may be achieved. Two areas that seem to be relevant to this movement from understanding to representation, namely (1) understanding the problem situation and (2) user involvement are discussed below.

## Understanding the problem situation

Eckert and Golder (1994) and Prins (1996) emphasize that identifying the elements of a problem and understanding and explaining how they interact with one another is a fundamental aim of OOA. Therefore, they argue, it is important that analysts applying object-oriented methods gain the much-needed understanding of the problem domain before they can begin to describe a particular situation using their methods. It is the depth and quality of this understanding that underpins, first, the identification of candidate objects from the situation and, then, from these candidate objects, the selection of appropriate and meaningful objects. As the examples in Table 1 show, current OOA methods offer a brief description of the sort of activity that should be carried out but they do not offer practical guidance as to how these things can be achieved (i.e. in the form of methods or tools). Consequently, OO analysts are left with little, if no, practical support to undertake this process of gaining a rich understanding of the problem situation.

From the Table 1, we can see that with OMT (Rumbaugh et al., 1991 and the approach of WirfsBrock et al., 1990) the focus is upon the vocabulary of the problem statement. A problem here is that the use and interpretation of a word in a problem situation may not be the same to all those involved: those involved will understand and interpret the same situation in different ways, according to their own experiences. They will, of course, express their understanding using their own terminology. Rubin and Goldberg (1992) argue that ‘although this basic approach may work for small systems, it is our experience that it simply will not scale up’. The problem here is that there may be hundreds and thousands of candidate objects if there is a large number of nouns and noun phrases in a statement of a complex problem. Prins (1996) offers a further criticism of this strategy based upon the fact that those advocating the approach ‘take as given that a written document is available. This is often not the case . . . Often many objects are identified this way that do not play any role in the system that must be produced.’

Alternatively, OOA (Coad & Yourdon, 1991), adopts a strategy that seeks ‘structures’, ‘things or events remembered’, etc. in a problem situation. However, their method does not explain how an understanding of these things is achieved and validated. Indeed, Losavio et al. (1994) have criticized OOA on the basis that it does not offer any ‘precise’ rule that could be applied in this sense. Judging from an analysis of the guidance given, it would seem that in practice experienced analysts are expected to be able to identify and select appropriate objects but at present practical methodological tools to help us achieve this are lacking.

## Greater user involvement

We can argue that OO methods are usually more concerned with the object model (e.g. object structure and inheritance) than the user's view of the problem situation. Different methods emphasize different aspects of the problem situation because of the concerns and interests represented in the corresponding object models. Consequently, the predominant view of the situation is that supported by the method and its use by the analyst (e.g. a data or process driven model) (Jacobson et al., 1995), as opposed to that view held by the user. For example, the application of different object-oriented methods (Liang et al., 1994) has shown that different methods may facilitate the abstraction of different objects within the same problem situation. Data-driven methods [e.g. OOA (Coad & Yourdon, 1991) and the object modelling technique (OMT) (Rumbaugh et al., 1991)] might identify and select objects by concentrating on the data qualifying a 'thing' or concept in the situation. Alternatively, process-driven methods such as that of WirfsBrock et al. (1990) may identify and select objects by focussing on the processes in the problem situation. However, the objects identified by these data or process driven methods might not be sensible and appropriate to the user. It is arguable that such methods have only applied the object model and have not taken the user's perspective into account. The lack of user involvement, of course, will influence the analytical process in which analysts consider what should be regarded as significant objects and so be included in the object model. It would seem sensible therefore for the user to play a central role in this process of object identification and selection so as to ensure the adoption of appropriate and meaningful objects.

## AN APPROACH TO IMPROVING OBJECT IDENTIFICATION AND SELECTION IN OOA

The problems encountered because of the lack of an approach to facilitate understanding of a problem situation has been recognized for some time. Research (e.g. Blokdijk & Blokdijk, 1987; Rubin & Goldberg, 1992) has been undertaken in order to overcome the problems of identifying and selecting objects. However, a criticism of this work is that the focus of attention has been upon modelling the tangible or 'hard' aspects (e.g. the given problem statement, predefined system boundary, etc.) of the problem situation. In this way the approaches address only those parts of an enterprise that have a tangible existence. Although such work is valuable, it does not allow us to address the more intangible aspects of a problem situation, aspects that often cause us most difficulty. Cook and Daniels (1994) have highlighted this problem and put forward the view that in current object-oriented methods there is no real emphasis on the understanding of problems in object modelling, in particular the more intangible or 'soft' aspects of the situation. They argue that this problem prevents object technology being used to its full potential and, in fact, damages the value of object technology. Consequently, in their own work they introduce an essential model that attempts to emphasize and describe a problem situation in the 'real world'. However, this model is still built by considering what exists in the problem situation.

A process of enquiry that attempts to bring about an appreciation of a problem situation is offered here as a useful approach to help the analyst and user to explore an area of concern. This inquiry method, which draws heavily upon the soft systems thinking work of Vickers (1965) and Checkland (1981), provides the tools and techniques to help the analyst to manage a structured investigation into a problem situation. The aim of the method is to enhance the appreciation and understanding in those involved in the investigation (i.e. analyst and user). As an understanding is developed of the processes involved in the situation the method then promotes the identification of the roles, responsibilities and ‘things’ associated with the defined processes. These roles, responsibilities and ‘things’ are then considered to be significant objects within the problem situation.

In advocating this approach, our objective is to include problem appreciation as a formal part of analysis in OOA. By using this method it is possible to enhance the process of object identification and selection by means of supporting users' involvement in the OOA process. In such a manner, the role of the analyst becomes one of facilitating users' specification of their own objects.

Bustard et al. (1996, p. 1) have also recognized the potential usefulness of ‘integrating business analysis with OOA to provide an effective overall analysis procedure’. Consequently, they advocate using soft systems methodology with Shaler and Mellor’s object-oriented analysis method. They use the notion of a conceptual model to identify business activities, to provide a basis for discussion with clients and to act as a framework for further analysis. This further analysis involves refining each activity (conceptual) model by determining ‘what precisely it is to achieve, including how it will interact with other elements of the system’ (Bustard et al., 1996, p. 55). ‘Interaction models’ are created out of this process but, unfortunately, we are not told how this process of detailed analysis is conducted. However, the authors admit that ‘In some cases, interactions will be between activities but often new descriptive elements have to be introduced to explain the source of inputs and destination of outputs.' This approach seems to call into question the rigour and 'validity' of the use of the activity model and the process by which they were developed. In our own approach we have avoided the need to introduce new elements by working only from the language of the activity model. The type of tables that are generated from the activity models in Bustard et al.'s work also seem to be influenced by the analyst/client's view of the data processing of the situation under exploration.

## 'Soft' system thinking and purposeful human activity

Approaches to inquiry that draw upon ‘soft’ systems thinking, such as SSM (Checkland, 1981), seek to bring about an appreciation of a ‘real-world’ problem situation before any attempt to alleviate the problematic situation. This contrasts considerable with approach to analysis and design that begin from a ‘given’ problem situation. The approach advocated here seeks to harness the appreciative inquiry process as a first step of OOA in an attempt to establish a deeper understanding of the problem situation before the development of object models. As a means of improving the process of object appreciation and consequent object identification we are currently investigating the potential benefits and practical implications of using a method of information systems requirements analysis that draws upon ‘soft’ system thinking. The method in question, the appreciative inquiry method (AIM) (Stowell et al., 1991; West, 1995), uses the same type of activity models as used in SSM as a way of exploring and describing a user’s view of some notional activity. Consequently, verbs are the modelling medium, and little attention is given to ‘objects’, other than the recognition that the verbs are associated with ‘things’, which may (or may not) be identified explicitly as nouns within the activity description. The activities described in these models are considered to be ‘purposeful’, that is they are recognized by someone to be fulfilling some desired purpose and therefore intention is signified. The notion of ‘purposeful activity’ contrasts with that of some ‘purposive’ element, a physical entity that plays a role of some purposeful activity, or ‘system’. Checkland (1981) gives an example of a hammer (which we might consider as a purposive element) as ‘a physical system designed with fitness for purpose in mind.... Designed physical systems exist because a need for them in some human activity systems has been identified’. Therefore, at a different level of analysis a purposive physical entity may be considered as necessarily being linked to some purposeful activity in its own right (the notion of system being dependent upon the transformation of input into output, and hence a ‘process’ or ‘activity’). If ‘purposive’ elements, which, it appears, can be equated with ‘objects’, can be described fully in terms of their systems components then we might have the basis for an approach to enquiry that facilitates object appreciation in terms of (1) the processes involved, (2) the identification of objects significant to these processes and (3) a way of inspecting the characteristics of the named objects that will fulfil the requirements specification of OOA. (Using Checkland’s description of the fundamental components of any purposeful activity we seek to identify: the transformation brought about by the activity, the actors and the victims or beneficiaries involved, the environmental constraints, the overall owners and the reason for the stated transformation being worthwhile.)

## An approach to improving the identification and selection of objects

In this section we describe a practical approach to the process of identifying candidate and significant objects that draws upon soft systems thinking. An example is given to illustrate the way in which meaningful objects can be developed out of activity models derived from an application of the appreciative inquiry method. The approach is described in terms of the different activities undertaken in order to develop an object model considered to be appropriate to the domain and that can be developed into a specification for an object-oriented software system. However, the description below concentrates upon the process of moving from the activity model to the object model. Some familiarity with soft systems thinking is assumed and reference to a detailed description of the use of AIM to undertake the process of domain appreciation is given. The term ‘client’ is now introduced to emphasize the change in role and status of what, in OOA terminology, was referred to as the ‘user’.

## Activity 1 — appreciating the problem situation

The context and content of the problem situation is understood and appreciated through the investigative and learning cycle facilitated by ‘soft’ system thinking (Stowell & West, 1994a). Analyst and clients need to understand the differences between the various views and interpretations of the problem situation; differences between different clients’ views are particularly important. This is achieved by focussing upon selected purposeful human activities that are regarded as being relevant to the problem situation. With the AIM this is achieved by asking the client to produce a systems map of the area of interest. An initial relevant topic is agreed between analyst and clients that becomes the central element of the map. The client is then asked to identify important factors relating to the central element and gradually a wide picture of the area is developed. The map can be used with an individual, with a group of clients or with many individual clients whose maps are eventually merged into a composite map as shown in Figure 2. (A more detailed discussion of this process is given in West, 1995.)

## Activity 2 — specifying purposeful activities

In activity 2 the different map elements are described in terms of the purposeful activity that they seem to encapsulate. To achieve this, the client is asked to describe the map elements by answering a series of set questions. These questions are derived from the CATWOE elements of SSM (Checkland, 1981), and they are used at this stage in order to help the client consider the activity, or transformation, that can be recognized from the map element. Once the transformation (T) of the map element has been identified other questions can be asked about the customers (C), actors (A), owners (O), environmental constraints (E) and about the 'world view' (W) that makes this purposeful activity meaningful. The analyst may then develop a succinct description of the purposeful activity relating to the map element in the form of a root definition, as in SSM. The relevant purposeful activities identified in activity 1 are then described in an activity model of the type found in SSM (i.e. conceptual model). (The processes involved in activity 2 are described in detail in West, 1995.)

## Activity 3 — Identifying and selecting the ‘actors’, ‘owners’ and ‘customers’ of each activity

Activities in the activity model above represent purposeful human actions in the problem situation. In the situation, individuals or groups (which we shall call ‘actors’) are responsible for performing the activities required by other individuals or groups (which we shall call ‘customers’), with an authority (which we shall call ‘owners’) allowing or affecting the performance. Therefore, actors, owners and customers are identified as basic elements of the problem situation and can be regarded as significant objects in the situation. For each activity, we can identify and select its actor, owner and customer by asking questions such as ‘who does this activity?’ or ‘who is responsible for this activity?’ (actor), ‘who allows the performance of this activity?’ or ‘who affects this activity?’ (owner), and ‘who requests/needs this activity?’ or ‘who is affected by this activity?’ (customer). In practice, it has proven useful to construct a table to record the answers to these questions (it also helps to ensure that no questions are omitted). At this stage we answer these questions by considering the role of the notional individual who is involved in each activity. For example, if we take the activity ‘interview patient’, then the actor can be identified as ‘patient interviewer’. Once we have translated the activities named in this way, we can then return to the ‘real world’ and ask, ‘Who, in the problem situation, plays the role of “patient interviewe”?’ This two-stage process is important in order to ensure that we are concentrating purely upon the processes in the activity model and are not trying to identify roles directly from the ‘real world’ situation. This is time consuming but it does enforce a useful rigour upon the process and offers a means of validating the identification of objects (i.e. the view of the problem situation represented through the activity model is translated into a list of objects associated with the activity model, and only with this model). The terms ‘actors’, ‘owners’ and ‘customers’ should be considered in terms of their meaning in the English language rather than as terms taken from either SSM or object-oriented analysis. In this way by ‘actors’ we mean the notional individual(s) who carries out an activity; by ‘owner’ we mean the notional individual(s) who affects, allows and controls an activity; by ‘customer’ we mean the notional recipient of the output of an activity.

## Activity 4 — Specifying significant objects

1 Specifying objects. The actors, owners and customers identified in Table 2 can now be specified as significant objects in an object model of the problem situation. According to the notion of clientserver contract described by WirfsBrock et al. (1990), in an object model our concept of 'actor' could be regarded as a 'server' object; our 'customer' could be regarded as a 'client' object; and our concept of 'owner', that offers input(s) or precondition(s) to the activities of the actor could be regarded as a 'support' object. These objects are then specified in terms of any object-oriented method. [We use OMT (Rumbaugh et al., 1991) as an example, in this paper.] All activities of an actor are defined as its operations in an object model. The data that an activity acts on is specified as attribute(s) of the object that performs that activity.

2 Specifying object structures. Object structures can then be defined based upon the relationships [e.g. associations and inheritance and aggregation defined by Rumbaugh et al. (1991)] among the actors, owners and customers according to their responsibilities in a problem situation. An association structure is defined if an actor/owner/customer inherently depends on another actor/owner/customer. An inheritance structure is defined by asking the questions such as, 'Are there actors/owners/customers that mean the same kind of things in the real world?', and 'Can they be specialized or generalized?' An aggregation structure is specified by asking the questions such as, 'Does any actor/owner/customer consist of other actor/owner/customer?'

3 Specifying the behaviour for an actor object. The behaviour of an actor object is defined by its operations and the links between the operations. A link between two operations A1 and A2 within the object is defined if the result of activity A1 is an input of activity A2 in the conceptual model. Links are identified by asking questions such as, 'Does an activity depend on any other in the system?', and 'If so, how does it depend on that activity?' The behaviour of the object is described by a statechart used by Rumbaugh et al. (1991). The operation of the object is specified as a state of the object. The input of the operation is specified as a condition for entering the state. The condition is attached on the link.

The process of identifying and selecting objects in a problem situation supported by this approach is illustrated in Figure 3.

## AN EXAMPLE

The example shown here relates to a study in which the analyst and clients were seeking to come to a better understanding of the way in which decisions are made when considering the discharge of patients detained under the Mental Health Act (see Stowell et al., 1995, for the full study details). The following activity model (see Figure 4) is taken from this study as the result of the first two activities of the process illustrated in Figure 3. This activity model acts as the basis for identifying and selecting significant objects from the problem situation.

In the third activity of the analysis process of Figure 3, a series of questions are asked about those activities in the activity model, such as ‘who interviews?’, ‘who requests the interview?’ and ‘who is interviewed?’ These questions and their responses are recorded in Table 2, with each row representing one of the model’s activities.

Table 2 is constructed purely from the logic of the activity model without any reference to the 'real world'. The production of this Table 2, which acts as a kind of mediating representation, is time consuming. However, this process is necessary to ensure the rigorous interrogation of the activities that supply the information to develop an object model of these activities. Once Table 2 has been completed it is taken back to the client and is used as an agenda for discussion with the client about the problem situation. Each activity is considered by the client and analyst and questions asked to elicit the 'real world' identity of the roles and contents of the table. For example, in relation to activity 1 ('interview patient and relatives') in Table 2, the questions asked for each of the three headings are:

Actor: 'Who is the patient and relative interviewer?'

Owner: 'Who allows or provides the resources for the interview to take place?'

Customer: 'Who needs to know the result of the interview?' and 'who is affected by the interview?'

Those individuals or groups who fill the ‘real world’ roles and activities of actor, owner and customer are then recorded as shown in Table 3. To show an activity and its actor clearly, the actor is followed by the order number of each activity, e.g. ‘(A1)’. For example, ‘(A2)’ follows its actor ‘appreciator of views and opinions’ in the second row of the Table 3. In the final activity of the process illustrated in Figure 3, all actors, owners and customers in Table 3 are regarded as significant objects in the problem situation and specified by an object model created by Rumbaugh et al. (1991). Objects, their attributes and operations (also followed by the order number of the corresponding activity in the conceptual model), and their relationships are all specified in the model, as shown in Figure 5. ## CONCLUSIONS

In this paper, we outline an approach that we believe may help to strengthen the process of object identification and selection, by considering the integration of object-oriented methods with a method of facilitating a rich ‘appreciation’ of a problem situation and adding a new activity to the existing OOA process, as shown by Figure 3. The method used to bring about the appreciation of the problem situation, AIM, provides a way of modelling purposeful activities in a problem situation into an activity model. These activities represent the purposeful human actions in the situation that associate with actors, owners and customers. The actors, owners and customers are regarded as the basic elements of the situation as they perform, allow or request the activities. We argue that they can be used as a base for identifying and selecting significant objects in OOA. Analysts are able to appreciate and understand such basic elements of a problem situation through the process of enquiry and then to specify them as significant objects into the object model by the process illustrated in this paper. The activities performed by an actor are specified as the operations of the object corresponding to the actor. The relationships between actors, owners and customers are specified as object structures including inheritance and aggregation. The activity model produced from using SSM may not provide much details of the data acted by purposeful activities, and consequently the identification of attributes for objects may be difficult in terms of this approach. One interesting issue that has arisen through the linking of the two methods is the benefit of clients being encouraged to identify and specify their own significant objects in the development of object models. The approach and example presented in this paper show a way of integrating object-oriented methods with the information requirements analysis process (Stowell, 1991; Stowell & West, 1994a, 1994b).

## Biographies

Ying Liang is a Lecturer in the Department of Computing and Information Systems at the University of Paisley. Her PhD thesis focused on object-oriented analysis methods and techniques. Her current research interests include object-oriented systems analysis, modelling and design, objects reusability and information systems requirements analysis.

Daune West is a Senior lecturer in the Department of Computing and Information Systems at the University of Paisley. Her PhD, from Portsmouth Polytechnic, was in Systems and Information Systems Development. Her research interests include the use of systems concepts and tools in the development of information systems, particularly during the process of information systems requirements analysis.

Frank Stowell is Professor and Head of the Department of Computer and Information Sciences at De Montfort University, Milton Keynes. His research interests are related to the organisational impact of IT and hence participative approaches to information systems development.
