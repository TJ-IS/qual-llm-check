# Contextualized access to knowledge: theoretical perspectives and a process-tracing study

Ji-Ye Mao & Izak Benbasat\*

Department of Management Sciences, Faculty of Engineering, University of Waterloo, Waterloo, Ont., Canada, N2L 3G1, email: jmao@mansci2.uwaterloo.ca, and \*Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC, Canada, V6T 1Z2, email: izak@unixg.ubc.ca

Abstract. This paper investigates the theoretical foundations and empirical evidence concerning contextualized access to task domain knowledge enabled by hypertext-style links. It examines several relevant theoretical perspectives, including theories of discourse comprehension, contextualized learning and the production paradox, and reports on an exploratory study in the knowledge-based systems (KBS) domain. Process-tracing data was collected using a 'thinking-aloud' procedure, and data analysis focused on some highly illustrative verbal protocols. Results indicate that contextualized access to domain knowledge can be critical for understanding KBS output, and that lack of it can cause comprehension difficulties. Contextualized access is highly effective for resolving comprehension difficulties arising from the users' lack of task domain knowledge and for reducing the motivational 'cost' of learning. We conclude that it has the potential for substantially increasing the effectiveness of information systems.

Keywords: Contextualized access, explanation, hypertext, knowledge-based systems

## INTRODUCTION

Over the past decade, hypertext and hypermedia have become a popular choice for electronic document management, most noticeably as the standard user interface on the Worldwide Web and for providing on-line help in windows-based applications (e.g. via WinHelp). A recent survey found that hypertext-based help and reference are nearly universal in on-line systems to support end-user learning and training (Kemske, 1996). Most of these systems are built to capitalize on the capacity of hypertext to allow access to knowledge in ways that cater to the different interests, needs and comprehension abilities of the users (Marshall & Shipman, 1995). However, as information and knowledge support, most of the hypertext-based applications are not integrated into other types of information systems (e.g. decision support systems and knowledge-based systems) to allow access in the context of the users' primary job tasks, such as problem-solving. Consequently, the users have to leave the context in which they are performing their primary tasks to access domain information and knowledge in a separate context or a different system.

There is more to the idea of using hypertext to link information items than just for easy access. Research has explored the integration of hypertext into other types of information systems, such as decision support systems (e.g. Bieber & Kimbrough, 1992; Minch, 1990), knowledge-based systems (Rada & Barlow, 1988; Moore & Swartout, 1990; Abu-Hakima & Oppacher, 1990; Kane, 1990; Mao et al., 1996) and computer-aided software engineering (CASE) environments (Fischer et al., 1990; Conklin & Yakemovic, 1991). In these systems, if the users are uncertain about particular aspects of the system output or are interested in more background information, hypertext allows users to access the most relevant knowledge in the context of the primary task.

For example, Desmarais et al. (1997) report on the use of hypertext embedded in a graphical user interface to a large utility company's customer database. Numbers on the screen (representing billing information sent to customers) have hypertext links to details of how the amounts are computed recursively. Thus, customer service representatives can click on the numbers to explain electricity bills to customers, which is a difficult task for certain payment plans and requires a good understanding of the task domain. Through the hypertext links, necessary domain knowledge is conveniently made available to support the primary task of the users.

In the above example, domain knowledge is integrated into the output of an information system in a natural and seamless way, so that access to the knowledge is instant and within the same context of problem-solving, as opposed to via searching in a different context. This is referred to as contextualized access to knowledge in this research. It appears that the potential of hypertext to enable contextualized access to domain knowledge in information systems is an interesting and challenging area. Although significant benefits can be expected from this style of access, there exists little research and empirical evidence about its cognitive basis.

This paper investigates contextualized access to knowledge in two parts. First, contextualized access is defined and discussed in conjunction with the use of hypertext to represent and access knowledge; its potential benefits are analysed from several relevant theoretical perspectives. Second, we report the results of an exploratory study in the domain of knowledge-based systems (KBS). This study addresses three specific research questions about contextualized access to explanatory knowledge. (a) To what extent does it facilitate and encourage access to relevant domain knowledge? (b) In what way is it useful and important for the users? (c) Is it equally important for novices and experienced professionals?

In this research, hypertext is positioned as an enabling technology for contextualized access to explanatory knowledge in KBS to facilitate users' understanding of KBS output, i.e. advice and recommendations. Two informationally equivalent KBS for financial analysis were compared in a laboratory experiment: one with contextualized access to explanations via hypertext-style links; the other without such access. Our process-tracing study involved two types of data, namely computer logs and verbal protocols (Todd & Benbasat, 1987; Ericsson & Simon, 1993). Computer logs captured the process of user–KBS interaction, including the number, type and context of explanation use. Verbal protocol data was collected to understand why contextualized access to explanatory knowledge was useful and important, and how such access occurred. The qualitative analysis of verbal protocols was supplemented with descriptive statistics.

In the next section, we introduce and discuss the notion of contextualized access to knowledge. We then analyse the benefits of contextualized access in the third section from several theoretical perspectives. In the fourth section, we describe the research method used for this study, details of the experimental systems and procedures for collecting process-tracing data. We then present the results of data analysis in the fifth section and the implications of the findings in the sixth section.

## CONTEXTUALIZED ACCESS TO KNOWLEDGE ENABLED BY HYPERTEXT

In this section, we define the concept of contextualized access first, and then illustrate the concept with an example of contextualized access to explanatory knowledge in KBS.

## Contextualized access to knowledge via hypertext

The popularity of hypertext-based knowledge representation and access can be justified from two related perspectives: (a) hypertext as a knowledge representation scheme, and (b) as a user interface modality that allows contextualized access to information (Conklin, 1987). As a knowledge representation scheme, hypertext is appropriate for knowledge storage and retrieval in information systems by virtue of its linking capabilities. It has been claimed that hypertext is ideally suited for learning or knowledge acquisition in complex domains (Spiro et al., 1988). Knowledge represented with hypertext preserves the rich associations among domain concepts, and corresponds to an expert's knowledge schemata (Jonassen, 1990). Therefore, domain concepts can be accessed in the context of other related ones to facilitate learning and comprehension.

As a user interface modality, hypertext can make domain knowledge more accessible to users from multiple perspectives, allowing explorations of varying levels of difficulty, detail and granularity. Furthermore, Stanton & Stammers (1990) assert that hypertext allows for different levels of prior knowledge among the user population and encourages exploration. Hypertext also facilitates the two-way communication between the system and users by allowing users to ask follow-up questions about problem-solving knowledge and to avoid irrelevant information.

Perhaps more importantly, hypertext enables contextualized access to task domain knowledge from various types of information systems. Such a style of access appears to have three distinct features: (a) there is no need for the user to leave the primary task context and break the continuity of the task; (b) relevant domain knowledge is accessible instantly with a single click of the mouse; and (c) there is no need for the user to search for the relevant knowledge because references to relevant knowledge are always identified with link markers such as buttons, coloured or underlined text. Although hypertext has been adopted as the standard way of providing on-line help in windows-based systems, in most cases access to on-line information is not contextualized according to the above definition. The reason is that there exist two separate contexts: the primary task context and the help context, where useful information has to be searched for based on keywords or from indices. In addition, the user has the burden of identifying useful information from potentially large amounts of irrelevant information.

Hypertext is a natural choice for implementing contextualized access because it can enhance both knowledge representation (organization) and access (interactivity). The two aspects are distinct but closely related to each other. Both of them are instrumental for contextualized access to task domain knowledge: the former permits task domain knowledge to be accessed in the context of related knowledge, whereas the latter enables access in the specific context of problem-solving. However, in general, hypertext-based access is not necessarily contextualized nor does contextualized access mean merely easy access. The notion of contextualized access will be illustrated next with the KBS used in this research as an example.

## An example of contextualized access to explanatory knowledge

Explanation plays a crucial role in the interaction between users and complex information systems (Johnson & Johnson, 1993). Explanations provided by a KBS are descriptions of what the KBS does, how it works and why its actions are appropriate (Swartout, 1987). Since MYCIN (Shortliffe, 1976), explanations have become a fundamental feature of KBS. They are essential for user acceptance, because explanations make the performance of KBS natural and transparent (Davis, Buchanan, & Shortliffe, 1977; Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994).

However, it is a challenge to provide useful explanations (see Gilbert, 1989; Southwick, 1991; Wick & Thompson, 1992). One of the major problems with most KBS explanations is the lack of knowledge. Often, the knowledge most needed for explanation is either not stored or inaccessible. For example, in rule-based KBS, which are the most widely applied and best understood, problem-solving knowledge is represented as a set of rules in a compiled form with all of the underlying justification removed.

In our study, we used an experimental KBS for financial analysis. A large part of financial analysis is based on the calculation and interpretation of key financial ratios. Before any measure or ratio is used, care must be taken that the method of its computation is thoroughly understood by the users (Bernstein, 1993). Hypertext was used to represent explanatory knowledge of the task domain, i.e. explanations on each financial ratio and the procedure for calculating it, and to link related explanations to each other. Furthermore, hypertext was used to enable the access to explanatory knowledge from the KBS output.

Figure 1 illustrates a recommendation screen in this KBS, and Fig. 2 represents a corresponding explanation on how the recommendation was reached. Concepts such as working capital, current ratio and acid-test ratio in Figs 1 and 2 are attached with circles (hypertext-style link buttons). If the user selects current ratio in Fig. 2 first and then HOW, the user will get Fig. 3, which explains the detailed calculation of this ratio, with hypertext-style links to other related concepts. In Fig. 3, domain concepts such as days sales in receivables and days to sell inventory can also be selected for further explanation.

The user has no need to go elsewhere for the task domain knowledge, and the availability of relevant domain knowledge is highlighted by the hypertext-style link buttons.

## THEORETICAL PERSPECTIVES

Theories of discourse comprehension, contextualized learning and the production paradox are discussed here as the cognitive basis of contextualized access to task domain knowledge via hypertext. They are also used to help interpret the results of data analysis.

## Cognitive processes of discourse comprehension

Discourse comprehension is the process of constructing a coherent mental representation of the information in a text (e.g. Dijk & Kintsch, 1983; Kintsch, 1988). The representation contains more than the information explicitly expressed in the text. Inferences are made as the result of cognitive processes that take a set of information, integrate the elements and produce some new information not given explicitly.

![](/api/attachments/WDMSK3EF/fulltext/images/8721e71d8f9896785305da0e7043cec79fd886c3801de43f3683376eb86c86a1.jpg)  
Figure 2. Contextualized access from explanations (reasoning trace).

The cognitive process model of discourse comprehension involves three intervening information-processing variables (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983). The first two are bridging inferences and memory reinstatements, which are useful for predicting knowledge retention and measuring the readability of text. The readability of a text is determined by the number of memory reinstatements that occur in processing the text and the number of bridging inferences required to construct a coherent meaning from the text. According to Kintsch and van Dijk (1978), the need for reinstating a text proposition from long-term memory occurs when a textual input cannot be related to the propositions currently held in short-term memory. In such cases, long-term memory is searched for possible antecedents to the current textual input. If the search is successful, the search result is reinstated in short-term memory, thus providing a coherent link between what was read before and the new input; otherwise, a bridging inference is assumed to occur to infer the missing link. Both the searches and inferences are resource-consuming operations and therefore sources of comprehension difficulty.

The third intervening variable is the macrostructure of text that reflects the meaning of elements that can be extracted from the surface level of the text. Macrostructure is the theoretical account of what is usually called the theme of a text, as opposed to ‘microstructure’ and ‘micropropositions’ representing the base meaning units. Macrostructure is of central importance in the processing of complex information. Without macrostructure, complex cognitive tasks, such as learning, recall, action and planning cannot be performed.

![](/api/attachments/WDMSK3EF/fulltext/images/c07395ec1e94b4383f43f2e107898394cab9b6ca65a738a376517ac95da91019.jpg)

Domain knowledge and experience also play an important role in discourse comprehension. Experts in the task domain are expected to process new information differently from the novices because prior knowledge constrains the construction of discourse comprehension and provides part of the context within which a discourse is interpreted (Kintsch, 1988).

## A discourse comprehension perspective to contextualized access

Cognitive theories of discourse comprehension are useful for conceptualizing the potential effects of contextualized access to domain knowledge. Typically, a text leaves some crucial causal relationships implicit, and readers have to supply this missing link from their own knowledge. For example, one would search his or her long-term memory for the meaning of ‘funds flow adequacy’ when presented with the following conclusion by a KBS:

The funds flow adequacy of the company is low.... There is a need to secure additional financing for operations.

If the concept is unfamiliar or not understood (i.e. not in long-term memory), memory reinstatements are not possible and bridging inferences may not be valid. Without knowing the meaning of this key concept, one would not be able to fully understand the implication of the conclusion and may even misconstrue the actual causal relationship.

Contextualized access via hypertext is advantageous for discourse comprehension: comprehension of text containing unfamiliar concepts should be deeper and faster with instant access to the domain knowledge. Memory reinstatements may be easier to make, bridging inferences more valid and construction of the macrostructure of the problem under consideration more efficient, given the instant access to external source of information.

Moreover, many natural features of contextualized access implemented with hypertext-style links are consistent with some of Mayer's (1985) recommendations for improving the understandability of text, such as underlining technical terms and providing a glossary of technical terms, so that unfamiliar concepts will not deter comprehension. With contextualized access schemes, important concepts and links to explanatory knowledge are highlighted. Another recommendation by Mayer is to include repetition of important ideas and to build redundancy into the passage to expose the reader to the main points in several places. Contextualized access schemes offer multiple opportunities (and paths) to retrieve the relevant knowledge at different places of the text.

## Contextualized learning and alleviation of the production paradox

The second relevant theoretical perspective relates to contextualized learning (Fischer et al., 1990), which emphasizes integrating learning into situations in which it is useful for solving real-world problems. Contextualized learning is effective when learning does not take place in a separate phase and place. A system supporting contextualized learning must help the problem-solvers to see where their knowledge is inadequate (to perceive breakdowns), to find the problem-solving knowledge they need for such situations, and to understand how generalized principles relate to particular situations.

To a certain degree, these requirements for contextualized learning can be naturally met with the use of hypertext-style links. If the relevant domain knowledge is made accessible via the links, the need for the knowledge element and the lack of it are highlighted. Also, if a user's understanding or reasoning process ‘breaks down,’ contextualized access may be convenient for repairing the process by supplying general principles as well as more specific task knowledge applicable to the situation in a timely fashion. In addition, contextualized access via hypertext also accommodates individual differences in prior knowledge and learning styles.

The third theoretical perspective originates in the notion of the production paradox (Carroll & McKendree, 1987; Carroll & Rosson, 1987). It refers to the conflicts between learning and working constantly present in work settings: learning is inhibited by lack of time and working is inhibited by lack of knowledge. Consequently, productivity suffers. However, the motivational ‘cost’ of learning may be reduced through the design of better learning support facilities and convenient and easy to use interfaces (Carroll & Rosson, 1987).

Contextualized access to task domain knowledge makes the exploration of knowledge natural and convenient. This reduces the motivational cost of learning, thus alleviating the production paradox. In summary, all of the three theoretical perspectives imply that contextualized access offers significant benefits by encouraging and facilitating access to domain knowledge in the process of problem-solving.

## A PROCESS-TRACING STUDY

We investigated the effects of contextualized access to knowledge in the domain of KBS by conducting a process-tracing study based on computer logs and verbal protocol data (Todd & Benbasat, 1987; Ericsson & Simon, 1993). It was part of a larger research project investigating contextualized access to explanatory knowledge in KBS (Mao & Benbasat, 1997). This section presents the background of the study along with details of the research method.

## Background

Several empirical studies of explanation use have been conducted in recent years (e.g. Lamberti & Wallace, 1990; Dhaliwal, 1993; Ye & Johnson, 1995; Gregor, 1996). Most of these studies emphasized that KBS and their explanations could be used as a tool for knowledge transfer and therefore affect the learning of users. However, a general methodological limitation of previous work was that there was no attempt to investigate the process and nature of explanation use. To overcome this limitation, the process of explanation use must be captured using process-tracing methods, as we did in this study with computer logs and the ‘thinking aloud’ procedure.

We conducted a laboratory experiment, which involved a financial analysis task for loan evaluation. Subjects were provided with 5 years of financial statements and financial ratios of a hypothetical firm. They were asked to use a KBS to assess various aspects of the firm's financial health. Then, based on the assessment, they made a recommendation regarding whether or not a commercial loan should be approved and, if yes, the amount.

## Experimental KBS

A simulated KBS was developed for the experiment. It provides two types of explanations: the first type is deep explanations, which present the underlying generic domain knowledge such as the concepts and procedures used by the KBS. The second type is reasoning-trace explanations, which relate to specific KBS recommendations, revealing the problem-solving process of the KBS. Both deep and reasoning-trace explanations are further decomposed into three more specific types, in terms of why, how and strategic. Deep explanations for each concept or procedure are provided in terms of its definition (How, e.g. Fig. 3), justification (Why) and relationships with others (Strategic). Reasoning-trace explanations for each KBS recommendation are provided in terms of its reasoning trace (How, e.g. Fig. 2), justification (Why) and control knowledge (Strategic).

The KBS displays three types of screens in the following sequence: (a) an information screen containing an index of relevant domain concepts and procedures, where the user can request relevant deep explanations from the index; (b) a data screen of relevant financial ratios calculated from the financial statements of the hypothetical firm to be evaluated; and (c) recommendation screens (e.g. Fig. 1), where the user can view KBS recommendations and request reasoning-trace explanations on the recommendations.

The KBS has two informationally equivalent versions: one with contextualized access to deep explanations via hypertext-style links, the other without such access. The contextualized access version has been illustrated previously in Figs 1–3 and 4a. The non-contextualized access version illustrated in Fig. 4(b) is described as follows: it has the identical explanations as the contextualized access version, except that there are no hypertext-style links (link buttons in Figs 1–3 do not exist). Deep explanations are available only through the information screen provided by default before the analysis (i.e. the provision of KBS recommendations and reasoning-trace explanations). When examining deep explanations, users can only access domain concepts one at a time, and only from the information screen. There is no mechanism in the KBS to highlight the interdependence among domain concepts and to allow users to follow the semantic links among domain concepts. Nor can users directly access deep explanations from recommendations (Fig. 1) or reasoning-trace explanations (Fig. 2); if users want to access deep explanations, they have to backtrack to the information screen to do so (by using the PREVIOUS SCREEN button).

## Subjects

There were two groups of subjects, novices and experienced professionals. Novice subjects included 28 undergraduate and MBA students in a business school; they either were specializing in accounting or had taken accounting courses extensively. They all had taken at least one financial analysis course but lacked work experiences. Twenty-five experienced professionals whose work involved financial analysis also participated. They possessed some related professional qualifications beyond the undergraduate degree, such as Chartered General Accountant (CGA) and Certified Financial Analyst (CFA) designations. They had on average 9.6 years of financial analysis-related work experience. Although we did not collect information on the ages of the participants, the professionals were on average at least 10 years older than the novices, as most of the novices had no full-time work experience.

Participants were reasonably familiar with windows-based applications, and their self-reported familiarity scores were 4.5 and 4.0 (on a scale of 1 to 7 ranging from ‘not familiar’ to ‘very familiar’) for novices and experienced professionals respectively. However, they had little prior experience with KBS for financial analysis; the self-reported familiarity scores were only 2.6 and 1.6 for novices and experienced professionals.

Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the two experimental conditions. Thus, we had a $2 \times 2$ factorial experimental design with two levels of access to explanatory knowledge and two levels of task domain knowledge.

## Verbal protocols as data

Verbal protocols can be collected ‘to gain information about the course and mechanisms of cognitive processes’ of the subjects’ internal states (Ericsson & Simon, p. 215, 1980). Ericsson and Simon suggest that the contents of thinking aloud and immediate retrospective reports (vis-à-vis those collected at the end of experiment) are valid, and that the validity and completeness of verbal reports can be enhanced by adopting appropriate methods.

Our analysis of verbal protocols focused on the process of user–KBS interaction, rather than the complete cognitive process of decision-making. Of particular interest was the role of contextualized access to explanatory knowledge in helping users understand KBS output, which accounted for only a small part of the whole process of KBS use.

Our objective was to explore the qualitative and quantitative impact of contextualized access through the use of hypertext on explanation use, based on verbal protocol data. We hoped that detailed verbal protocol data would help us understand, not only the outcome but also why and how contextualized access to knowledge is useful and effective.

## Data collection procedures

Given the high density of verbal data and the effort required for analysing it, only 20 subjects (five in each of the four groups) out of the total sample of 53 were randomly selected to ‘think aloud’ while working on the experimental task. Subjects first worked on a problem with the support of a tutorial KBS similar in form but simpler than the experimental KBS to be used. Those who were to provide verbal reports were also given two simple training tasks to practise thinking aloud, as suggested by Ericsson and Simon (1993). The ‘think aloud’ subjects were asked to verbalize while working with the tutorial KBS.

Subjects were instructed to ‘say out loud everything that passes through your mind for each step as you interact with the expert system.’ There was also a supplementary instruction, ‘it does not matter if your sentences are not complete, since you are not explaining to anyone else. Just act as if you are alone in the room speaking to yourself loudly.’ This instruction was to encourage the subjects to provide more thorough protocols, and to discourage them from explaining their actions (Ericsson & Simon, 1993), which might interfere with the natural cognitive process. In addition, subjects were told that if they were silent for more than 10 s, they would be reminded, so that they had a clear idea of what was expected. These instructions were tested on two pilot subjects.

After working with the tutorial KBS, subjects were then asked to work on the actual experimental task of financial analysis with the support of the experimental KBS. Computer logs captured every step of subjects' interaction with the KBS, including the use of every explanation. A tape recorder registered all that was said during the period when subjects were interacting with the KBS.

Each tape was first transcribed to record the information on the tape. Then, in the second stage, the verbal reports were matched against the printed computer logs and KBS screens. Thus, what was read from KBS output was differentiated from the thoughts of subjects, and each segment of the verbal data was labelled corresponding to the KBS screens. The taped session typically lasted between 25 and 40 minutes. It took about 8 hours for the first author to transcribe and highlight the verbal protocol of each subject. Data on the frequency and timing of explanation use was extracted from the log files by a short C program written for this purpose.

## DATA ANALYSIS

This section presents results of a qualitative analysis of verbal protocols that are highly illustrative of the effectiveness of contextualized access, and the difficulties caused by the lack of such access. It also reports descriptive statistics to show the behavioural differences between the two experimental conditions.

## Identification of pertinent verbal reports

Ericsson (1988) provides theoretical arguments and empirical evidence that think-aloud instructions to subjects who read easy texts tend to yield little verbalization beyond the reading of the text, whereas more challenging and complex texts produce much more informative think-aloud reports. However, to a large extent, information in these reports pertains not to comprehension per se but, instead, to efforts to overcome failures and difficulties of comprehension. This conclusion is important for this research because resolving difficulties of comprehending KBS output was expected to be the primary cause of explanation use. It also implies that verbal protocols associated with difficult KBS recommendations might be more informative than others.

Bereiter and Bird (1985) identified four main strategies that subjects relied on when they found text difficult to comprehend: (a) they would rephrase the text in simpler terms; (b) failure to comprehend the last segment could lead to backtracking, i.e. causing subjects to re-read the preceding text; (c) subjects would occasionally identify missing pieces of information by verbalizing questions (e.g. a text such as, 'No one knows exactly when ... but ...' may lead to the question, 'why did ... ?'); and (d) subjects would occasionally identify comprehension problems and engage in active problem-solving.

Although the majority of the recommendations provided by the KBS were relatively easy to understand and less open to dispute, a couple of them were particularly difficult. This phenomenon was initially noted by the first author during the experiment when he was alerted by some abnormal behaviour in response to a particularly difficult recommendation. The corresponding verbal reports caught his attention again during the transcription and coding process; some of the strategies to resolve comprehension difficulties, identified by Bereiter and Bird (1985) as described above, were evident. Many comprehension difficulties were related to one particular KBS recommendation, which was based on a key financial ratio unfamiliar to most subjects.

Consequently, this special instance became the focus of this paper, although there were other interesting verbal reports. Data on the overall access to KBS explanations were used as the basis for comparison. In doing so, we could make use of rich qualitative data to show details of the most interesting results. Our process-tracing data related to the special instance were particularly informative regarding the effects of contextualized access and users' expertise on explanation use.

followed by a long pause. He then resorted to reasoning-trace explanations, which were directly accessible from the recommendation screen (see Fig. 1 for an example), for more clues. He read the Why reasoning-trace explanation:

'…problem in its business cash cycle. So, maybe they are saying what, because of big inventories, they need to be streamlining. Maybe they have big inventories, 'cause they still made mistakes in the past.'

It appears that, because of the lack of knowledge of the key financial ratio, this subject could not understand the meaning and implication of the recommendation. He attempted to generate his own inference, by guessing, in order to make sense of the situation. Apparently, he was not satisfied with the information he had from the Why reasoning-trace explanation, and went on to try the How explanation on the recommendation:

'is based on the evaluation, . . . total cash flow from operation is insufficient to cover the total of capital investments. That's true.' He then read the recommendation again, 'there is a need to secure additional, need to secure financing for operations. Yes, I guess, through the inventories. It's not generating sufficient cash, or cash is very low, inventory is very high, too high.

Similarly, an experienced professional read the recommendation, ‘the funds flow adequacy is . . . low . . . How did you figure out that? Mmmm.’ He requested both Why and How reasoning-trace explanations on it, had a long pause, then flipped through the hard copy of financial statements (both were common indicators of comprehension difficulty):

'Net income, cash flow from operating activities, where, where change in cash. Oh, they were positive all the time. I don't know why this . . . low, sufficient cash from operations, the cash position has gone up each year. I don't know how, how, how, in 1988 to 92, value is 0.56, funds adequacy ratio. Five years. OK, disagree.'

Obviously, the terminology was causing comprehension difficulties, which led him to search through information that was conveniently available to him. However, because of the lack of contextualized access to the most relevant knowledge, he finally made a judgement without all of the information he needed.

Upon reading the recommendation, two other novices had comprehension difficulties, and both requested reasoning-trace explanations, which was the only additional information available in the context. One of them uttered, 'Ah, I don't think so. Why do you say that? So, they say, why they say what it is, it seems a kind of funny.' He then requested the Why reasoning-trace explanation on the recommendation, and checked the hard copy of financial statements. The other one read:

'the funds flow adequacy of the company is low, OK, this is saying that they are not generating enough cash from operations, which is wrong! They've got sufficient cash flows. Let's take a look at Why (reasoning-explanation on the recommendation).'

In both cases, the reasoning-trace explanations did not supply the important information they needed. Consequently, the comprehension difficulties remained. These examples clearly demonstrate that failure to understand a key concept can make it impossible to fully understand a KBS recommendation. Furthermore, if the explanatory knowledge is not available in the context of problem-solving and, instead, has to be searched for elsewhere, it is unlikely to be sought.

## Use of contextualized access to resolve comprehension difficulties

There was a completely different scenario in the contextualized access condition. As soon as the subjects started reading the recommendation, they noticed the term funds flow adequacy ratio, which was highlighted by a hypertext link button. Realizing that this ratio was key to the understanding of the recommendation, they immediately used hypertext to access the corresponding explanations. For example, a novice read:

'The funds flow adequacy in the company is low. It is not generating sufficient cash ... for operation.' 'How? funds flow.'

Subsequently, he requested and read the How explanation on the concept:

'5-year sum of cash from operations ... divided by ... to maintain dividends and current operating level. Mmmm. OK, that is low, capital expenditure, and investment in inventories, Mmmm. That's interesting. I would have to say, to get some further explanations.'

He then read the How reasoning-trace explanation on the recommendation, and finally said, 'Yeah, I agree with that.'

Similarly, an experienced professional read, ‘The funds flow adequacy, this is going to tell me How and Why. I’ll look at that.’ He then requested and read the How deep explanation on the concept. Subsequently, he mumbled, ‘never heard of that, never in my life. Now, I am going to ask it why it is important.’ He then requested and read the Why reasoning-trace explanation on the recommendation:

'… in this regard has an important bearing on its future ability to meet debt obligations. Of course, it does, 'cause they put everything in internal funding that you complained about earlier. That seems really odd. It doesn't have, you think it's going to grow and keep the same capital structure. Then you think it would borrow.'

The contextualized deep explanations provided necessary information for this experienced professional to evaluate the recommendation, although he was critical of the reasoning in this case.

In summary, subjects without contextualized access to knowledge re-read, stressed the terminology, paused and checked the hard copy of financial statements to deal with the comprehension difficulties. In contrast, those who had contextualized access usually checked the deep explanations to get the needed knowledge within the context; some of them even read the explanations repeatedly through the hypertext links, after having read them from the information screen initially. Hypertext-style links made a difference in highlighting what could have been overlooked otherwise and allowed the subjects to access the relevant domain knowledge in the right context. The above analysis shows that, in a problem-solving situation, it is contextualized access to, not just availability, explanatory knowledge that is useful and effective.

## Quantitative analysis

The findings from verbal protocol data triggered some quantitative analysis. Table 1 shows the number of subjects who requested deep explanations on the concept of funds flow adequacy. The difference between novices and experienced professional was not significant, but that between the contextualized access and control groups was highly significant (P < 0.001 based on contingency table analysis using chi-square tests).

Table 1 shows that, 20 out of 28 subjects in the contextualized access group requested at least one deep explanation on the key financial ratio. In contrast, without contextualized access, only one out of the 25 subjects did so; virtually all of the subjects ignored the unknown financial ratio when first seeing it on the information screen. Although some of them later realized that the concept was central to the understanding of the entire recommendation and tried other convenient sources (e.g. reasoning-trace explanations accessible from the recommendation screen and the hard copy of financial statements), most of them did not leave the primary problem-solving context to seek the deep explanations.

We compared this special instance with the overall use of the KBS consisting of 25 recommendations. When the number of deep explanations requested in this special instance (i.e. on the funds flow adequacy ratio) is compared with that for all of the concepts and procedures in the KBS, two results are particularly interesting. First, the effect of contextualized access on the users' likelihood of requesting deep explanations in this case of comprehension difficulty (which is about 20 to 1 as shown in Table 1) is much stronger than the average case: subjects in the contextualized access group, on average, requested three times as many deep explanations as the control group (13.9 vs. 4.6 during the entire process of KBS use). Apparently, users were likely to seek deep explanations in case of anomalies, while ignoring many other deep explanations that were also accessible in the context. This suggests that contextualized access to knowledge via hypertext would be extensively used when users are dealing with unfamiliar subjects.

Second, although novices on average requested nearly twice as many deep explanations as did experienced professionals (12.4 vs. 6.3), which was a natural reflection of the difference between their levels of expertise, this was not true in the special instance. The 25 experienced professionals as a group requested 17 deep explanations on funds flow adequacy ratio, whereas the 28 novices requested only 15 deep explanations. This seems to imply that contextualized access can be useful for not only novices but also experienced professionals. The result also indicates that, when using KBS, experienced professionals generally were attentive to the limit of their expertise and any anomaly in KBS output and would request deep explanations for what was beyond their knowledge.

To what extent contextualized access to explanatory knowledge is helpful for task performance? This question cannot be answered based on this particular instance because the subjects' judgement quality was measured only after they had gone through all of the 25 recommendations made by the KBS. However, our analysis of the overall explanation use shows contextualized access significantly increased access to explanatory knowledge in the context of problem-solving, which significantly contributed to the quality of problem-solving (Mao & Benbasat, 1997). This result strongly endorses the provision of contextualized access to explanatory knowledge for KBS users.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

Several important conclusions can be drawn from this study. First, our process-tracing data have clearly illustrated the difference between the availability of knowledge and contextualized accessibility to knowledge, and highlighted the importance of contextualized access. As illustrated in Table 1, explanations on the key financial ratio were requested by 71% of the users with contextualized access: this increased access in turn significantly contributed to the quality of problem-solving (Mao & Benbasat, 1997). In contrast, without such access, the key financial ratio was either overlooked or missed due to the lack of convenient access by virtually the entire control group, despite the fact that explanatory knowledge provided in the form of deep explanations was essential for comprehending KBS output. In other words, even if the knowledge is available somewhere in the system but the user has to search for it, it will not be used. This is likely to be the case in work situations, in which learning new knowledge is secondary but throughput (i.e. getting things done) is the primary concern. Therefore, to a large extent, whether explanatory knowledge is actually accessed or not depends on if it can be accessed conveniently within the context of problem-solving.

Second, hypertext is highly effective in encouraging and facilitating contextualized access to knowledge. Upon seeing the recommendation that was particularly difficult to understand, many subjects immediately noticed the key financial ratio involved in KBS output and took a look at the explanations using hypertext before starting deliberation on the KBS recommendation. As expected, hypertext link buttons not only enabled the access but also highlighted the key financial ratio involved in KBS output and signalled the contextualized accessibility of explanatory knowledge.

Third, another interesting observation is that, although it was possible to follow hypertext links to have an open-ended exploration of domain knowledge, most users were not interested in doing so. Typically, users would just take a look at the deep explanation they requested and then went back to the analysis. Apparently, their priority and focus were on problem-solving as opposed to learning domain knowledge. In other words, in such ‘work’ situations, the availability of contextualized explanations is unlikely to shift a user’s attention away from problem-solving. More importantly, this observation suggests that linking the underlying knowledge to the problem-solving processes with hypertext is perhaps more significant than merely linking different elements of the knowledge, although the latter has been a more popular application of hypertext.

Fourth, our analysis also reveals that the users' domain knowledge influences explanation use. Whereas contextualized access explanatory knowledge was available throughout the experimental KBS, it was used and effective to a greater extent in situations in which specialized knowledge on domain concepts was critical. We expect that the larger the disparity between user knowledge and the expertise displayed by the KBS, the greater the need for contextualized access to explanatory knowledge.

Lastly, experienced professionals are cognisant of the limit of their expertise and anomalies in KBS output; if necessary, they are just as willing to request explanations as novices. Whereas novices on average requested twice as many deep explanations as did experienced professionals during the entire process of the KBS use, experienced professionals on average actually requested slightly more deep explanations in the special instance when KBS output involved unfamiliar domain concepts. This result also implies that both novice and experienced decision-makers can benefit from contextualized access to explanatory knowledge.

The above findings are consistent with theories of discourse comprehension, contextualized learning and the production paradox and can be accounted for as follows. (a) Contextualized access to knowledge enabled by hypertext indeed has significant benefits for enhancing the understanding of output of information systems and resolving discourse comprehension difficulties by allowing rapid and timely access to relevant information without disrupting the flow of work. (b) It is conducive to contextualized learning by integrating task domain knowledge into the work process. (c) It can substantially reduce the motivational ‘cost’ of learning, thus alleviating the production paradox. These benefits are particularly significant in situations in which specialized knowledge on domain concepts is critical. The implications of our findings are not limited to the development of KBS. In any complex information systems, task domain knowledge has to be made conveniently available to users in the context of work to be beneficial.

Whereas contextualized access to knowledge appears useful and effective, its cost–benefit analysis is important from the practitioner's point of view. The benefits include enhanced problem-solving as a result of the enhanced access to explanatory knowledge in KBS and knowledge transfer from KBS to problem-solvers. These benefits are difficult to measure because they depend on how critical the KBS is and the number of users of the KBS and the importance of their learning and training to an organization. Our experimental system did not allow us to predict the development cost of contextualized access because it was a simulated KBS. However, in principle, if the underlying knowledge of a KBS is already documented with hypertext, the incremental cost may not be high: a simple method of making such knowledge available to the user is having an extra layer of KBS user interface to provide hypertext mark-up on keywords appearing in KBS output. Desmarais et al. (1997) provided a detailed cost–benefit analysis of a system with similar features to our experimental system. Their results indicate that the development costs can be a significant amount. The fact that there is a factor of 17 between their break-even points of the optimistic and pessimistic scenarios suggests that it is difficult to conduct cost–benefit analysis reliably for this type of systems. However, as more and more tools become available for supporting on-line knowledge documentation and access, the development costs are likely to fall.

Limitations of our study must be kept in mind when the results are interpreted and generalized. As with any lab experiment, the external validity of our results may be weak, despite our effort to minimize this limitation, e.g. by adopting a previously validated experimental system and a realistic task. The use of contextualized access to knowledge by experienced professionals might be particularly constrained simply because they were under the spotlight as subjects with the profile of experienced professionals. This could have led us to underestimate the effectiveness of contextualized access on experienced professionals. Moreover, the thinking-aloud procedure might further distinct the experimental condition from normal real-world settings, although it was unlikely to influence the participants' task performance (Ericsson & Simon, 1993). Finally, the potential novelty effect of the experimental system is another threat to the external validity. Because most participants have little or no prior experience with KBS, not to mention KBS with hypertext-based user interface, the pattern of initial use of the system is hardly the same as that after a long period of extensive use. Whereas the need for accessing explanatory domain knowledge may decrease in the long run (or after frequent use of the system), the effectiveness of contextualized access may decrease consequently.

In conclusion, whereas hypertext has become a popular choice for on-line documentation of knowledge and information, we have gone one step further by exploring the potential of the use of hypertext to enable contextualized access to knowledge. To this end, this research has made two significant contributions, by identifying the cognitive basis of contextualized access and examining its empirical evidence. Verbal protocol data played a key role in distinguishing comprehension difficulties caused by the lack of convenient access to knowledge, and the extensive use of explanatory knowledge to resolve the difficulties if the access to knowledge was contextualized. The results show that significant benefits can be expected from contextualized access; without it, mere availability of task information is of little use. Future hypertext applications to knowledge-based systems and other management support systems should focus on the provision of contextualized access to knowledge.
