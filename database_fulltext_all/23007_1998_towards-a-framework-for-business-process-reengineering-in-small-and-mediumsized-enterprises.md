# Towards a framework for business process re-engineering in small and medium-sized enterprises

Lih-Jen Chang & Philip Powell

Information Systems Research Unit, Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK, email: orspp@razor.wbs.warwick.ac.uk

Abstract. Business process re-engineering (BPR) is the vogue, although somewhat discredited, change management method. It is as an information technology (IT)-based and customer-driven approach to organizational change, undertaken to enable superior performance, such as cost reduction, shorter cycle time, higher product quality and increased customer satisfaction. Although the literature on re-engineering principles and prospects is large, there is little empirical research evaluating the success or failure of BPR initiatives. This is especially so in small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in which the potential for BPR benefits may differ significantly from that in their larger counterparts. SMEs are the fastest growing segment of most economies and are perceived to be more flexible and adaptable in terms of structure and speed of response than larger organizations. The literature speculates that SMEs have as good a chance of re-engineering successfully as their larger counterparts, although this is untested. Yet, with limited resources, such as IT and BPR expertise, and financial constraints, SMEs may find it difficult to re-engineer. This paper addresses the role of BPR in SMEs and develops a framework to assess its implementation potential. Factors that facilitate and inhibit BPR performance are identified and analysed as the basis for empirical testing. A set of propositions is developed that allows operationalization of the framework. The ultimate goal is to develop a process and tool set to assist SMEs wishing to undertake business process re-engineering.

Keywords: Business process re-engineering, small businesses

## INTRODUCTION

The business climate is driving businesses, large and small, towards more rapid change as competition is no longer constrained by national boundaries, information is capital, skilled team-based workers are decision makers and the management role is shifting from directing to coaching and team facilitation. In addition, the consumer is more discriminating, seeking high-quality, low-cost, rapid service.

It appears that traditional management systems designed for functional specialization to enable mass production are not suitable for quality-driven competition. Quality management initiatives, such as total quality management (TQM) and Kaizen, emerged in the 1980s reflecting this. From the early 1990s, competition and globalization forced enterprises to restructure and manage change better. Business process re-engineering (BPR) was advocated and promoted as the only solution to business survival. Enabled by information technology (IT) and emphasizing customer-driven, process-oriented management, BPR has delivered dramatic gains in quality, cost, speed and efficiency to some large firms. Paradoxically, high failure rates also indicate that BPR is risky if undertaken improperly as transformation involves a complex interaction of people, IT and new skills. Researchers have thus begun to emphasize the human aspects of re-engineering, such as motivation and empowerment, senior management support and training.

The BPR literature has focused on large firms, yet small firms have attempted to re-engineer. Small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are an important and growing sector in all economies. Mahesh and Garret (1996) note that SMEs, like larger firms, face challenges as they react to the changing environment such as the emergence of world markets and needs for better product quality, faster delivery and closer business partnerships. It is arguable if SMEs have more flexibility and adaptability because of their structure, ability to respond to market and team-based nature to enable BPR. As Hale and Cragg (1996) point out, there is a need to investigate the small firm arena to determine whether the same principles for BPR that apply to large organizations are suitable for SMEs, or if a different approach needs be taken by small businesses looking for radical change. It is important to investigate whether SMEs can benefit dramatically, like larger firms, by undertaking BPR, or if their limited financial and human resource, IT/IS (information system) and BPR expertise inhibit BPR performance. However, the need is to avoid the anecdotal or descriptive nature of much BPR research and to take a holistic view of its impacts. This paper explores these issues and develops a framework. The framework is used to develop a set of propositions that drive empirical work in assessing the application of business process re-engineering to SMEs.

## THE NATURE OF BPR

The term re-engineering emerged in the early 1990s (Hammer, 1990). According to Hammer, BPR is ‘the fundamental re-thinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements on critical measures of performance.’ This definition implies a discontinuity in the performance of an organization and enables Coulson-Thomas (1992) to argue that ‘incremental changes are no longer enough’ and that ‘transformation cannot be achieved without fundamental change’. Manganelli and Raspa (1995), however, argue that a radical strategy such as ‘starting over’ is not necessarily essential because ‘if a company is in business today, it must already offer some kind of value to its customers.’ Indeed, as Jennings (1996) argues, ‘Starting with a clean sheet of paper results in abolishing existing systems, skills, and structures which can lead to expensive rebuilding efforts.’ Davenport (1993) suggests process innovation and sees re-engineering as a strategy for change that must carefully consider complex implementation issues involving understanding the workforce, technology and culture. Although many argue that BPR is not new, it is the integration of process, IT and business transformation that differentiates BPR from past initiatives (Earl, 1989). The main elements of BPR are characterized as fundamental work process redesign, adding value to final customers, the integration of cross-functional specialization and exploitation of IT (Fig. 1).

As Fig. 1 demonstrates, re-engineering focuses on customers, emphasizing ‘value-addedness’ from their perspective as the key to competitiveness (Talwar, 1993). As Skrovan (1995) puts it, ‘improvements in execution must start by clearly focusing on the consumer value chain. Changes must add significant value from the consumer’s perspective in order to deliver any meaningful increases on the top line.’

BPR focuses on process. As defined by Davenport and Short (1990), a process is a ‘set of logically related tasks performed to achieve a defined business outcome.’ Entailing both physical and informational aspects, BPR involves functional integration. Stewart (1993) indicates that process management differs from function management in three ways: it focuses on external customers; employees with different skills are grouped together to accomplish a complete set of work (process); and information moves to where it is needed without passing through the management hierarchy.

BPR emphasizes horizontal integration that involves cross-organizational boundaries the analysis and design of work flows and processes within and between organizations (Davenport, 1993). Increasingly, there is emphasis on IT to integrate re-engineered processes. Venkatraman 1991) suggests that a firm can approach process redesign from two different perspectives,

Correspondingly, IT’s role has evolved from operative efficiency to become an enabler in creating and maintaining flexible business networks (Tinnila, 1995). Thus, Zairi and Sinclair (1995) suggest IT infrastructure can be a facilitator as well as an inhibitor in re-engineering. IT has been used to ‘hasten’ work rather than to ‘transform’ it.

Re-engineering also places a major emphasis on employees and their role in resolving problems. Although IT is central in BPR initiatives (Flynn, 1992), most researchers agree that process improvement involves changes to jobs and the social structure to increase motivation, reduce stress and improve performance by empowerment (Wastell et al., 1994).

Many researchers also stress the importance of commitment from top management (Barrett, 1994). Jarvenpaa and Ives (1991) highlight this as encompassing both participation and involvement, and indicate that this is strongly associated with technical innovations. Holland and Kumar (1995) detail the kind of executive support required for re-engineering: vision and perspective are needed to keep the re-engineering initiative on track, and executive time and energy keep the BPR efforts moving.

In practice, most authors suggest that a strategic approach and the development of a BPR strategy is key (Talwar, 1993). Teng et al. (1994) propose that critical inputs from both corporate and IT planning should be incorporated in BPR planning.

Although described as radical, BPR complements incremental improvement programs, such as total quality management (TQM) (Martinsons, 1995) as BPR is a selective, fast-paced, innovative, top down-driven change approach, whereas TQM is the opposite. BPR advocates suggest that the most significant difference between BPR and TQM is that incremental improvements do not fundamentally alter a process; rather, progress is limited by the inherent capabilities of the process. Sheridan (1991), however, reports that results are best when re-engineering is combined with quality improvement. TQM-oriented organizations are in a good position to use BPR techniques because of their greater use of strategic and process management techniques. Sinclair (1994) explains that organizations implementing TQM are more likely to define critical success factors (CSFs), key performance indicators and performance targets, and to assign responsibility for CSFs and develop teams responsible for core business processes.

## TOWARDS A FRAMEWORK OF SMES IMPLEMENTING BPR

As more organizations undertake BPR, implementation issues have become a major concern (Verespej, 1994). Although the literature discussing re-engineering principles and prospects is large and growing, few frameworks are offered. Further, approaches to BPR are mainly based on anecdotal evidence (Grover et al., 1995). There is little broad-based, empirical research evaluating the success of BPR implementations, especially in small firms. It is therefore paramount that the process and results of BPR interventions in SMEs is researched, especially given recent suggestions that a majority of re-engineering efforts fail (Champy, 1994).

The objective of this paper is to develop a framework, as the basis for later empirical testing, to investigate the following:

\- Why and when do SMEs undertake re-engineering initiatives?

\- Can SMEs, as their large counterparts, benefit from process redesign and achieve cost reductions, shorter cycle times, higher quality, and increased customers satisfaction?

\- Do SMEs have more flexibility, in terms of culture, IT use, structure, market response and cross-functional orientation to carry out re-engineering; or do limited financial, human and technological resources inhibit BPR outcomes?

Analysis of the framework allows the development of a set of propositions of the interaction between BPR and SMEs that can then be operationalized.

Ascari et al. (1995) emphasize four elements in an organization with regard to BPR challenges and pitfalls. The four elements are culture, structure, process and technology. This paper identifies unique SME attributes pertinent to organizational change, including size, team-based nature and financial/human resources in establishing a new framework. Modifying the work of Ascari et al. (1995) results in a framework of four classifications, structure, resources, culture and technology; these are discussed below (Fig. 2).

## STRUCTURE

Structure defines lines of communication and the degree of individual or collective responsibility and accountability (Ascari et al., 1995). As BPR emphasizes cross-functional teams, there is a tendency to move toward flatter organizations with a larger span of control. Also, as BPR adopts a broad perspective on cross-organizational processes along the value chain, it results in changes that span organizational boundaries (Sia & Neo, 1996). Thus, BPR must satisfy both internal (colleagues or suppliers) and external customers.

## Size and control

DeToro and McCabe (1997) emphasize that future organizations will rely more heavily on horizontal, or process, management. The transition from traditional to process management requires a radical change in thinking from a personality-based, top-down, command-and-control hierarchy to a flatter, process- and team-oriented structure. Sia and Neo (1996) also reinforce a shift in control towards higher reliance on automation, deferred review, self-management and the market. Accordingly, it is impossible to create a process- and customer-centred organization if people are measured and rewarded in traditional ways (Hammer, 1996). Compared with large firms, the management structures of small firms are flatter, and middle management plays a minimal role. Thus, they do not suffer from bureaucracy and cumbersome organizational systems (Lefebvre & Lefebvre, 1992). As Conti and Warner (1996) indicate, firms that can be successfully organized on process lines will inevitably be small. Hence, SMEs are generally considered to be flexible and adaptive and are perceived as easier entities in which to implement BPR. This is because flexibility offers the ability to respond quickly to forced changes. It also provides competitive advantage by enabling the development of new performance-enhancing features and exploiting first-mover advantages (Avison et al., 1995).

Proposition P1: as SMEs have simpler structures and greater flexibility, they will find it easier to implement BPR.

## Team-based operations

Brien and Buono (1996) emphasize that true team learning is the ability to share and build on individual knowledge, so that collective knowledge enables continual team and organizational improvement as well the ability to discover, develop and implement new ways of doing business. Glazer et al. (1992) emphasize that processes require integration, interactions, understanding others' functional perspectives and recognition of integration mechanisms. Small businesses encourage team and cross-functional orientations — it is suggested that every small business starts as an empowered team (Kinni, 1995). The lack of bureaucracy makes for efficient and informal internal communications. As Kinni (1995) reports, smaller companies often form re-engineering teams comprising mainly front-line supervisors and workers. The advantages of such a strategy are twofold: first, it allows smaller firms to maximize limited human resources, and second, including people from every level builds involvement and empowerment.

Proposition P2: the team-based nature of SMEs enhances their ability to integrate business processes.

## External relations

BPR is undertaken to satisfy both internal and external customers and suppliers. SMEs are considered adaptable and able to respond readily to changing needs of customers. Owing to fewer management layers, small businesses tend to be closer to their customers (Brady & Voss, 1995), and they have the ability to react quickly to keep abreast of fast-changing market requirements (Goss, 1991). However, there is considerable dependency upon maintaining the contracts with large corporations (the 'hollow factory' concept). In addition, SMEs have little power to influence market price and are unable to erect barriers to entry to the industry. Their positioning in niche markets is often based on a small number of customers (Hendry et al., 1991).

Recent research indicates that collaboration among SMEs, such as partnerships, co-operation and alliances, is an emerging approach to industrial competitiveness (Rosenfeld, 1996). The ability to share resources is especially important to SMEs that previously lacked the resources to participate in global markets. As Bonk (1996) argues, 'Perhaps the most important trend in integrating the world economy is the rapid growth of cross-border alliances between companies or in networks of co-operating organizations.' Maynard (1996) explains that strategic alliances encourage product innovation, bringing stability to cyclical businesses, expanding product portfolios, and forging new kinds of supplier relationships. However, Dyer (1996) contends that scarce entrepreneurial resources restrict the range of activities that are practical in a small business context.

Proposition P3: as SMEs are closer to customers and suppliers, they can implement BPR on an interorganizational scope.

## RESOURCES

Businesses are trying to do more with less and so they have re-engineered in order to reduce operating costs (Witherill & Kolak, 1996). Hagel (1993) asserts that the organization's energy needs to be harnessed and targeted if fundamental change is to lead to large-scale benefits. King (1996) also points out that BPR efforts stand a better chance of succeeding when the organization allocates its resources in line with business objectives. Resources, for SMEs implementing BPR, are financial capacity, human resource, research and development, and previous quality management experiences.

## Financial capacity

Binks and Ennew (1996) argue that ‘Restricted access to finance is potentially a significant constraint on the growth of small businesses.’ Haskever (1996) clearly indicates that two of the most serious problems SMEs face when trying to implement quality management are the owner/manager's lack of business experience and knowledge, and a shortage of financial and human resources. Thus, although survival is the first concern of SMEs, financial insufficiency or constraints may significantly inhibit SMEs undertaking BPR.

Proposition P4: owing to budget constraints, SMEs are likely to undertake BPR on a short-term basis.

## Human resources

Although the process focus dominates total quality re-engineering, a growing number of organizations see competence as the key to enduring performance and to making human resources most effective (Kochanski & Ruse, 1996). However, human resources in SMEs are limited. As Nash and Rock (1996) report, almost $60\%$ of owner-managed businesses fail because of the owner's lack of appropriate management experience. Woods (1996) points out that owners of small firms are caught up in day-to-day operations and lack perspective on the kinds of problems that can threaten the business. Further, small-business owners tend to rely on the popular and professional press for management information.

Worsham et al. (1997) indicate that, for small businesses, finding the right workers — with proper knowledge, skills, experience, attitude and work habits — can be extremely difficult. Ferrell (1996) notes that, SMEs are at a greater disadvantage than larger firms because they typically have few in-house technical experts to deal with new developments. SMEs lack the sophisticated management support that knowledge workers often find in larger organizations (Mackinnon, 1996). Thus, owners of SMEs face a challenge in attracting and retaining talented employees.

Proposition P5: lack of in-house professionals and expertise in SMEs may inhibit BPR activities.

## Previous quality management experiences

SMEs are often suppliers to larger organizations, and poor product quality adversely affects the competitive ability of their customers. As a radical approach to organizational change, BPR complements incremental improvement programmes (Martinsons, 1995). Yeo (1996) suggests that TQM and BPR do exist simultaneously within organizations and alternate with continuous improvements taking place between radical changes. BPR essentially extends the TQM concept of continuous/incremental improvement to drastic, IT-based, rapid improvement (Sharad, 1996). A survey by Shea and Gobeli (1995) also highlights that SMEs adopt TQM for different reasons such as promoting growth, changing customer expectations or improving company performance, and TQM principles and tools can be applied to improve small businesses. Sheridan (1991) emphasizes that results are best when re-engineering is combined with a quality improvement effort. As Sinclair (1994) elaborates, TQM-oriented organizations are in a good position to apply BPR techniques because of their greater use of strategic and process management techniques. In addition, Zairi and Sinclair (1995) report that organizations that have adopted TQM show greater use of strategic and process management techniques, benchmarking and self-assessment, which places them in a better position to use re-engineering techniques.

Proposition P6: SMEs are more likely to succeed in implementing BPR if they have previous TQM experience.

## CULTURE

BPR does not work without profound cultural change. Culture is a set of interdependent processes by which work gets done within an enterprise. Sisaye and Bondnar (1996) emphasize behavioural and cultural change is necessary for effective organizational change. Many re-engineering processes failed not because of technology but because of cultural difficulties (Coleman, 1997). Therefore, as Covey (1996) suggests, the only way to have an enduring competitive advantage is to create a culture that continuously produces the next technology by promoting learning and innovation. Unless senior executives are involved, re-engineering efforts will only create cynicism. Tersine et al. (1997) emphasize that, ‘A cultural transition is needed to move from traditional command and control mentality to a style that features leadership, teamwork, empowerment, entrepreneurship, and risk-taking’.

BPR generates a series of projects, ranging from process improvement through to radical transformation. All breakthroughs entail risk. Rather than being avoided, risk needs to be recognized and embraced. SMEs, as Storey and Cressy (1995) argue, tend to be more conservative than larger firms in their strategies and tend to change incrementally. However, firm owners' objectives encompass their attitude to risk. Although Hyvarinen (1990) indicates that most SMEs are willing to take risks and have the capability to enter small, new or risky markets, the attitude to radical change, however, is related to the ownermanager's personality and the organizational climate. According to Hirschfield (1994), when small firms are at start-up or rapid growth, it is more likely that a radical re-engineering approach will be adopted as the strategy. On the other hand, when ownermanagers adopt a highly centralizing, autocratic stance, it will be difficult to produce a conducive environment in which to re-engineer.

## Employee empowerment

Employee involvement facilitates a culture that marshals creative energies for problem solving and process improvements (Spector, 1995). As Flynn (1992) emphasizes, ‘Re-engineering from a social perspective involves changes to jobs and the social structure in order to increase motivation, to reduce stress and to improve performance by empowerment.’ A lack of managerial control at many small companies often allows employees to make decisions for themselves (Brady & Voss, 1995). This enhances internal problem solving and provides an ability to reorganize rapidly to adapt to changes in the environment (Goss, 1991). This creates an environment of ‘ownership’, and such organizations are referred to as more ‘organic’, allowing rapid exchanges of innovative ideas.

Proposition P7: employee empowerment will enhance BPR performance in SMEs.

## Management support and top-down communications

Commitment from top management has been emphasized by BPR advocates (Hammer, 1990). Holland and Kumar (1995) contend that, although vision and perspective keep re-engineering initiatives on track, executive time and energy keep the BPR efforts moving. Covey (1996) emphasizes that unless senior executives are involved, re-engineering efforts will only engender cynicism. Restructuring processes can lead to faster cycle times, greater customer satisfaction and lower costs, but only if the organization has a collaborative culture (Majchrzak & Wang, 1996). As Leonard (1996) reports, one of the major failure factors is not translating the re-engineered vision into appropriate cultural and behavioural expectations that can be communicated to all employees. However, as Ghosh and Chan (1994) indicate, decision-making in SMEs is dominated by the chief executive officer, which may hinder top-down communication, although making it easier to implement forced change.

Proposition P8: BPR projects in SMEs are unlikely to succeed without top management support and appropriate employee communications.

## Innovation

BPR is the innovation of processes that Davenport (1993) contends is synonymous with process re-engineering. He proposes ‘process innovation’ that encompasses ‘the radical improvement of business process performance through the use of innovative tools and work designs’. Innovation is fostered in decentralized, highly integrated, informal organizations, such as SMEs (Teng et al., 1994), and is crucial to their success. SMEs are often perceived as more fertile than larger firms in terms of fostering innovation (Carrier, 1995). Responsiveness, innovation and nimbleness, as Rock and Nash (1995) emphasize, are invaluable qualities for SMEs.

Proposition P9: the culture of innovation in SMEs will facilitate their BPR efforts.

## Strategic and business planning in SMEs

It is recommended that BPR initiatives start from a strategic perspective, and top management plays a decisive role guided by re-engineering experts. Schnitt (1993) stresses the integration of BPR initiatives and strategic planning, ‘to make strategy an integral part of the re-engineering process, several strategic initiatives must be undertaken to better understand the company’s markets, competitors, and the things that make the organization a success.’ Teng et al. (1994) also point out that the main elements using IT for BPR are BPR policy planning, an innovative environment and co-ordinated implementation. To ensure the long-term viability and success of BPR, these aspects need co-ordination. Proactive IT strategic planning can foster the development of ‘competitive systems’ that, according to Porter (1980), can reinforce strategies of cost, differentiation and focus.

However, formal strategic planning is not common in small firms (Hall, 1995), and McKiernan and Morris (1994) argue that the absence of formal strategic planning, or inadequacies in its process, can be directly linked to failure. The lack of strategic planning in SMEs, as Clayton (1996) points out, leads to outdated management practices and an autocratic style of management. As Ghosh and Chan (1994) note, the need for strategic planning is acknowledged by SMEs, but more in an ad hoc, logical incremental fashion than in a comprehensive way.

Proposition P10: the absence of formal strategic planning in SMEs, their narrower business vision and less ambitious goals may limit BPR outcomes.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS/TECHNOLOGY

Ribbler (1996) argues that most re-engineering is technology driven, with the role of IT changing from producing data to integrating processes with functions. Improving processes usually involves the implementation of cross-functional systems and process management. Thus, the goals of the IT function have shifted from cutting costs to increasing knowledge and flexibility. Successful companies build flexible organizational structures, often aided by innovative use of IT, to co-ordinate design, production, marketing and distribution, linking workers, management, suppliers and customers (Maital, 1994). Indeed, the primary reason for the success of enterprise computing is its ability to simplify transaction processing and eliminate non-value-added work while at the same time improving customer service. For instance, workflow software dramatically reduces the time and expense involved in re-engineering a process (Lefebvre, 1996).

## IT/IS infrastructure

IT infrastructure is the foundation of IT capability, in the form of reliable services, shared throughout the firm, and has usually been provided by the IS function. IT infrastructure include networks, management and provision of large-scale computing, electronic date interchange, management of shared customer databases, and research and development aimed at identifying emerging technologies (Davenport et al., 1989). Although competitive pressures and globalization have provided an impetus for innovation, particular attention is paid to the role of technology management as a success factor in technological innovation by SMEs (Cortese, 1996). Falling hardware and software costs are enabling SMEs become automated. Technological know-how may also increase SME flexibility, allowing them to recast production processes faster. According to Grover et al., 1995), IT infrastructure can be a significant barrier or enabler of the practical options available to planning and changing processes for BPR.

Gates et al. (1996) indicate that SMEs may have taken better advantage of the opportunities IT creates than big business. However, SMEs normally lack the qualified personnel to benefit (Eid & Moghrabi, 1995). Naylor and Williams (1994) also point out that SMEs lack formalization in IT strategy and show evidence of resource poverty restraining development. Friedman (1996) reports that SME owners often fail to recognize that new technologies can act as a source of competitive advantage.

Proposition P11: poor IT/IS infrastructure in SMEs may inhibit BPR performance.

## IT/IS expertise and end-user skills

IS managers, as Cone (1996) argues, must deal with technology that is constantly changing and provide it to indifferent users who have unrealistic expectations. IS experts are well positioned to play a significant role in supporting and leading many activities in re-engineering. As Khalil (1997) argues, this role may include managing complex large-scale BPR projects, identifying and maintaining a technological vision in support of BPR, developing process-oriented IS and managing on-going changes in processes. Kaplan (1996) points out that most organizations adopt technology without acquiring the skills and establishing the operating procedures to ensure that it is properly implemented. As for other human resources, SMEs lack IT/IS expertise and devote little to training end users.

Proposition P12: inappropriate IT/IS use and insufficient end-user skills will inhibit BPR performance in SMEs.

## IMPLEMENTING BPR

As McElroy (1996) proposes using projects, programmes and associated management processes can increase BPR success. Research suggests that BPR projects aim at core businesses as they generate the best gains. This paper highlights the issues of implementing BPR, viewed as strategic change, as to why, when and where SMEs initiate re-engineering.

## Re-engineering scope and scale

Levene and Braganza (1996) classify BPR initiatives by two dimensions: scope and scale. Scope includes the number of functions integrated to form the process (Rockart & Short, 1989). Scale is the extent of change in terms of how radical the BPR project (Hagel, 1993). Davenport and Short (1990) identify types of processes as interpersonal, interfunctional, and inter-organizational. Broadbent et al. (1994) contrast approaches to process redesign as simplification and innovation. Based on the strategic nature and radical orientation, Childe et al. (1994) depict a spectrum of process improvement activities, from personal/group improvement, quality improvement teams, process simplification, process re-engineering, business integration, to business re-engineering. Although Craig and Yetton (1992) argue that process simplification offers firms the potential to capture some performance advantages and to minimize risk factors, higher levels of BPR, such as business integration, involve organizational and job redesign and new developments in IT. The higher levels of BPR are, in turn, viewed as more strategic and radical with the potential for substantial gains.

Proposition P13: BPR projects in SMEs will involve lower scale and less scope, e.g. individual/group improvement and process simplification, as SMEs' policies are more conservative, and owners try to avoid risks.

## Motivation to re-engineer

The motivations and purposes for SMEs to re-engineer may be different from those for larger firms. Kinni (1995) argues that in SMEs re-engineering is often a response to positive trends — they tend to re-engineer from a position of strength. Two reasons are cited: first, small companies are already short of the time and resources needed to re-engineer, and it is unlikely that a small company that is faltering can muster the required energy; and, second, the by-products of success — rapidly increasing volume, straining processes and increased profits — are the impetus for small companies to re-engineer. They need to counter fast growth with processes improvement. Perry (1986) indicates that most small firms grow fastest from start-up until a ‘comfort’ stage is reached. From an organizational life cycle perspective, firms pursuing a growth strategy tend to re-engineer in order to enhance their external flexibility, i.e. relations with customers and suppliers, and internal efficiency, i.e. quality and production (Dodge & Robbins, 1992).

Proposition P14: SMEs are more likely to re-engineer their process during stages of growth.

## CONCLUSIONS

There is a notable absence of research that evaluates BPR in SMEs. This may be because, as MacIntosh and Francis (1995) report, ‘Smaller enterprises are less likely publicly to report their BPR efforts. More significantly however, it may be that BPR is not as relevant to them.’ However, it is argued that SMEs can benefit from re-engineering and are considered more receptive to implement BPR (Champy & Hammer, 1994; Manganelli, 1995). A framework is thus developed to identify and investigate factors that facilitate/enhance or inhibit BPR performance in SMEs. SMEs may undertake BPR, but with a different background from larger firms, and will use different methods. The aim is to present a holistic view of BPR drivers and inhibitors in SMEs and to overcome the partial aspects of much past research. To this end, a set of propositions are generated from the framework.

The propositions generated are summarized in Table 1. Most small enterprises, by definition, do not have the resources or the people to create rigid, hierarchical enterprises based around distinct functions; they tend to exhibit more informal communications and a less bureaucratic mode of operation. Simplicity and flexibility of organizational structure are of great value to SMEs undertaking BPR. The team-based nature of SMEs, easier communication and sound relations with customers and suppliers may increase their chance of success in BPR. Like their larger counterparts, SMEs are in a better position to carry out BPR projects with top management support, previous quality management experiences and empowered employees. However, a lack of in-house IT/BPR professionals and expertise, and strategic thinking, as well as financial constraints, may inhibit BPR performance in SMEs. These, however, need empirical testing. The propositions developed here allow operationalization of the issues identified and empirical testing is now in progress. Once validated and further refined the framework will form the basis of a process and a set of tools and techniques that will assist SMEs that wish to undertake BPR.
