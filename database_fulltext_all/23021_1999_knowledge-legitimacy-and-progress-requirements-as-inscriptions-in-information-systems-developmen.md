# Knowledge, legitimacy and progress? Requirements as inscriptions in information systems development

Christopher Westrup

Manchester School of Accounting and Finance, University of Manchester, Oxford Road, Manchester M13 9PL UK, email: Chris.Westrup@man.ac.uk

Abstract. Given the importance placed on requirements analysis for success in systems development, this paper seeks to illuminate some of the underlying issues common to requirements techniques. This view is offered as a contrast to frameworks that identify an apparent diversity of requirements techniques, on the one hand, and those that portray differences in requirements techniques based on a progressive improvement of these techniques. The central argument of the paper is that most requirements techniques share common characteristics with inscription devices, by translating problematic organizational situations into agreed representations on paper or on computer. These inscriptions are commonly seen as providing legitimate knowledge of the organization that cannot be shown to be 'correct' but which act as resources to resolve tensions in requirements formulation and enable information technologies to be implemented successfully in organizations. A consequence of this argument is that portrayals of progressive improvement in requirements techniques have more rhetorical force than historical accuracy, and this assertion is illustrated by comparing SOP (Study Organization Plan), a 1960s requirements methodology, with 'state-of-the-art' object-oriented analysis. Finally, given the problematic role of requirements techniques as inscription devices, guidance is given to those engaged in requirements analysis that focuses as much on the context of the application of the requirements technique as the requirements technique itself.

Keywords: Information systems, inscription devices, knowledge, legitimation, progress, requirements analysis

## INTRODUCTION

The importance of requirements analysis for success in information systems development is widely recognized, and a variety of techniques have been created to perform this task (Flynn, 1992). Within the information systems development literature, several frameworks have been presented that seek to demonstrate and classify the diversity of requirements analysis techniques (Byrd et al., 1992; Hirschheim & Klein, 1992; Hirschheim et al., 1995). Another influential strand in the literature has been the identification of diversity in requirements techniques by plotting a progressive improvement or refinement of ‘generations’ of requirements techniques through time (Couger, 1982; Hirschheim & Klein, 1992). This paper takes a different stance. It seeks to show the similarities among requirements techniques and argues that these similarities far outweigh any differences. It is argued that the common features reside in the aim of requirements techniques to provide legitimized knowledge inscribed on paper or on computers that seek to represent aspects of an organization.

In the first part of the paper it is argued that requirements techniques act as inscription devices that produce stable yet mobile inscriptions of the organizational context on paper or on computer (see Bloomfield & Vurdubakis, 1994). This argument is developed by considering requirements analysis techniques as mechanisms that are designed to produce ‘legitimate’ knowledge of organizations for use in information systems development. It is argued that there is no prior way of distinguishing a ‘correct’ representation of the organizational context derived by a requirements analysis technique. The portrayals of a progressive advancement of requirements techniques overstate their case and appear to be designed to convince that certain approaches are appropriate. To illustrate this point, the paper considers SOP (study organization plan), a requirements technique of the early 1960s, and seeks to show how it has many similarities to an inscription device for ‘state-of-the-art’ object-oriented approaches to analysis. Finally, using the concept of inscription as an organizing theme, a checklist of questions is provided for those engaged in requirements analysis. The purpose here is to assist those engaged in requirements analysis by moving the focus of attention from just the requirements technique itself towards an investigation of the context of the application of the technique.

## REQUIREMENTS ANALYSIS TECHNIQUES AS INSCRIPTION DEVICES

How can the process of requirements determination be conceptualized? Viewed simply, a requirement only becomes recognized as such when it is formulated in a specific way that is recognized as legitimate. For instance, a senior manager may say, 'We need x', an order that is noted down and taken as a requirement; on the other hand, the grumbling of the office worker about procedures may not have the same force. In general terms, a first step is the identification of needs and their formulation in writing or some other technique (such as data flow diagrams) — this is a process of inscription that translates something unclear and undefined into a representation on paper (see Latour, 1987). A second step is the relating of various inscriptions to produce a set of requirements that are accepted as legitimate. Let us look first at this process of inscribing, and then at what the outcomes of such a process may be.

It is well known, particularly in the philosophy of science, that language is theory laden (Bernstein, 1988). A naive empiricist belief that theory can be constructed from sense experience is inadequate because the way in which that experience is labelled and described is itself based on prior, if unrecognized, theory. Put simply, observation is conditioned by the observer's past experience, be that expressed through language or action. A good example is the Kuhnian notion of a paradigm that shapes observation and action. The consequences of such an argument for requirements analysis are twofold. First, any claim to realism — that a requirements technique faithfully represents what is in the world — must be flawed. Indeed, in these instances, it may be asked who stands to benefit by such claims? Second, it invites us to become aware of the importance of requirement techniques as inscription devices; a means by which the world may be labelled, demarcated and inscribed. A process of inscription is first of all one of transferring something that is 'out there' into an artefact, be it a mathematical formalism, writing, diagrams held on paper or on computer. Such a process implies that certain elements of the world are identified as important and then inscribed. The corollary of such an argument is that the processes of perception, that is labelling, are conditioned, to some extent, by the medium of inscription. For example, it can be claimed that a requirements analyst writing requirements in English will look at the world somewhat differently than someone who is inscribing that situation in first-predicate logic. At a more concrete level, it can be expected that these two accounts of the world are likely to be different, and that much of this difference resides in the medium of inscription.

The process of inscription is also affected by the conditions of the situation in which it takes place. A good example is when someone unaccustomed to shorthand tries to take notes of a conversation. This shows the material difficulties in creating inscriptions that may be overcome by, for example, using a tape recorder or camcorder. But, even then, the effect of the observer on the observed must be considered. Taping or videoing someone will change the way in which they answer questions (Van Maanan, 1988). In other words, inscription devices may alter the initial situation as well as producing a representation on paper or tape. In terms of requirements, the inference is that even forms of inscription that seem least invasive — ethnography or videoing — still have effects. What this boils down to is that the process of inscription is one of translation; one context is changed into another medium that stands in its place (see Figure 1).

Requirements techniques act as inscription devices and intervene or alter a situation, translating that situation into an inscription on paper or on computer. The nature of that inscription has important consequences on what then may occur. This is widely accepted in requirements analysis, but from a different perspective. Since the late 1960s, the use of English as an inscription of requirements has been viewed as problematic, leading to vast documents that may be incomplete, inconsistent and unclear (Layzell & Loucopoulos, 1989). More formal inscriptions such as data flow diagrams were seen as a substantive improvement. From our perspective, what the inscription, on paper or on computer, provides is a more concise account of the world, and it is one that is likely to be mobile, more stable and possibly combinable (Latour, 1990). Mobility simply means that the inscription may now be taken away from the situation and worked on elsewhere. Stability is acquired by an inscription rendering transient events into a fixed form that may then circulate elsewhere. Combinability enables inscriptions to be brought together and related together to produce a summary inscription. A good example of this is the data flow diagram, which is an inscription (on paper or computer) that ‘represents’ a part of an organization. This diagram can be taken away from the organization (it is mobile), and it represents that part of the organization in a durable form. A data flow diagram can be composed of a series of levels, each of which may be summarized at a higher level. At the most summarized form, the context level diagram provides an inscription formed from the combination of possibly hundreds of other diagrams. It is often in terms of combinability that requirements techniques are differentiated, with those being more combinable preferred to those that are not. Hence, data flow diagrams are preferred to ‘unstructured’ English.

Finally, what is the outcome of the process of inscription? Requirements as inscriptions enable those requirements to be mobilized to another location. There, the inscriptions can be manipulated by developers and management in order to optimize them and, most importantly, it is then possible to use these redesigned inscriptions to change the initial context (see Figure 1). Put differently, systems developers using inscriptions can analyse them at a location away from the interruptions of potential users and the clamour of the application area. The developers can move from analysis to design by manipulating these inscriptions on paper or on computer. And, once a design has been agreed, they can implement that design in hardware and software using inscriptions as a specification. Much of this subsequent work is the manipulation of paper or virtual worlds — inscriptions on paper or on computer. In short, the process of representation of requirements cannot be disentangled from the representations/requirements that are produced.

Arguably, requirements techniques act as inscription devices to produce legitimate knowledge about the organization. They translate the messy world of the organization into an inscription backed by the authority of a specific requirement technique or of the consultants/ developers who use that technique. What is legitimate knowledge for requirements has changed through time as the generations approach to systems development becomes apparent (Hirschheim et al., 1995). A 1960s technique would not be seen as appropriate today. The following sections of the paper seek to give two examples of the ubiquity of requirements techniques as inscription devices that question assumptions of simple progress in requirements techniques. In these sections, the argument is that there are considerable similarities between an early (and outmoded) technique called SOP from the 1960s and current ‘state-of-the-art’ approaches such as object-oriented analysis because both act as inscription devices.

## PROGRESS IN REQUIREMENTS ANALYSIS TECHNIQUES?

For systems developers to realize that requirements techniques have a history is itself an important first step as it points out that any technique is, at best, provisional. However, this paper goes further by identifying considerable similarities between techniques separated by some 30 years, findings that support the concept of requirements techniques as inscription devices.

The concepts of ‘generations’ never seems to go out of fashion in information systems (see Laudon & Laudon, 1996, pp. 195 and 239) with generations of hardware, programming languages, and information systems methodologies, with their requirements techniques either implicit or explicit. For the latter, Couger (1973, 1975, 1982) was instrumental in propagating this view, and it has been taken up most recently by Hirschheim & Klein (1992) and Hirschheim et al. (1995), who presented eight generations of ‘overlapping’ methodologies ranging from a pre-methodological era before the mid 1960s to the emergence of emancipatory approaches. The generations approach describes (and prescribes) a trajectory of progress from one generation to the next. For those using older generations of methodology it becomes clear to them what is the next move that they must make. Couger in 1975 illustrates these points well. On the one hand he is able to point out a ‘need’ for techniques to catch up with hardware generations:

'Concomitant with the increase in complexity of systems is the need for increased capability of systems analysis techniques. Yet, the evolution of systems analysis techniques has lagged hardware evolution by almost one full generation.' (Couger, 1975, p. 45)

On the other hand, he points to techniques such as PSL/PSA (problem statement language/problem statement analyser) and ISDOS (information systems design and optimization system) using computerized translation of problem statements into programming languages as the future direction for systems development techniques. With the luxury of hindsight, it can be seen that these generations did not materialize as he expected. Couger provides a good illustration of the reshaping of history as progress in a specific direction and how predictions on this basis are extremely problematic.

Recent information system literature on requirements and development methodologies consider that there was a lack of systematic techniques for development before 1970. To take two examples:

In the premethodology era [prior to 1970], systems developers used a variety of techniques to help them develop computer-based information systems.... They [techniques] were typically passed on to other systems developers, often by word of mouth. These rules or techniques were typically not codified and sometimes not written down.... Systems development was considered a technical process to be undertaken by technical people. In this era, systems development was all art and no science.' (Hirschheim & Klein, 1992, pp. 296–297)

'Early applications of computers were implemented without the aid of an explicit information systems methodology.... Few programmers would follow any formal methodology, relying on rule of thumb and experience.' (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988, original emphasis)

## SOP (STUDY ORGANIZATION PLAN)

The study organization plan (SOP) is one of three examples of a second-generation systems analysis technique given by Couger. It was developed by IBM, introduced in 1962, went into widespread use by IBM, was ‘extremely successful’, and remained in use up to 1979, when it was replaced by the technique called business strategy planning (BSP) (Friedman, 1989, p. 262). SOP uses three levels of analysis to ‘describe a business system’: an overview, a systems view and a detail view.

An overview of the business provides a ‘broad understanding’ of the ‘critical areas’ where most money is spent or profit made. It is a narrative description that is structured in a specific way beginning with a history and framework, followed by the objectives of the company and an assessment vis-à-vis the competition. This information is obtained from management or owners, but ‘managers often express goals quite generally and not in the specific terms required for the study. A rather searching self-examination may therefore be necessary to produce adequate goal statements.’ (IBM, 1975, p. 100, emphases added). This is a structuring of the organization on paper as an inscription. There is a template of how a business should be represented that each organization must follow, and the active nature of this representation is significant when it comes to ensuring that detailed goals are described. Managers or documentation are useful informants, but their information may have to be examined.

The second and third views, the systems and detail view will be described and discussed together. Both views are described in terms of the systems life cycle. The first stage, study and design, has three phases: understanding the existing system; determining the ‘true requirements’ of the system; and designing and describing a new system ‘to fulfil the true requirements’. (IBM, 1975, p. 102). Interestingly, and echoing contemporary concerns, users of SOP are also warned about the dangers of just automating the existing system. The three phases are described as follows:

'In the first phase, the aim of the methods man, systems engineer — whoever is observing and analysing the system — is to understand what the business or system does, and to a degree how it is done, in terms of activities that thread through the business. In the second phase, the observer analyses what is logically necessary (or, alternatively, what is specified as necessary by the controlling management) to accomplish the goals of the specific activity under study. In the third phase, a system which can meet those requirements is designed and then described.' (IBM, 1975, p. 103)

This process is enabled by detailed documentation. The role of the documents is to ‘provide a clear, clean, formal, description of what is involved in a system or application.’ (IBM, 1975, p. 103). They ‘guide the observer in making a logical review of existing practices and procedures. Other forms define and display true system requirements....’ They also help in communication. ‘If the forms are filled out as prescribed, anyone familiar with this text can read them and quickly determine what the system does ...’ (IBM, 1975, p. 104). In short, they are ‘a language for describing systems.’ (IBM, 1975, p. 103).

The details of each activity are entered on an activity sheet that subdivides the activity into functions; describes the inputs, outputs and files used for each function; and represents these functions in a flow diagram of operations, their inputs, outputs and resources. In using the forms, the ‘observer’ is advised to ‘think in terms of activities rather than in terms of sets of machinery or blocks on a table of organization.’ (IBM, 1975, pp. 123–124). Activities are seen as having specific characteristics:

'The characteristics of an activity that are implicit in the use of the term throughout this text are that it is self-contained and goal directed; that is, an activity is a set of operations aimed at a single goal or small number of related goals, with only a few connections to any other activity.' (IBM, 1975, p. 124)

In terms of the quotations cited earlier, it is clear that SOP is the opposite of what is claimed as true of pre-1970 techniques. SOP represents the organization on paper as an inscription by means of a detailed organization of representation. As shown above, the organization is represented as a series of activities that are linked to goals but have limited interconnections. These activities are represented in terms of operations carried out on data, and the interconnections are only represented as movement of data items between activities. Although data and the activities that process them are the underlying representation of the organization, money, labour and time are also represented as resources that are used in activities. In this representation, SOP portrays the organization as a logical system of information flows. Logical in the sense that everything does or should operate to pursue the goals of the organization through the processing of information. But, to make it ‘logical’, work has to be done to find out what these goals are, to make them explicit, so that the activities that do take place can be assigned to them. However, it is recognized that the representation/description of the organization may not be logical. If that occurs then the next step for the ‘observer’/analyst is to determine the ‘true’ requirements, which involves grouping similar activities together and ensuring that the flows from operation to operation are performed most efficiently. Only then is it possible to move on to the development of a new system.

SOP creates a graphical and tabular inscription of the organization with interconnections that may be followed from one inscription to another. Using the techniques of SOP and taking the appropriate forms, an inscription can be created that represents the organization. It does this by constituting it as an organization. It assigns a narrative as a history; it ensures that clear and 'adequate' goals are assigned. The company is subdivided into an organization of discrete activities that consume resources that it calculates and assigns. The 'threads'/activities of the organization are observed and represented with the analyst warned to think in terms of 'activities' and not be misled by the organizational procedures to be found there. In this way, a series of steps, shaped by forms to be filled, guide the analyst to produce inscriptions that may be taken away. These inscriptions translate the organization into key elements and they enable others who do not have to visit the organization to see what needs to be done — to calculate plans of action. When SOP was used it was a technique that enabled expertise to be deployed in a systematic and replicable form which would act to provide legitimate knowledge of the organization represented in terms of goals, activities and information flows.

In summary, this account of the SOP method shows that it was not an ad hoc approach. It was systematic, detailed and must have acted as a resource to produce an ordered set of inscriptions on paper that then could be taken away and provide the basis for redesigning the organization. The SOP method acted as an inscription device ordering the representation of the organization while claiming to simply represent it. Thus, SOP possessed the characteristics that, as argued earlier, are common to requirements techniques. In the next section the paper considers a modern technique, object-oriented analysis, to assess how it may act as an inscription device that provides legitimate knowledge of the organization.

## Object-oriented (OO) analysis

'The techniques that are so natural to human thinking should be applied to systems thinking. Readers of this book will not be learning an arcane and difficult formalism but rather learning to analyse the world in a way that seems natural to human thinking.... The object-oriented viewpoint is a major paradigm shift, requiring system developers to think about the world in a different way.' (Martin, 1993, pp. 3 and 13)

Object-oriented analysis is a group of techniques that have been created over the last 10 years (Shlaer & Mellor, 1988; Coad & Yourdon, 1991; Martin, 1993; Booch, 1994). Before their development, the most important methods of requirements analysis, both in theory and in practice, were those of structured analysis (Friedman, 1989). Although the use of object-oriented analysis may still be restricted to a minority of developments, many of the major protagonists of structured methods view object-oriented analysis as a set of techniques that should be used in the future for systems development (Coad & Yourdon, 1991; Martin, 1993). Martin speaks of a ‘paradigm shift’ in developers’ thinking of the world. Most widely used methodologies of systems development have been amended to incorporate object-oriented methods (Coad & Yourdon, 1991; Martin, 1993).

The development of object-oriented analysis took place after object-oriented design and object-oriented programming had been established (see Booch, 1994). Booch describes this move of object orientation from programming to analysis as a simple application of techniques from one area to another. It can be seen as the redefinition of organizations using concepts and notations developed for programming. Interestingly, one of the reasons put forward for the use of this notation is that it accords with the ways in which people act. As Martin (1993, p. 31) describes it:

'The OO [object-oriented] way of thinking is more natural for most people. [a]fter all, the world consists of objects. We start to learn about them in infancy and discover that they have certain types of behaviour. If we shake a rattle, it makes a noise. From an early age we categorise objects as we discover their behaviour. End users and business people think naturally in terms of objects, events and triggers.'

Despite object-oriented techniques originating as programming developments in an area that most would consider as the domain of computer science, Martin asserts that an object-oriented technique is in fact the most natural way of analysing organizations. Not only does it accord with how people actually view the world, but the concepts of triggers, events and objects are, as it were, intuitively obvious. As argued earlier, such a claim enables the organization to be analysed in a way that facilitates its translation into a computer-mediated formulation. Martin (1993, p. 46) makes these moves clear:

'This fundamentally different idea [object oriented analysis] about software and systems will enable us to build better systems and, particularly important, improve communication with end users. OO thinking will enable us to achieve more powerful automation of highly complex enterprises.'

There are even examples in which organizations are used as a metaphor for the object-oriented approach. Take, for example, Love's (1993, p. 26) description:

'Just as modern corporations are structured into departments, each with its own departmental files and procedures, it makes sense to structure software in the same manner. This is the essence of an “object”: the intimate linking of data with the procedures used to operate on that data.'

Many information systems academics view the object-oriented approach as facilitating a ‘dramatic change in the way we [developers] do systems analysis’ (Mathiassen, 1995). They see this approach as having two major advantages. First, it moves away from a top-down decomposition approach of structured analysis techniques, and, second, it enables partial solutions to problems to be developed with software components being added incrementally. Arguments such as Mathiassen’s show the great reliance being placed on notation and technique. Mathiassen suggests that the adoption of a new technique will, in itself, lead to a change in systems analysis. This form of argument reiterates the importance placed on notations as ways of representing organizations, with information systems academics proposing that a new notation leads to a reconceptualization of the organization from a top-down system to one of components that may be added to as necessary.

Despite appeals to the naturalness of object-oriented techniques, tension remains between structured techniques and object-oriented ones. Booch, for example, is at pains to stress the dangers of structured analysis to developers using object-oriented techniques '[w]hen the developer is unable to resist the urge of falling back into the abyss of the structured design mindset . . . polluting the design with preconceived algorithmic notions' (Booch, 1994, p. 161). It appears that those attempting to produce new techniques are aware of the importance of techniques in shaping practice. Techniques are not seen as neutral carriers of information but, using arguments that resonate with those of Latour (1990), are seen to influence the ways in which developers actually perceive the world that they analyse.

## Object-oriented notation

What about the object-oriented notation itself? An immediate observation is that object-oriented techniques do not share one notation, although there are moves to try and combine various notations into one (see Martin, 1993, pp. 375–403). There are five underlying ideas to object-oriented analysis: objects, methods, encapsulation, requests and inheritance (see Martin, 1993, pp. 17–28).

Various notations are in use to represent objects. As an example, one notation will be examined that was developed by Martin (1993) and is used in the information engineering methodology. Figures 3 and 4 give two examples of this notation being used by Martin: the notation that describes objects and their relationships and the notation used to represent events.

The Martin notation is typical in that it is predominantly a graphical notation and uses more than one type of diagram to represent the detail of objects and the functions they perform. Booch (1994, p. 172) drawing on Kleyn and Gingrich (1988) uses the analogy of televising a football match. Each diagram is like a different camera and camera angle; all these cameras are needed ‘to provide an understanding of the action taking place.’ Implicit in this analogy is the assumption that different diagrams are passively representing different aspects of a bounded though changing objective reality. The importance of this assumption is emphasized in Booch’s characterization of the role of diagrams/models:

'Collectively. different models are semantically rich: they are expressive enough to allow a developer to capture all of the interesting strategic and tactical decisions one must make during the analysis of a system . . . , and they are complete enough to serve as blueprints for implementation in almost any object-oriented programming language.' Here the assumptions of an objective domain are expressed in the capturing of requirements and the necessity for this process to be complete. Completeness is not simply taken as gathering all requirements but is construed as the ability to use these captured requirements in a number of different programming implementations. This definition gives an indication of the importance placed on requirements in providing the enabling conditions for programming.

Martin uses two types of diagram for analysis. The first are object structure diagrams that show objects and the relationships between objects (Figure 3), and the second are event diagrams (Figure 4). The notation and some of the approaches used to ‘recognize’ items in the notation for each diagram will be looked at in turn.

In the object structure diagram, defining objects is seen as an exercise of choice dependent on the intention of those classifying. Martin (1993, p. 69) puts it like this:

'Since Aristotle, mankind has tried to categorize the objects in its world. Now, systems analysts in an enterprise categorise those objects relevant to the software of that enterprise.'

For Martin, the decision is which objects to choose and not what is an object. Booch (1994, pp. 146–155) is more circumspect, arguing that several approaches may be used. In terms of notation, Martin uses rectangles to represent objects, whereas Booch uses cloud shapes.

Differences in approach to the analysis of objects between Booch and Martin exemplifies the tension between prescription and practice. Martin appears to be concerned with the setting of standards on notation. In doing so, he treats the issue of identifying objects not as a trivial issue but as a rational exercise of choice on the part of the analyst. Booch is more cautious and is concerned with the problems of practice as well as issues of notation. In terms of notation, he opts for a symbolically less well-defined icon for objects, and in his discussion of practice he focuses on presenting approaches that he suggests have worked. Although raising issues of the construction of object representations, Booch remains wedded to the concept of notation as a neutral vehicle that aids ‘creative thinking’ and does not admit that different approaches may lead to the construction of different objects. The outcome of both Martin’s and Booch’s approaches is to create a notation that can become mobile. In Callon’s (1991) words, once it becomes a notation, it leaves its history behind and can circulate between developers and others engaged in systems development.

In the object structure diagram, the relationships between objects are assumed to be capable of mathematical representation and are represented as a straight line. For example, the relationship between the object customer and the object order is one of the customer placing the order. Such a relationship is between one customer and one or more orders, but there will be many customers and many orders. This is expressed as cardinality constraints and is represented as ‘crows feet’. A series of other relationships, each of which is a mathematical property, can be similarly represented diagrammatically.

A completed object structure diagram shows a tidy interrelationship of discrete objects whose interrelationships exemplify formal properties. By focusing on certain relations, they have the converse effect of relegating the importance of other relations. To take an example (see Figure 3), the intricacies of relationships between a student and a lecturer are translated into a relationship that both share with a class and with their membership of the class of objects that are persons. Put differently, the relationships that are represented emphasize a mechanistic and logical conception of the activities in this particular area which characterize that area and demarcate it from others. Mechanistic in the sense that the variety of relations and their characteristics are knowable and logical because these relations can be reduced to a set of logical statements.

## Representing change

Turning to the second diagram (Figure 4), the event diagram, the notation represents the ‘dynamic’ behaviour of an object model. In this example, the model represents the process of organizing a class and shows the detailed events that may take place when an individual student registers for a class. Change is represented as discrete events (shown as shaped rectangles) in which each event has a specific structure. Martin proposes that an event is begun when a triggering condition occurs and certain preconditions are met. Depending on this combination of preconditions the outcome of the event is determined and certain post conditions will be met. This is graphically represented as a trigger rule and control conditions (a line between events and a diamond shape). For example, in Figure 4, the event of registering a specific student can only occur if two preconditions are met — the student's application is accepted and the class is not full. Martin (1993, p. 135) argues that ‘rules are encapsulated business knowledge’. Only the presence of a rule, its trigger and preconditions can be represented graphically, the rule itself is stored as ‘more formal’ English. The following are two examples of integrity rules that Martin (1993, p. 138) suggests as business knowledge:

IT MUST ALWAYS HOLD THAT

IF an employee has a marked parking slot

THEN this employee has a company car and is a manager with a salary greater than 60 000.

AFTER modify of salary of an Employee.E IT MUST HOLD THAT

The new salary of Employee.E is greater than the old salary of an Employee.E.

The notation of rule-based behaviour leads to the formulation of business ‘rules’ of this kind and the representation of the organization working according to rules. The basis of such an argument has come under attack from many quarters; the following are three examples. Collins (1987), applying Wittgenstein’s (1953) argument, showed that the application of rules depends on the context of their use which, if an attempt is made to exhaustively codify these as rules, leads to an infinite regress. Second, the difficulty of obtaining rules in the first place is well known. Experts have difficulty in articulating their expertise in terms of rules and multiple experts are often found to disagree on the rule base (Wellbank, 1983 Macintosh, 1985). Third, the issue of whether behaviour in organizations is rule based has not gained many adherents (Morgan, 1997). Despite these issues, this rule-based notation expresses organization in terms of rules, with the expectation that these rules are there to be found and then inscribed.

For the analyst and others the event diagram makes visible the ‘behaviour’ of the organization, but it is also defining organizational behaviour. It is assumed that behaviours must be discrete; they must be recognizable; they must be rule based, and so on. Activities that cannot be represented in this way do not exist in terms of the analysis of the organization. The move here is not just of making behaviour visible in the organization but of legislating what behaviour is and then making it visible.

Both Martin and Booch advocate entering these diagrams into CASE tools. For Martin this enables the creation of a repository that is a ‘vast amount of knowledge about the enterprise’ which can be cross-checked by software and whose objects can be reused in the development of other applications (Martin, 1993, p. 55). The move here is from a ‘paper world’ of organization to a hypertext world in which inscriptions become more stable, more mobile and more easily combined. The detailed representations and maintenance of the relationships between different forms of documentation could only be done if CASE tools can be used to automate much of the cross-checking necessary to keep all aspects of the models in agreement. Using a CASE tool can also enable models to be executed so that examples of processes such as registering a student can be followed through the model. This needs data on examples of each object to be held. Thus, for instance, the object lecturer must have data of several lecturers stored as instances of that object and so on. The execution of specifications held in CASE tools is a move towards the increased combinability of inscriptions. Not only can objects, rules, and relationships be represented on the same diagram but they can interact one with another to produce specific outcomes.

To summarize, the Martin object-oriented notation is an inscription device that produces a representation, on paper or in a case tool, that is tidy, clear-cut and logical. It represents the organization as a series of objects that interact in predefined ways, and it makes the organization visible as a specific formulation of rules, objects, events. In doing this, the notation creates a representation in which a legitimate organization is equated with the ability to be represented in these terms. Conversely, those aspects of the organization that are not amenable to this form of representation are ignored or regarded as pathological such as the influence of ‘political or historical’ factors (Booch, 1994, p. 161 footnote).

One of the features that Martin (1993, p. 62) considers as important in the object-oriented approach is the use of 'one consistent model' in the stages of analysis, design and programming of an information system. This approach is aided by its origins in programming, which eases the movement of inscriptions as analysis products for programming. However, representing organizations as object-oriented systems does not resolve the initial problem of translation into the object-oriented notation nor does it remove the problem of a difference between prescription and how the notation is subsequently deployed.

## SOP AND OBJECT-ORIENTED ANALYSIS APPROACHES AS INSCRIPTION DEVICES

Arguably both SOP and object-oriented analysis techniques share many features as inscription devices, although they do contain important differences that illuminate how requirements techniques have changed. Both SOP and object-oriented approaches seek to define the organization in a comprehensive notation that is developed and mediated by analysts. Both approaches make the organization visible in an inscription that can be manipulated and redesigned as is thought necessary. This visibility is obtained by translating the organizational context into inscriptions: paper and virtual worlds that are capable of manipulation. For both approaches, the techniques provide a legitimate language in which discussion on requirements will be formulated. This is useful because they create a vocabulary for requirements to be expressed, but each language of representation also shapes what is represented and excluded. In these examples, both approaches focus on, and seek to represent, a logical pattern of activities and information flows and, as a consequence, discussions on requirements are predicated on those terms. They exclude from their representation other areas of organizational activity, notably the less tangible political issues. Thus, both approaches have a role in constructing what is regarded as legitimate knowledge of the organization and this legitimacy accrues, at least in part, from the claims that both approaches provide a realistic portrayal of the organization. In short, both SOP and object-oriented analysis approaches share features that, as argued earlier, are common to most requirements techniques: their ability to translate the complexity of organizational life into an inscription on paper or on computer that may be manipulated to redesign the organization and to provide legitimate knowledge about what constitutes the organization.

Nonetheless, there are differences between both approaches that can be recognized in two aspects. Firstly, although SOP shares with object-oriented analysis the characteristic of representing the organization as activities and information flows, object-oriented analysis has a more detailed set of representations. Obviously, this can give object-oriented analysis a more fine-grained representation of that aspect of the organization. This is an advance on SOP. Secondly, object-oriented analysis is much more closely linked to programming than SOP, which assumes that programming is a relatively straightforward process. The experiences of the 1960s (see Naur & Randell, 1969; Wirth, 1974; Brooks, 1975) radically changed that perception. In contrast to SOP, object-oriented analysis produces inscriptions that are combinable, easier to manipulate, and very closely related to programming concepts of object-oriented programming. It is here that the systems development process has been simplified for systems developers because the inscriptions produced by object-oriented analysis need minimal translation to be reused in programming. In contrast to the assertions made by the protagonists of object-oriented analysis, progress has been made in linking analysis with programming and not in the depiction of the 'real' world.

To summarize, the evidence from SOP and object-oriented analysis shows considerable similarities between both approaches while also exhibiting marked changes in certain key aspects. Viewing requirements techniques as inscription devices that translate and produce mobile, combinable and durable representations of the organization enables these similarities (and differences) to be recognized. The consequences of such arguments are not restricted to historical or theoretical analysis as the issues of knowledge and legitimation remain central to those engaged in systems development and are likely to be closely linked to subsequent success or failure of the implemented information system.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR THOSE ENGAGED IN SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT

The arguments that are central to this paper — the problematic nature of requirements; the role of expertise to legitimate representation; the questions raised when viewing requirements as inscriptions — have very practical consequences. In requirements analysis, be it for software development or software package acquisition, a political process is being engaged in where the issue of convincing those involved of the validity of the resulting requirements is paramount. For all those who are engaged in the requirements process, some pertinent questions regarding requirements can be offered. These are important for managers, developers and users in helping to investigate the limitations of any requirements analysis technique being used. Although they cannot be a panacea, at least simple questions may lead to a more informed application of techniques and perhaps more representative requirements and subsequently a ‘better’ information system.

Three categories in requirements analysis need questioning: the nature of the inscription technique, the process of inscribing, and the subsequent use of the resulting inscriptions (see Table 1). For each category, questions are given that seek to explore issues important to requirements analysis. Most of the questions are self-evident and should give insight into the strengths, weaknesses and thoroughness of the requirements analysis process.

The questions in the inscription category seek to make visible the translation that is made in requirements from the complexity of organizational life to an inscription. This is done by exploring the limitations of the requirements technique: its partiality, its lack of completeness, its temporal and spatial boundaries. For each requirements technique and each site of application, the answers obtained will be different. For instance, an object-oriented technique will not represent people, skills or experience. It will not represent the political milieu of the application site. Its spatial boundaries will vary, but it will be restricted to one point in time. This simple questioning should help developers, managers and users to assess the worth of the object-oriented inscription and to seek to improve the representation of the organization if needs be.

The process of inscription is equally important and is usually not questioned because the legitimacy of the technique and those using it is assumed. The questions here are aimed at exploring the richness of the process by questioning the experience of the analysts, the amount of time spent on initial collection of data, the means by which the resulting inscription is to be checked, and what claims those using the technique are making. For developers, managers and users alike, it is useful to see how the inscription is produced as once agreed, the success of the future system is highly dependent on it.

The third category of questions examines the subsequent use of the inscription produced by the requirements technique. These questions can go to the heart of the political process of representation and different groups involved in the development process may well have widely diverging views at this point (if not before!). The questioning of the destination of the inscription highlights who is going to reorganize the inscription and thus the organization, and who has input into that process. The next question on the motivation for inscription may be controversial as most information systems development is driven by a variety of motivations. For example, if one motivation is cost reduction through reductions in the workforce, then the workforce may be able to clearly recognize this at an early stage and perhaps this process may be better managed though workforce consultation and involvement. The final question on the influence that managers and potential users have over the subsequent development is also important. Most researchers would agree that involvement is important, but many developments do not embody this principle. Once again, it may be that more and early attention to these difficult questions that illuminate issues to do with the legitimacy and the political nature of systems development may lead to the necessary compromises being reached earlier. In this way, early agreement on requirements and the process of requirements representation may lead to more stable subsequent information systems.

## CONCLUSION

In recent years much academic effort has been expended in classifying, and arguing for, a diversity of systems development methodologies in general, and requirements analysis techniques in particular (Couger, 1982; Olle et al., 1982; Byrd et al., 1992; Hirschheim et al., 1995). Although some of this work can be argued to simply pave the way for the introduction of yet another technique based on the identification of the failings of prior approaches (see Couger, 1982), much of the work has established an important basis for the consideration of approaches other than those labelled as functionalist (see Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). However, the dominant theme has been the creation, modification or identification of requirements techniques to aid the systems development process (Flynn, 1992; Hirschheim & Klein, 1994). This is important, but it neglects the crucial questions about how techniques are used in practice and whether best practice in systems development can simply be encoded in requirements techniques.

This paper seeks to address these approaches to the research on requirements techniques by drawing on the sociology of knowledge to show a different way of analysing these techniques. The aim has been to show the similarity among techniques by analysing them as inscription devices that translate issues from a problematic organizational environment into written inscriptions on paper or computer. In other words, requirements techniques are important resources in producing legitimate knowledge about the organization. This knowledge, encoded as inscriptions, decontextualize ‘requirements’ and make them capable of manipulation through analysis and design for subsequent implementation of a new information system. Two requirements techniques, SOP and object-oriented analysis, separated by some 30 years, were analysed to show their many similarities (and some differences) as inscription devices. Such an analysis questions the frameworks of diversity of requirements techniques, particularly those relating to generation of techniques. More practically, it identifies that the practices surrounding the implementation of a technique are of particular importance for the ‘success’ or otherwise of the subsequent information system. To help those engaged in systems development, particularly users, to assess and contribute to the process of requirements analysis, this paper identifies a series of pertinent questions that should be asked. Hopefully, these questions will shed more light on the processes surrounding the use of a requirements technique rather than just focusing on the technique itself in isolation from the context of its application.

A consequence of these arguments is that anyone who uses techniques in requirements analysis should be aware of what these techniques provide: a mechanism to decontextualize situations and to create homogeneous representations of these contexts. From the construction of this knowledge, power relations are exercised. Developers are able to define requirements, managers are able to manage a project, and users' 'needs' are expressed: all using the medium of a requirements technique as a resource. Critical questioning of requirements techniques and their ability to translate and inscribe organizational situations is needed. Given the focus on techniques as the mechanism for producing legitimate knowledge about organizations, it is not surprising that requirements analysis techniques are seen as so significant in the literature or that so many information systems developments subsequently fail.
