# An exploratory study of the value of pretraining end-user participation

Conrad Shayo, Lorne Olfman\* & Ricardo Teitelroit\*

Information and Decision Sciences, California State University, San Bernardino, USA, email: cshayo@wiley.csusb.edu and \*Information Science Department, Claremont Graduate University, Claremont, CA 91711, USA, email: Lorne.Olfman@cgu.edu and ricardot@traderjoescompany.com

Abstract. Although software training is widely seen as an essential contributor to the productive use of computers in organizations, research has not focused on identifying the processes through which software trainers assess end users' training requirements. This research draws from self-regulation theories to examine the impacts of pretraining end-user participation on the outcomes of software training. Self-regulation theories aim to explain why individuals behave in a certain manner in an organizational context. Pretraining end-user participation consists of a pretraining interview designed to learn the needs of the trainee, and to gain insights into a training design that can make learning the target software more relevant to the trainee. The context of the study was a training programme for a database retrieval software package in a medium-sized manufacturing company. Approximately half the trainees were involved in the pretraining interview process. Trainees also received one of two training approaches, either applications-based (relevant problem focused) or construct-based (generic training approach) training. Data were gathered from questionnaires, quizzes and interviews. Although no clear differences were found, the data indicate that pretraining end-user participation should be considered a potentially effective tool in enhancing training effectiveness. Pretraining end-user participation is more likely to suggest who will or will not use a software application after formal training, provide a more realistic view of the content and usefulness of the training, and facilitate motivation planning and management. The paper discusses some potential critical success factors for making pretraining end-user participation more effective.

Keywords: End-user training, end-user computing, involvement

## INTRODUCTION

Software trainers' efforts may be more effective if they understand their trainees' requirements before the design of training materials, selection of training methods and actual training (Panko, 1988). In systems development, it is essential to ‘get the requirements right’ before system design, selection of system development tool and actual development, because system failure or high maintenance costs have been directly attributed to poor requirements definition. Participation of end users in the development process before the system is designed is a key feature of getting the requirements right. Yet, few trainers have their trainees participate in the process of designing and developing their educational materials. They infer the needs of their trainees just as systems developers once attempted to infer the needs of their users. They ‘do it to them’ or ‘for them’ rather than ‘do it with them’ in partnership (Dooley, 1989).

One of the least-studied elements in end-user software training research has been the relationship between pretraining activities, training methods and later use of the software. Sein et al. (1987) showed that software training must encompass activities that take place before and after as well as during training. Researchers have essentially focused on the design and impact of training methods on learning and attitudes measured at the end of the training. By focusing attention on the impact of training sessions alone, researchers have rarely explored the design and impact of pre- and post-software training activities.

The purpose of this paper is to explore the value of pretraining end-user participation and training methods on software training outcomes. Pretraining end-user participation consists of a pretraining interview designed to actively learn the needs of each potential participant and gain insights into training design that can aid each trainee. An intensive research study using four cases in the form of four different training approaches was conducted in a field setting to assess their value with respect to the contributions of a pretraining end-user participation process. The paper discusses the theoretical framework for training evaluation, the case studies and the findings of the studies.

## BACKGROUND AND RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Compeau et al. (1995) present a framework for the training and learning process that includes three phases: initiation, training and learning, and post-training. They note that '[o]ne of the most perplexing questions for managers during this [the initiation] phase is the determination of training needs' (p. 24). Nelson et al. (1995) introduce the content-levels framework (Ostroff & Ford, 1989) for training needs assessment, and report a case study of the United States Internal Revenue Service's use of the framework. Their case study demonstrates the advantages of planning for training so that it can benefit individuals, subunits and the organization. Training needs were motivated by the nature of a particular systems development project, and training was designed to focus on different tasks and individual needs, and was aimed at achieving overall organizational goals. The training plan was achieved through self-paced modules that could be skipped if a person already had specific skills addressed by certain modules.

The training framework and plan outlined by Nelson et al. (1995) appears to be an appropriate approach to the initiation phase in cases where the software system is designed to address a specific task or specific sets of tasks. In the above-noted case, it was a system to enable tax agents to identify under-reporting of income. However, in a situation where the software system is a generic tool, such as a spreadsheet or a database query package, the breadth of tasks to be supported makes it more difficult to design a comprehensive and specific modular training plan that will lead to successful outcomes such as usage after training.

## Training outcomes

The goal of training is to produce motivated, knowledgeable users who will subsequently use their software skills on the job. Ultimately, the key outcome should be the actual use of software to achieve productive outcomes for employees. However, trainers (and researchers) also want to determine whether trainees are motivated, have appropriate knowledge, and are satisfied right after training. It is expected that positive outcomes right after training will lead to future usage, although this is not always the case. (The value of pretraining participation is evaluated mainly from the point of view of the information systems organization.) In the study reported here, the key outcome used to assess the value of pretraining end-user participation was measured by whether a trainee actually used the software after training. Measures of basic skills and motivation were also made during and right after training to determine whether the training sessions achieved their goals, and whether later usage could have been a consequence of training session outcomes rather than pretraining end-user participation.

A few studies have measured later usage as an outcome of training on generic software tools. Only about one-half of the trainees from these studies used the software after training, and usage ranged from infrequent to relatively high (e.g. Olfman & Bostrom, 1991; Olfman & Mandviwalla, 1994). These findings indicate that training resources are being expended to educate future non-users. Moreover, non-users are creating a cost burden because they are paying for training they do not use, and possibly denying potential users an opportunity to receive training (Casner-Lotto & Associates, 1988).

## Research questions

The impact of end-user trainee participation in the initiation and design of formal software training programmes on learning outcomes needs to be studied. Such study should explore the extent or impact of participation on learning outcomes and eventual system use. The appropriateness of end-user participation will depend on the type of software being learned. For certain software applications, user participation may be more critical. Shayo & Olfman (1993) noted that end-user trainee participation at the training initiation and design stage might be more appropriate for complex and unstructured generic tasks. A similar requirement is suggested in the systems development literature when the systems being designed will be used to perform complex or unstructured tasks (McKeen & Guimares, 1997).

Shayo and Olfman (1993) further examined why trainees who learned a generic software package did or did not use the software after attending training. They found that trainees' later usage was related to the extent to which their preconceived needs (goal sets) were matched to the content of the training. Examples of goal sets are: desire to learn the basics, learn tricks and shortcuts, and assess the usefulness of the software to the job context. In some cases, goal sets include multiple goals. Because trainees' goal sets on entering training vary widely (Carroll,

1990), they cannot be easily accommodated by a training curriculum, even one that includes a modular training plan.

Shayo and Olfman (1993) also found that trainees whose needs were not fulfilled during training either did not use the software or had to get additional training before they could use it. Trainees whose needs were fulfilled by the initial training were likely to use the software as long as they also had available resources. Such trainees were more likely to proactively press for appropriate computing resources. They also concluded that matching a trainee's goals through training design can effectively increase motivation to use the software and, therefore, usage.

Self-regulation theories explain that performance will be more effective if specific goals precede it, and that behaviour will be more likely if motivation (as measured by intentions) is higher (Locke & Latham, 1991). Software training designers must therefore match potential end-user trainee goals with the training goals.

One way to achieve goal matching is through needs assessment. However, as argued above, the type of needs assessment may depend on the type of software to be learned. Applications that are oriented to specific tasks are different than those that are generic. Needs assessment for generic software is likely to require more specialized information from users because the generic software will support a wide variety of tasks. Shayo and Olfman (1994) make a case for approaching needs assessment for generic software training in a manner similar to user requirements analysis. They suggest that end-user trainees' participation before designing training can provide a framework for setting achievable goals that will enhance training outcomes, including later usage. This differs from traditional needs assessment where the process aims to prepare a list of essential tasks, carry out task analysis to identify common training needs, and set training objectives (Nelson et al., 1995). Pretraining assessment for generic software requires direct participation of the future trainees because the range of tasks to be supported is very broad. This leads to the first research question:

RQ1: Can the direct participation of the future trainee end users in training design for a generic software package enhance software training outcomes?

Theoretically, pretraining participation should enhance training outcomes. Bostrom et al. (1990) framework for researching end-user training specifies three factors that influence training outcomes:

● the software to be learned,

● the individual characteristics of the trainees,

● and the approaches used to do the training.

Pretraining participation is an approach that can influence outcomes directly through the development of the trainee's mental model of the software or through an interaction with individual characteristics in the process of mental model development. For example, end-user participation in training design can interact with individual characteristics, such as self-efficacy expectation computer knowledge to allow the trainee to develop a point of reference to embark on using the software. In addition, it can directly influence outcomes by managing a trainee's expectations about the scope of the training. Self-efficacy expectation is defined as the belief that a person holds about his or her ability to perform specific behaviour (or a set of behaviours) (Bandura, 1991).

Pretraining participation constitutes one part of a training approach. Another part relates to the training strategy. Olfman and Bostrom (1991) define two training strategies for generic software: applications-based and construct-based. The applications-based strategy provides a more relevant task-focused training experience. The goal is to motivate trainees by emphasizing how the software can solve their individual business problems at the expense of emphasizing conceptual and procedural knowledge about the software package. The construct-based strategy reverses the roles by presenting software concepts in the context of generic problems and is typical of commercial software training systems.

It could be expected that if the initiation phase of software training for a generic tool (e.g. a spreadsheet package) includes participation of the trainees, one aspect of this process would be to identify tasks that trainees could work on during training. Yet, if the training and learning phase includes a construct-based strategy, this might reduce the effectiveness of the pre-training participation. Because construct-based training does not address specific trainee problems, there may be a loss of motivation that was initially generated by identifying these problems before the training process. This possibility leads to the second research question:

RQ2: How do the effects of pretraining end-user participation hold across different software training strategies?

It should be noted that a corollary of using pretraining participation as a method of needs assessment is that it may help trainers determine which strategy and other aspects of a training approach should be applied to produce the most effective training design.

It is common knowledge that a well-designed approach to generic software training that accounts for user skills and abilities can still fail. If employees are unable to acquire appropriate resources, or if their supervisors do not support them, or if post-training support is inadequate, then actual use of the generic software package is unlikely. Ideally, the needs assessment process should address these issues. However, it is realistic to assume that the needs assessment process may not produce this result. Argyris (1993) points out that espoused theories are different from theories-in-action. In other words, trainees and supervisors may report that resources will be available to ensure post-training success, but, in reality, these resources do not materialize during the post-training phase.

## RESEARCH METHOD

An intensive research approach was used to gather qualitative and quantitative data in a field setting. The intensive research method was chosen for this study because of the lack of previous research on pretraining end-user participation in software training and because of the limited number of trainees available for analysis. Lack of previous research led to the design of a study to examine how pretraining end-user participation might work in relation to traditional training approaches, and on how to conduct the pretraining interviews. Markus (1992) defines intensive research methods ‘as research strategies that focus on a few instances of a particular unit of analysis in depth rather than many instances more selectively’ (p. 25).

The research design was a type 4 case study (Yin, 1989). There were four embedded cases, with multiple individuals in each case as the units of analysis, all in one subject organization. The researchers formed the four embedded cases in order to facilitate the study of the various approaches to training. Thus, although the cases studied did not occur naturally, the manipulation did not introduce unreality in that it simply produced alternative ways in which normal training was carried out in the organization. The cases were four types of software training approaches:

1 pretraining end-user participation followed by applications-based training,

2 pretraining end-user participation followed by construct-based training,

3 applications-based training without pretraining end-user participation, and

4 construct-based training without pretraining end-user participation.

In applications-based training, the emphasis was on how the trainees could carry out some of their job-related tasks using the software. In construct-based training, emphasis was on general conceptual and procedural information. The aim of the study was to detect patterns among and between the various cases according to the research questions stated above.

The researchers were also charged with obtaining specific outcomes for the subject organization, namely to ensure that trainees were taught how to use the target software called CSRET, and to design training materials for future CSRET training sessions. (CSRET is a pseudonym for a client-server software package. All references to actual software packages and the company involved in this project have been disguised.) The researchers realized that this was an opportunistic case study (Yin, 1989), and that a limited number of trainees could be studied, but felt strongly that the drawbacks did not outweigh the possibilities of gaining new information on a research topic that had not been explored empirically. Moreover, the intensive research methodology was suitable because the implementation of the training was going to take place over time, it involved multiple actors, and could be influenced by unexpected events in the field setting (Benbasat et al., 1987).

## Site and subjects

The study was conducted at the business offices of a medium-sized manufacturing company. The MIS director felt it was time to move organizational database query capabilities directly into the hands of the end users. At that time, users were mostly dependent on MIS to run queries and reports on the organization-wide database. A few users were able to use an existing command level batch-oriented software package to create their own queries and reports. To move to a user-oriented GUI retrieval package, the company purchased and installed a Windows-based client-server data retrieval package, 'CSRET'. CSRET queries from the database into Microsoft Excel, a spreadsheet application. A team consisting of the researchers who conducted this study was hired to carry out this project. The study was aimed at an initial group of users who were given extensive training in the use of CSRET. This training programme was part of a larger project that also involved the design, development and installation of a Windows-based data dictionary. The data dictionary was built to aid users in translating the terse data names used on the server machine because CSRET is only a retrieval package and does not support a data dictionary. The server database is very extensive and the initial project only implemented data dictionary entries for a portion of the organization's databases.

Twenty-four trainees participated in the study (see Table 1). A total of 20 trainees went through both phases of the training. Three trainees completed phase one but did not attend the second session, whereas a fourth trainee attended some of each phase. Four MIS staff attended the training in order to prepare to become future trainers. They were not included in the study.

Employees were recruited for training by soliciting volunteers from the end-user community. In some cases supervisors attended the training; in a few cases they sent one of their subordinates. MIS charged a \$200 fee per person for the training. This fee was used to offset the rental of laptop computers to furnish a makeshift training room, and for food and refreshments. Trainees were randomly assigned to one of the four training approaches.

## General procedures

Each trainee completed an extensive pretraining packet that asked about computing background, reasons for taking training, attitudes toward using the software, self-efficacy expectation, and demographics. The self-efficacy expectation questionnaire asks the trainee to indicate confidence in using the software. There are 10 items in the questionnaire and the scores range from 0 to 10 for each item. The overall score can range from 0 to 100 (Compeau & Higgins, 1995). They also completed a functional job analysis (FJA) questionnaire, which identified their highest and predominant data activity levels (see Appendix A for a description of FJA). After each training session, a number of measures about knowledge and motivation were taken. These measures were used to assess the quality of the training but were also helpful in interpreting the results in terms of trainees' subsequent usage of the software on the job, which was the primary outcome measure for assessing the value of pretraining end-user participation. Moreover, each trainee had a post-training debriefing 6 weeks after the training.

The decision to use face-to-face interviews rather than a questionnaire was based on a number of factors. Foremost was the fact that pretraining participation for a generic software package seemed to be an unstructured process. It did not appear to lend itself to a simple questionnaire. Moreover, it was felt that if there was a possibility of a goal mismatch (e.g. a trainee expected to learn general principles about database systems rather than how to use a query tool), it was best to provide instant feedback, something that would not be possible with a questionnaire. Another reason for using face-to-face interviews was that there was no clear indication from experience or from the literature about the appropriate contents of the questionnaire. Thus, the interview protocol contained some potentially redundant questions. It also contained ‘ice-breaking’ questions that were not directly relevant to the needs assessment.

FJA provided important information on the design and content of both the initial and future CSRET training sessions. The output from a pretraining interview was a list of practice problems that the trainee would bring to the training session, and a surfacing of the trainee's perceptions of how the software could enhance his or her job. Trainees who expected to get something out of the training that they would not actually get were told that this would not occur. This approach created a perceived understanding of what could be reasonably expected from the training. Trainees were also told to consider whether they had the appropriate hardware and software resources to use the software after training. This was considered important because the training was delivered in two phases with a 1-week interval during which the trainees had to practise using CSRET on the job.

## Training environment and process

All training sessions were conducted in a makeshift training centre in a boardroom of the business offices, although software training was typically carried out off-site using outside vendors or in another of the company's facilities. In this case, because part of the training involved learning how to access local on-line databases, a local training facility was required. Each trainee had an individual workstation (a portable PC) with a network connection. The trainer had an overhead projector to provide demonstrations. Attendance at each session ranged from three to six persons. However, two persons who could not make the initial training sessions received one-to-one training. Trainees' evaluations of the training sessions were similar, regardless of size. All but one trainee gave very good or excellent ratings of the training, and the two who received one-to-one training gave the highest overall ratings.

## General activities

Training was carried out in two phases with a 1-week interval in between so that trainees could practise using the software on the job. Both applications-based and construct-based groups received detailed training manuals and exercise booklets. Training manuals (one for applications-based and the other for construct-based) were designed around a training manual used by a software training provider who previously had trained employees of the subject organization to use Microsoft Excel. It was deemed by the researchers to have good properties as defined in the software training literature (e.g. Wright, 1988). Trainees completed a quiz and a self-efficacy questionnaire before and after each training session. They completed attitude and intention questionnaires after the last session. They also completed a list of usage goals at the end of the second session.

The first training phase covered basic essentials about the software. These included building simple queries, and downloading queries into a microcomputer spreadsheet application, specifying and selecting fields and records, and sorting and printing queries. The second phase covered more advanced topics, such as joining tables, setting a timer, editing results, and ordering records.

## Training strategies

In applications-based training (ABT), during the morning, the instructor gave the trainees three demonstrations on how to solve specific work-related problems. In the afternoon, trainees were given time to practise solving the problems they brought to the training. The instructor moved around observing the participants and provided help when requested.

In construct-based training (CBT), the instructor followed a similar format to applications-based training in terms of the combination of trainer demonstrations and trainee practice. However, in the morning, some time was spent lecturing trainees on the key elements and concepts of the software. In the afternoon, unlike ABT trainees, CBT trainees practised on generic exercises prepared by the instructor in advance rather than on problems they brought to the training. The queries for the generic exercises were based on general queries that had been submitted to MIS before the acquisition of CSRET.

## Post-training debriefing

Trainees were encouraged to contact the instructor if they needed additional help after the training sessions were completed. About 6 weeks after training, each trainee again completed the self-efficacy instrument and had a follow-up interview with the researcher who had conducted all the pretraining assessment interviews. The follow-up interview covered questions about usage of CSRET, supervisor support and feedback, resource availability and reasons for usage and non-usage.

## FINDINGS

The following subsections summarize the outcomes for each of the training groups as well as reasons why trainees in each group did or did not use the software. The summary first focuses on the pretraining end-user participation groups (ABT and CBT), then on the no pretraining end-user participation groups (ABT and CBT), and concludes with a comparison of pretraining end-user participation and no pretraining end-user participation approaches. Appendix B contains details of usage behaviour by each group as well as summaries of key information garnered from post-training interviews.

## Summaries of findings

## Pretraining end-user participation/applications-based training

Before ABT, trainees and the pretraining end-user participation interviewer identified specific ‘tasks’ that could be done with the support of the software. Then, during training, their learning was aimed at how to do those specific tasks. Therefore, these trainees were expected to ‘hit the ground running’.

As can be seen in Appendix B, even though this group used their own task-related problems or practice exercises in the training, this was not the decisive factor in their decision to use the software. That decision seemed to be based on their previous data retrieval habits, peer support, supervisor's feedback-seeking strategies and other organizational support factors.

## Pretraining end-user participation/construct-based training

In this case, although trainees identified tasks particular to the software to be learned, they did not learn how to apply the software to those tasks specifically. Therefore, after the training, these trainees were expected to have more difficulty in transferring their learning directly to the job without additional practice and/or assistance.

As can be seen in Appendix B, the majority of trainees in this group found generic exercises, hands-on learning and the data dictionary to be useful. One thought the exercises were too generic, and this person did not later use CSRET. To the extent that trainees can frame their tasks to fit the learning in construct-based training, or to the extent their task set fits with the set used as examples in construct-based training, this approach could potentially be as effective as pretraining end-user participation with applications-based training. The self-efficacy expectations of two of those who used CSRET were very high even at the outset of training. This indicates that these trainees felt strongly about their ability to learn and use CSRET.

Pretraining end-user participation aims to identify appropriate tasks to be learned in the training session. Without it the trainee must identify the tasks independently. To the extent that a trainee carries out appropriate task identification, the outcome could be similar to that of applications-based training with pretraining end-user participation.

## No pretraining end-user participation/construct-based training

In this case, the trainee was neither ‘counseled’ on which tasks might be appropriate, nor given an opportunity to learn about specific tasks. The potential benefits of pretraining participation and applications-based training could be realized in this approach if a trainee could relate the generic tasks of the construct-based training to his or her own work tasks.

The trainees in this group were all pleased with the hands-on training. One thought that the overview was a waste of time. This may have been because she had a very good understanding of CSRET capabilities going into the training. The post-training self-efficacy expectation was relatively high for all trainees in this group. This is expected for construct-based training because learning is focused on the basics of the software.

Trainees in the no pretraining end-user participation groups adopted CSRET or not based in part on their knowledge of database management system concepts. As two of the trainees put it:

It is unlikely that pretraining end-user participation would have influenced their adoption because some trainees who received pretraining end-user participation also did not use the software for the same reasons.

## Overall comparison of training approaches

A pattern emerged from the data, but it was different from expected. The pattern reflected no apparent differences between the training approaches in terms of post-training outcomes. Instead, it showed the adoption of CSRET was based on need, whereas lack of adoption of CSRET was based on factors that are organizational rather than training related. The organizational factors included maintaining established relationships, continuing support of the old software, and providing data directly to users.

## DISCUSSION

RQ1: Can the direct participation of the future trainee end users in training design for a generic software package enhance software training outcomes?

It was postulated that pretraining end-user participation could lead to enhanced software training outcomes by increasing the motivation of trainees, thereby making them more amenable to mental model formation and by managing their expectations. There is no direct evidence to support this proposition. In fact, those who had a pretraining interview reported lower initial CSRET self-efficacy on average than those who did not have a pretraining interview. One could argue that this is evidence of better-managed expectations in that it indicates those who received the pretraining interview felt more realistic expectations about the effort and commitment required to effectively use the software. However, lower self-efficacy may also be an artefact of the lower level of previous computer experience that was reported by those receiving the pretraining interview.

The process of pretraining end-user participation led to mixed outcomes. Some trainees were motivated to use CSRET. A few of those used it regularly, but others did not because the full database was not available for use. Some of the latter group felt they would need a refresher course or additional training. Of the trainees who did not use CSRET, some continued to use the old software, which was supported by MIS despite the desires of the director of MIS to get out of that business. Others were sceptical of the usefulness of CSRET and expressed this attitude in the pretraining interviews. In effect, one of the uses of pretraining interviews might be to identify those users who will not benefit from training or to find ways to enable them to benefit from training.

In general, trainees were able to learn a lot about CSRET and improved their self-efficacy for using it as a result of the training. The training sessions were evaluated highly by almost all trainees, so one explanation for the lack of differences in training outcomes might have been the overall perceived effectiveness of the training sessions. Because the goal for the trainer was to provide the best training possible, he may have gone beyond the bounds of a particular training approach. In addition, the fact that many of the trainees reached a ceiling in terms of knowledge and expectations, regardless of the training focus, provides further indications for the quality of the sessions outweighing the training design differences.

RQ2: How do the effects of pretraining end-user participation hold across different software training strategies?

It was postulated that a trainee who had a pretraining interview might benefit most from applications-based training. There was no pattern of outcomes that suggests that a pretraining interview followed by applications-based training leads to more usage or higher expectations. Instead, it was found that trainees who had a pretraining interview specified which type of training strategy they preferred. This is a benefit of the pretraining interview that was also expected.

There was a surprising match between the pretraining preferences and the training method the trainees received, even though the trainees did not choose the type of training session they attended. Trainees were not given the opportunity to select a particular training method to ensure that the full range of approaches that were planned for study could occur. Only one trainee who indicated a predisposition for construct-based training was assigned to the applications-based group. No one who specifically indicated a predisposition for applications-based training was assigned to the construct-based group. This situation could have impacted exploration of how pretraining end-user participation is impacted by the two training methods. It does help explain the strength of training evaluations for the pretraining end-user participation groups.

## CONCLUSION

The intensive research approach used in this study was aimed at generalizing to the efficacy of using pretraining end-user participation in formal software training in a specific organization. The data indicate that pretraining end-user participation should be considered a potentially effective tool in enhancing software training effectiveness. Pretraining end-user participation is more likely to suggest who will or will not use a software application after formal training, provide a more realistic view of the content and usefulness of the training, and facilitate motivation planning and motivation management. Another potential advantage of pretraining end-user participation is that it can also be used to determine the most suitable training method for each individual. For example, construct-based training is suitable for end users whose goal is to get an overview of the capabilities of the software.

## Lessons for practice

Assuming that pretraining end-user participation is followed by the provision of excellent training, it seems that the interviewer/counsellor should focus more directly on organizational than on training issues. This may be difficult. The interviewer in our study did not shy away from such issues, but found that they are often politically motivated, and as such difficult to surface. For example, in this company, supervisors did not generally follow up on software training. One supervisor mentioned that she saw software training as a perk to be given to employees as insurance against possible layoffs. One other supervisor mentioned that it was up to the software trainees to figure out how they could enhance their productivity after the training — therefore, follow-up was not culturally acceptable. The two instances of follow-up noted in the debriefing interviews depended on the existing supervisor—trainee relationship and as such were not the norm.

Question 10 from the pretraining interviews (see Table 2) garnered some useful advice for the trainer. Factors mentioned by more than two interviewees indicated the need to use a simplified manual design, to gear training to the common denominator, to explain the advantages and uses of CSRET, to be able to clearly deliver the training materials, and to provide relevant examples. Additional factors included the need to provide training to functional groups by their level of computer expertise and provide tips and short cuts. Two trainees commended the pretraining participation effort and recommended it as one aspect of good training design.

Pretraining end-user participation should be useful for identifying users who require resources before they can actually use the software for which they are receiving training. Resources include both the time to practise after the training, and availability of hardware to run the software. In this study, pretraining end-user participation efforts were constrained because those installing CSRET and doing the pretraining interview and the training were an outside project team who did not have the necessary standing to help users acquire resources. In addition, the fact that no one in line management supported CSRET also contributed to this situation.

Pretraining end-user participation has the capability to identify factors that can prevent (or motivate) usage. In this study, the pretraining interviewer was benign in that the goal of the process was to prepare trainees for their future learning. However, in a situation where the interviewer has the power to tell trainees that they are not ready for training, there should be savings generated if those who will not use the software can be identified. Alternatively, there may be the possibility of the pretraining participation opening up avenues that will lead to future use. An internal MIS person will probably have a better ability to understand all of the cultural phenomena surrounding the training, and be firm in telling trainees that they are not ready for the training process. The costs of providing extensive training to users who do not use software are high.

## Future research

The difficulty of getting a full set of trainees to attend all the sessions pointed to the problems of doing this kind of research in a field setting. Some trainees were reluctant to complete questionnaires and to schedule interviews. Some of the end-user trainees feared that the written performance evaluations (quizzes) and verbal discussions with the researchers (interviews) might be used by supervisors for job evaluation.

It is not clear whether a questionnaire could be substituted for the pretraining interview in situations where the software to be learned is generic. Although the face-to-face interview is more costly, the experience gained from this study indicates that the interview process provides a much broader scope of analysis, and increases the likelihood that potential benefits of pretraining participation can be realized. Two persons refused to fill out any written material but did provide a post-training interview.

Pretraining end-user participation might be used in conjunction with Fine and Getkate's (1995) classification of data tasks (FJA) to help target the training effort to specific needs of the trainees, especially for construct-based training. In this study, no effort was made to match trainees to their predominant data level, and no clear pattern was found to indicate that the mixed training groups were considered ineffective either in terms of constraining learning or in terms of trainee evaluations. Future research should focus on how trainees could be matched, grouped and trained based on their predominant data level. Such future research should consider that not everyone needs to learn everything about a software package. Trainees could be grouped based on their predominant FJA scale. The training can then be more focused to particular needs. Training could be provided in modules based on the data functions of the FJA scale. For example, in the CSRET training the focus of the modules could be organized as follows: beginner (compiling, computing); intermediate (analysing, innovating and co-ordinating) and advanced (co-ordinating, synthesis).

Future research should also focus on the possibility of segmenting trainees in terms of their individual differences. Bostrom et al. (1990) and Sein et al. (1993) provide evidence of the differential effects of matching trainees to training methods based on individual differences.

## Concluding remarks

MIS should consider that its approach to systems development, where analysts and users work together to specify an appropriate design for a system, could be applied to the generic software training domain. However, the role of the pretraining ‘interviewer/counsellor’ requires further research to determine the extent to which it can produce effective training outcomes.

## APPENDIX A: DESCRIPTION OF THE FUNCTIONAL JOB ANALYSIS FRAMEWORK

Functional job analysis is a method for defining what employees do with data and artefacts. This framework is based on Fine & Getkate's (1995) functional job analysis (FJA) and was previously suggested by Mackay & Lamb (1990) for analysing software training needs. The method provides an analysis of tasks starting from simple and moving to complex. The 'data' component is the focus of behaviours related to working with a database query software package, CSRET. Data functions are defined in increasing order of complexity from level 1 (comparing) to level 6 (synthesis) (see Table 3). The focus of the capabilities of CSRET is on levels 3A and above. Trainees filled in a questionnaire that identified their highest data activity on the FJA scale, and also their most predominant data activity level. The predominant activity level is the one most often used in job related tasks.
