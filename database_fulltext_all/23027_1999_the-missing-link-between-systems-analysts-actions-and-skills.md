# The missing link between systems analysts' actions and skills

James J. Jiang, Gary Klein\* & Tom Means

Department of Computer Information Systems & Analysis, College of Administration and Business, Louisiana Tech University, Ruston, LA 71272, USA, e-mail: jiang@cab.latech.edu, and \*College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado, Colorado Springs, 1420 Austin Bluffs Parkway, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA

Abstract. System analysts approach tasks with different orientations to their actions. Likewise, they exhibit a variety of skills. A study is conducted to explore the links, or similarities, between these orientations and skills. The findings indicate that analysts do indeed have orientations corresponding to their skills. This trait enables information systems management to select project teams appropriate to the development context. The variety of skills and orientations needed for success can be chosen based on one trait. Training of analysts can be targeted more effectively to consider missing skills based on current orientations.

Keywords: System development orientations, system development skills

## INTRODUCTION

The selection of information systems (IS) analysts to form a project team is one of the more important tasks facing an IS manager (Hawk & Dos Santos, 1991). It involves consideration of the skills that individual analysts possess, the analysts orientations toward systems development and the project context (Dos Santos & Hawk, 1988). Researchers argue that different orientations and skills are more effective for developing different project contexts (Benbasat et al., 1980). For example, analysts with higher behavioural skills are more effective for developing advanced decision support applications, whereas a technical skill background is more effective for developing transaction-processing systems. Likewise, the analysts must promote the application of skills to effectively carry over their approaches or exhibit an action-based orientation along the lines of the required skills.

Skill needs for IS developers has received notable attention in the IS literature (Lee et al., 1995; Silver et al., 1995; Jiang et al., 1997). The literature suggests that the skills needed for IS developers are dependent on the contextual needs of their users and organizations (Leitherser, 1992). The dimensions of skill context include the organization, the responsibilities and the technology available. Within each context are four general categories of IS skills for successful systems delivery (Green et al., 1989). Those skill categories identified are technical, political, communication and management skills.

However, researchers argue that systems analysts subscribe to overly technical and economic design ideals (Kaiser & Srinivasan, 1982; Kumar & Welke, 1984). Analysts are viewed by their peers as technicians rather than managers, are said to lack knowledge of human needs and motivation (Forcht et al., 1987), and are machine oriented (Bell & Richter, 1987). This finding suggests that IS developers pay attention to technical and methodological issues and ignore social, political and psychological issues. Thus, the alleged cause of a system's failure is its developers' ignorance of political and behavioural skills.

Suggestions for improvements for those who develop systems include formal training or education in managerial, political and communication skills (Benbasat et al., 1980; Green, 1989; Markus et al., 1996). Others suggest changes that include a split approach to systems design by separating social systems design responsibilities from technical systems design responsibilities (Davis et al., 1992; Markus, 1983). However, these suggestions were based on an unproven assumption: IS developers have an undifferentiated technical-oriented approach to systems development. The proposed solutions (e.g. academia courses, in-house training programmes) are expensive and largely unproven, and their benefits can only be realized when the assumption holds true.

Orientation of an analyst is a consideration of the direction one's actions will take in completing project tasks. To help define systems analysts' action orientations, Dos Santos and Hawk (1988) describe a survey study of 30 systems analysts. Three different orientations were found: a user orientation, a technical orientation, and a political orientation. User-oriented analysts place great importance on user requirements specification and stress the importance of addressing real and important user needs. They are concerned with frequently monitoring a project's progress in terms of its ability to satisfy changing user requirements. Technically oriented analysts are concerned with the system technology and project control. They emphasize the need to use structured techniques and to freeze user specifications. Analysts with a political orientation act on the political aspects of systems development. They place greater emphasis on the use of steering committees to manage projects, political ramifications, and support from important individuals. Some systems analysts had a technical orientation; however, others had a user orientation or political orientation, which violates the assumption of alternative design and training-based suggestions.

Another complicating factor is the context of the system. Context may be viewed to vary by system type or by more in-depth frameworks of skill needs. The first view narrows the focus to the type of system being delivered to an established framework of system types. Research has examined the skills and tasks required by various system types and found them to often be unique. For example, information reporting systems vary from transaction processing systems along the dimensions of the response time to users' queries, the uniqueness of the data processing model used, the accuracy of the output reports, the frequency of the system be used by the users, and the output formats required in the system (Jiang et al., 1998).

A second context viewpoint is a framework for examining the IS skill needs for systems developers. The central idea behind the framework is that skill needs are dependent on effectively delivering system services to their users. Three major factors strongly influence the ways of delivering computing services: organization, technology and IS developers' responsibilities. The organization factor, including management culture, political influences, business processing, organizational growth and size, and government regulations, can significantly affect the organization's computing needs and the activities for delivering these services (Straub & Wetherbe, 1990). For example, a computer-aided design technology is more likely to be used by manufacturing firms than by financial service organizations. The availability of technology increases the new demand of IS services. New technology must be learned and WAN (Wide Area Network) technologies create the global operating environments for a firm. These techniques create new skill requirements for systems developers. The third influence of skill needs in the delivery of computing services is the IS developers' job responsibilities. Many studies have addressed the skill needs for individuals in different job categories. For example, Watson et al. (1990) suggested that different knowledge levels of skill training are necessary for programmers, systems analysts and technical-support personnel.

In either view of system context, the task of effective team assignments based on skill and orientation still exists. An important step in advancing knowledge on this subject would be the examination of the relationship between orientation and skill needs. Specifically, is there a positive relationship between the technical orientation and technical-oriented skills, user-oriented orientation and user-oriented skills, and political-oriented orientation and political-oriented skills? The establishment of such links would be an important step forward because it is likely that different orientations may result in focusing on certain skills and ignoring the remaining sets of skills (Benbasat et al., 1980; Jenkins, 1986; Forcht et al., 1987; Hirschheim et al., 1988; Green, 1989; Markus, 1996).

Determination of a link is one way to reduce the considerations in assignment of staff to projects would involve relationships between skills and orientations. For example, do analysts who have the user-oriented approach towards systems development also have the needed user-oriented skills? (Are they consistent?) If so, then are analysts with different orientations lacking certain skills? The answer to these questions can help IS managers better assign analysts to IS projects. The number of factors would reduced from three to two if skills and orientations are consistently linked. Such a link would enable practitioners to provide appropriate training programmes for different orientations of systems developers and to assign systems analysts to projects in which their strengths (relative to others) are most likely to be needed and their weakness will minimize problems. In the long run, this knowledge would also help customize education and training programmes to the needs of individuals.

## RESEARCH METHODS

The purpose of this study is to examine the relationships between system development orientations and perceived skill importance. Although there are limited studies in the IS literature, there are a number of models in social psychology that suggest a relationship between skill development and self-perceptions (London & Smither, 1995). The ‘general congruency model' (Korman, 1976) suggests that an individual's perception/evaluation (e.g. skill importance) is consistent with one's orientations (e.g. system development orientation). Specifically, the hypothesis that there is a relationship between IS personnel's perceived IS skill needs and their orientations towards system development will be tested. This is done by looking at three separate categories of orientation to see if any skill categories are closely related.

Limited empirical evidence leads us to expect subjects with technical-oriented orientation will have a significant positive relationship with the perceived importance of technical-oriented skills (Jenkins, 1986; Davis et al., 1992). Similarly, we expect subjects with political-oriented or user-oriented orientation will have a significant positive relationship with the perceived importance of political-oriented skills or user-oriented skills respectively.

## Samples

Questionnaires were given to six chief information officers (CIOs) from six large private organizations (including one of the largest consulting firms and one of the largest telecommunication firms in the US) The number of employees in these organizations ranges from 2500 to more than 25000, with an approximate average of about 1000 IS personnel. The CIOs in turn asked at least 40 of their experienced systems development staff to complete the survey. Respondents were systems analysts, IS project leaders and IS department managers, and they were assured that their responses would be confidential. The direct request from the CIOs resulted in a very high response. Two hundred and forty-four were returned with 239 used in the data analysis. Five were omitted because of response omissions.

More than half of the respondents (51%) had more than 9 years of work experience, and about half of the respondents (46.4%) had systems experience in more than five application areas. Only 5.9% had less than 2 years of work experience, and only 3.8% had systems experience in only one application area. The data showed that the respondents were experienced in the field of systems development. The complexity of systems they had developed varied, suggesting that the analysts collectively had been involved in large and small projects. The respondents were well educated, with 63% (149) having completed college and 22.3% (53) having completed a graduate degree programme. Seventy-one per cent (170) were men and 29% (69) were women. The sample was young but still represented a wide age spectrum. The first, second, and third quartiles of age were, respectively, 31, 34 and 41 years. About half the respondents had management responsibilities.

## RESULTS

To test the relationship between systems analysts' orientations towards development and the perceived importance of skills, first, the canonical correlation analysis was conducted. A brief discussion of the technique and detailed results appear in the statistical appendix. The technique showed three pairs significantly related to each other. This result indicated that there is a strong relationship between the two sets of variables — orientations and skill needs. Therefore, there is a positive relationship between subjects' orientation towards systems development and the perceived importance of systems development skills.

To examine further the relationship between these two sets of variables, the corrected intercorrelations between the observed factors were determined (Ghiselli, Campbell & Zedeck, 1981). Details appear in the statistical appendix and are highlighted in Figure 1. The results indicate that user orientation is positively and strongly related to communication skill and business skill, that political orientation is positively and strongly related to political skill, and that technical orientation is positively and strongly associated with technical skills. Equally interesting are two weaker relationships: (a) technical orientation is negatively associated with communication skills, and (b) political orientation is negatively associated with technical skills.

## IMPLICATIONS

The result of this study supports the predication that systems analysts' orientations and their perceived skill importance have a significant relationship. Analysts with a user orientation, a political orientation or a technical orientation place emphasis on the importance of communication and business, political, and technical skills, respectively. In addition, the results confirm the earlier studies which found that systems developers who are technically oriented ignore the importance of communication with their users (Kaiser & Srinivasan, 1982; Kumar & Welke, 1984; Lyytinen, 1988). On the other hand, analysts who have a strong political orientation seem to overlook the importance of technical skills. Thus, there is a lack of skills when the full spectrum of orientations is not present. For project success, the skills needed are represented by the variety of orientations.

More importantly, these findings suggest that the assignment of analysts to a project team has an effect on the success of a project. Because the development of an IS project requires the pooling of a variety of sources of expertise and organizational resources, the selection of the 'right' analysts to form a team is critical. It cannot be assumed that all skills are covered in a random team. But, one can effectively compose a team without expensive training or alternate development approaches. The finding of the consistence between one's orientation and skills leads to the realization that assignments of analysts to a project requires only a match of one's strength (orientation or skills) with the context of a project.

Analysts with a user orientation towards systems development appear to be better suited for the development of systems having less structured problems, i.e. an evolutionary development methodology is preferred (Naumann & Jenkins, 1982). The value of user-oriented approach increases as the problem's relevant knowledge is possessed by the users. When systems are to support higher level decision making, much of the knowledge and expertise required for the system is available only from users and can be difficult for systems analysts to understand. For example, Hawk and Dos Santos (1991) suggests that user-oriented approach is crucial for defining the requirements of decision support systems (DSS) because only a user knows what it means to be informed by a system. The lack of knowledge about and difficulty in understanding decision support applications can also have a negative influence on the quality of project leadership provided by systems analysts. Furthermore, managers at higher organizational levels are accustomed to influencing decisions that affect them and are reluctant to relinquish control over these decisions to others. As a result, user-oriented systems analysts should have a greater impact on the development team's ability to deliver a system that meets user requirements when users are at higher levels than if they are at lower levels.

On the other hand, analysts with a technical orientation and less competence in communication and business skills seem to be better suited for problems of a more structured nature; for example, a problem in which the emphasis is on determining and freezing precise specifications. The value of technical-oriented approach increases as a system's requirements well understood by systems analysts. For transaction processing systems, the use of data is more structured. Its problem tends to be either well structured or to have relatively well-known solutions, and the consequences of excluding the user from the design may be limited to political considerations (e.g. resistance to change). In addition, lower level employees tend to become reticent, allowing others to take the lead. Researchers also have found that in a TPS (Transaction Processing System), users place more weight on system performance than decision quality and personal impacts (Jiang & Klein, 1998).

Analysts with a political orientation appear to be better suited to develop a system in a less controlled process environment. For example, Alavi (1984) found that technical-oriented analysts are more easily frustrated by changing system requirements or development environments. On the other hand, political-oriented analysts are more likely to seek project control and management support issues.

Should each project have each orientation of systems analysts represented to be certain all aspects of a system's development are covered? There appear to be two general points of view. One perspective suggests that IS teams having a ‘cohesive’ and ‘right’ approach of systems development will perform well (Hawk & Dos Santos, 1991). An opposing perspective views what have been considered ‘cohesive’ as actually detracting from project success. These views contend that too much similarity may lead team members to overlook potential problems as they seek to avoid ‘conflicting opinions’. Brown et al. (1990) suggest that a ‘cohesive’ group may make serious errors in judgement because it rejects constructive criticism, overlooks other important factors, and concludes that ‘conflicting opinions’ are an inevitable and necessary component of project success.

The success of a project involves aspects of all three orientations and all critical skills. Teams should be organized to incorporate systems analysts having all differing but major orientations, helping to ensure success of the system. Based upon the results of this study, the success of systems can be accomplished by assigning or hiring analysts who have orientations different from other members and/or by exposing and training systems analysts within their organization to different development processes.

Different training programmes should be provided based upon the analyst's needs. The conventional behavioural skills emphasized in training programmes for all systems analysts are too narrow. Individuals with user or political orientations may need to receive a more technical-oriented training programme. Perhaps more important than any other suggestion is that IS management and individual analysts must work together more closely to determine the skill training needs for individuals. Meanwhile, individual analysts must also have an open mind to learn the skills that they may perceive as unimportant. Of the human factors that have been cited as personal variables critical to the successful performance of systems analysts, Swanson et al. (1991) identified open mindedness or closed mindedness as a critical success factor.

This study also suggests avenues for future study. First, a more formal investigation of applying the instrument to the categorization of IS personnel should be conducted. This issue is important and needs to be addressed in a future study to make the conclusions applicable. Second, another important issue not addressed by this study is the extent of the exact skills used in each orientation. Identifying these skills is important because it will help academia better develop its curriculum. An IS programme with the resources constraints may want to pursue a ‘market-niche’ strategy or a ‘breadth and relevance’ programme to match the needs of their targeted employers.

## STATISTICAL APPENDIX

The formal hypothesis for the study is:

Ho: There is no relationship between IS personnel's perceived IS skill needs and their orientations towards systems development.

For each of the orientations, the following hypotheses are proposed and tested:

Ho1: There is no significant relationship between technical-oriented orientation and the perceived importance of technical-oriented skills.

Ho2: There is no significant relationship between user-oriented orientation and the perceived importance of user-oriented skills.

Ho3: There is no significant relationship between the political-oriented orientation and the perceived importance of political-oriented skills.

## Questionnaire assessment

To refine the questionnaire further a principle components analysis was conducted for each scale. The principle components analysis of system orientations indicated three factors. These three factors were retained for a factor analysis with varimax rotation. The results of this analysis are shown in Table 3. The criteria used to identify, distinguish, and interpret factors were that a given item should load 0.50 or higher on a specific factor and have a loading no higher than 0.40 on other factors. The three factors were examined against the three factors found in Dos Santos & Hawk (1988). The homogeneity of the items within each measure was established further by computing their internal consistency reliability coefficient (coefficient alpha) applying the formula as recommended by Cronbach (1951). The three alpha-values for each measure are 0.77, 0.83 and 0.73. The Kaiser measures of sampling adequacy (Kaiser et al., 1970) indicated the adequacy of the sample size (MSA = 0.8574). Examination of the factor analysis revealed that three factors were identical to the three factors identified by Dos Santos & Hawk (1988) — user oriented, political oriented and technical oriented.

The results of principle components analysis of skill needs are shown in Table 4. The same criteria used above were applied to identify, distinguish, and interpret factors. Four factors were identified including communication, political, technical and business skills. The Cronbach alpha-values for each measure are 0.73, 0.74, 0.63 and 0.76. The Kaiser measure of sampling adequacy is 0.8783, indicating the adequacy of the sample size. The four factors were examined against the factors found in Greens (1989) study. It revealed that the first three factors (communication, political and technical) are identical with only two items that load into an additional factor (named — business knowledge).

Canonical correlation is a widely adopted technique for analysing the relationship between two sets of variables (Cooley et al., 1971). The results are shown in Table 5. These showed that three pairs of canonical variates are significantly related to each other (P-value less than 0.01). A canonical variate is a linear combination from the variable set, and each canonical variate is uncorrelated with all the other canonical variates of either set except for the one corresponding canonical variate in the opposite set. The first, second, and third canonical relations were 0.7337, 0.7158 and 0.6771 respectively. This is a strong relationship between these two sets of variables. Therefore, the general null hypothesis (Ho) is rejected. Also, the corrected inter-correlations between the observed factors were determined (Ghiselli, Campbell & Zedeck, 1981). The results are illustrated in Table 6. They indicate rejection of the remaining null hypotheses.

## Biographies

Dr James J. Jiang is an Associate Professor of Computer Information Systems at Louisiana Tech University. His PhD in Information Systems (IS) was awarded by the University of Cincinnati. His research interests include IS project management and knowledge management using the Consonance approach. He has published over 50 academic journal articles in these areas. Dr Gary Klein is the Couger Professor of Information Systems at the University of Colorado in Colorado Springs. He obtained his PhD in Management Science at Purdue University. His specialties include information system development and mathematical modelling with over 50 academic publications in these areas.

Dr Thomas L. Means is a Distinguished Professor at Louisiana Tech University. He has his doctorate in business communication from Brigham Young University. He has published in leading business communication journals and has co-authored two communication textbooks.
