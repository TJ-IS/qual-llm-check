# Interfirm collaboration through interfirm networks

Feng Li & Howard Williams

Department of Management Science, Strathclyde Business School, University of Strathclyde, 40 George Street, Glasgow G1 1QE, UK, email Feng@mansci.strath.ac.uk

Abstract. Since the late 1980s, interfirm (computer) networks have been diffusing very rapidly. They have not only been adopted by firms across the whole spectrum of sectors; the applications based on such networks have also been extended from routine transactions to strategically more important processes. Using recent evidence gathered from six in-depth cases studies in the UK and a telephone-based survey, this paper will illustrate how the development of interfirm networks has facilitated the emergence of new interfirm collaboration in strategic areas. The empirical evidence has been drawn from the retailing, motor manufacturing and electronics industries. To conclude, the paper will highlight several key issues that have emerged from the study, identify three layers of barriers to the development of interfirm networks, and outline a future research agenda.

Keywords: Interfirm network, interfirm relation, strategic collaboration, EDI, barriers to interfirm working

## INTRODUCTION

Interfirm (computer) networks were first introduced in the 1970s when some large firms placed computer terminals in their customers' offices and offered these customers direct access to certain information (such as stock availability and price) held on their central computers (Large, 1987). Until the late 1980s, the development of such networks had been slow and fragmented both in terms of the sectoral coverage and in the range of applications they supported (Rochie, 1993). However, more recent evidence suggests that interfirm networks are diffusing rapidly into an increasing number of sectors (Cunningham & Tynan, 1993; Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1995; Teo et al., 1997). The applications based on such networks are also being extended from previous routine transactions (such as electronic ordering and invoicing) to more complex and strategically more important processes (Rochie, 1993; Alt et al., 1994; Howells & Wood, 1995; Li, 1995).

The rapid diffusion of interfirm networks has facilitated profound changes in interfirm relations (e.g. Rotemberg & Saloner, 1991; Rochie, 1993). In particular, strategic interfirm collaborations based on such networks have emerged in a number of sectors (e.g. the joint development of new products by companies along value chains). New tendencies are still emerging due to the rapidly changing business environment, the continuous computerization of corporate activities, the rapid development of advanced telecommunications infrastructure and services, and national and international policies and initiatives in developing the information society and economy (e.g. White House, 1993; Bangemann, 1994; CCTA, 1994). In particular, the extraordinary growth of the Internet in the last few years has opened up numerous opportunities for firms large and small to penetrate new markets and forge new interfirm relations, although to turn this potential into reality many extremely difficult barriers have to be overcome (Widdifield & Grover, 1995; Quelch & Klein, 1996).

Since the late 1980s, a growing number of studies on interfirm networks have been carried out. One of the primary benefits held out for such networks is their potential to transform interfirm relations (Rochie, 1993; Howell & Wood, 1995). By facilitating the cost-effective diffusion of information between firms and the development of common knowledge, interfirm networks can reduce the co-ordination costs associated with the marketplace and permit increased collaboration between firms (Butler, et al., 1997).

However, the empirical evidence cited by many previous studies has shown a different picture. With the exception of some well-known case studies, interfirm networks have so far primarily been used to support existing collaborative or exchange efforts rather than to facilitate new interfirm collaborations. Few surveys have revealed widespread, innovative use of interfirm networks in strategic areas. Although new forms of interfirm collaborations based on ubiquitous information and cheap communication have been demonstrated by some cases (Rotemberg & Saloner, 1991), most firms trying to replicate such success have encountered difficult practical problems. They have to cope not only with incompatible standards and unreliable technologies, but also with major non-technical barriers (such as sharing sensitive business information with each other). These problems have to be overcome before even routine applications based on interfirm networks can be developed. Introducing interfirm networks is also a painful learning process, and firms are naturally sceptical about the implications of these systems. In fact, our research has found that successfully developing routine applications with interfirm networks is probably a necessary first step for firms to learn to trust and work with each other via the new electronic medium. Only when the firms have benefited from these routine transactions will some of them be prepared to explore the possibility for more complex and strategically more important applications.

The purposes of this research are threefold. First, given the rapid development of interfirm networks, this research intends to uncover how interfirm collaborations based on such networks have evolved and identify what new interfirm collaborations have been developed by leading companies in recent years (especially those with the potential to diffuse into the wider economy). Six in-depth case studies drawn from the motor manufacturing and retailing sectors in the UK will be used to illustrate some of the tendencies. Second, results from a survey of 74 firms will be used to reveal to what extent innovative practices identified in the case studies have been adopted in the electronics industry in Scotland. Third, through comparison of the results from the case studies and the survey, some gaps in our understanding of interfirm collaboration through interfirm networks can be revealed, which will allow us to identify the main barriers to interfirm networking and outline a new research agenda for the future.

In the next section, the technical infrastructure for interfirm networks is outlined. Then the paper will review previous studies briefly before illustrating how the collaborative efforts in our case studies have been extended from routine transactions to strategically more important processes; investigate whether this tendency is evident in the Scottish electronics industry by using the survey results; and highlight some key issues emerged from this research. After that the paper will identify the main barriers to the development of interfirm networks and interfirm collaborations, and outline a future research agenda.

## INTERFIRM NETWORKS: THE INFRASTRUCTURE

Interfirm networks refer to the computer and telecommunications infrastructure developed, operated and/or used by two or more firms for the purpose of exchanging information that support a business application or process. These firms can be suppliers and customers in the same value chain, or strategic partners or even competitors in the same or related market (Forenego, 1988; Li, 1995). Interfirm networks can take many different forms, ranging from dedicated, closed group interfirm networks via semiclosed group networks based on value added network services (VANS), such as electronic data interchange (EDI), to completely open systems based on open mediums such as the Internet.

The first category interfirm networks are built with interface proprietary to a particular group of firms. As such, firms wishing to join the network often need to invest in the special hardware and/or software for the system. The reasons for adopting this type of interfirm networks are divergent. In some cases, they were intended by some firms to lock in customers and lock out competitors. In others, it was because common standards were not available when these networks were first introduced, or because available open technical solutions cannot provide the sophisticated technologies or the level of confidentiality required by the partners for particular applications. Some new strategic collaborations between firms developed in the last few years are often based on this type of interfirm networks.

The second category interfirm networks, currently the most widely used form, are based on standard, common purpose computing facilities and communication protocols, very often, using VANS. VANS describes the electronic communication services provided, usually by a third party, to two or more trading partners that not only establish an information link between the participants but also assist and add value to the communication process in some way (DTI, 1987). One most widely used form of VANS today is EDI. So far, EDI has primarily been used in the subcontracting area and has been proven most effective in supporting operational level applications, mainly because of its limited technical capabilities and the existence of multiple technical standards. To support more complex and strategically more important applications and processes, some firms have preferred to maintain dedicated data links between their computer systems by themselves, i.e. the first category interfirm networks (Li, 1995).

In both categories of interfirm networks discussed above, all parties involved are predetermined and all participants have agreed to trade electronically. These types of interfirm networks are sometimes referred to as ‘electronic alliance’ (Kokuryo & Takeda, 1995). However, new developments such as the Internet have now opened up the possibility – in theory, and to a limited extent, in practice – for instant encounter and trade between unpredetermined members (Benjamin & Wigand, 1995; Steinfield et al., 1995). By using the World Wide Web (WWW) browser, any person on a networked computer can now access servers anywhere in the world. A firm wishing to offer its products and services can now simply store an electronic catalogue on its server for the world to look at. It is now also possible for any firm to establish electronic communications with any other firms at very low costs. This third category of interfirm networks are sometimes referred to as the ‘electronic market’ (Kokuryo & Takeda, 1995; Steinfield et al., 1995).

Clearly, it takes more than just the technical possibility to make such open trading and communications work. On the one hand, some firms may not be interested in the electronic market, because dedicated interfirm networks using proprietary interfaces, and, indeed, even networks based on VANS (such EDI networks), can serve to lock in customers and lock out competitors. There are few real incentives for these firms to adopt open group systems and open up to increased competition. On the other hand, most commercial transactions between firms are based on trust, which is currently lacking in the electronic market (Handy, 1995). Kokuryo & Takeda (1995) proposed the establishment of the so-called ‘platform businesses’ – organizations similar to credit card companies in the retail markets (which has nothing to do with the so-called ‘platform organisations’ made popular by Ciborra, 1994, 1996) – to tackle this problem. However, the difficulties and uncertainties involved in the process are enormous. International, or at least regional, efforts are needed to overcome the extremely difficult barriers. Some recent, large-scale schemes, such as the United Nations backed ‘Global Trade Point Network’ and the ‘Tradecard’ project set up by the World Trade Centres Associations, may serve to overcome some of the barriers in the near future.

## COLLABORATION THROUGH INTERFIRM NETWORKS

Despite the growing need for collaboration between firms, collaborative ventures often fail. Differences between the collaborating firms in terms of aims, culture, structure, procedures, professional and natural languages, accountabilities, and the sheer time required to manage the logistics of communication often mitigate against success (Huxham, 1995). This is certainly true of interfirm collaborations through interfirm networks.

## Interfirm networks and interfirm relations: previous studies and this inquiry

The rapid development of interfirm networks is having profound implications for interfirm relations and for the future form of organizations. Since the late 1980s, an increasing number of studies on interfirm networks have been published. Some studies illustrated how interfirm networks can give firms (particularly the large ones) multiple benefits, for example lower costs, shorter lead-time, lower stock levels and improved cash flow (e.g. DTI, 1987; Large, 1987; Emmelhainz, 1987; Rochie, 1993). For small firms, the benefits and danger of being locked in or locked out of such systems were also highlighted (DTI, 1989; Amin & Dietrich, 1991). However, many of the visions on interfirm collaboration through interfirm networks were based on the evidence from a handful of case studies while implying that the findings were universally applicable. It has only been since the early 1990s that some studies began to systematically test hypotheses about, for example, the use of EDI and changes in the buyer–supplier relationship and the critical success factors for services such as EDI, often using quantitative survey data from particular sectors in particular geographical areas (Picot et al., 1993; Howells & Wood, 1995; Nidumolu, 1995; Teo et al., 1995, 1997; Tan, 1998).

Despite the significant progress made by researchers in the last few years, however, many important questions still remain unanswered today. For example, why has the diffusion of interfirm networks been much slower than expected? Why have some innovative interfirm applications developed by leading firms almost 10 years ago failed to diffuse into the wider economy? What are the main barriers to the development of interfirm networks and applications? How have such developments evolved over time? In particular, given that closed and semiclosed interfirm networks (i.e. electronic alliance) have been in existence for several decades, it is interesting to examine how such networks have affected interfirm collaborations in both routine transactions and strategic processes.

Electronic alliance – that is interfirm networks with predetermined participants – has been widely in use along value chains, and such networks are sometimes referred to as centred (interfirm) networks (Bloch, 1987; Forenego, 1988) or electronic hierarchies (to distinguish them from electronic markets) (Steinfield et al., 1995). Profound changes in interfirm relations associated with such networks have begun to emerge in recent years. Interfirm networks along value chains are usually initiated by a large firm at the centre of the network in order to interact electronically with regular suppliers or/and buyers. This is typically to reduce the transaction cost, stock level and lead-time. In some cases, the system has been developed through the natural extension of the centre firm's internal computer and communication systems. The development of centred networks often entails the extension of the centre firm's internal gains of computer networking to its suppliers or/and customers, in order to reduce the total cost of the final products and to improve the responsiveness of the value chain to market changes. Sometimes, such networks are imposed on trading partners by the centre firm. The costs to each firm are normally paid back by productivity gains, increased sales or secured long-term contacts, and sometimes the development is directly subsidized by the centre firm (Bloch, 1987; Li, 1995).

Previous studies have found that electronic alliance can often serve to stabilize existing relations between suppliers and buyers, and raise the entry barriers for potential competitors (e.g. Emmelhainz, 1987; Large, 1987; Rochie, 1993). This is because setting up and maintaining interfirm networks require close co-operation between participating firms, which helps them to foster a closer partnership and encourage the sharing of information. The use of such networks can also contribute to the removal of barriers to co-operation by reducing errors typical of paper-based communications; and should any errors happen, these networks can also facilitate problem resolution, because the problems can be more easily traced and be dealt with more quickly and effectively. In addition, introducing interfirm networks requires both time and money, and their maintenance often requires special skills, and there are also security and reliability concerns. Switching partners (suppliers or buyers) would therefore by definition be costly and time-consuming. All these factors serve to stabilize existing interfirm relations.

## Interfirm collaboration through interfirm networks: the empirical evidence

The evidence from our case studies and the survey confirmed most findings by previous studies discussed in the last section. More importantly, our case studies also suggested that once two firms have established a successful interfirm network at the routine transaction level, new and strategically important applications based on the same or more powerful links are often developed. A particularly interesting tendency is the development of new collaboration in strategic areas. Although this tendency was not confirmed by our survey, the survey did suggest that although 32% of the firms surveyed used some form of interfirm networks 18% had two or more different types. This result perhaps indicates that some firms have begun to appreciate that a single class of interfirm networks can only play a limited role, and different types of interfirm networks are need for different types of applications.

## From routine transactions to strategic processes: the case study evidence

The development of interfirm collaboration in strategic areas is clearly reflected in a London-based large retail chain and its regular suppliers of quality clothes. The retail chain is one of the largest in the UK, with an annual turnover of over £6 billion in 1995. In the late 1980s, an interfirm network was developed between the retail chain and some large suppliers using third-party EDI on Tradenet for electronic ordering and invoicing. The general benefits from the development included improved communication, reduced administration costs for all participants (less paper work handling and less relaying), closer co-operation, increased business volume and improved quality of services to final customers. However, it is interesting to note that in some cases, after the electronic trading became operational, applications beyond basic transactions have been developed – either using the same interfirm network or installing new and more powerful links.

An interesting application was developed between this retailer and a regular clothes supplier (a firm with 3000 employees and 15 factories in different parts of the UK). The EDI link between them was not only used for electronic trading, but also for exchanging the supplier's latest designs. Using local software at both ends of the link, each design can be coded into a series of digital figures representing the particular patterns, materials and colours of the new design. These figures can be transmitted to the retailer via EDI and then decoded, so the retailer can modify the design before issuing orders. This system is extremely important to both firms because the supplier can frequently send its latest designs to the retailer, and, in turn, the retailer can respond to the supplier more frequently and quickly, and inform the supplier of its latest demand and order more frequently. The business volume between them has since increased considerably, and the buyer–supplier relationship is consolidated by the network.

In the case of the retailer and a very large supplier, some more interesting interfirm collaborations have been developed after the EDI network became operational. Being a large firm itself, the supplier took the initiative to set up a more powerful communications network with the retailer in order to capture the latest market information (for example the latest customer preferences in terms of colour, materials and styles) from the retailer's stores all over the UK. The information is then incorporated into the supplier's latest design and production activities. The network is also used for the exchange of colour pictures of the supplier's latest designs (rather than technical designs as done by the other supplier illustrated above), which the retailer's purchasing staff can view and modify before issuing orders. This network has served to strengthen the relationship between them, giving this supplier a unique advantage over others.

Similar evidence has been found in the manufacturing sector. We examined the experience of a Japanese car manufacturer and regular suppliers in the UK. The car manufacturer only started production in the UK in the mid-1980s, and by the early 1990s it employed a total of 4500 employees in the UK, making well over 100 000 cars a year. In 1992, the manufacturer imposed electronic trading on all regular suppliers. The benefits to all suppliers included early delivery requests from the car manufacturer, which can help the suppliers to minimize inventory holding and achieve better production scheduling, and by interfacing EDI data with the suppliers' internal IT systems, administration costs can also be reduced. Because investments in EDI by the suppliers did not have to be dedicated, the car manufacturer only invested in the extensive facilities necessary to support the data transmissions but required all suppliers to fund their own communication links. Similar links with subsuppliers were also made possible and actively encouraged by the car manufacturer.

The success of this electronic trading network opened up numerous opportunities for new application development. The first application is the implementation of the so-called ‘synchronous supply’ with some local suppliers: some components (e.g. car seats) are only delivered to the car assemble lines by the suppliers – with the right variants and specifications – 12–15 minutes after the car manufacturer issues the order. Apart from the locational proximity between them and the convenience of electronic ordering based on EDI, one key to this application is that the suppliers are granted direct access to production planning and forecasting information stored on the car manufacturer’s mainframe computers, so the suppliers can plan their own production before receiving orders. Otherwise, this application would not have been possible. For suppliers in other parts of the UK and in Europe, the lead-time from order to delivery has also been reduced dramatically. By the mid-1990s, the car manufacturer shaved the average inventory and stock holding for European-originated parts to less than 1 day. Essential to the improvement is the sharing of business information between business partners via the interfirm networks.

The second application was in the area of interactive, joint development of new products. This application is an essential part of the car manufacturer's new strategy in sharing expertise, costs and risks with business partners. The car manufacturer is currently working with the technically more competent suppliers for the development of the next car. So far, few suppliers have the technical capability to supply a complete product unaided (as a ‘black box’), but the car manufacturer is developing what was described as a ‘grey box’ solution. In this approach, the car manufacturer supplies CAD data on the space the component occupies and its technical specifications; the supplier will then begin to design the component, passing it back to the car manufacturer via interfirm networks for feedback. Through interactive communications, any problems caused by the relative lack of engineering skills in the suppliers can be resolved. Essential to this application is the support of broadband communication networks between their computers.

The third application is in the area of manufacturer-dealer relations. Although the in-bound material flows in this car manufacturer is of world-class standard, its out-bound logistics is similar to most European car manufacturers. The inventory level for finished cars is currently 2.5 to 5 months of production (even in Japan the best figure for leading companies is still as high as 2 months). The working capital tied in finished cars is huge, and the potential for improvement is enormous. The key to improving the inventory level of finished cars is to improve the relationship between dealers and manufacturers and by-pass the distributors, so the manufacturer can cut the scheduling and delivery time. More importantly, the support of sophisticated interfirm networks and the sharing of a wide range of business information between the car manufacturer and the dealers and between the dealers themselves are essential. Dealers using the system need to be able to monitor the stock movement of other dealers and to check production in progress in the factories, but many firms are currently not prepared to share such information with competitors! The non-technical barriers to this application are extremely difficult to overcome. The managing director suggested this could be ‘a 20-year job’, and its success may ultimately depend on radical changes in the mindset of business leaders on the nature of firms and markets and interfirm relations. We will come back to this issue later in the paper.

## A survey of interfirm networking in the Scottish electronics industry

Although interfirm collaboration in strategic areas was identified from several case studies, our survey of the Scottish electronics industry did not reveal widespread development of such applications. The survey sample consists of 74 firms randomly selected from the Directory of Electronics and Support Firms in Scotland, which represent 15% of all such firms in Scotland. Because the survey was conducted by telephone, the response rate was 100%. The results from the survey suggested that the primary objective of firms in developing interfirm networks is not to improve the efficiency of existing information processing activities, but it is recognized that such information can benefit the business. For example, only 27% of firms highlighted reduced costs as a very important consideration when installing an interfirm network, whereas 86% were motivated by the potential to access a greater quantity of better quality information. Almost every firm considered the ability of interfirm networks to supply more information or improve the quality of information already communicated to be a very important influence on their decision to introduce them.

Despite such encouraging intentions, however, once one looks in more detail at how firms are using interfirm networks, it becomes apparent that most firms are using the technology to support existing patterns of interfirm activities, rather than facilitating new forms of collaboration or collaboration in new areas. Firms appear thus far to have focused attention on improving access to information, but insufficient attention has been paid to considering how to use that information innovatively to improve the business. In particular, most firms still essentially associate a given piece of information with a particular task, and there is limited realization that as well as such standard usage the same information reanalysed or reformatted might be of value elsewhere in the organization or to other business partners (e.g. suppliers). Sixty-nine per cent of the surveyed firms suggested that the introduction of interfirm networks had facilitated closer working relations with trading partners, but trading patterns remained virtually the same for 71% of the firms. Essentially, interfirm networks were mainly used to support established business relations rather than to help the firms to generate new business opportunities or to support new collaborations.

The majority of survey respondents were of the opinion that interfirm networks are mainly suited to the communication of hard or factual information, because electronic communication is typically bereft of many of the contextual clues that attend more personal communications. However, nearly 50% of firms have substantially reduced their use of traditional communications media such as postal services and the telephone after the introduction of interfirm networks. Thus, it can even be questioned whether the introduction of interfirm networks is leading to enriched communications between firms.

## Discussion: main issues from the research

A number of interesting issues have emerged from this research. The first is related to the contrasting results between the case studies and the survey. Although the case studies suggested that interfirm networks are increasingly used to support new interfirm collaboration in strategic areas, the survey revealed that most firms in the Scottish electronics industry are not using interfirm networks this way. These is no easy explanation for this. One possible reason is that most firms are probably still experimenting with interfirm networks and they have not yet progressed beyond routine applications such as straightforward transactions. Indeed, even at the operational level, many firms are experiencing serious technical problems in terms of system reliability and incompatible standards and networks. Several survey respondents pointed out that the reliability of EDI is problematic, and there is insufficient trust by the firm to allow EDI data to be fed directly into production schedules or computer-controlled machines. In many cases, the use of EDI was merely restricted to peripheral areas such as processing financial transactions, and messages were invariably pulled off the machine and manually checked. Such technical problems may have prevented some firms from developing new collaborations in strategic areas.

Another possible reason is that firms are perhaps at different stages of interfirm networking, so a common timetable for developing strategic collaborations based on such networks can prove problematic as each firm may want different things. Moreover, perhaps the most important reason of all is the current mindset of most business leaders about firms and markets and interfirm relations. As revealed by our survey, the majority of firms today simply want finished products from their suppliers, and they have little interest in tapping into the suppliers' expertise and resources to improve the viability of the final product (as has happened in the case of the car manufacturer and some large suppliers described earlier in the paper). Equally, most suppliers simply respond to the demand of their customers, rather than helping to define them. Successful collaboration between firms in strategic areas often requires business leaders to change this inward-looking mindset. As competition intensifies and as rapid technological developments redefine the balance between transaction and transformation costs (Burtler et al., 1997; Kelly, 1997), increasingly firms will have no choice but to focus on their core competence and share expertise and risks with each other. Therefore, we anticipate seeing more and more collaboration between firms in strategic processes in an increasing number of sectors.

Another important issue from this study is that in order to develop interfirm collaboration in strategic processes, a necessary first step is perhaps to develop routine applications with interfirm networks, which can provide important learning for the firms involved and nurture the development of trust between them. Although we did not manage to establish a definite causal relationship between the development of routine and strategic interfirm applications, the evidence from our case studies seems to indicate such a trajectory. Only when firms have benefited from these routine transactions and learned to trust and work with each other via the electronic medium will some of them be prepared to explore the possibility of more complex and strategically more important applications. Further research is clearly needed to examine this issue.

Similar to what most previous studies have found, our research has confirmed that interfirm networks can often serve to stabilize existing relationships between suppliers and buyers, reduce transaction costs, stock level and lead-time, and raise the entry barriers for potential competitors. The co-operation between participating firms involved in developing interfirm networks can often help them foster a closer partnership and encourage the sharing of information; and switching partners can be both costly and time-consuming. However, from a competitive perspective, a more stable interfirm relationship is not always desirable or beneficial under the current volatile business environment. To survive, firms often need to maintain an increasingly flexible relationship with suppliers and buyers. This is perhaps another reason why many firms have still not developed strategic collaborations to unlock the potential of interfirm networks, because they do not fully understand the long-term implications of such developments. More systematic research in this area can theoretically inform business leaders about the advantages and potential pitfalls of interfirm networks.

## THE THREE LAYERS OF BARRIERS TO INTERFIRM NETWORKING

The study has revealed several barriers to the future development of interfirm networks. There are not only serious technical barriers but also cultural and political barriers that are extremely difficult to overcome – such as sharing sensitive business information with suppliers, customers and even competitors; the integration of business processes between firms; the control of one firm by another; the co-existence of competition and collaboration; and many other related issues. Many of these barriers have to be overcome before interfirm collaboration in strategic areas can be developed.

The evidence from our research suggests that the main barriers to the future development of interfirm networks exist at three levels. At the operational level there are still many technical problems (such as unreliable systems and incompatible standards and networks). Such problems have led to the lack of trust of interfirm systems, which have prevented some firms from developing new collaborations in strategic areas. Although recent developments in technology, infrastructure and services have significantly relieved this problem, technical barriers will continue to constraint the development of interfirm networks.

A more difficult barrier exists at a second level. Issues such as sharing sensitive business information (e.g. stock availability, forecasting and production planning data) with suppliers, buyers and even rival companies still worry some business leaders. However, if such issues are not resolved, many strategic interfirm collaborations are simply impossible to develop and the full potential of interfirm networks cannot be achieved. How to resolve such problems, however, is beyond the scope of this paper.

Even when firms are prepared to share certain business information with each other, the success of interfirm collaboration based on interfirm networks is still not guaranteed. As has been argued earlier, differences between the collaborating firms in terms of aims, culture, structure, procedures, professional and natural languages, accountabilities, and the sheer time required to manage the logistics of communication often mitigate against success. Of particular importance for the effective collaboration between firms is perhaps the development of ‘common knowledge’, without which firms simply cannot collaborate effectively (Huxham, 1995). As such, the success of interfirm collaboration through interfirm networks is much more difficult than many people have anticipated. This to some extent explains why the enormous technical potential of interfirm networks has not been fully exploited so far, and why firms are more inclined to develop strategic applications once their collaboration at the routine level has been successfully introduced.

## FUTURE RESEARCH

Given interfirm networking is a rapidly evolving phenomenon, more systematic studies are clearly needed. We believe three types of studies are particularly useful. First, continuous efforts are needed to identify new interfirm applications based on interfirm networks. It is important to identify and conceptualize such applications as soon as they are developed, because many of them have the potential to diffuse into the wider economy. Business leaders need to understand the potential benefits and pitfalls of these innovations before introducing them. A particularly important technical focus might be the Internet, which certainly has the potential to support radical changes both within and between firms.

Second, there is still a strong need to examine the diverse applications and development trajectories of interfirm networks in a systematic fashion, perhaps using large scale quantitative surveys in specific sectors and/or in specific geographical areas. Like most information systems, interfirm networks are highly flexible, which allow firms to undertake radically different actions for fundamentally different purposes. The advantages of such studies will not lie in their discussion of isolated examples but in allowing researchers to reveal the general patterns and common features of the current transformation in specific contexts. The findings can also be used to test whether new tendencies identified in leading firms are being taken up in the wider economy.

Third, interfirm innovations are often closely related to organizational innovations within the firm (such as corporate restructuring, business process re-engineering, lean production and just-in-time). In fact, many interfirm collaborations are not possible without radical changes within the firm. It is therefore important not only to examine interfirm collaborations through interfirm networks in isolation but also in conjunction with studies about organizational innovations through information systems within the firm. Understanding the relationship between intra- and interorganizational innovations and the underlying principles behind these divergent phenomena will provide important insight into the next generation of organizational theories for the information economy.

## Biographies

Feng Li is the Director of Postgraduate Research in the Department of Management Science, Strathclyde University. For the last 10 years his research has focused on organizational innovations through intra- and inter-organizational information systems (including the Internet). He is the author of two books (published by John Wiley & Son and Macmillan) and a series of journal papers, and he speaks regularly at international IS and management conferences. He is an editor of the Journal of Management Systems.

Howard Williams is Professor of Business Computing in the Department of Management Science, University of Strathclyde. His main research interests concern the design and use of complex interorganizational networks in shaping the nature of economic activities and decision-making behaviours. A particular interest is the way in which the availability, quality and cost of public telecommunications intersect with the diffusion of interorganizational networks such as the Internet.
