# Information management in process-based organizations: a case study at Xerox Ltd

Philip Seltsikas

Aston Business School, Knowledge Management Research Group, Aston University, Aston Triangle, Birmingham, B4 7ET, UK, e-mail: p.seltsikas@aston.ac.uk

Abstract. The process-oriented holistic enterprise is a new organizational form in which the business process may be perceived as the basic organizational construct. Process management on an enterprise-wide scale implies a complex integration of business processes. The increased organizational complexity places heavy demands on a firm's information management capability, and this needs to be managed in a way that best supports the process model. This research has found that in the case of Xerox Ltd, a company that has adopted a holistic approach to process management, the information management process is key to supporting this method of organizing. The main finding is that, in order to support process management, a particular type of information management is required. This includes a need for enterprise-wide information systems and the centralization of systems development and maintenance. However, the process management is made possible by this type of information management. This may be referred to as the duality concept of information and process management. The research presents findings from an in-depth participant observation study conducted at the Xerox Ltd headquarters in the UK, and also includes data collected from Xerox France and Xerox UK.

Keywords: Process management, information management, enterprise-wide information systems, holistic management, organizational co-ordination, business processes

## INTRODUCTION

The functionally oriented organizational form, with its characteristic hierarchical control ‘structure’, is now commonplace. However, the increasing intensity of business competition, particularly with respect to the global market-place, has put unprecedented pressures on the functionally oriented form. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, ‘organizational flexibility’ received much attention as being the key to business survival (see, for example, De Leeuw & Volberda, 1996). In parallel, information technology (IT) has been identified as a major agent of change and is considered by many practitioners as the solution to new organizational flexibility (Willcocks & Smith, 1995). The dramatic advances in IT during the 1990s have allowed new, previously unimaginable, organizational forms to develop.

Central to this has been the convergence of computing and telecommunications technologies and the vast improvements in intersystem compatibility and connectivity. This has allowed both time and space dimensions to be ‘collapsed’. Improvements in the price/performance ratio of these technologies has meant that their widespread application became feasible (Scott Morton, 1992).

Although the impetus for dramatic organizational change has been strong, together with increasing recognition that such change is essential to business survival (MIT, 1996), few firms seem to have taken the ‘risk’ of implementing a process-based reformation. However, some firms can be identified, such as Xerox, who seem to have been ‘forced’ to abandon the functionally oriented organizational form simply to survive (see, for example, Watts, 1994).

It would appear that the process-based organization (Hammer, 1990; Scott Morton, 1992; Davenport, 1993; Earl, 1994) is emerging as the ‘new organizational form’ (MIT, 1996) with the ‘business process’ as the basic organizational construct. Management of the functionally oriented business form requires an emphasis on mechanisms for control. The management of a process-based enterprise, which exploits distributed self-governance or empowerment and is explicitly more complex, also requires an emphasis on mechanisms for co-ordination.

The research that is reported here was conducted in collaboration with a senior manager at Xerox Limited (XL) — the Xerox operating company that trades in the European, African and Middle Eastern markets. Most of the field work was conducted at the XL headquarters in the UK. Xerox is a large multinational which was forced to adapt its working practices in the 1980s in order to survive international competition: 'From 1976 to 1982, Xerox's share of world-wide copier revenues dropped by half, from 82 to 41%. Japanese companies, led by Canon, Minolta, Ricoh, and Sharp, were mostly responsible' (Jacobson & Hillkirk, 1986, p. 3). In 1988, XL's management instigated a radical re-engineering programme and by 1995 the entire firm was organized around 'business processes' (Seltsikas, 1999).

XL is now ‘divided’ into six ‘core business processes’, which together represent XL’s ‘process architecture’. These are shown in Figure 1 and are described below.

● manage for results (strategic management);

● time to market (business and product development and delivery);

\- integrated supply chain (which includes planning materials and resources, managing demand fulfilment, manufacturing and distribution);

● market to collection (which includes market intelligence, sales operations, order fulfilment, customer payments and customer relationship management);

\- customer services (which includes developing services strategies, managing services operations, the provision of operational services); and

\- infrastructure (which includes finance, human resources, information management, and facilities).

Xerox performs all of the activities that are represented in the value chain (Porter, 1985) — from design to production, through sales and after sales service. The company produces a diverse range of products, which are aimed at different market segments. To cope with the different demands and complexities of each segment, five main business groups were set up. The main business groups are (1) the office document product group, ODP (e.g. medium-sized copiers); (2) the production systems group, PSG (e.g. reprographics copiers); (3) the Xerox channels group, XCG (e.g. desktop copiers and facsimile machines); (4) Xerox document services, XDS (e.g. document outsourcing and consultancy); and (5) document supplies group, DSG (e.g. paper, toner, etc.). These groups can also be differentiated by ‘product volume of sales’ and ‘profit margin’ [e.g. high volume, low margin (XCG and DSG), and low volume, high margin (PSG and XDS)].

The senior manager involved in the research was the Director for Business Processes and Information Management (BP&IM). The question in his mind was how ‘information management’ (IM) should support such a different organizational form — the author has referred to this organizational form as a process-oriented holistic enterprise (POHE). In today’s global enterprise, IM has become an imperative enabler and supporter of the business, without which many processes cannot be viably performed. Thus, it is of utmost importance that the IM process is well understood and its characteristics in the context of ‘new organization’ explored. The significance in understanding IM is heightened as IT and IM becomes an increasing part of products and services. It is no longer simply a mechanism to speed up or automate a process. Without it, much of business as we know it today would simply not exist.

The ‘feeling of uncertainty’ expressed by the Director for BP&IM provided the impetus (see Checkland & Scholes, 1991) for conducting the research. Three main areas were investigated:

(1) the nature of a process-oriented enterprise; (2) the nature of the IM support process within such an enterprise; and (3) the relationship between process management and IM in such an enterprise.

In order to understand these three areas, the research sought to identify the substantive elements of both process and information management. Figure 2 describes the author's strategy for investigating these areas and it provides a conceptual model.

The figure represents the interaction of the elements. The key assumption is that given a suitable ‘mix’ of elements, the POHE can be made to work effectively (as measured by process metrics). These ‘elements’ are identified and presented in the Results section.

The author had ‘joined’ the business processes and information management group at XL and participated in various ‘projects’, which are described in the following section. The BP&IM group is part of the infrastructure core process (see Figure 1). It acts as the central management team for the XL-wide co-ordination of business processes and information management activities. Its ‘operations’ can be broadly divided into three areas: (1) the development of business processes; (2) the provision of solutions or applications to support the business (processes); and (3) the provision (planning and management) of a physical information infrastructure (servers, networks, PCs, etc.). Day-to-day maintenance of the technical infrastructure has been outsourced to EDS plc.

## METHODOLOGY

Lincoln & Guba (1985) posit that inquiry has passed through a variety of ‘paradigm eras’, which may be characterized by particular ‘basic beliefs’. The paradigm eras that they refer to are prepositivist, positivist, and post-positivist. The prepositivist period, which was from the time of Aristotle through to the early eighteenth century, was largely concerned with quiescent observation. The positivist era began when scientists started to ‘reach out and touch, to try ideas and see if they worked’ (Lincoln & Guba, 1985, p. 19).

Positivist research is typically characterized by a nomothetic approach to inquiry. In many cases, the positivist approach seeks to ‘test’ certain hypotheses, which are ‘drawn’ from existing knowledge in the field (May, 1997). The result of a positivist inquiry is usually to produce law-like generalizations, which are in essence probabilistic statements. This type of research output has proved, and continues to be, useful, within the boundaries of its intended purpose. The objective of positivist research is to produce findings that may be useful for prediction and control (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). The majority of information systems research has been based on a positivist paradigm (Nandhakumar & Jones, 1997), while at the same time there has been an increased recognition of the significance of social issues associated with research into computer-based information systems (Walsham, 1995). However, at the levels of ontology, epistemology and axiology, there are serious issues regarding the assumptions of a positivist paradigm, which when made explicit cast doubts as to the paradigm’s merit for use in conducting social research. These ‘questionable’ assumptions include (Lincoln & Guba, 1985):

\- an ontological assumption of an external reality, parts of which may be examined separately;
- an epistemological assumption that the researcher may be separated from that being researched;

\- an assumption that what is true at one time and in one place may also be true at another time and in another place;

● an axiological assumption that the positivist methodology assures results that are free of bias.

When this author's research began, there were several methodological problems. The first was that literature in the field of IM had not concerned itself with the problems of organizations that had taken a holistic approach to process management (as had Xerox). Similarly, the literature concerned with business process management has largely focused on the problems of business process re-engineering (BPR), usually up to the point of ‘creating’ a process-based organization, but not beyond that point. The question of the nature of a process-based organization, i.e. what it means to be process oriented, is relatively foreign to existing literature. Therefore, as the ‘context’ for the research was unclear, it seemed necessary to conduct the study using a methodological approach that was suited to ‘exploratory’ work — with the aim of providing models to allow for a deeper understanding of IM in a process-based organization. The assumptions of the post-positivist paradigm at the levels of ontology, epistemology and axiology include:

\- an ontological assumption of multiple realities that are ‘constructed’ (see, for example, Berger & Luckman, 1967);

\- an epistemological assumption that researcher and researched are inseparable;

● an assumption that only idiographic statements are possible;

\- an axiological assumption that research is ‘value bound’.

These assumptions are virtually the reverse of those that characterize the positivist paradigm. For researching IM in a process-based organization, existing literature provides little direction. Because of this, and because the main research ‘tool’ was the qualitative research interview (Cassell & Symon, 1994), the assumptions of the post-positivist paradigm seemed to be most appropriate. Lincoln & Guba (1985) refer to research that is based on a post-positivist perspective as ‘naturalistic inquiry’. Its appropriateness for this research is articulated in the following:

'In doing research from a naturalistic perspective, N [the researcher] is forced into the natural setting because he or she cannot specify, without an a priori theory or hypothesis, what is important to control or even to study. Until N has spent some time in the setting he or she cannot specify the focus . . . in more than rudimentary form, or place boundaries on it. N could not design a contrived study (an experiment, say) because he or she would not know what to contrive. . . . Since N cannot specify the precise form of the data to be sought, he or she must fall back on an open-ended adaptive instrument: the human being, who, like the “smart bomb”, can identify and wend its way to (purposefully sample) the target without having been precisely programmed to strike it.' (Lincoln & Guba, 1985, p. 43, emphasis added)

Naturalistic inquiry has many parallels with grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). The objective is to generate theory from the (usually qualitative) data. The data collection methods and the research data are discussed in the following section.

## Methods and data

The research was conducted using two main methods — participant observation and qualitative research interviews. The researcher had joined the business processes and information management group at XL headquarters and was involved in various projects. The author's intention was not to conduct ‘action research’ (see, for example, McNiff et al., 1996) and he attempted to keep his ‘intervention’ to a minimum. However, as a member of several project teams, he could not avoid some intervention. For example, other members of the teams often asked the author for ‘his opinion’. Whether their actions were influenced in any way by his responses would be difficult to determine. The projects in which the author ‘participated’ included:

● the planning of information management strategy for the Europe, Africa and Middle East region;

\- the alignment of information management activity with business process owners' predicted medium-term (5–10 years) requirements;

\- the selection of a business partner (firm of consultants) for a major (XL-wide) information management project.

The activities that the author conducted as a team member included interviewing (for the projects), data collection and co-ordination of some of the teams' work. To many Xerox employees, the author was seen as someone who worked for BP&IM and not as an Aston Business School researcher. This level of involvement constituted participant observation (see, for example, Gummesson, 1988) and provided an excellent means to form a deep understanding of the case under study. The participant observation method gives an opportunity for the researcher to ‘immerse themselves in the day-to-day activities of the people whom they are attempting to understand’ (May, 1997; p. 133). Using this method, the author had spent in excess of 12 months investigating the case site. The length of time spent investigating the case site is extremely important to this mode of research.

‘… we believe it to be the case that the probability that findings (and interpretations based upon them) will be found to be more credible if the inquirer is able to demonstrate a prolonged period of engagement (to learn the context, to minimize distortions, and to build trust), to provide evidence of persistent observation (for the sake of identifying and assessing salient factors and crucial atypical happenings), and to triangulate, by using different sources, different methods …’ Lincoln & Guba, 1985, p. 307

The data that have been collected through this mode of research include:

\- the researcher's field notes of observations;

\- the researcher's notes pertaining to the work he was involved in (e.g. meeting notes);

\- hundreds of documents, including memoranda, company manuals, training documents, copies of presentations, project plans, etc.

The second research method that the author used was the qualitative research interview (see, for example, Blaxter et al., 1996, pp. 153–156). These interviews were generally semi-structured in nature (see, for example, May, 1997, pp. 111–112) and aimed to elicit the respondents' understanding of information management and of process management. Twenty in-depth research interviews were conducted. Each interview lasted from 90 to 180 minutes. The interviewees were mostly senior IM people and included respondents from BP&IM at the 'centre' as well as from Xerox France and Xerox UK. The research interviews were tape-recorded and were subsequently fully transcribed.

## ANALYSIS

The interview transcripts were investigated using a computer software package that is designed for qualitative data analysis — QSRNUD•IST (non-numerical unstructured data indexing searching and theorizing, developed and manufactured by Qualitative Solutions and Research Pty). The transcripts amounted to approximately 700 pages of typed (single-spaced) dialogue. The QSRNUD•IST software was an invaluable tool for sorting and indexing this large quantity of qualitative research data. The analysis of such an immense database was conducted using techniques similar to those described by Dey (1993) and has been based largely on the 'steps' of the 'constant comparative method' provided by Glaser & Strauss (1967). The QSR NUDIST software was invaluable in facilitating this process. This initially involved the generation of 'categories' for classifying the 'data units' (a data unit is usually a sentence or paragraph). Various types of relationships were identified and used to form categories. These included, for example, means—end or cause—effect relationships. To illustrate, the following is an example relating to costs. The costs levied by 'central IM' (the IM group at XL headquarters in the UK) for the provision of a new system was expressed by several respondents as the cause of resistance from 'entity-IM managers' to adopting certain systems (entity-IM managers are the senior managers of the IM groups in the XL operating companies, e.g. IM France, IM Germany, and IM UK).

‘It started out as something between the central control [and the entity IM managers]. And I went out to each country and said, “OK what are your requirements?” And they got quite excited until they found out how much it was going to cost them to install one of these systems. And they all went very quiet.’

Another respondent argued that the requirements of the entity-IM managers could not be met by the ‘centre’ (XL headquarters, BP&IM) if they involved unreasonable cost.

'Anybody can come up with ideas that are “Oh, wonderful idea about using this technology”, [but] its got to be in a way that you can implement at a reasonable cost...’

The next major ‘step’ in the analysis of the data was to combine the categories to produce a reduced set. Miles & Huberman (1994) consider this to be a major ‘component’ of data analysis, which they call ‘data reduction’. From a list of relationships, the analysis draws together those elements that may be grouped. In this case, the final category groups were reduced to three (those relevant to the focus of investigation): process management, information management, and process and information management together. The previous examples of category units would be grouped under the information management ‘integrated category’. As more ‘data’ were added to the categories, they became saturated — ‘... so well defined that there is no point in adding further exemplars to them’ (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). The research findings can then be articulated and theory is constructed from the data.

## RESULTS

Although the identity of the case company has not been disguised, the individual identity of respondents has. This is because the research data were collected by assuring anonymity to each and every respondent. The results reveal that in order to effectively manage a process model (vis-à-vis process metrics), a specific type of information management is required. Conversely, this information management capability makes the process model viable.

## Research problem

The majority of the interview data that have contributed to these findings was provided by 'programme managers' of the BP&IM group at XL's headquarters (central IM). The programme managers normally had IM responsibility for either a particular application (e.g. SAP — the general name given to enterprise software produced by the Germany-based firm SAP AG), or more typically for a particular 'core business process' (e.g. market to collection, see Figure 1). As has been mentioned previously, the main areas of concern to be investigated were: (1) the nature of a process-oriented enterprise; (2) the nature of the IM support process within such an enterprise; and (3) the relationship between process management and IM in such an enterprise. To allow for a clearer understanding of the results, the 'information management' observations have been presented first, followed by 'process management', and then the issues that concern both.

## Observations: information management

At XL, IM (the BP&IM group) receives much of its direction from ‘the business’. This direction normally comes from the managers of the core operational process areas — the ‘process owners’ (POs). The POs provide support to the business groups. The usual points of contact between BP&IM and ‘the business’ are between the process owners and the programme managers. Through this arrangement, IM is inextricably linked with the business.

By using a business process focus, IM at XL tends to look at answering the problem of ‘how can systems or applications support the operations’. This is an operational approach to IM. It differs from a traditional IM approach that looks at ‘what information are we trying to get out of it?’ — a management information approach to IM.

XL's BP&IM group takes what they term a package approach to systems development (sic). 'Packages' (off-the-shelf applications) are evaluated, not by looking at the 'outputs' that can be produced but by asking 'Can the system do what we want it to do — support this process and therefore support this operation?' So systems development starts with the definition of the business processes and aims to match a system (package) to support those processes. 'Flexible' applications are investigated that allow process variation. Questions such as 'is the system efficient in terms of the in-process metrics?' are asked (e.g. with a menu-driven system, it may take several seconds to navigate to each screen). The flexible system needs to be oriented to support efficiently the process flow. The 'data' in the system is seen as secondary to developing a system that effectively and efficiently supports a 'particular way of doing things'. The focus is thus on the way of doing things — the process.

XL perceives this ‘operational’ approach to IM as being a more pragmatic approach to systems development that is quicker than traditional data-centred methods. However, with the process-driven approach, enterprise-wide data are lost because those who manage each process area believe that they require their own data. Each have different definitions for many of the same things (e.g. customer) and the BP&IM group has a key IM task of pulling all this together.

A good example that illustrates this problem was given by the programme manager who was responsible for the customer services (CS) core process and its systems. The following is a summary of ‘her’ account:

In a business process-driven organization, a problem may arise with customer queries. These can be different. There might be a query on the payment of the customer's account, or to do with equipment that has been ordered but that has not arrived, or maybe a CS query about what's happening to the status of their previously logged service problem, or there might be a query on their order (production order). Each one of these 'touches' a different core process, market to collection, governance to results (finance), customer services, or integrated supply chain (respectively). There are four different queries, and four process areas — potentially four different systems. Usually, systems have been provided for process areas but to the customer it's one query. Do they phone four different people? Do they phone one person? Does the query get logged and managed in one system that feeds off the other four? Or does it get logged and managed in each one? Or there may have been a problem such as a mistake on an invoice making the customer very angry. Then the next day, the same customer calls for service, we need to speak to the customer very carefully — but now they're talking to a whole different department, who are unaware of the previous query.

What is needed to co-ordinate IM in a POHE is a strong IM arbitrator who is able to centralize the management of IM and to pull enterprise-wide data together. This is one of the fundamental aims of the BP&IM group. This, however, is difficult to achieve in a company that was once heavily reliant on functional organization and that has an applications portfolio in excess of 1200 applications, many of them legacy and designed to support business functions.

In terms of IM, one of the key benefits of adopting a process-focused approach is that IM is much closer to ‘the business’ and understands why things are carried out in the way that they are. This contrasts with a common IM approach, in which it builds what ‘IM thinks is best’. In the Xerox case, that which is lost in focusing on processes by adopting an operational approach to IM is an emphasis on the strategic use of data — enterprise-wide data.

## Observations — process management

Process management is dividing a business into the way it operates, ‘with an end-to-end view’. Taking the market to collection (MTC) core business process as an example, a traditional organization (functionally oriented) may divide the business into a sales department, an order processing department, and an invoicing department — each of which would ‘do their own thing’ and ‘hand off’ the paper work by sending it to the next department. The next department would do their own thing, and hand the work to the next department; and so forth.

By taking a process perspective, Xerox look at this as a single (end-to-end) 'market to collection' process. Questions such as 'When a salesman takes an order, how does it flow through the organization?' are asked. Work is perceived as a flow from the customer, through the organization. Each of the aforementioned 'traditional departments' do something that may still need to be done in the process organization, but the difference is to look at the overall flow and to ask questions such as: 'Is that satisfying what the customer wants?', 'Is it efficient?', 'Does each subprocess provide the information required to complete the process?', etc. With process management, someone is responsible for focusing on the entire process (the process owner).

Functional heads of departments were usually only concerned with their own department. The holistic nature of process management on an enterprise-wide basis is important. The bringing together of previously disparate processes and their management as a single process is intended so that ‘improvements’ can be ‘systematically’ used. The holistic process model gives an ‘end-to-end’ process view of the organization — functional boundaries are ignored.

A process perspective also involves ‘process metrics’. These are ‘measures’ that are defined to ‘monitor’ each step of a process. Metrics can identify where a process is ‘broken’ and these information processes can be used for process improvement. Each subprocess has an ‘efficient’ set of ‘work instructions’ in which performance measures, in terms of process metrics, are defined. In-process metrics can be measures such as time and cost. For example, in the customer service process, the number of ‘broken calls’ to a call-centre are important. In the case of broken calls (a broken process), the process owner may consider the following. Can a call to a call-centre solve the customer’s problem? Perhaps the call-centre operator cannot because of a lack of expert information. They may then pass the call on to an analyst. Can the analyst solve it? Perhaps not, it might be a problem with the hardware. The analyst may pass the call on to a hardware specialist. If the hardware specialist cannot solve the problem because, for example, a part is needed and he/she needs to say ‘goodbye’ to the customer then this call is considered to be broken. Before Xerox adopted a process orientation, these types of things were not measured, but they are now considered to be extremely important. By using a system of process metrics, such as the analysis of broken calls, it is possible to use further metrics. For example, how long is a broken call?; or how long does it take to resolve the customer problem. One respondent explained:

'OK, we might have a hundred broken calls, but we're dealing with them in . . . within 2 h . . . so that overall it's a 200-h delay, but still the customer's not upset. Or we might have 20 broken calls but we're not dealing with any of them for 3 days, the customer's really pissed off. So it's not just about, you know numbers, it's about what's happening with them as well. So customer satisfaction, I guess, is another metric. You . . . because you could define . . . in those terms, you could define more than a day as poor customer satisfaction potentially.'

The Xerox model of process management is a holistic approach to defining the organization-wide business processes. The objectives of this model are published on the XL Intranet. In summary they are: (1) to make processes effective (to produce desired results); (2) to make processes efficient (to minimize resources used); and (3) to make processes adaptable (to customer and business needs). The organization-wide processes are represented in diagrammatic form and are known as the Xerox business process architecture (Figure 1 is the basis of this model). The BP&IM group are the ‘developers’ of the model.

## Observations — process and information management

Information management at XL is largely ‘funded’ by the process owners (the managers of the core business processes). These POs are effectively the ‘customers’ of BP&IM. BP&IM work closely with the process owners to define the business processes that will support the business groups (the POs' customers). BP&IM then find a package that 'best' supports these processes, and provide the 'system'. Their 'interaction' normally 'ends' there.

Any on-going requirements, such as changes to the applications database (process improvement), is not seen by BP&IM to be included as part of their ‘role’. During the ‘development’ of systems, BP&IM spend a large amount of time defining the process requirements with user groups (who tend to be managers from the process areas). BP&IM then choose the system/application, and subsequently involve users (actual) to advise of any changes that may be needed. This detailed ‘process’ is resource intensive. However, future ‘change requests’ are dealt with as a routine task. The subsequent changes (or requests for change) are seen as coming from an ad hoc environment. In other words, there is no systematic process for process improvement. With the emphasis on a process orientation, the perceived ‘ownership’ of a system lies with the PO. Once BP&IM hand it over to the business, it loses overall ‘co-ordination’.

Each XL ‘entity’ (Germany, France, UK, Northern Europe, Southern Europe, Central and Eastern Europe, Middle East and Africa) has its ‘own’ business processes. ‘Empowerment’ and the decentralization of responsibility has led to an IM environment consisting of more than 1200 different systems and applications. When BP&IM had brought ‘representatives’ from each entity together to discuss system developments, there were huge debates because of the differences in each country’s processes.

In order to manage effectively IM within a process-oriented model of organization, XL (BP&IM) believe that IM and process management need to be centralized. The assumption is that a central IM model will better support a process model. The current IM environment at XL is highly fragmented and does not appear to support effectively the process model. The strategic IM plans that are currently being implemented aim to centralize IM and process management. This is to be achieved through the implementation of centrally supported (and located) core XL-wide applications.

## Major outcomes

Holistic (organization-wide) process management implies complex integration. This complex integration may be managed with the use of modern information technologies, but it requires information management of a particular kind. There is a relationship between process management and information management, but, often, it is difficult to distinguish these two areas. In other words, to talk of process management is to talk of carrying out a particular type of information management. However, without ‘enterprise-wide’ information management that is able to ‘connect’ the business processes and allow the organization to manage the complexity that is produced, process management would be virtually impossible.

This case study of Xerox Ltd would suggest that the IM elements which are needed to support effectively a process model should include enterprise-wide information systems (e.g. SAP), centralized systems development, centralized on-going maintenance, centralized physical hardware (e.g. server farms — a single location for all the computer servers), an IM group that is closely linked with the business that adopts business priorities, and an emphasis on strategic (enterprise-wide) data together with a focus on continuous operations support for the business processes. IM that is organized in this way may better support a process model.

This model of supportive IM depends on the existence of several process management (PM) elements. There needs to be clear process responsibility, process owners need to be ‘close’ to IM, process metrics need to be fully exploited and, in particular, there needs to be a process for process improvement. Process management needs to be centralized to allow the organization to cope with the complexity of the process architecture. The centralized IM model requires a centralized process model. The standardization and simplification of business processes which can be achieved if an appropriate IM model is adopted produces (among other things) considerable cost advantages and may allow XL to meet better its customers’ requirements. ## CONCLUSION

As has been shown in this paper, the relationship between process and information management is a complex one. The elements that are described in Figure 3 are only a summary of possible elements and represent those that were prominent in this research. In reality, it is virtually impossible to separate PM from IM. It is difficult to envisage a process-based organization with PM that is not enabled by IM. The manner in which IM and PM are inextricably linked can be referred to as the duality concept of process and information management.

Management who are serious about adopting holistic approaches to process management need seriously to (re)consider the way in which IM is to be organized. A POHE requires information management processes that are very different from those that are normally encountered. Unless managers are willing to face this challenge, efforts to manage by process (at least in contexts similar to XL's) are likely to fail.

Exploratory research, such as that which is described here, normally raises more questions than it gives answers. There are many areas that warrant further investigation. Xerox Ltd first moved to management by processes and were then forced to change their IM to better support this model. It has taken XL approximately 10 years to implement fully a process model — IM capabilities have changed dramatically in this time. Comparison with firms that have changed their IM in order to embrace a process model would be interesting. Perhaps in this latter case the substantive elements of PM and IM would be different? Another area of interest that warrants investigation is the future nature and location of the IM group. In a process-based organization, as IM becomes increasingly structured along process lines, the future of a separate IM group is uncertain. Although many of the salient elements of the PM/IM model call for a centralization of IM activity, this does not exclude the possibility of some type of distributed IM responsibility — distributed among process groups. Further research would be needed to assess the viability of any such developments in the PM/IM model.
