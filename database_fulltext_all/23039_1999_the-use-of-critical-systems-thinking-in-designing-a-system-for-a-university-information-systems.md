# The use of critical systems thinking in designing a system for a university information systems support service

Lorraine Warren and Peter Adman\*

Department of Business Strategy, Lincoln School of Management, University of Lincolnshire and Humberside, Brayford Pool, Lincoln LN6 7TS, UK, e-mail: Iwarren@lincoln.ac.uk and \*Computer Centre, University of Hull, Cottingham Road, Hull HU6 7RX, UK, e-mail: P.Adman@ucc.hull.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper reports the practical application of critical systems thinking (CST) to an information systems design (ISD)-related problem. The potential benefits of using CST for ISD are explored, followed by a description of an intervention to derive a design for part of a UK university's IS user support service. More specifically, total systems intervention (TSI) was used as a meta-methodology to operationalize CST and to guide the course of the intervention. The intervention serves as a vehicle to gain insight into the potential value of CST to ISD-related problems, and concludes with reflections on the actual benefits realized and suggestions for further research.

Keywords: Critical systems thinking, information systems design, total systems intervention, user support service, meta-methodology

## INTRODUCTION

There is ongoing concern in the IS community over the extent of dissatisfaction with the outcomes of investments in information systems/information technology (IS/IT). One could cite the widespread public disquiet over ‘the year 2000 problem’, or high-profile failures such as the abandonment in 1993, at a cost of £80 million to the Stock Exchange, of the TAURUS project, which aimed to create a system for the London financial markets (Currie, 1994). More generally, the wide-ranging (14 000 organizations) recent Economic and Social Research Council/Department of Trade and Industry survey in the UK (OASIG, 1996) concerning the outcomes of IT investments makes depressing reading, reporting that:

\- 80–90% do not meet their performance goals;

● about 80% of systems are delivered late and over budget;

● about 40% of developments fail or are abandoned;

● less than 40% fully address training and skills requirements;

● less than 25% properly integrate business and technology objectives;

\- only 10–20% meet all their success criteria.

In the light of the above, it is hardly surprising that there is a widely agreed agenda for improvement. It has been argued recently (see Jackson, 1997a for the most comprehensive account) that critical systems thinking (CST), which considers human aspects, technology and broader organizational issues, may have value for those involved in IS design (ISD). As yet, however, there are few documented instances of the application of CST to ISD-related problems beyond Jackson's own account (1997a) of an intervention in North Yorkshire Police, and a report of an intervention then under way concerning the student record systems at the University of Luton (Lehaney & Clarke, 1997). This paper provides support for Jackson's claim in describing an intervention guided by CST to improve the operation of a UK university's IS user support service, namely the Academic Computing Services Unit (ACSU). The aim was to derive an improved design for the 'front end' of the service, i.e. that part of the service dealing with user requests, queries and problems. More specifically, total systems intervention (TSI) was used as a meta-methodology to operationalize CST and to guide the course of the intervention.

In the next section, the potential benefits and drawbacks of using CST in ISD-related situations are discussed. This is followed in the third section by a description of the setting for this intervention and a description of how it was carried out through to a practical outcome. In the fourth section, the actual benefits and drawbacks are compared and contrasted with those expected from the theoretical literature; finally, conclusions drawn as to the potential value of CST to ISD-related problems and also on the potential of TSI to operationalize CST.

## CRITICAL SYSTEMS THINKING AND INFORMATION SYSTEMS

We begin this section by establishing the theoretical context for the potential value of CST for ISD; this is followed by a discussion on putting CST into practice using TSI. The section concludes by discussing the context of success for linking CST and ISD.

## Theoretical context

The systems movement has seen its traditional, ‘rational’, ‘hard’ models unable to cope with the increasingly complex and turbulent organizational world, with its conflicting human perspectives. In response, alternative systems-based approaches to organizational improvement have emerged, reflecting different (and conflicting) research paradigms. During the late 1970s and 1980s, organizational cybernetics and soft systems thinking evolved as important strands of systems thinking, with Beer’s viable system model (VSM; Beer, 1981, 1985) and Checkland’s soft systems methodology (SSM; Checkland, 1981) as notable milestones. In terms of sociological paradigms, the functionalism of hard systems thinking was being challenged by the structuralism of organizational cybernetics and the interpretivism of soft systems thinking. Notwithstanding the emergence of these newer perspectives, systems thinking was still subject to a barrage of radical critique for being inherently managerialist in its outlook (Rosenhead, 1976; Ulrich, 1981; Jackson, 1982). It was also acknowledged that the plethora of systems methodologies now available all had strengths, weaknesses and limitations, yet there was little guidance on which to choose, or how to match a given methodology to a particular problem or situation. These criticisms led to the emergence of critical systems thinking (CST) in the late 1980s and the 1990s, as debates from social theory became influential in the systems movement.

There is much discussion in the literature over what is meant by ‘CST’ and indeed its potential benefit (or otherwise) to the theory and practice of management: CST is still an evolving phenomenon, with different manifestations. It is possible to identify two main strands, however. The first can be identified with the work of Ulrich (1983), which attempts to make explicit and enable challenge to ‘boundary judgements’ in social systems design; it operates through critical systems heuristics, a mode of questioning intended to reveal the normative content of design processes. The second arose from direct attempts to develop systems thinking through Habermas’s theory of knowledge constitutive interests, that is to address the technical, the practical and the emancipatory interest by means of pluralistic combinations of existing methodologies. This second strand of thinking produced a number of authors arguing for explicit fundamental commitments for CST, albeit using different definitions and terminologies. Jackson (1991) lists five; Schecter (1991), Flood & Jackson (1991a) and Midgley (1997a) list three. Midgley’s commitments are:

\- critical awareness — examining and re-examining taken-for-granted assumptions, along with the conditions which give rise to them;

\- emancipation — ensuring that research is focused upon ‘improvement’, defined temporarily and locally, taking issues of power (which may affect the definition) into account;

\- methodological pluralism — using a variety of research methods in a theoretically coherent manner, becoming aware of their strengths and weaknesses, to address a corresponding variety of issues.

The intention is that these commitments are considered as of equivalent importance; they are interdependent themes (Midgley, 1997b). Clearly, the commitments raise complex (and still debated) questions for both theory and practice. CST has been subject to critique at both levels. Practically, CST presents a complex set of commitments for researchers or practitioners to operationalize (see below). Theoretically, CST's claimed linkages to Habermas are now acknowledged to be not entirely successful (Spaul, 1997; Jackson, 1999); secondly, postmodernism presents challenges to the very notions, or ‘grand narratives’, of critique, emancipation and pluralism. Several authors are looking to post-modernism as a source of inspiration for further development of CST, e.g. Flood (1996); Flood & Romm (1996a); Ormerod (1996, 1997); Taket & White (1995, 1997). At present, these authors tend to concentrate on the diversity realizable through methodological pluralism, rather than on how events unfold at the microlevel through the play of language.

Fuller discussions on the theory and practice of CST can be found in Ulrich (1983); Schecter (1991); Flood & Jackson (1991a, 1991b); Jackson (1991); Ellis (1995a); Flood (1995); Flood &

Romm (1996b); Munro (1996); Jackson (1997b, 1999); Mingers & Gill (1997). Notwithstanding this critique, CST claims to provide an agenda for intervention which represents an advance on earlier manifestations of systems thinking.

Having set out the nature of CST, we now turn to the suitability of this second strand of thinking for ISD, through exploration of the three commitments identified above. The notions of critical awareness, pluralism and emancipation are evident in the emergent IS domain (and, by implication, ISD), though not within an explicit integrated framing concept such as CST. Each in turn is considered below.

Critical awareness is evident through the challenges to the established orthodoxy in IS. Although a number of authors have noted the dominant identifiable orthodoxy as still adhering to a ‘rational’ philosophy, criticisms of traditional approaches have led to a proliferation of approaches from a wide range of underlying philosophical standpoints. Orlikowski & Baroudi (1991) concluded on studying the approaches and underlying assumptions in research papers in IS that 95% were based on a positivist epistemology. livari (1991) identifies seven major schools of thought in ISD: software engineering, database management, management information systems, decision support systems, implementation research, the sociotechnical approach and the infological approach. He suggests that all seven schools adhere to positivism as their underlying epistemology, with only the school of decision support systems laying claim to any anti-positivistic notions.

Notwithstanding the conclusions reached by the authors referred to above, challenges to this dominance have arisen over the last decade or so from a range of different perspectives, in some part as a result of the influence of Burrell's and Morgan's four-paradigm framework (Burrell & Morgan, 1979) in the field of organizational analysis. Authors such as Boland (1985), Hirschheim (1985, 1992), Winograd & Flores (1986), Hirschheim & Klein (1989), Nissen et al. (1991), Galliers (1992), Hirschheim et al. (1995), Checkland & Holwell (1997), Myers (1997) and Stamper (1997) have all explored other paradigms in attempts to move away from over-emphasizing the technological at the expense of the human context for IS. Not surprisingly, as philosophies, methodologies and methods have proliferated, IS has also developed attempts to formalize complementarism, most notably Lyytinen & Klein (1985), who argued for Habermas's work on knowledge constitutive interests to support a broader perspective on research in the IS area. Other examples include 'multiview' (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990); the use of Giddens's structuration theory as a meta-theory (Walsham & Han, 1991); the 'front-ending' of 'hard' systems methodologies with softer, problem structuring techniques such as SSM (for example, Stowell, 1995; Savage & Mingers, 1996; see also Brinkkemper et al., 1996).

As to emancipation, it is possible to again highlight the work of Klein & Lyttinen (1985), who argue that IS design should seek to serve all knowledge constitutive interests — the emancipatory and the practical, not just the technical. Klein and Hirschheim (1987) build on this later. Markus (1983) and Robey & Markus (1984) focus on the interplay among power, politics and ritual in the ISD process. Although the concept of user participation in IS design is well established, particularly through the pioneering work of Mumford & Weir (1979) of the ‘sociotechnical’ school of thought, emancipatory methodologies attempt to go beyond stakeholder participation, to improve a problem situation for the disadvantaged, and to enable the disadvantaged to take responsibility for the process themselves. In IS/ISD since the 1990s, the notion of emancipation has been infrequently addressed; rather, the post-modern challenge has resulted in efforts towards understanding IS as social constructs, for example Poster (1990), Bloomfield (1992), Anderson (1994), Crowe et al. (1996) and Low et al. (1996).

In summary, Jackson (1997a) argues that whereas each commitment of CST has been addressed separately in the IS domain, their combination through CST can give a strong impetus to the development of IS as a discipline. He goes on to describe the use of CST in guiding ISD during a project carried out for North Yorkshire Police in 1991, claiming that adherence to the tenets of CST contributed to the success of the project. However, this raises the complex nature of ‘success’ and assessing the benefits (or otherwise) of an intervention, to which we return at the end of this section.

## Operationalizing CST

As stated earlier, putting CST into practice is problematic, as consideration of complex issues is necessary and inherent. Midgley (1996) clearly identifies CST as a research perspective; continuing the tradition of systems thinking, CST is inherently interventionist, with a declared intent to change or improve problem situations. As such, the use of CST in this intervention could be characterized as a form of practitioner-led action research, in that a need for change or improvement is identified, improvement is achieved (or not), and reflective critique is presented for the purposes of informing the theory and practice of CST and ISD. Thus, the intervention has commonality with other action research perspectives in the IS domain, such as Mumford's ETHICS (Mumford, 1995), Checkland's soft systems methodology (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Holwell, 1997) and Avison's and Wood-Harper's Multiview (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1991). These are all attempts to take into account not only technical considerations, but also the diversity of human perspectives, which impact on information systems design, development and implementation. One of the best-known attempts to put CST into practice as an action research perspective is total systems intervention (TSI). This was established by Flood & Jackson (1991a; presaged by Jackson & Keys, 1984; and Jackson, 1987). TSI has most in common with Multiview, as both are intended as meta-methodologies to orchestrate the complementary implementation of a range of methodologies in action research settings (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996). Thus, TSI and Multiview would claim to 'sit above' and guide the use of methodologies such as ETHICS and SSM. Whereas Multiview locates its pluralism in the consideration of the technical and the social at different times in an intervention, it is claimed that TSI's pluralism has a firmer philosophical foundation (Jackson, 1997b). Its creators stress that TSI is not to be applied as a 'recipe'. As an intervention progresses, participants' views of the main problem areas change, and there is a need, therefore, to continually cycle round the three phases, changing, as appropriate, that which is the dominant methodology. Decisions are made based on reflective critique at each stage, in the tradition of action research.

This first version of TSI consisted of three phases — creativity, choice and implementation. These are now described, followed by a summary of the critique in the literature.

In the creativity phase, managers are encouraged to ‘think creatively’ about their organizations, using various metaphors as ‘lenses’ to provide insight into different aspects of organizational problem situations. The metaphors of ‘machine’, ‘organism’, ‘brain’, ‘culture’, ‘team’, ‘coalition’ and ‘coercive system’, derived largely from Morgan (1986) are recommended. (Although TSI does not preclude the use of other metaphors.)

In the choice phase, the metaphors generated in the creativity phase are mapped to the 'system of systems methodologies' (SOSM) in order to match methodology to problem situation. In the SOSM, problem situations are mapped according to two axes: simple/complex and unitary/pluralist/coercive, according to the degree of (dis)agreement between participants. Problem situations are then mapped to methodologies according to Table 1.

The implementation phase consists of the application of the chosen methodology(ies) to yield change.

This early version of TSI has been subjected to considerable critique at both the theoretical and practical levels, not least by one of its creators (Jackson, 1999). Tsoukas (1993a, 1993b) and Spaul (1997) dispute TSI's claimed grounding in Habermas's three human interests and its claim to be meta-paradigmatic. Tsoukas (1993a) also remarks on the lack of conceptual development regarding the use of metaphor in the creativity phase. Brocklesby (1994) argues that the SOSM might be used post hoc to justify the use of a favoured methodology. Later, (Brocklesby, 1995), he also notes the difficulties for individuals in working across a range of paradigms. Mingers & Brocklesby (1996) note that TSI's emphasis on 'whole methodology management' is rather inflexible. Further criticism of TSI can be found in Ormerod (1992, 1996), Cummins (1994) and Midgley (1997a). Concern over the lack of available methodologies to deal with coercive situations is widespread, although it could be argued that this is not the fault of TSI itself. There are, however, some positive accounts in the literature, including Green (1993a,1993b) and Ho (1994), and, notwithstanding his own critique, Jackson (1999) still favours the 'spirit' of TSI and calls for its further development as 'critical systems practice (CSP)' (Jackson, 1999, p. 19). At the time of the intervention (1997), the most widely practised alternative guidelines (for a comparative review of alternatives, see Midgley, 1997a) for operationalizing CST was TSI's reconstituted form (Flood, 1995, 1996; and see Flood & Romm 1996b, 1997 for further accounts of subsequent developments), which adds a recursive structure to each of the three phases, with no recourse to the SOSM. Flood's local systemic intervention (LSI) is claimed to result from a post-modern critique of TSI, which encourages 'diversity management' through 'divergent creative thinking of different sorts' (Flood, 1996, p. 114). However, it was our view that the recursive structure made the approach rather unwieldy, a view later echoed by Tsagdis (1996). Thus, bearing all the criticisms in mind, the original version of TSI was chosen for this intervention.

## Summary

What, then, are the benefits and drawbacks that we can expect from using CST to guide an ISD-related intervention? One difficulty in identifying these is that there is as yet little in the literature focusing directly on the practical benefits of CST, rather hope and optimism that the drawbacks of earlier modes of systems thinking (hard, soft and cybernetic) will be overcome, while their benefits will be realized. Thus, CST should benefit from the highly structured ‘system building’ capabilities of the hard and cybernetic methodologies; human perspectives will be revealed and valued through soft methodologies; overall, a spirit of human improvement should prevail. At the practical level, this is a little nebulous, reflecting the concentration by many adherents of CST, at this early stage in its development, on philosophical and theoretical debate, rather than identification and publication of practical benefits. This is clearly an area which warrants further research. The still evolving nature of the subject is reflected in Midgley’s comment ‘There can . . . be no absolute definition of what it means to undertake a critical systems intervention’ (Midgley 1997b, p. 339).

There is a tendency in the literature to evaluate CST interventions on their own terms, with fairly brief discussions around the three underpinning commitments of critical awareness, emancipation and methodological pluralism (for example, Clayton & Gregory, 1997; Warren & Ellis, 1997). Midgley (1997b) goes a little further, arguing for more emphasis on exposing boundary judgements and increased awareness of the interdependency of the three commitments in evaluations of interventions: 'the possibility of methodological pluralism supports the practice of being critical of system boundaries, which in turn supports the ability of actors to pursue well-developed understanding of improvement' (Midgley, 1997b, p. 342; he also indicates that the sequence should operate in reverse order). Midgley defines improvement as 'when a desired consequence has been realized through intervention, and a sustainable improvement has been achieved when this looks like it will last into the indefinite future without the appearance of undesired consequences (or a redefinition of the original consequences as undesirable)' (Midgley, 1997b, p. 340). In common with Flood & Romm (1996a), he warns against universalizing understandings of improvement, making clear that it is relevant only to a given local and temporal context. Midgley's discussion is useful; inadvertently, he also provides an example of another difficulty with evaluation: evolving terminology. Here, Midgley uses the term ‘improvement’ from the original CST commitment in preference to the ‘grand narrative’ term ‘emancipation’, presumably to reflect a more post-modern perspective. Flood (1996), Ulrich (1996) and Jackson (1999) still use the term emancipation, but all in different ways.

Clearly, notions of benefits and drawbacks for CST interventions are not clear cut, they are problematic and context dependent. Summarizing Flood (1996) and Midgley (1997), the following criteria for success were generated:

1 Achievement of a desired consequence, as understood by the actors in the situation.

2 Sustainable improvement, locally and temporarily defined, as understood by the actors in the situation.

3 Improvement achieved through critical awareness and methodological pluralism.

We return to these criteria in the fourth section. In terms of drawbacks, there are concerns with both the theoretical underpinnings of both CST and TSI, which have been clearly identified above; further, there are concerns for researchers attempting to use TSI in practical situations. These must be borne in mind for future critique.

## THE INTERVENTION

In line with the action research perspective, this section begins with a description of the situation that generated the project, which forms the basis of the intervention. This is followed by a discussion of the intervention itself; the section concludes with a brief report of the practical outcome. In the next section, reflection and critique are presented through an exploration of the criteria for success listed in the previous section.

## The project

The setting is a medium-sized university in the UK with some 10 000 students and around 900 academic staff. The Academic Computing Services Unit (ACSU) is staffed by around 35 staff, including academic-related, technical and clerical grades. Collectively, they provide campus-wide IS support to all academic staff and students from a central location (the ‘centre’). The ACSU offers a wide range of IS services to support teaching, research and administrative activities. In brief, the ACSU was finding it increasingly difficult to respond favourably to an ever-increasing demand for user support.

Nationally, the growth in popularity of end-user computing over the past decade has placed pressures on IS support services; both user numbers and the diversity of applications in use tend to increase, often without concomitant increase in support service resources. This is particularly so at universities in the UK for the following reasons:

\- the doubling of student numbers since 1992 without corresponding increase in IS support staff numbers;

\- the heterogeneity of IS-related activity, as individuals pursue complex research-related activities;

● the increased interest in electronic communication (Internet access, for example);

\- the heavy training demand created by the entire student population changing every 3–4 years;

\- the additional support requirements created by recent externally funded initiatives to promote the use of computers in teaching and learning (the Computers in Teaching Initiative, the Information Technology Training Initiative and the Teaching and Learning Technology Programme);

\- the need to maintain a ‘leading edge’ technological image to attract students in a competitive market.

There was a recognition within the management of the ACSU that these national pressures were causing a local problem of increased demand, as manifested by queues at public access points in the centre. Various reports from ACSU staff and comments from service users suggested that the organization of the ACSU's 'front end', that is the way in which users' requests, queries and problems were handled, did not help the situation. The 'front end' consisted of the following elements. There were two official physical user access points in the centre — the 'Reception desk' and a separate 'help desk'. The Reception desk was publicized as the first point of contact; its staff dealt directly with a wide variety of simple enquiries, including small sales. More complex enquiries were referred to the help desk, or a specialist member of the ACSU staff could be brought in to assist. Telephone enquiries to Reception were also dealt with. At peak times, however, the volume of demand could lead to queues building up, both of users seeking information/help and of incoming phone calls needing attention. The help desk was intended to process more complex enquiries and could be accessed in person, by telephone, or by referral from the Reception desk; again, lengthy queues often built up. As queues lengthened, response times became unpredictable and users often began to get agitated and even aggressive. To complicate matters, hardware-related problems were investigated by a separate group of technicians who operated in a mobile fashion. They could be contacted by telephone on a separate line from the Reception desk, or via the help desk. The user had to decide who or which service to contact for best results, which sometimes meant queuing twice. To avoid queues, many academic staff would bypass both the Reception desk and the help desk by telephoning individual ACSU staff directly in their offices. Sometimes, the same user would try several different ACSU staff one after another until they were satisfied. From the ACSU staff's viewpoint, these ad hoc and unscreened interruptions were disruptive and frustrating. Unfortunately, there was no system of call logging or follow-up monitoring of enquiries, which often led to 'black holes' for users. Another difficulty was that powerful users of the ACSU would sometimes expect to 'jump the queue', regardless of the urgency of other problems in hand at the time. ACSU staff also commented on the unrealistic expectations of some users, who were often unaware of the inherent complexity of what might appear on the surface to be a 'simple' problem.

The ACSU management were aware that there was little that could be done to address the national concerns identified above. Moreover, there was little likelihood of a significant increase in resource at institutional level. However, it was felt that the situation might be alleviated significantly by initiating this project to improve the ACSU's ‘front end’, with the aims of shortening queues, maintaining fair queuing procedures, and clarifying lines of communication; moreover, there was a felt need for a culture change, in that the ACSU's activities should be more customer driven. Thus, the decision was taken to redesign the ACSU's activities around its front line service. The trigger for the initiation of this project was the opportunity for change presented when the long-serving Director of Computing Service retired; this led to the creation of a new post, Dean of Information Systems (DIS).

A steering group (SG) of three people, including the DIS and one of the authors of this paper (henceforth known as the 'Facilitator' for simplicity), was initiated. [The other author took little part in the mechanics of the intervention, acting (to use Baskerville and Wood-Harper's term) as a 'monitor' to review the research process and act as a sounding board for discussion and consultation as and when required during the intervention.] The Facilitator was also part of the design group (DG), which had four selected permanent members of the ACSU staff, although several other staff were invited or chose to attend meetings of the DG. Another group, the environment group (EG) worked closely with the DG on issues concerning accommodation and physical conditions. The Facilitator, through the DG, were primarily responsible for completing the project.

## The intervention

## Creativity phase

The first task of the creativity phase was an information gathering process carried out largely by the Facilitator to garner a wide range of stakeholder perceptions of the problem situation. The account presented here is based on:

● the past experience of the Facilitator;

● observations of the Reception area by both researchers;

\- the Facilitator's participation in project planning meetings as Facilitator;

\- documentary search of past minutes of the university's IT User Group meetings, which included representatives of staff, students and key groupings in the ACSU.

At an early stage, it was possible to define broadly the problem situation in systemic terms. Whereas the ACSU is our system of interest, the university should be included in the intervention as a wider containing system. The main activities of the university, research and learning, often involve the use of IT, with varying levels of support from the ACSU staff. The nature and frequency of the interaction is such that the users of the ACSU are perceived almost as part of the system, but not quite: a rather permeable system boundary. Although further consultation among representatives of the wider system would have been desirable, this was restricted to the minutes of the IT User Group meetings, as indicated above, plus ad hoc informal conversations with the Facilitator and other members of the ACSU staff. Further consultations did not take place because of time constraints.

It soon became clear that as part of becoming more customer centred, a new information system was required to support redesigned information flows both within the ACSU and between the ACSU and the university ‘customers’. A redesign of the Reception area was also necessary. At this point, the project became a major undertaking, in which the new design was analysed, designed and implemented by the ACSU staff for themselves.

The creativity phase suggests the use of metaphor analysis to gain insight for participants in problem situations. As researchers, we spent some time ourselves considering the neurocybernetic (brain) and organism metaphors for the ACSU internal communications and its relationship with the wider university respectively. Although this aspect of the creativity phase yielded some insight into this project, we did not pursue the idea at any length.

## Choice phase

The choice phase requires a mapping of the problem situation onto the SOSM.

We used the characteristics of the SOSM to attempt our mapping, rather than metaphor analysis, because metaphors were not emphasized in our creativity phase. It was easy to conclude that the problem situation was ‘complex’, in terms of the following characteristics (summarized from Flood & Jackson, 1991b):

● many elements and interactions;

\- loose interaction with probabilistic behaviour;

● evolved over a time;

● attributes not predetermined;

\- purposeful ‘subsystems’ with own goals;

\- open to environment and behavioural influence.

With regard to types of relationships between participants (the DIS, the DG, the EG and the other ACSU staff), given the overall general agreement between these participants about the project and the need to make improvements, it was possible to rule out the ‘coercive’ category. The ‘unitary’ and ‘pluralist’ categories are identified by the following characteristics:

The fit between the above and the displayed characteristics of the current situation is by no means clear cut. It was concluded eventually that the situation could be mapped onto the ‘unitary–complex’ sextant of the SOSM. However, the participant type could be placed somewhere along the ‘unitary’–‘pluralist’ continuum, i.e. the perception of agreement/disagreement varied between participants, particularly between groupings within the ACSU. The need for an action-oriented approach resolved this ambiguity, resulting in the choice of Mumford’s ETHICS (effective technical and human implementation of computer-based systems) (Mumford, 1995)

to allow for participation in the project and to allow plenty of scope for considering organizational issues. QUICKethics (quality information from considered knowledge) was devised as an aid to determining the information requirements of the new system. QUICKethics is presented by Mumford as the ‘front end’ to ETHICS and claims it is based on Beer’s viable system model (Beer, 1981, 1985), which was attractive to us in view of the strong need for improved communication in the new system. The claim that it is based on the VSM has been subject to critique, however (Adman & Warren, 1998). Given the extent to which participant pluralism was evident in the analysis, the use of soft systems methodology might well have been beneficial; however, this option was ruled out through lack of time.

## Implementation phase

Mumford presents QUICKethics as the ‘front end’ to ETHICS and implies that the two should be linked closely together in usage, although how this should occur is not clear (for further discussion of QUICKethics, see Avison & Fitzgerald, 1995). In this case, it seemed more appropriate to us to use the two methodologies separately, with ETHICS (the more open-ended consultative phase) generating an agreed design, followed by the application of QUICKethics to generate information requirements for that new design. Thus, the complementarist principles of TSI were followed, with different methodologies becoming dominant at different stages of the intervention. Our expectations were that the ‘softer’ participative phase of ETHICS would foster a spirit of ownership over the decision-making, which was needed as to how the new system would operate. QUICKethics would provide a ‘harder’ framework for rigorous treatment of information flows in the new system. Lack of space presents a full description and critique of the implementation phase. Hence, a summary only is provided here; a fuller treatment is presented elsewhere (in a forthcoming publication by Adman and Warren).

In summary, ETHICS has a sociotechnical pedigree, and consists of a set of logical, sequential steps claiming to enable technical requirements and human needs to be considered side-by-side. It is not necessarily aimed at producing a computer-based solution because the emphasis is on participative design with the right balance between the social and technical aspects of the complete system. ETHICS claims to attach an equal importance both to human factors, such as ‘needs’ and ‘job satisfaction’, and to ‘technical factors’. However, it is characteristically positivist, aiming to identify ‘cause and effect’ relationships to solve problems. ETHICS provides a sequence of steps, which can be summarized as below, although there have been variants presented (for example, Hirschheim, 1985b).

1 diagnose user needs and problems;

2 set efficiency, effectiveness, job satisfaction and quality objectives;

3 develop a range of alternative designs to meet those objectives;

4 choose the most appropriate strategy from the above;

5 choose hardware and software, design the system in detail;

6 implement the new system;

7 evaluate the system once it is operational.

Mumford provides further detail on how these steps are to be addressed. In terms of Mumford's guidelines, steps 1–4 were approached fairly systematically through DG discussions, the preparation of lists by the DG and others, and interviews of selected staff by the Facilitator. Although with regard to information gathering some ground had already been covered in the creativity phase, the discussions now took place with more focus and direction. Steps 3 and 4 seemed to collapse into each other as a strategy for the design emerged from the discussions, rather than any formalization and choice of set alternatives. In other words, a ‘working model’ for the new system emerged and was refined through a series of iterative discussions. New roles, tasks and responsibilities were identified and consolidated.

QUICKethics was then brought in, essentially embedded within ETHICS at step 5, to address the requirements of the working model. QUICKethics identifies five levels of activity, or information requirements, as illustrated in Figure 1. Each level must be addressed if a ‘viable system’ is to result.

Staff were asked to examine the new roles in the model and list the kind of information necessary to support performance at the various levels. The Facilitator collated this information and produced a summary of the new system, which was explained to all ACSU staff in three staged workshops; further modifications were made during these workshops. The decision to accept and implement the new system was unanimous (albeit with some trepidation!).

## REFLECTIONS

As identified earlier, the notions of success and failure for CST, and indeed ISD, are problematic. We now examine the criteria presented in the Critical Systems Thinking and Information Systems section (above) in turn.

1 Was a desired consequence achieved as understood by the actors in the situation?

The answer to this would be yes, the design was derived, accepted and implemented with the unanimous agreement of all the ACSU staff involved in the project. Although no formal evaluation has yet taken place, informal feedback from the ACSU staff, and the staff and students of the university has been very positive. Query handling now operates in a streamlined way; customer focus has improved, and the Reception environment is more pleasant and relaxed.

2 Is improvement sustainable, locally and temporally defined?

Again, all the indicators so far are positive. Midgley (1997b) counsels that all understandings of improvement are bounded in the sense that a limited set of actors consider a limited set of variables in making those judgements. Hence, there is a continuous need to be critically aware of what boundary judgements are being made. In this project, the DG needed to take care in handling the ACSU's boundary with its customers, indeed that was one of the key focuses of the project. Although participation by customers was somewhat limited, this does not seem to have generated any difficulties so far, in that customer feedback has been good. There were also boundaries to consider during the design process. First, the internal boundary between the ACSU staff who were not part of the DG, but who nonetheless would be affected by the new system in a significant way. Second, there were the hierarchical boundaries between the Facilitator and the rest of the DG, and also between the DG and the SG. The flexible participation in the design process meant that, in practice, the boundary between the DG and the ACSU staff became blurred, which mediated against marginalization or exclusion, even though information gathering was somewhat selective and time spent constrained. Concerning the hierarchical boundaries, no significant conflicts arose during this project to give rise to any serious notions of coercion or discussions of power issues, so it would be inappropriate to extrapolate from our notion of ‘understanding’ to that of ‘emancipation’. However, it would be naive to say that pre-existing tensions were not noticeable among staff: they were omnipresent at the microlevel of individual conversations, the ‘jockeying for position’ and the ‘small victories and defeats’ commonplace in everyday conversation. These sometimes needed sensitive management as they occurred.

## 3 Was improvement achieved through critical awareness and methodological pluralism?

Different methodologies (ETHICS and QUICKethics) were used in a complementary manner in accordance with the principles of TSI, i.e. each becoming dominant at a different stage in the project. We were constantly aware of the different assumptions underlying the chosen methodologies, and their consequent strengths and weaknesses. The use of ETHICS certainly militated against the tendency of some to jump to a technological ‘solution’. It is difficult to say how much of this could be attributed to the CST/TSI process, and how much resulted from the considerable experience and sensitivity of the Facilitator and his long-established working relationships with many of the team in question. However, even for a neophyte practitioner, it is hard to imagine that the CST/TSI process would not engender some degree of critical awareness.

Overall, we would conclude that this was a successful ISD project and that the use of CST was a contributory factor in that success. We now return to the theoretical and practical weaknesses of both CST and TSI.

Taking a broad perspective first, a number of authors, including Ellis (1995b), Herrscher (1996), Hutchinson (1996) and Warren et al. (1998), have noted a lack of acceptance of (critical) systems thinking outside the academic community. Even using fairly familiar methodologies, the learning curve for CST/TSI was steep — if any deep understanding beyond the ‘recipe’ is to be achieved. Overall, we were far more muted in our regard for TSI than for CST. Our experience with TSI confirmed the findings of others to a large extent. The most obvious danger is that there would be an attraction for some in using TSI merely as a problem-structuring recipe to achieve a degree of accountability with a neatly documented end product without reference to the underlying commitments. In practice, TSI was rather unwieldy, with little sense of any cycling through the phases in an iterative way, once the methodologies were chosen — yet attempts to formalize this process, for example Flood (1995) referred to earlier, appear even more cumbersome. Given this unwieldiness, it is not surprising that some of those involved in the project, who were working in a very busy environment, were at times reluctant to engage with the nature of the methodologies being used (ETHICS and QUICKethics), let alone the meta-methodology of TSI. Not surprisingly, the Facilitator had to guard against the project becoming ‘expert driven’ at times, thereby detracting from the participatory ethos (a finding also noted by Green, 1993b). In terms of emancipatory praxis, as noted earlier, little conflict was unearthed during this situation, except at the microlevel. The processes of power evident during this intervention did not directly impact on what was a successful outcome. Should they do so, however, CST provides no practical guidelines on how to proceed. It is clear that we know very little about the ‘language games’ of management interventions and perhaps this is where future research should be directed.

Looking at the stages of TSI, we did not find the metaphorical analysis presented as part of the creativity phase to be very useful in this project. This reflects Tsoukas's (1993a) concern that the question of how analogies and metaphors can be conceptually developed to yield understanding has not been seriously addressed by many who seek to use them in a somewhat unrestrained way. We would argue for a shift towards the qualitative study of user-generated metaphors in the early stages of a project, along the lines of Kendall & Kendall (1993) or, more recently, Warren (1999). The SOSM of the choice phase found greater favour (in contrast with Green, 1993b), although it was a rather blunt instrument, given that most situations requiring organized intervention are likely, almost by definition, to be deemed complex. Whereas the SOSM should be used critically for guidance, there is little to guard against descent into pragmatism. As to implementation, we did not consider ourselves to be working at a metaparadigmatic level, rather hopping from one set of assumptions to another in a strangely disturbing way. Jackson (1999) refers to the need to orchestrate and manage critique between paradigms, rather than above, through a process of ‘reflective conversation’ (Morgan, 1983) and calls for further research in this area. We would support that call and would suggest that in-depth studies of the conversations which take place during live interventions would be a useful place to begin.

## CONCLUSION

This paper began by setting out the agenda for improvement confronted by the applied discipline of information systems. Arguments for the potential value of critical systems thinking to the IS discipline were then put forward. The main part of the paper described a practitioner-led intervention using CST to derive a design for a new information system. By reflecting on this project, it was concluded that CST contributed to the success of the project. In terms of future efforts, more research on the practical benefits of CST, and how such benefits might be identified, needs to be carried out. Our enthusiasm for TSI was much more muted than for CST, which may at present be a barrier to CST's adoption in ISD-related situations. The potential value of CST would suggest that this would be a productive area for future research. Our final conclusion, at the time of writing, is that CST can be regarded as a useful position, philosophy or way of thinking, albeit theoretically flawed, which does not as yet have a corresponding way of acting, i.e. we still have some way to go towards developing the modus operandi of ‘critical systems practice’, the term mooted by Jackson (1999). On the basis of our reflections on our (successful) intervention, we concluded that the CST/TSI journey (and our intent to produce this account of it) focused too much of our attention as researchers on the application of the (meta-) methodologies themselves, i.e. the technology of the intervention, at the expense of studying the process of intervention, i.e. how events unfolded at the microlevel through the play of language of the actors in the situation: the minor ‘victories and defeats’ commonplace to everyday conversation. Our view is that future development of CST, theoretical and practical, needs to take place through working with practitioners in ‘live’ situations to gain insight and understanding at this microlevel. This would suggest that we look to post-modernism for theoretical inspiration, in common with, inter alia, Ormerod (1996) asserts, it may be ‘...at least possible that post-modernism offers an underlying philosophical stance that could support consulting practice’. At present, the post-modern endeavour in systems thinking is largely directed towards the orchestration of diversity through methodological pluralism, with a spirit of contextual sensitivity. Perhaps we should now turn our interest to the ‘language games’ of intervention.
