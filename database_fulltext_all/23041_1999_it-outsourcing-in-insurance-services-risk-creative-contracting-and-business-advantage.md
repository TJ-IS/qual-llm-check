# IT outsourcing in insurance services: risk, creative contracting and business advantage

Leslie P. Willcocks & \*Mary C. Lacity

Templeton College Oxford, Kennington, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK, e-mail: leslie.willcocks@templeton.oxford.ac.uk; and \*School of Business Administration, University of Missouri, 8001 Natural Bridge Road, St. Louis MO63121-4499, USA, e-mail: mary.lacity@umsl.edu

Abstract. Risks in IT outsourcing have received little systematic academic attention, despite growing evidence of wide disappointment and difficulty as well as some successes in outsourcing IT. This paper explores the 1993–98 case history of Polaris, a software intermediary in the European insurance sector. Despite the considerable risks inherent in outsourcing business-critical technology, Polaris was found to be achieving business advantage from such a strategy. This paper details 10 major risk areas in IT outsourcing distilled from previous research. It focuses on the sourcing, contractual and management choices made at Polaris. It assesses, against prior research findings, the degree to which these choices mitigated short- and long-term risk, and whether learning points flow from the analysis for other IT outsourcing arrangements.

Keywords: Competitive advantage, contracting, information technology, outsourcing, risk

## INTRODUCTION

The growth in the significance and size of outsourcing deals is paralleled by increasing concern with the actual management of an outsourcing venture, and in particular with the issue of risk mitigation. Risk here is taken to be a negative outcome that has a known or estimated probability of occurrence based on experience or some theory. Risk is the likelihood of loss as a consequence of uncertainty (Willcocks & Margetts, 1994). Risk is a key issue because of the mixed press and results that emerge when both the trade and academic literatures are reviewed (see, for example, Loh & Venkatraman, 1992; McFarlan & Nolan, 1995; Strassmann, 1995; Lacity & Willcocks, 1996, 1998; Poston, 1997; Thomas & Schneider, 1997; Klepper & Jones, 1998; Collins & Phillips, 1999; Delaney, 1999).

In information systems studies, risk has been heavily researched in certain areas, but in particular for software development (see for examples only Boehm, 1991; Griffiths & Newman, 1996; Lyytinen et al., 1998) and in the related area of project management (examples include

Willcocks & Griffiths, 1994; Keil, 1995; Morris, 1996). Although these studies often embrace examples in which external IT suppliers are involved, in practice this has rarely flowed into detailed analyses of risks in IT outsourcing. Indeed, types of IT outsourcing risk, the salience of these, and their mitigation have received surprisingly little systematic attention in the academic literature, the exceptions mainly being Earl (1996); Ang & Toh (1998); Jurison (1998); Klepper & Jones (1998); Lacity & Willcocks (1998); Willcocks, (1998).

Empirical research into the 1991–98 period has shown that ‘total’ outsourcing (80% or more of the IT budget under third-party management), especially long-term, large-scale, single-supplier, deals have been particularly risky (Strassmann, 1997; Lacity & Willcocks, 1998, 2000). Thus, as one example, Willcocks & Lacity (1999) analysed 116 sourcing decisions over 8 years, assessing objectives against outcomes, cost savings expected against those achieved, and ‘satisfaction’ levels in client organizations. They found 38% of total outsourcing arrangements successful, 35% a failure and 27% with mixed results. By comparison, 77% of selective outsourcing arrangements (typically 15–25% of the IT budget under third-party management) were successful and 20% ended in failure, whereas 3% had mixed results. (The in-house sourcing comparison was 76% successful.)

Even so, large-scale total outsourcing deals have continued to be entered into with, for example, the Inland Revenue-EDS, Sears-Andersen Consulting and British Aerospace-CSC arrangements signed since 1994 in the UK; similar deals have also been signed elsewhere in the 1990s, for example Xerox-EDS in the USA, Lend Lease-ISSC and Commonwealth Bank-EDS in Australia, and United Bank of Switzerland-Perot Systems in Switzerland. If such deals exhibit the highest risk, then nowhere does the outsourcing of IT emerge as a low-risk enterprise, unless those risks are offset by the adoption of specific practices. It becomes important therefore to identify the salient risks and to examine how they unfold and are handled in specific situations, with a view, if possible, to identifying commonalities and lessons.

Although there is a limited literature on which to draw for the identification of salient risk, an exploratory, analytical framework can be distilled from case study and survey work by Lacity & Willcocks (1998, 2000) and others. Drawing on this work, the main reasons emerging for failure/negative outcomes in IT outsourcing deals have been various combinations of:

● treating IT as an undifferentiated commodity to be outsourced;

\- incomplete contracting;

\- lack of active management of the supplier on contract and relationship dimensions;

● failure to build and retain requisite in-house capabilities and skills;

● power asymmetries developing in favour of the vendor;

● difficulties in constructing and adapting deals in the face of rapid business/technical change;

\- lack of maturity and experience of contracting for and managing ‘total’ outsourcing arrangements;

\- outsourcing for short-term financial restructuring or cash injection rather than to leverage IT assets for business advantage;

● unrealistic expectations with multiple objectives for outsourcing;

● poor sourcing and contracting for development and new technologies.

(See also Auwers & Deschoolmeester, 1998; Willcocks & Fitzgerald, 1994a, Lacity et al., 1996; Thomas & Schneider, 1997; Ang & Straub, 1998; Ang & Toh, 1998; Currie & Willcocks, 1998). Even supporters of 'total' outsourcing to a single supplier — such as Polaris represents here — point out that it can be a high-risk strategy (McFarlan & Nolan, 1995). Such risks, it has been suggested, can be mitigated by adopting a multiple vendor approach (Cross, 1995; Hoffmann, 1996) or by a selective outsourcing approach on shorter-term contracts (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; Willcocks & Fitzgerald, 1994a, 1994b). More recently, within the context of large-scale single supplier outsourcing, we have seen attempts at more creative and mature approaches to leveraging deals in order to gain business advantage. These include 'value-added' outsourcing whereby vendor and client combine their capabilities to market IT products and services; client and vendor taking equity holdings in each other; 'co-sourcing', involving 'performance-based' contracts; and the creation between the vendor and client of a 'spin-off' company selling IT services on to the wider market (Currie & Willcocks, 1998). There have also been signs of more creative and informed forms of contracting in order to mitigate risks across long-term deals (Willcocks & Lacity, 1998).

Much of the academic attention in all this has focused on two areas that we will also investigate further in the Polaris case study, namely decision-making frameworks and contracting. But, when it comes to risk mitigation, what has lacked serious academic attention, we will argue, are two critical areas that demand managing. As pointed out by Willcocks & Fitzgerald (1994b) and Feeny & Willcocks (1998), one of these is retained in-house capabilities. The second area is the formation, development and sustaining of client–vendor relations. As already explicated in academic research, IT outsourcing agreements eventuate in interorganizational relationships as a result of the resulting dependency that arises (Grover et al., 1995; McFarlan & Nolan, 1995; Kern & Willcocks, 1996). Paradoxically, however, with a few notable exceptions (Henderson, 1990; Klepper, 1994, 1995; McFarlan & Nolan, 1995; Willcocks & Choi, 1995; Kern, 1999), the area in IT outsourcing that has received the least research attention so far is this relationship issue, and particularly the characteristics that determine effective and ineffective outsourcing relationships.

## RESEARCH APPROACH

The Polaris case study offers an opportunity to examine these issues, and how the client organization sought to mitigate risk. Polaris is an innovative IT outsourcing arrangement in which the supplier operates as an applications management service for a company whose main line of business is selling software products and services in the UK insurance sector. As such, the arrangement is one of ‘total’ outsourcing, though, as will be seen, it represents a ‘selective’ one for each of the insurance companies investing in Polaris. The case provides considerable learning on how risks can be mitigated and the lessons are considered in the analysis section after the case history.

The research consisted of 45 face-to-face semistructured interviews among 15 Polaris, insurance company and five Logica staff at different times across the 1996–98 period.

Interviewees in each organization ranged from senior business and IT managers to operational staff. Interviews were also tied to site visits. First interviews were typically between 40 and 75 min in length, whereas follow-up interviews were of 20–40 min duration. Each was recorded and transcribed, the total comprising 326 single-spaced pages of material. The companies also made a range of documents available throughout. These included internal memos, strategy plans, operational control documents, meetings of minutes. In addition, we utilized published sources on the insurance sector and on individual companies, including annual reports. In seeking information, our search was focused on understanding the context in which sourcing decisions were made and operationalized, the chronology of events held to be significant by respondents, differing and common interpretations of these events, difficulties and issues. This focus included respondent observations on risk factors and how they developed, were managed, and with what outcomes.

Data analysis followed closely the approach described in more detail in Willcocks & Kern (1998). Briefly, Sanders (1982) prescribes four steps for interpreting transcribed interviews. First, the researcher describes the facts of the phenomenon, with facts here understood as socially agreed realities; agreed, that is, among all the respondents (though checkable against other sources also). In step two, researchers determine how participants ascribe meaning to reality by how participants perceive cause and effect. In step three, researchers identify themes across the interviews and, in step four, we assess our own views as to how the accumulated evidence can be interpreted, and whether specific patterns, themes and interpretations emerge from the rich data. This last set of interpretations makes up the bulk of the case study. As Lincoln & Guba (1985) and Craig Smith (1990) point out, the validity of interpretive analysis defies quantification. However, so far as space constraints allow, we have included excerpts from the transcribed interviews to help readers to judge for themselves the validity of our analysis (Table 1).

## CASE STUDY: POLARIS — IN SEARCH OF BUSINESS ADVANTAGE

## Background

Polaris was set up in 1993 by seven major UK-based insurance companies. The objective with Polaris was, in the words of one senior insurance company manager, to ‘co-operate in order to compete’. By creating and utilizing a common set of standard software tools, the insurance companies using the indirect broker–customer channels hoped to compete more effectively against the direct insurers coming into the market. By late 1998, some 34 insurance companies and syndicates, together with their brokers and software houses, were using Polaris software in the UK market (internal documents). According to four managers in Polaris and allied insurance companies, there were also possibilities of utilizing the software in Internet and satellite distribution channels, and for more complex insurance products.

In the early 1990s, direct writers, particularly the highly successful Direct Line company, made major competitive inroads into the very large UK markets for more commodity type products, such as motor and household insurance. As new start-ups with no legacy systems, and dealing directly with customers through the telephone, direct insurers could typically achieve a cost advantage of between 5% and 10% off the price of insurance premiums relative to the more traditional insurance company–broker–customer channel. A Polaris director indicates the further business problem:

‘Before Polaris, it could take up to 6 months to code up a new insurance product, and 2 months just to change an existing product. This meant that if the premium structure altered it was taking up to 2 months to get the information disseminated into the different broker software packages, during which time you could be writing business with poor premiums for the customer, or unprofitable for the insurance company’.

There was the added problem of thousands of brokers holding different information about the same products:

'It's not so much that there's somebody in the middle, it's the fact that there are so many — hundreds or thousands — who could be in the middle, and that they should all be having the same information.' (Account manager, Logica)

The net result was that the broker-based channels used by the major insurance companies were losing significant amounts of business.

Polaris was set up to reduce significantly the costs of the broker distribution channel to insurers, software houses and brokers, and allow insurers to provide better, competitively priced products and amend their rates, terms and conditions quickly. The issue was to give the broker channel the same sort of competitive costing and response time possessed by the direct channel. These business goals were to be realized by creating a common platform for new software for licensing to insurers and software houses, instead of allowing each software house to use its own development methods. Software houses would need to undertake the one-time task of incorporating interface programs — provided by Polaris — into their own systems.

In the past, insurers had used a number of software houses to develop their products, each using their own methods. This approach pushed up the costs of bringing products to market. It also meant brokers were using different software products. A further point of concern for the insurers was that the software houses had assumed a large measure of control over the production and distribution of insurance policies.

## The Polaris solution

Polaris's plan, as detailed by insurance company and Polaris personnel, was to develop a single platform of industry-standard software templates and formats to help insurers and software houses to bring products to market more cost-effectively. As a consortium, the members sponsoring Polaris did not want to invest in building a large infrastructure for the project. A small team of people was seconded in from the member companies in order to manage the development. Initially, the team relied heavily on consultants and contract programmers to do the programming and systems analysis and design. In practice, the volume of software grew more rapidly than expected as the initiative expanded from motor policies to household, small business insurance and other lines. As one senior manager at Logica observed:

'I guess it was a measure of its success that this thing actually started to run away with itself, and before they knew it this small team of contractors had grown into quite a large one, and to some extent self-fulfilling, and was starting to behave like a software house, which in many ways it was.'

Over the next 3 years, two major pieces of software were developed. Sitting in the insurer's office, productwriter was designed to enable insurers to define insurance products — the data to be captured, type of policy and form to be produced, the insurance rates and algorithms to be used on those rates, and the format of electronic trading messages. The insurer can produce a diskette or send the product electronically to the software house. Here, a second software product called run time environment (RTE) was designed to integrate productwriter with software house and broker systems, allowing for onward distribution of products to brokers. The objective was to achieve the dissemination and availability of product and rate changes within 48 hours. Brokers gain by also obtaining a wider choice of up-to-date products from many more insurers, plus the benefits and speed of electronic trading. This is across the whole product-processing cycle, including fast and accurate quotations, mid-term adjustments and policy renewals.

The overall operation is shown in Figure 1. The figure also illustrates how the insurance industry standards body — the Association of British Insurers (ABI) — provides data standards to Polaris, which is then also responsible for maintaining a data dictionary of standard insurance information drawn upon by underwriters in the insurance companies. The ABI is responsible for market data in the dictionary, but Polaris is wholly responsible for the data dictionary and its distribution and integration. Members of the Polaris community requiring enhancements to the live dictionary make their requests directly to Polaris, who then manage the ratification process through the ABI.

## Moving to outsourcing

After 2 years of development, Polaris managers found they were more engaged in running a software house than an insurance business. Too much time was spent on looking after the system and not enough on how to extend its coverage in the market among insurers, brokers and software houses:

‘We were getting distracted; we were solving problems and in retrospect it was mostly useful front-end stuff, but time was passing and the sponsors were getting frustrated with lack of market penetration.’ (IT operations manager, Polaris)

Polaris also had some expensive contractors on board, allied to a lack of management clarity and control in the IT area. The Logica account manager offered a further view:

'It's fair to say that quite a lot of the money going into Polaris was actually being wasted because it wasn't being spent on things that would really improve its coverage in the market; it was perhaps playing around with interesting technology'.

Moreover, according to insurance company respondents, there was a lack of commercial discipline in the relationship between Polaris and its shareholders. Was Polaris to make money as a software house or was it a strategic investment with the benefit coming back indirectly to investors through more insurance business? By 1996, the weight of director and shareholder opinion was that Polaris needed to be profit generating through software development and that it needed a partner who understood the software business to develop and maintain its existing and future commercial systems. According to a Polaris director:

'The hardware was irrelevant, the value was all in the software. What we wanted was a very professional company with a good understanding of the insurance business to take responsibility for the future of our software.'

Six vendors bid for the outsourcing contract, and these were reduced to a shortlist of three. The managing director of Polaris outlined the selection criteria used:

'The basis of decision factors were, firstly, quality in terms of the delivered product and the way the software was developed and maintained. There's a lot of maintenance in this software. Cost saving was fundamental — the Polaris directorate were concerned about the growth in development costs and wanted that more tightly managed. We also wanted to reach the stage where we could look for more stability in the software — by knowing what base we were working from. We also realized that other technologies were coming along — like the Internet and digital television — that the Polaris solution was suitable for, but we did not want to build up expertise in these ourselves. Thus, the range of expertise and the ability to pull in specialist skills easily and quickly were important. We also needed an uncontroversial outsourcing partner because there were already software houses in this market, so we needed one acceptable to all because of its reputation'.

In early 1997, Logica was confirmed as the preferred supplier and, after a due diligence period of 3 months, the companies signed a 7-year £14 million (\$US22 million) contract. The price was fixed for 3.5 years, with Polaris having the right to terminate at that point if they wished. Subsequently, 6 months' notice of termination would be required from either side, with Logica obligated to smooth any transition to any other arrangement or supplier. Logica were contracted to provide a helpdesk service on the existing software and dictionaries, training, development and implementation capability and support for new releases, including to software houses and insurers (100 person–days per quarter), software maintenance, technical consultancy, and technical support to the selling process. This was to be paid for on a fixed-fee basis, which subsequently covered 90% of Logica's revenue from the contract, with extra work charged by time and materials (details from contract and internal memos). The description by a Polaris manager met general agreement among other respondents:

'It's provision of an applications management service? it's primarily people. It really is the outsourcing of essentially a software business that forms a discrete entity within a group of insurers. What's nice about it is it's very clear what its business is — to build and support a first class software product for the business benefit of the sponsors so that they can enable the intermediaries to do better business.'

In fact, according to both Polaris and Logica respondents, Logica had not been in the business of application management for very long, but then nor had any of their competitors. Polaris chose to mitigate risk by retaining responsibility for general management, sales, marketing, financial control overall, and, in terms of the systems, for the logical business descriptions of the functionality required. This was to cover logical process and data descriptions, and building the logical data models against which the Polaris dictionaries are written.

Before the contract was signed, the two sides spent much time designing a service level agreement that covered every aspect of the work, with over 20 sets of measures of performance, and also points at which terms could be varied. Thus, service levels were set in terms of critical software faults fixed in a defined time period, and response within a defined time, and tasks were defined in terms of, for example, number of days' development time, or number of training courses. According to an IT contract manager at Polaris:

'We definitely wanted detail in the SLA, and it was good because that's how they [the supplier] also wanted to work. We argued over that detail, but it was pretty clear by the end that we both knew it was workable arrangement — they were getting the level of return they needed, and we could monitor closely what was being done — remembering this stuff was pretty essential for our own clients.'

## Progress at Polaris 1997–99

In the 1997–99 period, the Logica systems team stayed a relatively stable 30 in number, with 16 staff on the Polaris side. External contractors were reduced from 25 to 15 in number, these now becoming paid and under the control of Logica. The total number was expected to reduce as major developments came to an end. Cost efficiencies were achieved by replacing external contractors with permanent Logica staff, introducing better procedures in the support effort, utilizing Internet and e-mail communication for administration and fully documenting processes and the software, once developed. Additionally, as part of the new disciplines, Logica now made sure that a business case was presented to the Polaris advisory board for every major development in the systems area. According to one senior manager at the supplier, Logica's first priority was to stabilize, then improve, the level of service:

'There was a real risk when we took this over of the wheel falling off, that all the contractors would walk out, and that Polaris's credibility would just plummet, because they'd say — they've outsourced it, it can't be any good?'

Respondents from both Polaris and the supplier generally agreed that the changes represented real improvements in operations. The two service objectives were achieved during 1997 and early 1998, with levels of service levels being revisited annually thereafter.

Although Logica brought control and clarity into management and service, Polaris also sought to mitigate risk by their own policy on retained skills. In the 16-strong Polaris group were people building the logical data dictionaries, doing accreditation testing of EDI messages, managing the contract, providing consultancy support to insurers writing products using productwriter, and two doing systems support and project management. The group included a considerable amount of technical know-how, but of a specified type. According to the Polaris managing director:

'You cannot give up technical know-how, it just doesn't work . . . we have technical knowledge in EDI message design, the use of productwriter to build business products, in-depth know-how on the dictionary. Where Logica is involved is more in the development of the underlying tools, and changes to those or to the physical dictionaries. We are on the logical side, they (Logica) are on the physical side, but we do maintain a degree of technical expertise in the application of the business skills. I haven't given that away and have no intention of doing so either.'

Additionally, a business planning group was formed to develop a view of Polaris several years out. This continued to run into 1999. According to various stakeholder respondents, while the insurers and brokers represented provide very much a business view, at the same time they produce a view of what the technology must do at the logical level, and Logica, also represented on the group, then determine how it can be physically implemented, and make the business case to the Polaris Board.

According to insurance company and Polaris respondents, Polaris also mitigated risk through three people responsible for detailed monitoring of the contract. These dealt daily with the

Logica service delivery manager, although by 1998 there was also a monthly review meeting of the four main Logica and Polaris managers. The service level agreement and measures acted as the mainstay for both client and supplier, while development falling into or outside the 100 person-days per quarter fixed price were controlled and signed off through the business planning group. Price changes for Logica staff were tested against the market, although the way the contract was written Logica offered their rates at a discount from their standard rates. The market situation found both Logica and Polaris concerned about pricing, as one Polaris manager revealed:

'The price controls are pretty important to us both, given the labour shortages and price volatility we have been experiencing — no one here sees an end to that at the moment.'

There were change control procedures also written into the contract in which either party could ask for the pricing and measurement mechanisms to be varied in the event of major changes, such as that occasioned by the 1998 expansion of Polaris into business and commercial lines.

## Future developments

As at early 1999, Logica had stabilized and improved the service and Polaris was functioning very successfully in the market with over 36 companies and syndicates using the software and plans for 50 or more within 3 years. Cost savings were coming through individually for the main insurer, software house and broker stakeholders. The technology was also allowing brokers to focus on factors in the insurance products other than just price. The indirect market was holding up well against the direct insurers, and Polaris was assisting in that process. In mid-1998, a merger between Polaris and CLMI — operating in commercial lines — expanded the insurance products that productwriter would cover. According to a Polaris senior manager:

'I think the way Polaris will go forward is we will broaden the number of business classes and within each business class we'll become capable of handling more complex business.'

Moreover, Polaris technology allowed new channels, in particular Internet and digital TV, an easy way of developing and selling insurance products. Ironically, as one Polaris respondent pointed out, this meant that Polaris could also be sold to direct insurers, enabling them to cut their costs as increased competition in the direct markets — not least from traditional indirect channel insurers also using direct channels — pushed up the marketing costs. In all these scenarios, Logica, or any other supplier operating as the development shop for Polaris, remained a fundamental building block for the development of future business, providing support to brokers and insurers, development and refinement of software, technical understanding, and control and clarity in technical management.

## ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION OF THE POLARIS-LOGICA CONTRACT

The degree to which business marketplace advantage for Polaris has been premised on IT outsourcing is in fact unusual (for evidence, see Lacity & Willcocks, 2000) and cannot be appreciated fully without understanding the business context in which the IT supplier — Logica — is being relied upon to perform. Polaris and its software products have been at the heart of a significant competitive strategy designed by major players in the general insurance market aimed at bolstering failing profitability and growth in the indirect broker channel against the direct insurers. The original aim was to reduce costs, and so be price competitive in the major motor and household insurance markets. But, by the late 1990s, Polaris products were expanding to other insurance classes, in particular commercial lines, could handle more complex forms of insurance, and could also support product differentiation in different insurance markets. It could also be plugged into more direct channels relatively easily, for example Internet and digital TV. In these circumstances, outsourcing represented a refocusing strategy, allowing Polaris staff to develop the business, a cost-containment strategy, and also a way of guaranteeing up-to-date technical skills and know-how in a difficult, supply-led IT labour market.

At the same time, high dependence on the supplier represents a significant, if conscious, business risk for Polaris. The creative, risk aspect is allowing a supplier to be so responsible for aspects of a commercial IT product and service as opposed to back-office tasks and the more obvious ‘useful commodities’ such as, typically, datacentres, payroll and IT maintenance tasks. This means that the initial selective sourcing and vendor selection were in fact business-critical decisions. Moreover, active management, creative contracting on an on-going basis and close working with the supplier are undoubtedly business-critical tasks. The risk-mitigating practices adopted at Polaris are shown in Table 2 against the risk factors discussed earlier.

Given that the business risk from outsourcing was so high, and that Polaris has been effective in both business terms and in its outsourcing arrangement, it is valuable to analyse in more detail how risk was mitigated and what risks remain. We achieve this by blocking the analysis into five primary areas that emerge for us from the case, namely outsourcing type and scope, vendor selection criteria and process, the contract, active management of the supplier, and retained capabilities and skills. We subsume the points summarized in Figure 2 into the analysis that follows, and compare the findings here against previous research.

## Outsourcing: type and scope

Organizations run large risks when they are not clear about the overall business rationale, cannot clarify to themselves the detailed financial and technical reasons for going down the IT outsourcing route, and fail to carry out the necessary analysis needed to arrive at realistic expectations about what IT outsourcing can achieve (Willcocks, 1998; Willcocks & Lacity, 1999). The strength of the Polaris approach to outsourcing has been the very clear business rationale to outsourcing — it allows Polaris staff to focus on growing the software business, while the supplier provides development capability and implements and supports the technology/software. Moreover, the inter-related financial and technical reasons were strong and clear.

On detailed analysis, given the small scale of Polaris and the reluctance of its shareholders to move further into technical capability, a supplier could provide more effective cost and operational management of the development and maintenance shop. Moreover, although it has not been always the case (see Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; Lacity & Willcocks, 1998), suppliers were indeed available who could provide the requisite up-to-date technical expertise at more competitive prices than Polaris itself could achieve.

Polaris also made clear distinctions between ‘core’ and ‘non-core’ business and IT assets and activities, only outsourcing what they perceived as the ‘non-core’. Thus, Polaris retained intellectual property rights and ownership of all its software products and services, essentially only outsourcing technical ‘doing’ skills and their management. The outsourcing of software development has often been experienced as high risk (Ang & Toh, 1998; Willcocks & Lacity, 1998; Kumar & Willcocks, 1999). However, the software products were largely stabilized before outsourcing and some in-house technical understanding was maintained. Moreover, for future systems development, the business and logical requirements were the preserve of Polaris staff, whereas the supplier was mainly responsible for their technical implementation — all recognized as risk-mitigating factors in prior studies (see, for example, Willcocks & Griffiths, 1994; Keil, 1995; Lyytinen et al., 1998). It was also clear that the small size of Polaris did allow a degree of partnering and mutual learning to develop over time. This offset the risk all too commonly affecting development effort, especially as a consequence of outsourcing, namely that of disconnection between business requirement and technical development because of failure to work as a multidisciplinary team (Feeny et al., 1997; Ang & Toh, 1998).

At the same time, much of the Logica technical effort at Polaris has been support and maintenance. The outsourcing arrangement might prove less effective if the business required major new software developments, in which case the lower risk approach would be to contract-in resources to work under Polaris management and also build up in-house technical understanding (Willcocks et al., 1997).

## Vendor selection: criteria and process

Poor vendor selection criteria and process have been one source of relative lack of success in IT outsourcing deals (Michell & Fitzgerald, 1997; Currie & Willcocks, 1998). Polaris offset the latent risks by being very clear about the requirement (see the Managing Director's six-point description above). It was also clear that, because quality of resource was business critical, cost was not, and could not be, the dominant discriminating criterion. Previous research has shown that selecting the lowest bid can incur risks of deteriorating service and opportunistic behaviour (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; Lacity & Willcocks, 1998). This detailed specification pointed to a niche IT services player with experience and good understanding of the insurance business, and with a successful track record of providing skilled, technical resource on a project management basis. Because the provision of high-quality skills was paramount, the supplier also had to have a good track record of working successfully on small as well as large contracts. This specification ruled out virtually all the big outsourcing players as strong candidates.

Risk was also mitigated by inviting competitive bids and comparing these with the internal service. Earlier work has shown the positive impacts on risk amelioration by such a practice (Lacity & Willcocks, 1996). A further risk-mitigating practice suggested by previous research (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993; Willcocks et al., 1995) saw Polaris establishing detailed metrics against which the supplier performance could be monitored. Moreover, this was achieved in a 3-month due diligence period and included in the contract before it was signed, thus reducing the risk of subsequent extracontractual disputes diverting management effort (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993).

## The contract

In the Polaris–Logica case, there is no doubt about the consciously central role of the contract in mitigating risk. Historically, long-term contracts have turned out inflexible in the face of business volatility and rapid technological changes (Lacity & Willcocks, 1998). One more recent trend shown up in 1998 survey work has been more staged contracting (Willcocks & Lacity, 1999). This can be seen in the Polaris deal with the possibility of termination after 3.5 years, and a right to terminate with 6 months notice thereafter, though the declared intention is a 7-year contract period. Risk is also mitigated by a continuity clause requiring the vendor to smooth transition to any preferred arrangement in the event of termination. The contract also has highly detailed price–service level resource requirement stipulations, with mechanisms in place for regular review to ensure all prices are competitive. Regular review builds in the contractual flexibility needed to deal with changes in volume and/or type of business and technical requirements. There are also a battery of 20 sets of performance measures against which the supplier is monitored regularly and can be penalized on.

## Active management of the supplier

Robust contracts mean little if the client then sees outsourcing as ‘spending, not managing’ and turns away from active management of the supplier (Currie & Willcocks, 1998). The significant risk here lies in consequent failure to leverage (and redirect as necessary) vendor performance. In fact, up to early 1999, Polaris was found to be maintaining very active management of the supplier, reflecting the high business dependence on the supplier’s ability to perform. Three people were monitoring Logica’s delivery against contract and on-going requirement, and there were regular reviews between operational and senior management staff from both companies. The business planning board met regularly to set future direction and also acted as a sounding board and sign-off for business cases for IT changes suggested by the supplier.

Polaris staff became much more involved in hands-on management of IT rather than just monitoring the supplier, the closer the tasks moved to eliciting detailed business requirement. At the same time, Polaris did not retain all four capabilities required for active supplier management detailed by, for example, Feeny & Willcocks (1998), namely informed buying, contract facilitation, contract monitoring and vendor development. The strongest areas at Polaris would seem to have been contract monitoring and vendor development. The risks of not having high performing capabilities in all these areas, but diluting these roles across a few individuals, is partly explicable by the relatively small size of Polaris, and so of the management task. Additionally, the small size means much more personal interaction and informal facilitation between supplier and Polaris staff generally, thus developing the relationship dimension more strongly than is often possible in larger deals. This can offset the risk by providing more informal channels for active management of the supplier, as suggested for example by Kern (1999) and Klepper & Jones (1998).

## Retained capabilities and skills

Research has shown that nine core IS capabilities need to be retained in the contemporary organization in order to run any IT sourcing regime effectively (Feeny & Willcocks, 1998). The business-facing capabilities are relationship building and business systems thinking. The technical capabilities relate to technical architecture and technology ‘fixing’. Governance is provided by leadership and informed buying capabilities (see above for the three other market-facing capabilities). According to Feeny & Willcocks (1998), organizations with weaknesses in any of these nine areas incur associated difficulties. Some struggle on, whereas others have been observed to offset such risks by developing gradually the type of capabilities that were initially missing. In the case of Polaris, the first point to make is that the relatively small size of the business, and of the IT operation, will inevitably mean a dilution of the fully fledged model, though one would still expect the nine components to be represented somewhere within the in-house team.

The second point is that, in fact, Polaris has not outsourced all of its in-house IT — a small in-house IT office function has been retained, and its role and relationship with the business managers of Polaris remains unproblematical. It is also the case that the business side of Polaris — dealing with and selling to the insurers, software houses and brokers — is very much the preserve of Polaris staff. However, as Logica staff's understanding grew, they were increasingly utilized in support roles here, as technology experts — an indication of the growth of the relationship dimension in the outsourcing arrangement and another risk mitigation factor (Kern, 1999). Polaris also retained business systems thinking capability in its management group, expressed in the Business Planning Board. Relationship building was a less distinctive capability. We noted this capability as spread through several roles, but estimated it as less necessary for two reasons: the small size of the business, and also the strong interactions we observed as having developed between Polaris and supplier staffs.

Much the more significant area, however, where risks were paramount and needed to be offset, was in the creation and maintenance and support for the Polaris software products and services. The issue of retained skills for leveraging supplier performance has already been discussed above. Polaris would seem to have offset much of the risk of handing over its technology future to a supplier in two main ways. The first was to be clear that Polaris itself would be responsible; this was expressed by Polaris retaining in-house skills for arriving at the detailed business requirements for all future technical developments. In this scenario, Logica were responsible for technical implementation only — a lower risk route, recognized as such by research (Boehm, 1991; Willcocks et al., 1997; Kumar & Willcocks, 1999). Second, Polaris retained a great deal of ‘doing’ technical know-how in specific areas — namely building logical data dictionaries, EDI message design, use of software to build business products, systems support and project management. According to a Polaris director, these essentially involved what he termed ‘technical expertise in the application of business skills’. Interestingly, however, what Polaris had ceded to the supplier, quite consciously, was the ‘technical fixing’ capability relative to the software products — something that our own research shows should be retained in-house (see above). This, perhaps, was the creative part of the Polaris–Logica contract but also was the biggest reason for dependence on the supplier, and so represents the biggest business risk in the contract.

Undoubtedly, into 1999, this continued to be the most significant business risk, but it was being offset somewhat by careful choice of a suitable supplier, strong contracting and monitoring practices, relationship development over time and retention of some technical capability in-house. There was also the high-profile nature of the work, and reputational aspects in the market that earlier research has found to act as an incentive to supplier performance (Willcocks & Fitzgerald, 1994a). One also needs to countenance the business risk of not using the supplier. After all, Polaris could not provide for itself high-quality, cost-effective, well-managed development people during a critical period in its business development; in practice, outsourcing successfully allowed Polaris to redirect its attention into the business rather than the technical areas.

## CONCLUSION

The Polaris case helps to illustrate the multiple sources of risk in IT outsourcing arrangements. Polaris is a total outsourcing deal in which management and delivery of business critical software and technology has been ceded to a single supplier. Ostensibly, and based on previous research findings, this represents a high-risk approach. However, much of this risk has been mitigated: by careful delineation of outsourcing type and scope, strong vendor selection criteria and processes, detailed relevant contract terms, retained capabilities and management processes, and close vendor–client partnering. Risks always remain in such business ventures, and their mitigation is highly dependent on retained skill and active, anticipatory in-house management. Polaris would seem to be successful because of the initial awareness of risks, subsequent application of many principles that also happen to be supported in the research literature, and because of the creative contracting and partnering aspects that formed the foundation for this particular outsourcing arrangement.

In many ways, Polaris may become typical of more creative and less risky ways forward for those organizations contemplating the ‘total’ outsourcing route. As such, many of its practices may be analytically generalizable across other such deals, though the relatively small size of the Polaris operation was a significant risk-mitigating factor in itself, and needs to be borne in mind when attempting to apply these lessons to billion dollar ‘total’ outsourcing deals. Such large-scale IT outsourcing arrangements introduce an altogether different level of complexity and associated lack of clarity into the management puzzle, and also introduce more obviously than at Polaris the possible advantages, but at the same time risks and additional management costs, of a multiple vendor approach as practised into 1999 by, for example, BP Exploration, ICI and JP Morgan (Cross, 1995; Hoffman, 1996; Currie & Willcocks, 1998; Lacity & Willcocks,

2000). In such cases, as Kern (1999) has found, securing the mature client–supplier relationships that we observed in the Polaris case becomes much more difficult, but their achievement becomes both an expression of and a contribution to effective risk mitigation in IT outsourcing.
