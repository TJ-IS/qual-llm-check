# Virtual positions: an examination of structure and performance in ad hoc workgroups

Caisson M. Vickery, Thomas D. Clark, Jr\* & John R. Carlson $^{\dagger}$

Acquisition Center of Excellence, National Reconnaissance Office, OC/ACE, 14695 Lee Rd, Chantilly, VA 20151, USA, email: vickeryc@nro.mil, \*Louisiana State University, College of Business Administration, Baton Rouge, LA 70803-6302, USA, email:

tclark@unix1.sncc.lsu.edu, and ${}^{\dagger}$ Baylor University, Hankamer School of Business, Department of Information Systems, PO Box 98005, Waco, TX 76798-8005, USA, email: John\_Carlson@mis.baylor.edu

Abstract. This paper examines the strength and performance of virtual positions within an organizational setting. Access to US Air Force systems acquisition teams provided a unique opportunity to study the phenomenon for a large number of virtual positions in a stable setting, thereby controlling for the differing aspects of organizational structure that usually make such positions difficult to study. A measurement scheme for the strength of the virtual position is developed and validated. A hierarchical regression analysis of research constructs dealing with power, autonomy, allegiance, structure and performance confirms that stronger virtual positions perform better in complex rather than simple task situations. Finally, managerial implications are discussed.

Keywords: Ad hoc workgroups, IT-facilitated workgroups, virtual organization, virtual positions, virtual teams

## INTRODUCTION

Organizations are increasingly facing dynamic environments that require high-speed adaptation (e.g. Baskerville & Smithson, 1995). Often, the time frame in which change is required is too small for the organization to respond with formal structural changes; increasingly, organizations are utilizing virtual positions to respond to the increasing dynamic complexity (Mackenzie, 1986a; Hircshhorn & Gilmore, 1992; Mowshowitz, 1992). Virtual positions are temporary groupings of workers that meet current task requirements without formal change to the organization. Whereas traditional organizations are often rigid and inflexible (Mintzberg, 1983), the use of virtual positions augments the flexibility of such organizations by allowing for temporary and informal adaptations.

The availability of sophisticated information technologies has both enabled and encouraged the formation of virtual positions within a single ('parent') organization or between two separate organizations to cope with the degree and velocity of change (Mackenzie, 1986b). The virtual position is an ad hoc temporary grouping of individuals with diverse abilities and responsibilities to respond to task requirements in ways that are not specifically delineated in the organizational charts of an organization. One of the challenges in studying such groupings is the difficulty in comparing them because of their diverse membership and goals.

For this reason, the field of government contracting — specifically, procurement of US Air Force systems — provides a rich context for the study of virtual positions. In this field, a Systems Program Office (SPO) contains a number of agencies responsible for procuring various system components. These agencies provide the basis for the architecture and power structure of the organization. Within this structure, temporary ad hoc acquisition teams form that are composed of members having differing parent organizations; as such, these acquisition teams are virtual positions within the SPO. As there are a relatively large number of them operating at one time, an adequate sample of them can be accessed to provide statistical validity. In addition, there are a number of teams in an SPO with a common purpose, providing the consistency of task and culture without which research controls are difficult. The structure of the teams and SPO will be discussed in detail in Methodology.

These teams are made up of a contracting officer, a programme manager and their corresponding subordinates. The relationship between the contracting officer and the programme manager has been an issue of long-standing concern for effective acquisition practices in the Air Force. Looking at this structure in the context of virtual positions provides a unique opportunity to study this emerging theory. Although some task responsibilities are clearly spelled out by regulations and office instructions, others are often performed by the virtual positions. Moreover, the subordinate workers for the two offices provide a classic example of conflicting allegiance. Allegiance can be to the programme management office or the contracting office (the parent organizations) or to the group specifically performing the task at hand (the virtual position). As such, the allegiance of the members of this group, along with the structural aspects of the group's design, will have integral effects on group outcomes. This combination of allegiance structure and group architecture defines the strength of the virtual position. Groups with more internalized control and decision-making designs and allegiances tied to the group are considered to have stronger virtual positions.

The research reported here examined how the strength of virtual positions affected performance and focused on the antecedents of that performance. Our purpose is to advance the understanding of virtual positions and to provide insight into effective ways of developing and managing these important new organizational forms.

## THE NATURE OF VIRTUAL POSITIONS

Organizations relate to each other for many reasons and through many different conduits (Levine & White, 1961; Weick, 1969). Many types of interorganizational relationships have been extensively studied (e.g. Pfeffer & Salancik, 1974; Pfeffer & Nowak, 1976; Schoorman et al., 1981; Harrigan, 1984; Schopler, 1987; Borys & Jemison, 1989). However, the new framework of organizational change suggests that increased interconnectivity and interdependence driven by advanced information technologies will supersede traditional organizational structures and ties (e.g. Scott-Morton, 1991; Baskerville & Smithson, 1995).

The virtual position is a relatively new form of organizational relationship. Virtual positions are considered to be one type of virtual organization (e.g. Shao et al., 1998). Although some research has been performed on the effects of the virtual position (Mackenzie, 1986a, 1986b; Larson, 1992), the organizational phenomena that promote this form of linkage have largely been ignored. In addition, the virtual position has been treated as either existing or not (Mackenzie, 1986b; Mowshowitz, 1992); no measure of the level or strength of the virtual position or its antecedents has been put forward.

The virtual position is a temporary network of independent departments, groups or people linked together to share skills, costs and resources. The virtual position is formed to fill a void where tasks arise that are not covered by the fixed organizational structures of any of the parent organizations; virtual positions may form within a single organization or between multiple independent parent organizations. Virtual positions are formed to cope with organizational or environmental changes and are considered to be natural phenomena (Mackenzie, 1986a, 1986b). Central to the idea of the virtual position is the formal or informal communication network that develops between the parent organizations (Mackenzie, 1986a; Mowshowitz, 1992). As such, the availability of advanced communication technologies supports the rapid development of virtual positions within a firm and is seen as their central enabling technology (e.g. Mowshowitz, 1997).

The virtual position is similar to a matrix structure in which each membership is based on project or task, however the phenomenon of the virtual position remains unique and the differences between the two structures are important to note. The matrix structure takes two major forms: the permanent matrix and the shifting matrix (Mintzberg, 1979). In each of these forms, the membership of the work group is based on the project or task at hand, however in the permanent form, as indicated by its name, the membership is unchanging. The shifting form, in contrast, allows for flexibility in membership as projects change.

There are two significant differences to discuss. First, the regulation and clarity of reporting structures in matrix forms and the virtual positions are distinct. In both cases of the matrix structure, the hierarchical structure of the interdependencies is clearly delineated (Mintzberg, 1979); however, in the virtual position, the clarity of the reporting structures is more blurred. Second, for each of the matrix structures, the membership of the work group is permanent for the life of the project. Work groups are defined based on projected tasks and remain static based on the task (Hodge & Anthony, 1991). However, the membership of the virtual position may be in flux during the life of the project (Mackenzie, 1986b).

It is also important to note the distinction between regulated and unregulated virtual positions. Regulated virtual positions, which are the closest in nature to the matrix structure, are those that have set memberships and are formally recognized by the organizations. On the contrary, unregulated virtual positions are ‘interwoven into the normal organizational architecture in haphazard and idiosyncratic, confusing and often illegal ways' (Mackenzie, 1986b; p. 635). Virtual positions that exist between regulated and unregulated conditions (for example, with defined leadership but changing membership) may best be described as semiregulated.

The virtual position has been studied in terms of its regulation, i.e. as either regulated or unregulated, but it is suspected that it is the strength of the virtual position that will most affect performance. In addition, the degree to which the virtual position is regulated, in the sense that its decision-making authority is predetermined by the parent organizations, is a determining factor in the effect the virtual position has on the allegiance structure of its members. We believe that membership in semiregulated or unregulated virtual positions may create non-traditional commitment structures.

The virtual position has been shown to provide certain efficiencies in the areas of innovation and flexibility (Mackenzie, 1986b; Pennings & Harianto, 1992a). In fact, the propensity to network through such forms has been shown to be the prime predictor of innovation (Pennings & Harianto, 1992b). Because of their ability to respond to situations without the encumbrance of organizational inertia, these forms of networked relationships are more flexible and adaptable than more traditional organizational designs (Larson, 1992).

## RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESES

Using this background, a research model describing the strength of a virtual position and its effect on performance was developed (Figure 1). The model suggests that characteristics of the parent organization and the nature of the relationship between the virtual position and the parent organization will influence characteristics of the virtual position. It is these characteristics of the virtual position that determine the strength of the virtual position. Finally, the strength of the virtual position will have an effect on performance, moderated by task complexity.

## Architecture of the parent organization

The antecedents of a virtual position lay in the parent organization(s). The architecture of the parent organization is the design of social and work systems that make up the organization. It is made up of structural variables, centralization and formalization, all of which are related to the restrictiveness of the organization's control of organizational members and subunits (e.g. Nadler et al., 1992; Ouchi, 1977, 1978, 1979). Centralization is the level of control and responsibility that is retained by the parent organization and formalization is the lack of autonomy employees are given to carry out their assigned tasks through strict rules and regulations.

Structure is imposed on the organization as a means of control (Fredrickson, 1986; Miller, 1987). A restrictive structure would have low empowerment and autonomy of employees, low delegation and span of control, while maintaining high specialization and departmentalization. Likewise, more restrictive structures would tend to have higher degrees of centralization and formalization. Thus, the structural restrictiveness of the parent organization is seen to influence the strength of the virtual position through its effects on member allegiance and the decision-making autonomy of the virtual position. Higher levels of restrictiveness would be expected to cause members of the virtual position to show more commitment to the parent organization than to the virtual position. Therefore,

hypothesis (H)1: a restrictive structure in the parent organization will be negatively related to virtual position allegiance in members of the virtual position.

Structure restrictiveness reflects the control strategies of the parent organization (Mintzberg, 1983). Moreover, through tight coupling, this structure is expected to be reflected in the ‘control climate’ of the virtual position. Parent organizations with more restrictive architectures are expected to increase the perceptions of the restrictiveness of the control climate in the virtual position. Therefore,

H2: a restrictive structure in the parent organization will be positively related with perceptions of a restrictive control climate in the virtual position.

## Relationship between parent organization and virtual position

The nature of the relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position is defined by the power retained in the parent organization and the levels of formal supervision and reporting required of the virtual position by the parent organization. Mackenzie (1986a) describes the level of supervision and reporting as determining factors in the existence of the virtual position. The higher the frequency of required reporting, the less likely the virtual position is to form. Likewise, high levels of formal supervision will discourage the existence of the virtual position. By exercising power in the relationship, the parent organization can restrict or give freedom to the virtual position.

The sources of power have also been shown to exert influence over other organizations (Pfeffer, 1981; Yukl & Falbe, 1990; Falbe & Yukl, 1992). The sources of power are control of resources, control of information and solidarity, whereas the bases of power are coercive power (threat of retribution), reward power (offer of reward), persuasive power (ability to persuade) and knowledge power (legitimate expertise) (Pfeffer, 1981). These are important to the extent that the parent organization uses power to maintain control of employees. Strict retention of power forces commitment to the organizational structures and causes allegiance to the organization.

This relationship can be said to be tightly or loosely coupled. High levels of retained power, formal supervision and formal reporting indicate a tightly coupled relationship between the virtual position and parent organization; the converse is true of a loosely coupled relationship. Thus, certain organizational structures and power relationships will encourage characteristics of the virtual position that produce a strong virtual position. By retaining power, the parent organization maintains high influence over its subparts (Yukl & Falbe, 1990; Falbe & Yukl, 1992). By retaining the power, the parent organization forces commitment to the parent organization. Likewise, the levels of formal reporting and supervision required will influence member allegiance through the normative and continuance obligations to the parent organization (Meyer & Allen, 1991). If supervision and reporting levels are high, individuals will be forced into obligations to the parent organization (normative) and be more likely to fear potential consequences of their actions in the virtual position (continuance). Therefore,

H3: a tightly coupled relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position will be negatively related to high virtual position allegiance in members of the virtual position.

The levels of perceived autonomy and potency define the control climate of the virtual position. Likewise, the nature of the relationship between the virtual position and the parent organization is defined by retained power in the parent organization and the levels of formal supervision and required reporting. Because high levels of retained power indicate influence and control strategies, such levels will be consistent with tightly coupled relationships. This retained power will reduce the autonomy and potency of the virtual position. Similarly, high levels of formal supervision and required reporting will reduce the autonomy and potency of the virtual position. Therefore,

H4: a tightly coupled relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position will be negatively related to perceptions of potency and autonomy in the virtual position.

## Strength of the virtual position

The virtual position is defined by identifying issues of task authority, rules and regulations, and by supervision (Mackenzie, 1986b). By modifying Mackenzie's identifying factors, a measure of strength can be developed. As a result, strength of the virtual position is defined by three issues: first, the freedom to use unique solutions or scripts to solve problems; second, the degree of goal autonomy for members of the virtual position; and third, the degree to which individuals feel their performance in the virtual position will affect the formal evaluation of their overall performance. Member allegiance and the control climate in the virtual position are posited to be the major determinants of the strength of the virtual position.

## Member allegiance

Member allegiance is defined as the level to which the virtual position's members feel committed to the parent organization or to the virtual position. Member allegiance is a product of organizational commitment and commitment to the task. Organizational commitment has been clearly shown to affect the effort put forth by individuals (Porter et al., 1974; Allen & Meyer, 1990; Meyer & Allen, 1991). Here, allegiance is viewed as a multidimensional construct: allegiances will lie towards the parent organization, the virtual position, or to both.

Allegiance to the organization is seen to develop in three levels: continuance (the lowest level of commitment) is based on avoidance of costs or retribution from the organization; normative commitment is based on feelings of obligation to the organization; whereas affective (the highest level of commitment) is based on a deep, personal involvement in the organization (e.g. Mowday et al., 1979). The matrix shown in Table 1 represents the affect of these allegiance measures on the virtual position's ability to function separately from the parent organization. Resulting autonomy will be seen in the measures of strength of the virtual position: use of unique solutions, independence of goals and perceived effect of performance on evaluation.

The relationships in Table 1 constitute the postulated operationalization of allegiance and will be examined in the analysis. The cell defined by low virtual position commitment/high parent organization commitment is hypothesized to restrict the strength of the virtual position. The cell defined by high virtual position commitment/low parent organization commitment is hypothesized to strengthen the virtual position. The cells defined by low virtual position commitment/low parent organization commitment or high virtual position commitment/high parent organization commitment are hypothesized to have little affect on the strength of the virtual position.

## Control climate in the virtual position

The perceptions of autonomy and potency of its members determine the control climate in the virtual position. By its very nature, the virtual position will be strengthened through variables that encourage potency and autonomy among its members. It is the strength of flexibility and the ability to adapt to a dynamic environment that characterizes the potential advantage of the virtual position. Both autonomy (through task and outcome interdependence) and potency affect task effectiveness (Shea & Guzzo, 1987). Autonomy is the extent to which members feel free to perform their tasks and potency is the belief by members that the group can be effective (Shea & Guzzo, 1987). The control climate of the virtual position can be restrictive or nonrestrictive. A restrictive climate in the virtual position is postulated to reduce the strength of the virtual position.

In summary, as member allegiance in the virtual position shifts from the parent organization to the task, the members will have more allegiance to the virtual position. This allegiance combines with the restrictiveness of the control climate of the virtual position to define the strength of the virtual position's empowerment. Therefore,

H5: the interaction of member allegiance of the virtual position and the climate of the virtual position will be positively related to the strength of the virtual position.

## Performance

Theory holds that increased performance is a major reason for using virtual positions, which perform best when tasks involving high uncertainty exist (Mackenzie, 1986a, 1986b). Government contracting procedures are unique in terms of the results expected from the process. For example, the contract manager is expected to negotiate a ‘fair and reasonable’ price rather than the best possible price (Federal Acquisition Regulation). Although there are multiple possible measures of performance, examining customer satisfaction best addresses performance in this context. Customer satisfaction itself is a multidimensional construct that will be explored in more detail in the Methodology section. It is posited that stronger virtual positions will be more able to meet the needs of the task because of the ability to deal with unique and uncertain situations (e.g. Mackenzie, 1986a, 1986b), and therefore will achieve higher customer satisfaction. Thus,

H6: the strength of the virtual position will be positively related to its performance in terms of customer satisfaction.

## Task complexity

For tasks of low complexity, the design or existence of the virtual position is less important because there is less need for the organization to adapt to complex demands. As a result, low-complexity tasks will cause no resulting increase in performance related to the strength of the virtual position. That is, simple tasks may be accomplished equally well by a variety of organizational forms. As task complexity increases, it is expected that the role of virtual position strength in determining performance will become more important. Therefore,

H6a: the relationship between the strength of the virtual position and performance will be moderated by task characteristics.

## METHODOLOGY

Data used to investigate these hypotheses were collected using a multimethod approach based on surveys, interviews and the analysis of archival data. The data were collected at the Aeronautical Systems Center at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, OH, USA. This Center is responsible for the acquisition of aircraft for the Air Force. The Center consists of individual buying entities, or SPOs. Each SPO is responsible for the purchase of a major aircraft or for the systems attendant to a specific mission, such as reconnaissance. SPOs are autonomous operating units headed by a senior executive and are analogous in action to for-profit organizations. SPOs contain multiple parent organizations and a variety of virtual positions (the acquisition teams) exist within each SPO. In this research context, the virtual positions are typically engaged in developing request for proposals (RFP), communicating with vendors concerning the project requirements, evaluating proposals and vendor selection. Virtual positions generally last between 3 and 6 months, depending on the size of the contracting project. Membership in a virtual position is voluntary, although the programme manager will attempt to promote a desired mix of competencies and experiences (e.g. Österlund, 1999).

The instrumentation found in Appendix A was distributed to acquisition teams within six of the 10 available SPOs at the Center. Officials within the Center identified the available teams; the only restrictions to access within the Center were based on the security classification of the projects involved. In addition, team history was not accounted for in this research because of the strict request of anonymity by the organizations involved. As such, no data were collected about teams' previous working relationships or success.

## Subjects

Eighty-four teams (consisting of 273 individuals) representing over half of the active teams at the Center responded to the survey. Respondents ranged in age from 23 to 63 years with $85\%$ being men. The majority of respondents were non-managerial $(38.6\%)$ or low-level management $(33.3\%)$ . Only $25.8\%$ reported being mid-level management and $2.2\%$ reported being upper-level management. The majority of respondents $(56\%)$ reported having attained a master's degree, $39.7\%$ reported a bachelor's degree only, whereas $1.1\%$ reported higher than a master's degree.

## Construct measurement

Scales measuring the constructs of interest were selected or originally developed for this research (see Appendix A). All survey scales were based on a seven-point Likert scale using a selection of anchoring values (described below). A brief discussion of each scale, along with its performance in this survey, is presented next.

## Architecture of the parent organization

This standard scale is composed of 16 items measuring the dimensions of centralization and formalization (Aiken & Hage, 1966). The respondents to this scale were the individual team members. This application of the scale had reliability (coefficient alpha) of 0.87.

## The nature of the relationship

This scale was originally developed for this research and is based on constructs identified as important to this relationship: power, formal supervision and formal reporting. A 15-item scale was developed to tap these perceptions in individual team members. The scale had a reliability of 0.74.

## Member allegiance

Allegiance is represented by standard organizational commitment measures of affective commitment (a deep personal involvement in the organization), normative commitment (based on feelings of obligation to the organization) and continuance commitment (the avoidance of cost or retribution from the organization). This allegiance must be measured for the team members in terms of both their allegiance to the virtual position and their allegiance to the parent organization. A 15-item scale (Mowday et al., 1979) was adapted for use on each allegiance dimension. Both subscales had reliabilities of 0.92 in this application.

## Control climate of the virtual position

This seven-item scale was also developed for this research and is based on the constructs of perceived autonomy and potency of the individual team members that compose the virtual position. In this research context, the scale had a reliability of 0.79.

## Strength of the virtual position

This strength is operationalized here to have three levels: weak, moderate and strong. These levels are measured by examining the following characteristics based on Mackenzie's definition of the virtual position: the freedom to use unique solutions rather than routine standard operating procedures, the existence of autonomous goals in the virtual position (relative to the parent organization), and the perception that performance in the virtual position has impact on the formal evaluation of its members. An eight-item scale was developed to measure these dimensions and possessed a reliability of 0.75.

## Task complexity

Task complexity is operationalized here to have three levels: low, moderate and high. A high-complexity task is a ‘new task’ (i.e. a task that is perceived as requiring new thought and to be independent from previous tasks). In this research context, buying a new product is a high-complexity task requiring large amounts of new information and problem solving. Moderate complexity is represented by the ‘modified rebuy’ (i.e. a decision based on previous purchases; although previous solutions are similar, they do not fit in this situation; as such, the modified rebuy requires limited problem solving). Low-complexity tasks are ‘straight rebuys’ (i.e. a decision based on the purchase of a previously acquired item requiring little additional problem solving or information) (Hutt & Speh, 1989). Measurement of this construct is based on examination of secondary data from case files and interviews with respondents.

## Performance

The dependent variable in the model is operationalized here as customer satisfaction. Six representative interviews with contracting and programme managers were conducted to examine this proxy. Each interviewee was experienced in the field of government acquisition and the issue was discussed at length in an open-ended style. The interviews revealed four major issues as important: satisfaction with the process, satisfaction with the speed of task completion, satisfaction with the product, and satisfaction with the cost of the product. An eight-item scale was developed to tap each of these four aspects of customer satisfaction. The scale was administered both to individual team members and to an identified end-user representative. The end-user representative acts as a liaison between the acquisition team and the operational community (and is not a member of the virtual position). The reliability of the scale in this application was 0.84.

## Instrument validation

Before data collection, a pilot study was conducted to verify the structure of the model and the accuracy of the instrumentation. Members of acquisition teams were given the instrumentation and then interviewed using the structure of the model as a guide. The information obtained through this process was generally supportive of the model and scales; the definitions of constructs and purpose of the instrumentation appeared clear to the test subjects. As such, the instrumentation required only minor modification. This instrumentation was then used to survey the research sample.

The data were subjected to item and factor analysis (the reliabilities are reported above). Item analyses indicated no appreciable ability to increase the reliability of any scale by dropping any items. In addition, the data were factor analysed (Table 2). As seen in Table 2, the factors suggested by theory emerged from the analysis with two exceptions: power retained in the construct ‘nature of the relationship’ and autonomy of goals in ‘strength.’ A discussion of these two results follows.

First, theory suggests that the factors that make up the nature of the relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position are the level of formal supervision, the level of reporting required and the power retained by the parent organization. Both the level of supervision and the level of reporting emerged in the factor analysis. However, power retained by the parent organization split into two factors: power (positive) and power (negative). When statistically forced to the expected three factors, power retained by the parent organization did emerge as unidimensional. Briefly, an examination of individual items reveals that the power (positive) items measure an individual's ability to complete his or her job. The power (negative) items measure a dependence on the parent organization. This indicates, and follow-up interviews confirmed, that individuals differentiate between an ability to do one's job and the dependence on help. However, because this differentiation (although interesting) is not significant to this research, power was treated as unidimensional in the analyses.

## RESULTS

Given the structure of the model, what is a dependent variable in one instance will be an independent variable in another. Because of this, hierarchical regression analysis is the preferred method to partition the total variance in the model (Pedhazur, 1982). The moderating variable, task complexity, was tested using interaction terms in the regression analysis. In addition, the results of these analyses were verified through follow-up interviews with key respondents. This combination of approaches provides greater confidence that the results we report here accurately capture the behaviour and consequences of the virtual positions studied. The model was tested on both the team and the individual level.

Hypotheses 1–4 were tested at the individual level of analysis. This level of analysis is dictated because individual members of the virtual position are answering questions about different parent organizations, making averaging responses across members invalid. However, the team level analysis was used for hypotheses 5, 6 and 6a. This level of analysis is appropriate here because data may be averaged to obtain accurate measurements for these constructs. A discussion of the analyses starting at the right side of the research model (following the structure of the hierarchical regressions) is presented next. Variables were entered in three blocks: control, strength and characteristics of the virtual position.

Hypotheses 6 and 6a, relating to the moderating effect of complexity, are tested by models 1 and 2 (Table 3). The proper way to test for a moderation effect is through interaction (Venkatraman, 1987), however an initial test of the interaction term proved to be non-significant (P = 0.1619). The model was then tested with complexity as a direct effect on performance (Table 3). As a control variable, complexity explains $\approx 9\%$ of the variance in performance at a significance level of P = 0.0006. Hypothesis 6a is therefore not supported, but complexity is identified as an important control variable with a direct effect on performance. In support of hypothesis 6, strength and complexity together explain 34.3% of the variance in performance at a significance level of P < 0.00001.

## DISCUSSION

Overall, the architecture of the parent organization has a significant effect on both allegiance and the control climate of the virtual position and, as a result, virtual position strength. The nature of the relationship between the virtual position and the parent organization has a moderate effect on architecture in the virtual position, but no effect was found on member allegiance in the virtual position. Major implications are discussed next.

## The role of virtual position strength

Support for hypothesis 6 indicates that strong virtual positions will perform better in terms of customer satisfaction than weak virtual positions. The implications of this finding support the idea that virtual positions have performance advantages in terms of dealing with uncertainty (Mackenzie, 1986a, 1986b; Mowshowitz, 1992). The finding here advances this theory, suggesting that not only are there measurable degrees of the virtual position but that as the virtual position becomes stronger performance is increased. When combined with the organizational flexibility advantages found in the virtual position structure suggested by Mowshowitz (1992), a new management perspective emerges: by properly establishing and actively managing the virtual position, executives may realize the performance advantages suggested by strong virtual positions. Through the virtual position, the manager may proactively address uncertainty through the gain in flexibility.

Hypothesis 6a states that the relationship between strength of the virtual position and performance is moderated by task complexity. As discussed, the moderation effect was not supported by these data; however, a significant effect was found for task complexity when it was tested as a control variable with a direct effect on performance. This correlation was counterintuitive, indicating that the virtual position performs better in complex environments than in simple environments. Although not expected, this result may be explained by focusing on the structure and purpose of a virtual position. Simple tasks are performed well in most organizational structures (e.g. Mintzberg, 1983). The virtual position, by its nature, may confound this seemingly natural effect. By confusing the issue of chain of command, individuals in the virtual position are given more to deal with. When dealing with complex situations, the virtual position provides freedom of action through the use of unique solutions and the feeling that positive performance in the virtual position will positively affect success in the parent organization. These effects combine to increase performance. However, in a low-complexity situation, simple scripted solutions and controlled situations have been shown to be quite effective (Hogarth, 1987). By its nature, the virtual position is neither scripted nor controlled, and thus may be better suited to deal with complexity than simplicity.

## Factors affecting the strength of the virtual position

Hypothesis 5 states that the interaction of member allegiance in the virtual position and the control climate of the virtual position will be positively correlated with the strength of the virtual position. The results that support this hypothesis suggest that, through careful management of the commitment levels of the members of the virtual position and by fostering autonomy and potency among virtual position members, strong virtual positions will emerge. When commitment to the virtual position is high and commitment to the parent organization is low, the strongest encouragement of the strength of the virtual position occurs. Conversely, when commitment to the virtual position is low and commitment to the parent organization is high, the strongest discouragement to the strength of the virtual position occurs.

Support for hypothesis 1 indicates that a restrictive structure in the parent organization is negatively related to virtual position commitment in the virtual position. That is, as the architecture of the parent organization becomes more restrictive, allegiance to the virtual position is reduced. This suggests that managers who wish to enhance performance of virtual positions should provide a less restrictive architecture that allows for the flexibility and allegiance that is the hallmark of the virtual position.

Finally, support for hypothesis 2 indicates that a restrictive structure in the parent organization will be correlated negatively with autonomy and potency in the virtual position. As the structure of the parent organization becomes more restrictive, feelings of autonomy and potency are reduced. By reducing restrictive structure in the parent organization, the control climate of the virtual position becomes more encouraging to strong virtual positions.

## The nature of the relationship

Whereas a loosely coupled relationship does foster autonomy and potency in the virtual position, no support was found for a relationship between this construct and member allegiance in the virtual position. Thus, whereas a loosely coupled relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position will encourage strong virtual positions, this effect occurs only through the control climate of the virtual position (hypothesis 3 was moderately supported) and not through allegiance (hypothesis 4 was unsupported).

This last finding is difficult to interpret because the theory supports such a connection. Moreover, the relationship between these two constructs was supported by the pilot study interviews. This would seem to indicate a possible measurement problem that hasn't been uncovered. A factor analysis was performed combining the two constructs 'nature of the relationship' and 'architecture of the parent organization' to test whether they were measuring the same information. However, the anticipated results emerged from the factor analysis, revealing the theorized dimensions of the constructs with no appreciable cross-loadings.

Follow-on interviews were then conducted to examine why the theoretical connection between the nature of the relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position and member allegiance was not supported. Two possible explanations emerged from the interviews. First, the more rigid structure of typical military organizations may be confounding this relationship, and, second, members of the virtual position did not clearly separate the parent organization and the virtual position in their minds. Each of these potential explanations is discussed next.

First, interviewees suggested that the more structured nature of the military environment caused the relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position to be perceived as restrictive. Military organizations have tightly structured chains of command; this generally causes higher levels of reporting and closer supervision in the organization. This natural restrictiveness of the nature of the relationship for this study may have confounded any expected effects.

Second, interviewees stated that they did not consider themselves to be exclusively a member of either the parent organization or the virtual position. Many respondents considered themselves members of the SPO and the acquisition team (virtual position). The parent organization, as defined in this study, was a major element of the SPO. This caused confusion in the minds of some respondents. Follow-up interviews indicated that some respondents were not sure whether some questions addressed the parent organization or the SPO. In addition, some interviewees believed that the relationship between the two organizations is so seamless that measuring this construct would be inherently difficult. Thus, the problems described above may be the cause of the lack of support for hypothesis 3 and the moderate support for hypothesis 4.

## CONCLUSIONS

In the context of this study, the virtual position is an ad hoc grouping of individuals that exhibits blurred hierarchical reporting relationships, interdependence of task, resource sharing and dependency with multiple parent organizations and its own allegiance structure. Although the virtual position is part of the parent organization, it is also an entity unto itself. The virtual position is not only a phenomenon within an organization, it is a type of organization. Our results support the interpretation that a less restrictive architecture in the parent organization and a loosely coupled nature of the relationship between the parent organization and the virtual position will encourage strong virtual positions. In turn, strong virtual positions perform better in complex situations.

Theory suggests that the virtual position structure will have advantages in terms of flexibility, responsiveness and adaptability. The results here show the advantages of co-operation, allegiance and performance. Interestingly, the positive correlation between parent organization commitment and virtual position commitment suggests that increased commitment to the virtual position actually will increase commitment to the parent organization.

Although the recognition of the advantages of the virtual position is important, it is paramount that managers understand what the virtual position is and what they are trying to accomplish through its promotion. The virtual position is built around unmet task needs, not traditional organizational boundaries. As such, it should have the attributes of decentralized decision making, task focus, control throughout the life cycle and cultural change. By promoting the virtual position, the manager must understand that control is being relinquished in favour of co-operation and performance.

No matter how the virtual position is perceived by the manager, two important questions remain to be addressed by managers. First, how does one create a virtual position? The results of this study show that by managing allegiance and control climate in the virtual position one can promote stronger virtual positions. By encouraging autonomy and potency in the virtual position, performance is improved.

Second, does the virtual position lose its advantages as it becomes regulated? Organizations tend to codify successful organizational changes. The virtual position derives much of its advantages from its ad hoc nature. If managers attempt to regulate and control the virtual position, its advantages may be lost. A reasonable proposition would be that as virtual positions are regulated they will lose their feelings of autonomy and potency, and thus allegiance changes. This, however, is an issue for future research.

The challenge remains to answer the above two questions. The virtual position may be seen as both a process and a structure. The manager will be required to relinquish control in order to take advantage of the virtual position and must be able to manipulate the process without stifling the natural flexibility of the virtual position. Balancing these elements is the key to higher performance.
