# The effect of transaction costs on the decision to replace ‘off-the-shelf’ software: the role of software diffusion and infusion

Grant Castner & Colin Ferguson

Department of Commerce, The University of Queensland, St Lucia, Queensland, 4072, Australia, email: castner@commerce.uq.edu.au

Abstract. This study examines the extent to which software diffusion and infusion affect the decision to replace ‘off-the-shelf’ software, using the example of spreadsheet software. Transaction cost economics motivates the propositions that higher levels of software diffusion and higher levels of software infusion act as disincentives on the firm’s decision to upgrade or change off-the-shelf software. Our focus on software diffusion and infusion extends previous research that only examined hardware infusion and diffusion. Results from the study, using a survey, newsgroups and interviews, generally support the propositions and show that the more software is diffused and infused into a firm, the less likely it is to be changed or upgraded. More specifically, the results also show that, while software infusion acts directly to reduce the likelihood of software replacement, software diffusion only affects it indirectly through its effect on infusion.

Keywords: Diffusion, infusion, software replacement, transaction cost economics

## INTRODUCTION

Despite the strategic importance of software as a component of a firm’s information system, not all firms immediately replace (change or upgrade) their existing software whenever a superior alternative becomes available (Raho et al., 1987). This phenomenon is especially true of spreadsheet and other off-the-shelf software where manufacturers release upgrades on a regular basis. Although some firms replace software as soon as the revision is available, other firms replace on a less frequent basis. The purpose of this research is to develop and test a model to explain the interfirm variance in the likelihood of software replacement, using spreadsheet software as an example.

We argue that two factors, software diffusion and software infusion, play an important role in affecting the likelihood of software replacement across firms. Software diffusion is the degree of dispersion of the software throughout the firm. It is not merely the availability of the software, but also the number of people within the firm who actually use it. Software infusion is the degree of penetration of software into a firm (see Hann & Weber, 1995). That is, software infusion measures the extent to which an organization relies on the software, as opposed to how widely it is used across the organization (software diffusion). We believe that greater diffusion and infusion of software in a firm is costly and acts as a disincentive to the firm’s decision to replace the software.

Our contribution to the diffusion and infusion literature is that we focus on software diffusion and infusion rather than on hardware diffusion and infusion. Also, most other related research focuses on factors that promote diffusion in a firm (Moore, 1987; Lind et al., 1989; Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Hoffer & Alexander, 1992; Zmud & Apple, 1992; Bretschneider & Wittmer, 1993; Straub, 1994). This study extends this earlier research by examining the effect on the firm once software diffusion and infusion has occurred.

Researchers and practitioners need to understand better the relationship between diffusion and infusion of software throughout a firm and the need for future software replacement. A number of studies have identified a reluctance on the part of firms continually to adopt new or improved technology (Zmud, 1984; Raho et al., 1987; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990). Our research attempts to explain how this reluctance is related to the decision to replace software. The results are likely to help information systems managers to plan the replacement of software more successfully by instituting measures that minimize the costs normally associated with diffusion and infusion without reducing the firm’s productivity.

The paper proceeds as follows. The next section details the research model and formulates the research hypotheses. The research method is then described, followed by the analysis and results. The paper concludes with a summary of the key results, limitations of the study and directions for future research.

## RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESES

## The decision-to-replace and transaction costs

We use transaction cost economics literature (for example, see Williamson, 1975, 1979) to develop and build a model describing the relationship between software diffusion, software infusion and the likelihood of replacement. Figure 1 depicts the research model. This model suggests that, while diffusion and infusion covary, they act independently as organizational differences to affect the decision to replace. Because the replacement is a transaction, transaction costs play an important role in the decision to replace. Uncertainty, frequency and the specificity of the investments required to conduct a transaction are the major attributes that differentiate transactions (Williamson, 1979; Joskow, 1988).

Uncertainty exists because of the difficulty in specifying ex ante every contingency that has some bearing on the transaction. The greater the uncertainty, the higher the possible costs. Frequency refers to the degree of recurrence of the transaction. Transactions can generally be categorized as one-off, occasional or repetitive, and each of these has associated costs.

Specificity is the degree to which the investments that form part of the transaction are specialized or unique to the transaction. The greater an asset’s specificity, the greater the costs in shifting the asset to another use. We focus on software replacement at an organizational level, requiring organizational level measures that affect the asset specificity, uncertainty and frequency of the replacement transaction. These measures are hypothesized to be software infusion and diffusion.

## Software diffusion and the likelihood of software replacement

As a software package becomes more widely dispersed (i.e. diffused) across a firm, we argue that it becomes more difficult to replace. Replacement is more difficult because of the increased costs involved. Widespread dispersion is an indication of the acceptance of the current software, resulting in a firm’s unwillingness to change. Also, greater dispersion increases the uncertainty and risk of any replacement by increasing the number of people and tasks that could be adversely affected. For example, greater diffusion increases the number of licences held and, consequently, the number that need to be replaced. Consequently, diffusion results in higher costs to upgrade or renew licences.

As uncertainty surrounding a transaction increases, it is more likely that the transaction is internalized within the firm, rather than conducting the transaction in the market place (Williamson, 1975). For reasons of frequency and prohibitive production costs, a firm purchases spreadsheet and other ‘off-the-shelf’ software on the market, rather than producing it internally. However, a decision not to replace this type of software is a decision not to use the market for the transaction, but to deal with the problem internally. Firms opt to adapt the existing software and related procedures rather than replace it. Also, the outcome of any choice is a future event, and never entirely certain (Taylor, 1974). There is always an element of risk involved in any choice. That is, because the replacement of software involves a choice between existing software and new software, there is an element of risk involved.

Higher levels of diffusion increase this uncertainty and risk. Uncertainty is increased because of the greater number of people affected by the choice and the number of instances where problems may arise. For example, as the number of computers and users increases, uncertainty as to whether all employees are able to adapt increases. Risk is similarly affected. For example, the risk that a copy of the software has an error increases as the number of copies of the software (diffusion) increases. This resulting higher uncertainty and risk reduces the likelihood of replacement.

In summary, the aggregate cost of replacement, in terms of both transaction and production costs, is affected by the degree of software diffusion throughout the firm. This level of diffusion inversely affects the likelihood of software replacement. This suggests the following hypothesis:

H1: The level of software diffusion has an inverse effect on the likelihood of software replacement.

## Software infusion and the likelihood of software replacement

Software infusion is the degree of penetration of software into a firm’s operations strategy, governance systems and social systems (Hann & Weber, 1995). For example, as particular software becomes more infused in a firm, i.e. as more macros, templates and files unique to the firm are developed and implemented, the software and the people maintaining and using it take on a specific identity with the firm – they become highly specific firm assets. Consequently, replacement threatens the value of these assets to the firm. If the software is changed (e.g. Lotus for Excel) or upgraded (e.g. Excel 5.0 to Excel 7.0), firm-specific knowledge either becomes redundant or is costly to transfer. The extent of knowledge redundancy or transfer costs depends on the differences between the replacement and the original software. If it is a change from one product to another, the redundancies and costs are likely to be higher than if it is from an upgrade to the next version. Upgrades usually facilitate a smoother transition to the new version, suggesting that work practices are less likely to be affected, but some upgrades can also introduce major differences.

The knowledge is specific to the particular version of software (as amended by the firm), and not generic knowledge applicable to all spreadsheets (for example) or even the particular spreadsheet software in use. The replacement reduces the value of the human capital of the firm to the extent that the human capital cannot be freely transferred to the replacement software. Examples of this reduction in the value of human capital include knowledge of the features of the old package that are not present in the replacement software and knowledge of keystrokes that are not present or change in the replacement software.

A reduction in the value of human capital also results from a lack of knowledge of the replacement software and the resultant need for additional training. The reduction in human capital is a result of the loss of scale economies created by repetitive use of the current software (see Ball, 1989). When scale economies are lost as a result of the introduction of something new, such as replacement spreadsheet software, costs (i.e. scale diseconomies) are incurred.

Unless there is a sufficient discrepancy between the opportunity cost of not adopting the replacement spreadsheet (or other software) and the costs in terms of lost human capital and other specific assets, the replacement does not occur. We believe that the greater the infusion of the software, the greater the transaction costs resulting from losses in the value of human capital and scale diseconomies. We also believe that these transaction costs are able to outweigh the benefits of adoption and use of replacement software (i.e. the opportunity cost of non-adoption of the replacement software is low). Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2: The level of software infusion has a direct inverse effect on the likelihood of software replacement.

## RESEARCH METHOD

## Software

We focus on spreadsheet software to test the research model because there are numerous spreadsheet packages available, and these are updated regularly. Also, most organizations use spreadsheets to varying degrees, and the infusion of a spreadsheet throughout the organization is more variable than that of word-processing software. These reasons support the selection of spreadsheets to test the model, but the model could apply equally to other offthe-shelf software.

## Sample

The sample comprises 500 medium-sized Australian private sector firms. A medium-sized firm is defined as having annual revenues of between \$A50 million and \$A200 million for the financial year ending in 1994. We randomly selected 500 firms from Beck (1995) and Moffett (1995). Although the inclusion of public companies, private companies and partnerships in the sample increases the external validity of the sample, internal validity is likely to be compromised. Internal validity suffers because of the different environments under which these various types of firms work. For example, only public companies have the requirement of satisfying outside shareholders. However, in this study, there is likely to be little effect on internal validity because the competitive pressures triggering the software switch come from compet ing firms rather than from other sources. To mitigate further the impact on internal validity, an industry control variable is used to determine whether the competitive environment and othe industry factors do influence the likelihood of spreadsheet replacement.

We selected medium-sized firms because of resource constraints and other practical considerations. Large firms may have numerous information systems managers that control software-related decisions for their individual departments. The diffusion, infusion and likelihood of spreadsheet replacement is not then consistent across departments. The conclusions of this research apply to the individual departments, but not necessarily to the firm as a whole. Although inconsistent software purchasing may also occur in medium-sized firms, the likelihood is substantially reduced. Small firms are also not included in the sample because, although size is not likely to be a relevant factor in most cases, for very small firms (for example, those with fewer than 10 computers), size may begin to play a role because little planning and reduced risk are involved. Information systems managers were asked to respond to the research instrument because they are more likely to have the requisite knowledge of spreadsheet use. Results from an interview conducted with an information systems manager suggest that most managers have sufficient knowledge to complete the instrument.

## Measures

## Software diffusion

Earlier studies focused on measuring the factors influencing diffusion rather than diffusion itself (Moore & Benbasat, 1991), or only operationalized the construct using one-item scales (Hann & Weber, 1995). Other studies narrowly focused on the adoption aspects of diffusion (Bretschneider & Wittmer, 1993). Only one previous study provided a well-developed diffusion scale (Segars, 1994).

The level of diffusion of software is normally represented by the degree of availability of the software and the number of departments and individuals who use the software. Availability relates to the number of occurrences of the software throughout the organization, in other words the number of computers or terminals that run the software. This has sometimes been described as information technology adoption (Huff & Munro, 1985; Lind et al., 1989). Diffusion has been measured by the extent of implementation (Hoffer & Alexander, 1992) and by the number of microcomputers per employee (Bretschneider & Wittmer, 1993). However, adoption does not necessarily mean diffusion within an organization (Hann & Weber, 1995). The same applies for the extent of implementation of information technology. For example, if the majority of the occurrences of the software are located within a small number of departments, diffusion has not occurred. The result was the inclusion of an item about the percentage of actual users of the software.

In this study, software diffusion is measured using a three-item scale (see Appendix, scale A). Two items were derived from two earlier studies (Huff & Munro, 1985; Hann & Weber, 1995). We developed the third item. Participants responded to the first item on a scale ranging from 0 (not widespread at all) to 10 (very widespread). The response to the other two items is indicated by assigning a percentage.

## Software infusion

Apart from Segars (1994), little effort has been made to develop a scale to measure infusion. While Segars (1994) items are a useful aid in constructing the scale items, they are general measures of information system infusion and do not relate specifically to the infusion of spreadsheet software. Another study looked at the infusion of supermarket scanners and was able to identify different applications for which the supermarket scanner was used (Zmud & Apple, 1992). These different stages of application represented different levels of infusion. Although it is difficult to identify distinct stages of spreadsheet software infusion, a similar approach can be used. Scale items identifying the extent of saved functions, macros and other data and the reliance placed on these spreadsheets are used to determine the extent of infusion.

In this study, software infusion is also measured using a three-item scale (see Appendix, scale B). The first item, eliciting responses on the general nature of the importance of spreadsheet software to the firm, derives from Hann & Weber (1995). We developed the other two items to measure the extent of use of user-developed macros and the reliance placed by the firm on spreadsheets. Participants responded to the first item on a scale ranging from 0 (no penetration) to 10 (penetration to a large extent). Items two and three are completed by assign ing a percentage in response to the question.

## Likelihood of spreadsheet replacement

We measured the likelihood of spreadsheet replacement in one of two ways (see Appendix, scale C). The respondent was asked to indicate the number of days, if known, between the last replacement and the next expected replacement. If the date of the next expected replacement was not known, the respondent was asked his or her opinion on the probability of spreadsheet replacement in 1, 2 or 3 years.

## Procedures

## Scale validation

Data were collected by mailed questionnaire. Before distribution of the questionnaire to the study participants, we conducted qualitative tests to assess that the scales used to measure the construct were both valid and reliable. We assessed content validity of the software infusion and diffusion items by asking 12 experts in the areas of information systems and/or survey design to rate how strongly the items represented software diffusion and software infusion on a scale from 1 (weak estimate) to 10 (strong estimate). The experts also provided comments on the scales. The mean response for software diffusion was 8.00 (median 8.00; SD 0.71), whereas for infusion, it was 8.11 (median 8.00; SD 0.78). Because this test is a qualitative procedure, there are no definitive guidelines as to what minimum score should be achieved. The high scores, however, do suggest that the items exhibit content validity.

Discriminant and convergent validity were assessed ex ante by testing the unidimensionality of the scales using sorting techniques between two rating groups (see Moore & Benbasat, 1991). The results from this procedure suggest that the items are measuring two distinct constructs, namely software diffusion and infusion.

Finally, four information systems managers from organizations that fell within the definition of the research sample were asked to complete the questionnaire to assess the degree to which it could be understood. We interviewed one of these managers to assure the questionnaire’s clarity further. The interviewee and three other respondents indicated that they could knowledgeably complete the questionnaire and had no major criticisms of its structure and style.

## Administering the instrument

We distributed the questionnaire in May 1996. Owing to time and resource constraints, we could not send a follow-up. To increase the response rate, we implemented a number of standard practices that included giving respondents an address, telephone number and an email address if they had any problems with the questionnaire (de Vaus, 1990).

## Responses

Of the 500 questionnaires mailed, 179 were returned. Ten of these questionnaires were blank and were dropped from the analysis. The remaining 169 responses represent a response rate of almost 34%.

In the subsequent analysis, we focus on the probability-of-replacement-within-1-year item because levels of diffusion and infusion are more likely to be stable within this time period. Therefore, those respondents providing a response to this probability-of-replacement question became the sample upon which the analysis is based. Respondents who provided an actual date of future replacement were also included in this sample. If the replacement date fell within the 1-year period, the respondent scored 100% (probability of replacement), while those planning to replace subsequent to the 1-year period scored 0% (probability of replacement).

Of the 169 who responded, two did not fit the sample criteria, 12 did not answer all of the diffusion or infusion items, 24 did not answer the replacement item, and one case was omitted because it was an outlier. The final sample size is 130, including 91 who responded to the probability-of-replacement question and 39 who responded to the number-of-days-betweenreplacement question.

The measurement model is assessed by confirmatory factor analysis on the factors and their relevant indicators, as shown in Figure 2. Confirmatory factor analysis is the appropriate procedure, because there is a priori theory supporting the inclusion of certain items with certain factors. Confirmatory factor analysis first involves analysing the fit of the model. The chi-square statistic is one measure of the goodness of fit, where the null hypothesis is that the data fit the model well. A significant chi-square statistic means that the null hypothesis is rejected and implies poor model fit.

However, because of the sensitivity of this statistic to even minor discrepancies in model fit, especially with large sample sizes (Bentler & Bonett, 1980), other goodness of fit indices are considered. One of these alternative measures is an adjusted chi-square statistic, where the chi-square statistic is divided by its degrees of freedom. If the adjusted chi-square statistic is less than five (Wheaton et al., 1977) or, alternatively, less than two (Carmines & McIver, 1981), the model is considered to be a good fit. Further indices of fit include the normed fit index (NFI), the non-normed fit index (NNFI) and the comparative fit index (CFI) (Byrne, 1994). The recommended lower threshold is 0.90 (Dunn et al., 1993).

Constraining the correlation between the factors of diffusion and infusion to unity provides a test of discriminant validity. A significant degradation in model fit with the unity constraint imposed supports the conclusion that there are separate factors, rather than simply one factor. The relevant statistic is the difference between the chi-square statistics of the constrained and unconstrained models.

Two further tests of convergent and discriminant validity (model fit) are the Wald test and the Lagrange multiplier test. The Wald test gives a list of parameters that, when fixed, improve model fit. The Lagrange multiplier test has an opposite function. It provides a list of parameters that, when free to vary, improve model fit.

The final test to assess unidimensionality is to analyse the path coefficients. The path coefficients are the standardized regression coefficients derived from the structural equations implicit in the model illustrated in Figure 2. A z-statistic provides an assessment of the significance of the path coefficients.

In addition to assessing the validity of the results, the reliability of the data is also relevant. Cronbach or coefficient alpha is one method used to assess the reliability of the survey data statistically (Cronbach, 1971). Other methods of assessing reliability used in this study are a measure of composite factor reliability and a measure known as average variance extracted (AVE) (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Composite factor reliability measures whether there is a sufficient relationship between the scale items and their respective constructs. The suggested lower threshold before reliability can be assumed is 0.70 (Jöreskog, 1971). AVE indicates the amount of variance that is captured by the factor, as opposed to the variance resulting from error. The suggested cut-off for AVE is 0.50 (Segars, 1994).

In order to test the hypotheses, structural equation modelling is used. This method of analysis allows testing of the direct and indirect effects of infusion and diffusion. Structural equation modelling is the preferred method of analysis because of the unspecified relationship between diffusion and infusion. For the research model in this study, only one structural equation is needed:

## Supporting evidence

As discussed previously, we conducted an interview before distribution of the survey to aid survey construction. We structured the interview to ensure that the respondent was at first unaware of the research hypotheses and, as result, his responses provide supporting evidence to the empirical findings. In addition, we conducted a telephone interview with a survey respondent.

The first interviewee (from a financial institution) indicated that his firm had only upgraded its word-processing software three times in 20 years. This was driven by the time required for file conversion to the new software, usually 6–9 months. He said this occurred even if the replacement was only an upgrade. That is, the sheer cost of file conversion was a disincentive to replace. This strongly suggests that diffusion plays a significant role in the replacement decision.

Evidence from the interviewee also suggests that software infusion affected the likelihood of software replacement. For example, the firm decided to replace its current successful applications suite with a technically superior package. However, use of the replacement software ran at about 25% of the level of use of the former software, even after extensive training on the new suite. This reduction in use suggests that staff viewed the switch as too costly, because they had substantial knowledge and expertise on the current software. We believe that this implies that software infusion reduced the likelihood of software replacement.

The telephone interviewee (from a mining firm) elucidated that a complete change in spreadsheet software by her firm is only considered after a careful estimate of all direct and indirect costs, suggesting that software infusion and diffusion affect the replacement decision. Moreover, in a previous position, she indicated that software upgrades were introduced ‘by stealth’ because of staff reluctance to move from the existing software. This reluctance strongly suggests that staff saw too much personal cost in switching, i.e. the level of infusion affected the likelihood of software replacement.

Additional comments on the survey and responses to a message we posted in the internet newsgroup, comp.apps.spreadsheets, provide further supporting evidence. Comments received on the survey were consistent with those from the newsgroup and are not discussed here. The message posted was:

‘I am currently undertaking research into what factors influence the decision to replace/ upgrade spreadsheet software in a business. Basically, why do some businesses replace their software as soon as the next version becomes available, while others retain their existing packages for long periods? Any comments you may have would be appreciated.

We received four responses to the message posted on the newsgroup. One respondent indicated that, in his experience, a significant number of staff in an organization continue to use an older spreadsheet despite the company upgrading to a newer version. The problem was even more pronounced, in his opinion, when the replacement was not simply an upgrade but a complete change of spreadsheet software, suggesting that infusion does affect the replacement decision.

Independent postings to the newsgroup, i.e. those not in response to our posted question, provide further evidence. For example, there were postings on the difficulty of updating links between spreadsheets created in Microsoft Excel 5.0 and transferred to Excel 5.0c, two very closely related versions. Other examples include the addition of the user’s own tips in Excel Help screens and the impossibility of converting Excel pivot tables to Lotus 1-2-3 format because Lotus does not have that feature. All these problems were caused by an organization upgrading or changing its software, indicating that software infusion does affect the replacement decision.

## LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

## Concluding comments

This research shows that the more spreadsheet software is diffused and infused into a firm, the less likely it is to be replaced. We believe that this reluctance to replace is a result of perceived high transaction costs that are likely to be incurred if software replacement takes place – transaction costs generated through the broad diffusion and deep infusion of the software into the firm. These transaction costs include the purchase price of the software but also, more importantly, the related costs of training, file transfer costs, compatibility costs and lost productivity costs. The transaction costs resulting from a high level of diffusion and infusion are exacerbated further by the use of suites of applications, such as Microsoft Office or Lotus Smartsuite. Replacement of one application in the suite may necessitate replacement of the entire suite, further increasing possible transaction costs and reducing the likelihood of replacement.

However, while the results show that software infusion acts directly to reduce the likelihood of spreadsheet replacement, software diffusion acts only indirectly to affect spreadsheet replacement likelihood through its effect on infusion. Therefore, software that is widely used (diffused) throughout the firm may remain easy to replace. It is only that software that is also important to the firm, software that has been infused into the firm’s operations, that reduces the likelihood of software replacement. For example, a firm that has spreadsheet software installed on every computer but only uses it for simple routine tasks will not be as reluctant to replace it compared with a firm that has built spreadsheet applications to perform important organizational tasks.

In this study, we focused on characteristics of the software itself, namely its diffusion and infusion, but recognize that the software replacement decision does not occur in a vacuum. Firm characteristics may also play an important role in software replacement. Factors such as the effect of operating system and hardware constraints, issues of power, management support, resource availability and resource control, social pressure to change, customer/supplier relationships and the technical competence of staff may also be important. These are firm-wide characteristics relevant to any purchase decision, including the decision to replace spreadsheet or other off-the-shelf software. We have focused on software diffusion and infusion because these are software-specific rather than firm-level factors. All of these additional firm-wide factors provide possible avenues for future research. Systematically examining their effects on the software replacement decision would allow a more comprehensive model to be developed.

Our study represents an initial insight into what impact the achievement of software diffusion and infusion has on subsequent decisions to replace spreadsheet and other off-the-shelf software. It illustrates the need to instigate procedures to minimize the transaction costs involved in the replacement of software. Otherwise, the attainment of software infusion and diffusion, while vital to securing the full advantage of software, causes problems in subsequent software replacements.

## Limitations of the research

A critical assumption of the model is that there are available more recent spreadsheet packages that provide benefits to the firm, if implemented. A further assumption is that the organization has the requisite knowledge of these improvements. This second assumption, judging by the detailed information received from all types of respondents, is valid. The first assumption has mixed support. While interview evidence suggests that most upgrades or changes do provide benefits, a number of comments from the survey and an internet newsgroup are indicative that some information systems managers perceive no benefit from upgrades, at least in the short term. Of course, this lack of benefit may result, as predicted, from high levels of infusion and diffusion. The assumption that more recent spreadsheet software does provide benefits is probably reasonable, but warrants further investigation as to whether any factors, other than infusion and diffusion, impact on the benefits of replacement to the organization.

Interview evidence and numerous comments suggest that hardware constraints are a factor in the likelihood of replacement. This may especially be the case in the current environment on account of the rather more onerous hardware requirements of the Windows 95 (and now Windows 98) or NT operating systems. The timing of the survey may also have reduced the external validity of the survey, because it may have coincided with organizational reluctance to implement a different operating system.

We believe these potential threats to the study results are mitigated by our efforts to present a parsimonious research model that captures and represents a small part of what is a dynamic and continuous process. The inclusion of every relevant variable results in a model that is so unwieldy that it fails to have any theoretical or practical benefit. The parsimony of the model is one of its strengths. Another strength of the model is that it is grounded in theory from a number of disciplines, rather than based solely on information systems theory. The principal strength of the model is that it examines the effect of infusion and diffusion once these objectives are reached, rather than simply looking at the factors that influence infusion and diffusion.
