# Organizational change and virtual teams: strategic and operational integration

Paul J. Jackson

School of Business and Management, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex, UB8 3PH, UK, email: Paul.Jackson@brunel.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper focuses on the challenges involved in integrating virtual teams strategically and operationally into organizational systems. It argues that an understanding of virtual team integration must consider both the context within which virtual teams are being introduced and processes involved in their implementation. Such an approach demands that we consider the strategic rationales involved in teamworking generally, and virtual teams in particular. By looking at change processes, attention is drawn to the social, cultural and political dynamics that affect the implementation and operation of virtual teams. This analysis is used to suggest further avenues for research on virtual teams, as well as pointing to practical considerations

Keywords: Virtual teams, organizational change, social and political processes

## INTRODUCTION

Information technologies have played a leading role in supporting many recent changes in organizational designs and processes. From business process re-engineering (Hammer & Champy, 1993) to dynamic network enterprises (Parker, 1996), contemporary innovation finds IT at the heart of business transformation. Indeed, many developments would be unthinkable without it. This is particularly the case so far as ‘virtual teams’ are concerned. Here, the use of computer networks, email, videoconferencing and faxes open up the possibilities for new systems of group support, as well as new team structures and processes (Lipnack & Stamps, 1997; Boutellier et al., 1998; Roberts et al., 1998; Townsend et al., 1998; Nandhakumar, 1999; Yan & Louis, 1999).

Virtual team developments certainly suggest a host of new opportunities for individuals and businesses alike. They point to new applications of the latest communications technologies. But they also bring with them a host of new questions and challenges. These go far beyond the obvious ones, such as ‘which technologies are most appropriate for virtual team working?’ Although such questions are important, the successful integration of virtual teams into business structures and practices must also wrestle with a number of strategic rationales, as well as the problem of change management and implementation. Only by broadening the scope of team working discussions can managers and information systems planners make appropriate decisions about the role virtual teamwork will play in their organizations. And without careful attention to the management of change, the success of virtual team innovation is likely to be jeopardized.

To address the questions of implementation and change, this article first looks at the background to developments in virtual working and the role of IT in contemporary organizational change. It then looks specifically at the growth in team working and explores the role of virtual teams in relation to this. We then examine the strategic change management issues, against which developments in virtual teams have to be understood. It is argued here that certain forms of team working — particularly those that accompany radical business redesign — are likely to produce social, cultural and political ripples across an organization. Understanding the nature of such ripples, and how they can be dealt with and managed, will be essential for successful implementation. In the next section, the functional integration of virtual teams is considered. It is argued at this point that the complexities of team dynamics, knowledge sharing, and management and control must be understood in order to effect appropriate virtual team solutions. Only when this is done, it is suggested, can we begin to identify the sort of IT architecture suitable for virtual teams.

## VIRTUAL WORKING AND THE KNOWLEDGE ECONOMY

In recent years, a new language has grown up in the way we talk about work and organizations. It is a language coloured by the images and metaphors of ‘cybertechnologies’ and ‘virtual reality’ (e.g. Barnatt, 1995). As such, we hear talk of ‘virtual organizations’ (e.g. Hale & Whitlam, 1997; Hedberg et al., 1997), ‘virtual corporations’ (Davidow & Malone, 1992), ‘virtual enterprises’ (Parker, 1996) and ‘cyber-businesses’ (Barnatt, 1995). As part of this, we also hear talk of ‘virtual teams’ (Lipnack & Stamps, 1997; Boutellier et al., 1998; Roberts et al., 1998; Townsend et al., 1998; Nandhakumar, 1999; Yan & Louis, 1999). Although to some extent these concepts may lack a certain precision at times, they seek to characterize emerging organizational phenomena that have important implications for the way we work.

There is, perhaps, no defining set of attributes that captures the essence of ‘virtual’ work phenomena. For instance, although some argue that virtual organizations are bound up with the idea of ‘continuous or institutionalized change’ (Hale & Whitlam, 1997, p. 3), for others they are predominantly about the use of technology to sustain interorganizational networks and alliances (e.g. Hedberg et al., 1997). Despite such areas of disagreement, Barnatt (1995) suggests that discussions on virtual working:

‘... are associated with the use of cybertechnologies to allow people separated by time and distance to work together cohesively.’ (p. 64)

So far as virtual teams are concerned, we could argue that this theme above all others is the central matter of interest. It not only helps to describe what virtual teams are (people working together across time and distance thanks to technology), it also suggests why we might be interested in them (in order to act cohesively despite temporal and spatial barriers). In the information systems literature, these issues have most closely been associated with developments in computer supported co-operative working (CSCW), groupware and group decision support systems. They are also linked to the debate on ‘teleworking’ (Jackson & van der Wielen, 1998; Nilles, 1998). However, facilitating communications and knowledge exchange between individuals, teams and enterprises is a theme at the heart of discussions more generally about contemporary organizations.

In accounts of the ‘knowledge’ or ‘information’ economy (e.g. Castells, 1996), knowledge exchange and creation — particularly in support of organizational learning and innovation — have become key priorities for the achievement of competitive advantage (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). As part of these discussions, considerable interest has also been paid to the potential enabling role of information technologies (Parker, 1996; Björkegren & Rapp, 1999). In this sense, the use of IT to support learning and co-operation between groups and individuals forms part of a broader research and development agenda.

## IT AND THE NEW CORPORATE AGENDA

Today, organizations are having to deal with a whole set of challenges and transformations. Not only are technologies changing rapidly — and often radically — so too are the environments within which enterprises operate. Parker (1996, pp. 5–6) argues that businesses, and their supporting IT functions, simultaneously face six converging ‘revolutions’ that are forcing them to rethink their structures and processes:

\- A redefinition of markets — moving from the regional and national towards the continental and global.

\- A redesign of organizational structures — from hierarchies based on management control, to horizontal forms built around teamwork and empowerment, and encompassing the moves towards strategic alliances and virtual enterprises.

\- A restructuring of the economic bases of national and global economies — from industrial to information jobs, products and services.

\- A shift to customer-driven planning — from producer focus to consumer driven.

\- A growing global society and social awareness — with individuals having greater access to information, communications and networked computers.

\- A growing influence of information-related technologies in the business and social environment — with IT and computer networks seen as the enablers of social and organizational transformation.

There is considerable support elsewhere for such an analysis (e.g. Castells, 1997; account of ‘networked society’). It is in this context that the potential for virtual teams must be understood.

## TEAMS AND VIRTUAL TEAMS

The growth in virtual teams brings together two broader sets of developments. The first of these, as discussed above, relates to the increase in virtual working at large. The second concerns the general interest in teamwork.

## The age of teamwork

According to Torrington & Hall (1998, p. 335), the 1990s are seen by many as the ‘age of the team and teamwork’. There is now widespread and growing enthusiasm for the idea of self-directed, self-managing and empowered teams (Badham et al., 1997, p. 146). Of course, the idea of teamwork is not new. As Torrington & Hall (1998) point out, clear forerunners can be seen in autonomous working group initiatives of the 1960s and 70s. Such developments, though, were premised largely on social rationales. Concern about the quality of working life in mass production systems, characterized by repetitive, deskilled and fragmented work, led to a number of experiments in job redesign (McLoughlin & Clark, 1994). In this sense, ‘job enrichment’ rather than enhanced performance was the key driving force. Today, however, this situation has reversed. For Badham et al. (1997), changes to product markets, technologies and the nature of competition now favour job designs based on autonomous teams.

These rationales reflect the new business dynamics as set out by Parker (1996) above. Whereas Fordist-bureaucratic structures — with their management hierarchies, functional divisions, and fragmented and repetitive jobs designs — may well have been suited to systems of mass production and stable markets, where customization, innovation and learning lie at the heart of competitive success a whole new system of production is needed (e.g. Heckscher & Donnellion, 1994; McLoughlin & Clark, 1994; Tidd et al., 1997). For Torrington & Hall (1998), because teams provide improved flexibility and communication, they are expected to lead to higher performance and greater commitment, as well as improved organizational learning. They are also seen as a way of enhancing quality (e.g. Wellins et al., 1991).

Badham et al. (1997) point to the growth in team-based cellular manufacturing (TBCM) as an illustration of such self-managing teams. In this instance, machines are grouped together in 'cells', with teams of multiskilled workers empowered to undertake the day-to-day operating decisions over planning, execution and monitoring of work. Here, then, an entire process or group of tasks becomes the responsibility of the team or cell.

But cellular manufacturing only represents one type of team and one set of rationales for teamwork. A variety of teams can be found in the modern workplace, each formed for different reasons. Torrington & Hall (1998, p. 337) identify three key dimensions along which teams are likely to differ: time span, interchangeability and tasks and functional range. We will now discuss each of these in turn.

## Time span

Some teams are set up to solve a specific problems while others may be project based and disband once the project is complete. Other teams will be relatively permanent though. These include production teams, as discussed in the context of cellular manufacturing, for whom the task is ongoing.

## Interchangeability

Teams differ in the range of specific skills required and the expectation of all members to learn these skills. In production teams, interchangeability of skills may be central, with all members able and expected to learn all skills (as is the case in cellular manufacturing). In other instances, such as cross-functional teams, each member is expected to bring their specialist skills to use for the benefit of the team. As such, they are not expected to be able to learn the skills of other members (as is the case, for instance, in product development teams).

## Task and functional range

In contrast to traditional mass production systems, in which work is subject to high levels of fragmentation, many production teams are designed to cover a whole task area, involving a wide range of activities (again, this reflects the principles of cellular manufacturing). Other types of teams may span a range of functions, as is the case with cross-functional teams, in which research, development, marketing and production staff may all be involved.

Torrington & Hall (1998) use these dimensions as a framework for representing different types of teams. A summary of the points raised by this is shown, as adapted from Torrington & Hall (1998, pp. 337–43), in Table 1.

This suggests that accounts of virtual teams must recognize the issue of heterogeneity, and that team roles and functions may fall more or less into stable categories.

## Virtual teams as teams

Given the centrality of technology in virtual team discussions and the role it plays in making teams flexible in time and space, it would be easy for technology and spatial form to obscure the differences among virtual teams. As we have just seen, the longevity, interchangeability of skills, and tasks and function range may vary between teams. We can presume that their internal dynamics — the roles adopted, leadership styles, needs for creativity, etc. — will also differ in each case. In this sense, we need to disabuse ourselves of the notion that all virtual teams can be treated as more or less the same, and that a kind of ‘generic model’ of virtual teams can be created. The challenge we face is thus: on the one hand, to create a body of knowledge that can inform research and practise across all types of virtual team, and, on the other hand, to identify important points of difference between varieties of teams. (This is particularly important when addressing the issue of IT support for team processes.)

There is little evidence in the literature of any attempt to do this. Townsend et al. (1998), for example, treat the term ‘virtual team’ generically, and use it to describe groups that are geographically and/or organizationally dispersed, assembled using an IT, and rarely, if ever, meet face-to-face. They then go on to say that such teams may be temporary, in that they are set up for a specific task, or permanent, such as a strategic planning team. In either case, membership is deemed to be fluid, with the arrangements in question intended to realize team synergies.

Clearly, here, a variety of teams are pulled together under the umbrella of ‘virtual’ working. Although their ‘virtualness’ may make this a meaningful thing to do, the category may be rather too broad for many purposes. We have seen that organizations may turn to teamwork in a range of situations, with the roles, rationales and constitution of teams varying widely. But there is another point here. Such ways of conceptualizing virtual teams do not tell us whether there is something unique to them over and above their flexibility across time, space and organizational borders. Although virtual teams certainly represent a new form of working, does virtual working lend itself to entirely new types of team? There is rather less evidence for this. For instance, much of the discussion on the subject is concerned with research and development/new product development teams (e.g. Boutellier et al., 1998; Roberts et al., 1998; Townsend et al., 1998; Yan & Louis, 1999). Although the arrangements and question certainly reflect the attributes of virtual working as discussed above, there is little to suggest they represent a new type of team entirely. Indeed, as Townsend et al. (1998) themselves note, virtual working comes about in these cases in order to reconstitute teamwork arrangements that have become fragmented and dispersed because of broader organizational changes.

However, what are often new are the tactical innovations that accompany virtual teams. As Roberts et al. (1998) and Boutellier et al. (1998) illustrate, ‘global development teams’ allow for continuous production cycles, with ‘team shifts’ in different parts of the world allowing 24-h production. Given that one of the key rationales in this case might be to shorten product development times, there are clear benefits in virtual teams. But again, the idea that product and software developers should work in teams, and that different shifts may work on the same production process, is not in itself new.

Having clarified some basic terms, let us now put this discussion in a broader context by looking at strategic issues involved in IT-enabled change.

## THE STRATEGIC CONTEXT OF (VIRTUAL) TEAM WORKING

As we have seen from above, because of the variety of purposes and types, the whole raison d'être for team working may vary between cases. As such, the strategic context within which we consider virtual teams will be more or less important. To develop this point, let us examine the broad issues relating to strategy and IT-enabled change.

## IT-enabled change

There is now a significant body of literature dealing with ‘IT-enabled’ strategic change (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Luftman, 1996; Parker, 1996; Scott-Morton, 1991; Hsiao & Omerod, 1998). Although much of this has been concerned with the development of frameworks for understanding and managing such change, the literature does identify particular types of strategic change involving IT. Hsiao & Omerod (1998), for instance, discuss four change ‘archetypes’. In the first of these, ‘process engineering’, IT is used to improve business efficiency by supporting a redesign of processes and organizational structure. ‘Structural reconfiguration’, on the contrary, is mostly concerned with modifying organizational architectures in order to support corporate streamlining. The third archetype, ‘human renewal’, refers to instances of cultural change, in which IT is used to support interdepartmental co-ordination and personal empowerment. Finally, ‘IT transformation’ describes the situation where IT is used to support innovation in processes, job roles and organizational culture, resulting in an overhaul of business structures. According to the ‘strategic alignment model’ (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Henderson et al., 1996; Luftman, 1996), four strategic perspectives on IT and change can be identified, with either business or IT strategy being the key driver of change.

One consequence of such changes may be a growth in team working supported by IT. However, the new teams that come from this may be part and parcel of different business models.

## Business models and team working

For Victor et al. (1996), before any decisions can be made about change strategies and focuses, management needs to be certain of the business model that will be appropriate given the competitive dynamics of the change context. These authors introduce the ‘product-process-change’ model, which characterizes business models according to: (1) the dynamics of product change, and (2) management and production processes. This allows them to contrast ‘old’ and ‘new’ competitive strategies.

Old competitive strategies include ‘mass production’ and ‘invention’ models. Mass production models reflect traditional Fordist-bureaucratic ways of organizing, in which both product markets and production processes were stable, with efficiency through repetition, deskilling and work fragmentation being the key objectives. Invention models, on the contrary, are geared towards markets that are dynamic in nature, with a corresponding need for production processes to be similarly dynamic. The emphasis, here, is on creation through intellect and skill (Victor et al., 1996, p. 77). High levels of knowledge and skill are applied to create small volumes of new products, with continuous innovation the key to corporate survival.

‘New’ competitive strategies are said by Victor et al. (1996) to be those of ‘continuous improvement’ and ‘mass customization’. With the former, product markets are deemed to be relatively mature and stable; corporate survival relies on dynamic processes, in which constant advances in quality, speed and cost allow producers to acquire a competitive edge. Such businesses are said to be customer or market focused, the needs of which are satisfied through continuous process improvements. Central to this model are mechanisms for organizational integration and collaboration, particularly among different specialist groups. As such, team-based structures are seen as the best way of ensuring value-adding processes (Victor et al., 1996, pp. 80–1). They therefore include the cross-functional product development teams, as described in Table 1 (see also, Boutellier et al., 1998; Madhaven & Grover, 1998).

In the case of mass customization, businesses operate under conditions of dynamic product change. Here, customers are viewed as making increasingly unique and unpredictable product demands, with products and services tailored to their needs and desires. Here, then, predicting changing customer preferences and shifts in market demand becomes much more challenging. Businesses can thus only compete by being able to respond flexibly to market changes (Victor et al., 1996, pp. 84–5). Organizations here are built around sets of ‘modular process capabilities’, which when linked together allow them to vary products rapidly and yet inexpensively. The modular capabilities in question may, say Victor et al. (1996), be embodied in individuals, teams or manufacturing devices, which are loosely coupled together. Whereas decisions about what to produce may be made centrally, how this is carried out is pushed down to the level of the modular units. In this sense, such units reflect the attributes of manufacturing ‘cells’ discussed above (Badham et al., 1997), and fit into the ‘self-managing service and production team’ category in Table 1.

## The strategic context for virtual teams

The implications of this discussion for virtual teams are clear: IT-enabled change may well require understanding and analysis within a strategic business and IT context. As part of this, consideration needs to be given to the business model an enterprise should adopt, or has adopted, in order to respond to the competitive conditions of its marketplace. As part of these decisions and developments, a set of further organizational changes — either planned or emergent ones — are likely to follow. These will be essential for strategies to lead to operational change. Teamwork, where this does result, is thus not an end in itself. Its putative rationales exist within a broader strategic context. This underlines the fact that in understanding and managing virtual teams, we need to know what ‘type’ of team we are concerned with, and what role it plays alongside other elements of business structures and processes.

To summarize, we have seen that many strategic developments point towards the growth in virtual teams. These include, for instance, the move towards more horizontal organizational structures, a growth in teamwork generally, increased use of IT to connect people dispersed in time and space, and the globalization of business structures and expertise.

As Townsend et al. (1998) and Boutellier et al. (1998) point out, such changes mean that team building may have to transcend both organizational and national boundaries. This is because expertise needed for many work processes is increasingly unlikely to be located in the same office or organization. In this sense, for Townsend et al. (1998) at least, many organizational changes, far from encouraging team-working developments, may well frustrate the formation of teams. For Townsend et al. (1998), flat structures, interorganizational co-operation and globalization make the move from face-to-face teams to virtual teams an ‘imperative’.

The sort of changes of which virtual teams may be a part suggest a radical overhaul of business processes and structures. However, strategy-driven approaches to IT-enabled change tend to see such developments flowing unproblematically from the realm of planning to operation (e.g. Luftman, 1996). As we will now see, this is unlikely to be the case.

## Managing team-related change

We have seen so far that the development of team working is likely to be part of more far-reaching organizational change. Where virtual teams are concerned, a central role is played by advanced information technologies, adding a layer of technical complexity to what may already be a quite dynamic situation. Because the redesign of business processes and structures is far from a simple ‘technical’ matter — but involves significant social redesign — such changes will always be open to disturbances and threats. The successful implementation of change thus demands a robust understanding of change processes — particularly their micropolitical dimension — and how they can be managed (Badham et al., 1997).

According to Buchanan & Boddy (1992), the more radical that change projects are, the more open they are to organizational disruption and failure. According to Badham et al. (1997), there are two aspects to radical change. The first concerns the issue of ‘breadth’ — the degree to which change is central to the organization’s strategy and survival and demands modifications throughout the organization. The second relates to the degree to which such modifications mark a significant departure from existing ways of doing things. This is certainly the case, for instance, with many business process redesign exercises and business transformation initiatives (see Hsiao & Omerod, 1998). We could also place certain types of virtual team innovations in the category of radical change — particularly those involving cross-functional teams and self-managing service and product cells. The general point is that the more radical change programmes are, the more politically controversial they are likely to be. This is because the activities and interests of a wide range of stakeholders may be threatened, as is certainly the case with the move towards more ‘horizontal’ organizational designs (see Parker, 1996; Townsend et al., 1998).

Unlike routine change, such projects are also likely to be highly complex. As Badham et al. (1997) note, there may be a high degree of uncertainty as to what to do and how to do it, objectives may be less clear, and resource requirements will be less well known. In addition, it may be less easy to create shared perceptions of goals and to build and maintain necessary commitment. For this reason, Badham et al. (1997) suggest that more time will be spent ensuring effective communication to encourage flexibility, address perceptions and generate and regenerate involvement. It is with good reason that Buchanan & Boddy (1992) characterize such changes as ‘vulnerable’. In these cases, they say, traditional ‘project management’ approaches to change are likely to prove inadequate. Managers and change agents will instead need to develop more political ‘backstage’ skills and techniques. The need to enrol and re-enrol support, neutralize dissent and resistance, and secure resources will demand networking and even Machiavellian skills.

But having addressed the strategic change context and the problems in managing the changes that stem from it, the integration of virtual teams into organizational systems is likely to prove problematical because of the specific issue of ‘virtuality’. Is it is to this matter that we now turn.

## THE FUNCTIONAL INTEGRATION OF VIRTUAL TEAMS

Many approaches to virtual teams tend to concentrate on the role of technology and the part it can play in supporting team interactions. There is a tendency in such accounts to focus on the degree to which IT can substitute face-to-face interaction (e.g. Lipnack & Stamps, 1997; Townsend et al., 1998). The main contention in such accounts is that the ‘richer’ the medium, the less need for face-to-face encounters. The problem with such approaches is that they provide only a poor conceptualization and theorization of work processes. There is often an implicit ‘information processing’ view of work and organizations, in which work communications are largely seen as explicit and open to ready articulation and understanding. Where communications are not so explicit, this typically includes such matters as vocal intonation and body gestures, which ‘rich media’ are usually deemed able to convey.

Depending on the teams involved, of course, communications may involve more or less tacit knowledge. Before we can judge whether IT can indeed substitute for face-to-face interaction, we thus need to have a better understanding of team processes. To do this, we will firstly look at the issue of ‘boundary management’ in teams. We will then take a cognitive perspective, in which team members are seen to engage in a process of ‘knowledge creation’. We then look at the issue of culture, before concluding with a discussion of trust in teams.

## Boundary management and virtual teams

Given that virtual working discussions make so much of the fact that organizational, temporal and spatial boundaries are becoming eroded, it would be tempting to presume that boundaries no longer matter. After all, in accounts of virtual organizations and teams, boundaries are seen as ‘barriers’ — restrictions that frustrate the free flow of ideas, information and expertise. It is certainly the case that bureaucratic structures contribute significantly to ‘bounded rationality’, poor organizational learning and difficulties in innovation (Morgan, 1997). But, as Yan & Louis (1999) show, boundary management remains an important aspect of organizational life. These authors suggest that in the context of general boundary erosion — delayering, business networking, re-engineering and virtual working — the locus of organizational boundary activities has migrated from the level of the organization towards the work unit. In many cases, this means that teams, of varying types, have the prime responsibility for grappling with boundary management issues. According to Yan & Louis (1999), there are three key sets of activities involved here: boundary buffering, boundary spanning and bringing up boundaries. We will discuss each in turn.

## Boundary buffering

Here, boundaries are put up to seal off or demark the work group or team from outside disturbances that might threaten its integrity. Administrative, cultural and political pressures need to be dealt with. A key buffering activity is thus to prioritize the team's objectives to preserve it as a unit and to ensure that its resources — including the time and energy use of its members — are not diverted elsewhere.

## Boundary spanning

As well as sealing its boundaries, work units and teams must work across those boundaries. Activities here involve bargaining, negotiating, co-operating and coalition building in order to further the interests of the team and to secure its goals. In such cases, team members may take on the roles of ‘ambassadors’, lobbying for and securing resources and support. Important here is the fact that boundary spanning may not occur through formal channels or defined roles; indeed, informal, social networks may be formed and mobilized for the purpose.

## Bringing up boundaries

Boundary activities focus on the internal dynamics of the team. Attempts will be made to attract team members' energies, attachments and interests so as to focus them around the team's goals and mission. A key part of this will be managing ‘identity’ — ensuring that a group has a strong identity, to which team members attach their own.

Boundary management activities are clearly of the utmost importance to modern organizations. Yan & Louis (1999) note that in the absence of bureaucratic structures, and the well-defined sets of roles and responsibilities that went with them, it is increasingly left to the individual to deal with demands on time and energy. Given that such individuals are likely to work on several projects, and be members of several teams, activities such as ‘bringing up boundaries’ are clearly of central importance to team success. Moreover, as Yan & Louis (1999) point out in the context of cross-functional teams, there is likely to be competition for scarce resources, support and legitimacy. A great deal of effort may need to go into negotiating with other teams and external parties in order to build coalitions and networks to secure team objectives. As noted above by Boddy & Buchanan (1992), such activities are common in the context of organizational change and project working — particularly where these are complex and involve multiple dependencies. It is here that the political ‘backstaging’ activities of team leaders come to the fore.

Although boundary management activities are important for team integrity and success, it may be difficult to exercise them in certain virtual work arrangements. Given that informal networks may have to be built and mobilized to secure support and resources, ‘back regions’ (Goffman, 1969) are likely to be important. These are places beyond formal work settings, and even beyond the watchful gaze of work colleagues. Lobbying, negotiating and general ‘poli-ticking’ shies away from the formal public stage. In this case, technological intermediaries may be avoided all together. This is particularly so where the medium in question may have an ‘informating’ capacity (Zuboff, 1988); in other words, it captures and records the communications involved — perhaps in the form of a video, or in the text that is part of an email transmission.

The creation of ‘identities’ is clearly important for managing team boundaries. No matter how rich the medium, teams that are dispersed across time and space, rarely meeting face-to-face, may find difficulties in engendering the sense of team identity that might be present in more traditional settings. This may be particularly important where individuals are members of several teams, some being traditional face-to-face ones in which a strong sense of identity has been created. Of course, identity certainly can be created using the new media. But this is likely to rely heavily on communications and interactions of a less formal nature. Recent research illustrates the importance here of narratives and story telling (Galpin & Sims, 1999). Where teams have opportunities to engage in narratives that helped to clarify their common purpose and role within the wider organization, a stronger sense of team identity is likely to result. The development of such narratives may well take time and be better suited to more informal situations — ideally, those occurring face-to-face. Although information technologies may certainly play a key role in substituting face-to-face encounters, the key dynamic is thus not simply media richness but the opportunity to engage in forms of story telling that allow individuals to make sense of their roles and relationships.

As well as the dynamics of boundary management, certain types of virtual team must also be geared up for creating and sharing knowledge. It is to this issue that we now turn.

## Knowledge creation in virtual teams

In addition to understanding the technical, social and political aspects of virtual teams, we also need to focus on understanding the process of knowledge creation. This is particularly so in cross-functional and product development teams, from where the new ideas and products come that are intended to secure competitive success (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Tidd et al., 1997). According to Madhaven & Grover (1998), this requires us to take a cognitive perspective on team processes, in which cognition is seen to be distributed across team members. Here, the members of a team are viewed as bringing their skills, knowledge and strategies to bear on team processes. The challenge in creating and managing teams is thus to combine these in order to ensure the efficient and effective creation of knowledge.

The challenge for virtual teamwork becomes apparent here given the importance of tacit knowledge (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Much of what we know is not held or communicated explicitly. It is only acquired and passed on in the realm of action — in other words, it is knowledge learned by doing. Madhaven & Grover (1998) draw upon Badaracco (1991) to describe such knowledge as ‘embedded’. Combining the embedded knowledge of a team’s members is much more difficult to do, say Madhaven and Grover, than combining explicit knowledge. Here then, embedded knowledge is seen as the potential resulting from a team’s interactions and its store of tacit knowledge. As such, product development is viewed as a process of transferring knowledge from its embedded form and ‘embodying’ it in new products and services.

Madhaven & Grover (1998) claim that the efficiency and effectiveness with which knowledge is transferred from embedded to embodied forms depends on a number of key variables. These include ‘T-shaped’ and ‘A-shaped’ skills. Given that new product development teams comprise people with different types of expertise, the interaction of different ‘knowledge types’ is likely to lead to new ideas and ultimately new knowledge. This process of ‘creative abrasion’ depends, say Madhaven and Grover, on maintaining meaningful and synergistic conversations. This goes beyond mere social skills and requires the experts involved to be acquainted with the potential systemic impact of their tasks and knowledge. The skills to do this are seen as T-shaped. A-shaped skills, on the contrary, refer to the team leader’s role in motivating and managing the knowledge creation process. Ideally, say Madhaven & Grover (1998), this requires expertise in two or more disciplines, allowing the individual to combine insights synergistically from multiple knowledge sources.

Two further variables that influence knowledge transfer include ‘shared mental models’ and ‘new product development routines’. The former reflects the fact that our understanding of the world is based on unconscious assumptions about the way the world works (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995, pp. 69–71). Where such assumptions are shared and common language exists through which they can be articulated, deeper levels of intersubjectivity are likely to develop. In such cases, the language involved is likely to be rich in symbolism and metaphor (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Madhaven & Grover, 1998). This is because, according to Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995, p. 12–3), the tacit and intuitive nature of much (shared) knowledge makes it difficult to express, and leads to a heavy reliance upon figurative language and symbolism. The ability to learn and communicate in such language is unlikely to be a simple matter of ‘media richness’. It may rely heavily on shared experiences in particular action contexts. This suggests that such knowledge is not only tacit, but that it is also situated (Björkegren & Rapp, 1999). As such, it may depend critically upon on a shared appreciation of context, not only so that figurative language can be understood but also for it to be learned in the first instance.

‘New product development routines’ also underline the importance of tacit knowledge acquisition through situated practices. Here, Madhaven & Grover (1998) remind us that patterns of behaviour in organizations are also underpinned by stocks of shared knowledge. In this case, such knowledge is the product of the team because in working together the team will have established regular and predictable practices and ways of interacting that are only appreciated tacitly by individual members. The development of such practices — and internalizing a tacit awareness of them — is likely to take time. But given that such practices may be central to efficient and effective knowledge creation, it is a price that teams and organizations must pay. However, where teams have developed particularly effective new product development routines, they are likely to be a key source of team and organizational success. It may thus make sense to keep such individuals together in a team so that the routines they have developed can be exploited for future projects.

In sum, a cognitive approach to knowledge creation in teams thus forces us to consider tacit knowledge and action contexts. It also demands that we understand how the mental frameworks are developed that allow team members to communicate and understand one another. This means that, from the outset, we cannot presume that members of virtual teams will share common mental models, figurative and symbolic language or particular routines and practices. Given the heavy reliance on shared action context for these to develop, the lack of face-to-face interaction prevalent in virtual working is likely to prove problematical. This may be especially the case where different expert groups are brought together in virtual situations, each having different perspectives, assumptions and views of the world. In such cases, whether or not the latest and richest media are used, the knowledge creation process may prove difficult. To build on this point, we will now look at the issue of ‘media richness and personal interaction’.

## Media richness and personal interaction

There is general agreement in discussions of virtual working that face-to-face encounters provide the richest form of human interaction. Typically, this boils down to matters of ‘media richness’ and the degree to which multimedia technologies can still convey such matters as vocal inflections, body language and facial gestures. It is perhaps no surprise that writers such as Townsend et al. (1998, p. 20) see desktop videoconferencing systems (DVCS) as the ‘core system around which the rest of virtual team technologies are built’. DVCS, they go on to tell us, ‘re-creates the face-to-face interactions of conventional teams, making possible more complex levels of communication among team members.’ At the level of ‘communication’, such technologies may well appear to provide an ample substitute for face-to-face situations. But, if we look beyond the narrow notion of communication and instead recognize that effective teams may rely on establishing a deep dialogue, involving the exchange of tacit knowledge, the meaning of interaction begins to change.

According to Madhaven & Grover (1998), effective knowledge creation and exchange relies on ‘rich personal interaction’, which is not necessarily equivalent to ‘rich media’. Not only do they argue that direct, face-to-face interactions are needed for efficient and effective knowledge transfer, they also argue that these need to be frequent and informal. Only frequent interaction, they claim, provides the opportunity for internalization of tacit knowledge. And only through informal settings can the social ties and networks be formed that allow learning, spontaneity and serendipity to happen. As Nandhakumar (1999) shows, where virtual teams have sought to engineer informal settings using DVCS, the experience is generally found to be disappointing and somewhat unnatural.

For these reasons, we might well be sceptical of claims (e.g. by Townsend et al., 1998) that technologies such as groupware, intranets and DVCS can ‘match or even surpass the effectiveness of face-to-face teams’ (see also Lipnack & Stamps, 1997). But, let us now look at the issue of trust.

## Trust and virtual teams

Another common theme in accounts of virtual teams is the notion that the relations involved should be based around trust. There is rather less discussion, though, as to the form this might take and how it might be achieved. Indeed, some accounts seem to presume that trust will develop simply because it is so important. In their book on virtual teams, Lipnack & Stamps (1997, p. 17) posit that:

‘Through interactions near and far, people develop trusting relationships, the invisible bonds (and baffles) of life . . . As important as positive relationships and high trust are in all teams, they are even more important in virtual ones. The lack of daily face to face time, offering opportunities to quickly clear things up, can heighten misunderstandings. For many distributed teams, trust has to substitute for hierarchical and bureaucratic controls.’

By contrast, Handy (1995) posits that trust cannot be presumed but must be managed. Nonetheless, apart from arguing for the benefits of creating belonging and community, he does little to tell us about the different forms trust may take or the ways in which we can intervene in order to shape trusting relationships. More useful ways of thinking about trust distinguish between the role of individuals and their trusting beliefs, interpersonal trust — as developed, for instance, within teams — and institution-based trust where trust is generated through more formal and organizational mechanisms (Madhaven & Grover, 1998; McKnight et al., 1998; Nandhakumar, 1999).

McKnight et al. (1998) show how contemporary organizational changes — the growth in a cross-functional teams, business networking and joint ventures — heighten the issue of trust and the need to develop trusting relationships. This is all the more important, they say, in situations that are unstructured and characterized by high degrees of novelty and ambiguity. Although convention would have it that trust starts low and is built over time, McKnight et al. (1998) demonstrate how important antecedents may even make trust high at the start of relationships only to decline over time. These include, for instance, the disposition of individuals and the extent to which they believe that others can be trusted. The reputation accorded to individuals and groups may also be important. For example, when one hears second-hand that another person is trustworthy and professionally able, this is likely to produce a high trust antecedent.

Madhaven & Grover (1998) distinguish between ‘trust in team orientation’ and trust in team members’ technical competence. As we have seen, given the competing demands on individuals’ time and energy, securing commitments to the team is vital. This is particularly so, according to Madhaven and Grover, for interorganizational and cross-functional teams. Here, the withholding of information can damage the process of knowledge articulation and internalization, thus frustrating the conversion of knowledge from embedded to embodied form.

Trust in the technical competence of other team members may be particularly important in cross-functional teams and especially where project goals may be relatively ambiguous. In these cases, individuals and organizations may need to take care in demonstrating the skills and attainments of members, for example through disseminating details of track records (Madhaven & Grover, 1998). The need for such information contrasts with certain arguments made about virtual teams and the technologies that support them. For example, Townsend et al. (1998) suggest that certain group support systems for brainstorming and group discussions allow individuals to ‘turn off their identities’ and interact with ‘relative anonymity’ (see also Birchall & Lyons, 1995). However, where technologies do promote anonymity, they may succeed in doing quite the opposite to promoting trust in other team members’ technical abilities.

## Cultural differences and virtual teams

Although virtual teams are seen as somewhat of a panacea for resourcing dispersed and global organizations, we may need to remember that in certain circumstances more effective human resource solutions may be possible. As Roberts et al. (1998) show, difficulties in establishing cross-cultural trust and the need for contextual knowledge of other cultures may mean that more conventional workforce strategies are deployed. The importance of culture is well established in the literature on organizations, as well as information systems (e.g. Walsham, 1993; Caldow & Kirby, 1996). Where organizations operate globally, a broad cultural perspective, as well as particular cross-cultural expertise, may be needed. In certain circumstances, the tacit nature of cultural knowledge may mean that learning it demands sustained presence in the culture in question. Indeed, as Roberts et al. (1998) point out, for some organizations assigning people to work in different countries may be part of an explicit strategy of human resource development, not simply an exercise in securing the expertise needed for given projects or jobs.

For these reasons, Roberts et al. (1998) suggest that global workforce strategies should include a number of alternatives to ‘virtual deployment’. These include ‘aspatial careers’, in which individuals spend a considerable amount of time in a foreign country in order to experience the cultural complexities involved in working there. In addition, ‘awareness building assignments’ may be used in order to broaden an individual’s cultural awareness, as well as to address human resource needs. The role of virtual deployment is seen here to be limited to assignments in which there is little requirement for cultural knowledge or even cultural sensitivity.

Let us move now to discuss what we can conclude from the above analyses.

## DISCUSSION

The literature on virtual teams suggests an ‘ideal type’ of arrangement, in which experts can be brought together at a moment’s notice, despite the barriers of time and space. There is also the suggestion that such teams will not tend to endure, but disband as soon as a particular problem is solved or project completed. The analysis presented above suggests we should be cautious about such approaches. Far from identifying an ideal type of virtual team, we have seen that a robust understanding of virtual team phenomena must recognize the diversity of team types. Because of these differences, the sort of tasks that virtual teams are charged with, as well as the people who constitute them, may vary significantly. This will have an important bearing on the longevity of teams, the time it takes them to learn to work together effectively and the requirements for face-to-face interactions.

Organizations need to ask some basic questions if they are to understand how to introduce and support virtual teams as part of their business processes. Given that teamwork may be part of a bigger strategic picture, managers need to be sure of the contribution that teamwork should make towards competitive success. In situations characterized by ‘mass customization’, self-managing product and service teams are likely to be important. Such arrangements, even virtual ones, will depend upon a general transferability of skills between team members. Virtual working may offer the possibility here of 24-h global service and production, shortening delivery times and improving quality of service to customers. Because such teams are not composed of diverse expert groups, and are not primarily concerned with the creation of new knowledge, there are likely to be few problems in the area of cognitive processes. By their very nature, product and service teams are likely to be relatively permanent arrangements. Such teams may thus be in a good position to develop a strong sense of identity, as well as to secure trusting relations. Key issues will thus be ensuring fairness and equity within team operations.

Virtual teams that are constituted for product development purposes will be different in nature. In these cases, diverse expertise will be present, with the arrangements not intended to be permanent. The key purpose of such teams is ‘knowledge creation’. Given the range of expertise, such teams will involve different mental maps of the world, a variety of assumptions, and also different norms and values by which team progress and success may be measured. The knowledge held by individual members, as well as that created by the team as a whole, is likely to be largely tacit in nature. It may, for instance, be embodied in the practices and interactions that take place between team members. Such knowledge will also be created and articulated through a figurative and symbolic language developed by the team itself. We have also seen that tacit knowledge may also rely heavily on situated practices — in other words, shared action contexts involving frequent and rich interaction. Moreover, the efficiency and effectiveness of knowledge creation and transfer will need high levels of trust within the team. Taken together, such dynamics suggest that virtual teams that never meet face-to-face may not be the ideal arrangement for product development.

It is not surprising that where virtual teams are discussed in the literature, such as the discussion by Bourdreau et al. (1998) of Kodak, members of the development team had previously worked together in face-to-face settings. Such rich forms of interaction are likely to have produced the ‘shared mental models’, figurative language and ‘product development routines’ that Madhaven & Grover (1998) cite as so important for efficient and effective knowledge creation and transfer. In this case, trust and cultural appreciation is also likely to have developed. Moving from such a situation to working together as a team split among more than one site may be a relatively simple step compared with working in a virtual team from the beginning.

A similar situation can be found in the account given by Boutellier et al. (1998) of dispersed product development teams at IBM. These authors note that, although much of the work on developing new software systems may take place in global teams, at important junctures time is also spent in face-to-face settings. For instance, during the ‘design phase’, the need to develop a ‘common understanding’ demanded the presence of project leaders in conventional work settings for several days. Thereafter, email and other media were used to facilitate co-ordination within and between teams. Boutellier et al. (1998) conclude that travel still plays a major role in dispersed teams, although this is increasingly supported by IT. They also point to the importance of the face-to-face ‘socializing phase’ at the start of a project.

This suggests that where complex projects are concerned, as characterized by diverse expert groups and issues of knowledge creation, there is still heavy reliance on face-to-face interactions. Moreover, for such teams to work effectively and efficiently, time needs to be given over to creating a common understanding, developing team processes and routines, and generally creating trusting and open relationships. This cannot be achieved overnight, as some discussions of virtual teams presume. The social, cultural, behavioural and cognitive processes involved take time to develop. Once this has occurred, it is unlikely that organizations will simply choose to disband teams, given their investments in time and energy and the mutual understanding that they now possess. Indeed, it may be one or two projects down the line before such teams start to reap the benefits from these investments.

The lessons for managers and information systems planners are that before consideration can be given to the best means of IT support attention needs to be given to the role a particular virtual team is playing in broader business processes, as well as the type of team it is. After this, a thorough appreciation of team processes is required. Steps may have to be taken here to address the need to encourage processes of boundary management, knowledge transfer, trust building and cultural appreciation. The above analysis suggests that, in many cases, virtual work arrangements must be supplemented by face-to-face interactions. Identifying where and when these should take place must be part of the broader process of designing the information architecture that will support virtual teams.
