# Telehealth acquires meanings: information and communication technologies within health policy

Ela Klecun-Dabrowska & Tony Cornford\*

Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK, email: ela.klecun@brunel.ac.uk \* Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK, email: t.cornford@lse.ac.uk

Abstract. Over the last decade, British health policy has increasingly considered the role of information and information technologies in the provision of health care. Recently, there is a growing interest in new health applications, often referred to as telemedicine and telehealth, and incorporating a diverse range of services that can be provided in electronic form over various telecommunications networks. This paper discusses how such use of information and communication technologies (ICTs) in health acquires meanings through a policy process, and what implications it has for telehealth in particular. This is done through a hermeneutic exploration of four principal UK health policy papers published between 1989 and 1998.

Keywords: Health policy, hermeneutics, information society, telehealth

## INTRODUCTION

Health care, alongside education, housing and employment, is considered to be fundamental to citizenship and, in Britain, health policy has been a significant part of the political agenda since the establishment of the National Health Service (NHS) in 1948. In the last decade, it has been given a particularly high priority. This is in part because of peoples’ strong feelings regarding health care and the NHS, and their perception of growing pressures on the welfare state and the health service. Wider trends in society, including demographic and health trends, specifically the ageing of the population and the increase in chronic, long-standing illnesses (itself largely a result of ageing), as well as peoples’ rising expectations, are seen to put increased pressure on health care provision, forcing a re-evaluation of current practices and the underlying philosophy behind them.

Against this background, a number of influential health policy papers have been issued by recent UK Governments. Principal among these are: Working for Patients (DOH, 1989), The Health of the Nation (DOH, 1992), The New NHS: Modern. Dependable (DOH, 1997) and Our Healthier Nation (DOH, 1998). (Separate documents were produced for Scotland and Wales, taking into account regional differences in health care provision.)

This succession of white papers and green papers articulate a changing vision of health and medicine, and their authors have set out (quite deliberately) to reform the NHS. Initially, information and communication technologies (ICTs) have been seen as having a conventional role in improving administration and service delivery. More recent papers start to reflect new ideas for telemedicine, telecare and telehealth. These terms refer to services that can be provided in electronic form over various telecommunications networks. Telemedicine facilitates remote patient care in an institutional setting, for example linking hospitals with remote surgeries and enabling remote diagnosis. Telecare refers to services that provide care for people away from institutions, typically in their own homes, for example monitoring elderly people as they lead their normal lives. Telehealth is perhaps more encompassing and includes opportunities for health promotion among a wide range of people including the young and healthy.

Our research is concerned with telehealth as an emerging phenomenon. We are interested in the process by which telehealth is given meanings in national health policy and strategy, in local strategies and in individual initiatives and projects. This paper reports a subset of our research, namely our search for unfolding meanings of telehealth in this sequence of key policy documents, and highlights problems, contradictions and potential tensions in those meanings. However, the concept of telehealth is new to health policy, and it is only explicitly discussed in a few passages of the latest documents. Furthermore, discussion of telehealth is inevitably related to proposals for the employment of ICTs in other areas (e.g. in hospital information systems). Thus, in order to improve our understanding of telehealth, we need to acquire a broader understanding of how health policy embraces ICTs. This paper seeks meanings of telehealth in this policy context but, more generally, seeks to unfold meanings of ICTs and how different intertwined factors present in policy documents shape understanding and expectations for health telematics.

It is easy to overlook the importance of policy documents and to suggest that they become of relevance to information systems only when they are enacted. Certainly, we do not claim that policies are always carried out or that they become concretized in real-life projects. We do, however, believe that policy documents, while not defining telehealth, are an important part of developing its meaning – in Swanson & Ramiller’s (1997) terms, providing some of the organizing vision. For example, policy documents set the context for the development of new technologies and services, and they provide the language of discourse as policy is (to whatever degree) enacted. We could also interpret policy as providing the framework within which telehealth will be judged as a success or a failure. In very simple terms, if policy emphasizes efficiency and cost cutting, services that do not contribute towards these goals, even if offering a better service for the population, may be deemed as failures. Thus, we believe that government policy is an important factor shaping health services and, hence, the forms that health telematics and telehealth take. This is particularly true in the UK because the majority of the health sector is under direct government control within the structures of the NHS.

We start our paper by introducing our theoretical perspective and research methods, based on the hermeneutic tradition, particularly as developed by Gadamer. In section after that we discuss two models (Miles’ scenarios of information society and Boland’s fantasies of information) that form a part of our foreknowledge and that we have found to be particularly useful in our interpretations of policy papers. The first reading of the papers introducing main policy proposals is presented in the section entitled Scenarios for information society and fantasies of information. The following section discusses alternative readings. The Potential implications of policy documents for telehealth section considers the implications of policy papers for telehealth. Finally, there is a Conclusions section.

## HERMENEUTICS AS A THEORETICAL PERSPECTIVE AND A RESEARCH METHOD

## Hermeneutics and the concept of meaning

In our research, we are presented with a problem of interpreting policy documents and finding in them the meaning(s) of telehealth. Hermeneutics, an established but very diverse theory, offers insights as to how such tasks may be performed. We draw on hermeneutics on two levels, theory and methodology, to clarify our theoretical stance (stemming from the interpretative tradition) and as a research methodology for reading texts.

In this research, we engage in a hermeneutic process (hermeneutic circle) to gain (some) understanding of health policy documents and the meanings they give to health telematics. While reading the policy papers, we move between ‘parts’, sections of the document directly referring to health telematics, to the ‘whole’ document, thus trying to understand specific state ments within the context of the overall policies. However, the ‘whole’ could be understood as something much bigger. The policies themselves might, for example, be considered in the light of the health and medicine debate, other national and international policies, political discourse and general trends in the society. The process of reading and interpreting is not finite; there is no definite point at which our understanding becomes complete. Each reading, through the dialogue with the subject, enriches our understanding. Thus, in this paper, the first reading of the policy documents focuses on proposals as they are presented. The subsequent readings use Miles’ framework and Boland’s fantasies of information to reveal challenges and problems inherent in the text.

Hermeneutics was initially concerned with interpreting the Bible; then, the approach was extended to other texts from different cultures and times. More recently, hermeneutics has come to encompass an approach to the study of all products or ‘objectifications’ of the human mind, including institutions, works of art, literature and technology (Burrell & Morgan, 1985). Hermeneutic tradition incorporates different approaches and points of view. Even concepts of meaning and interpretation are understood in different ways. Thus, initially, meaning of a text was seen as a given, and the process of interpretation involved unfolding this meaning. This resulted in striving for objectivity and the use of scientific methods. The notion of validity of the interpretation was linked in particular to uncovering the author’s intentions. However, this view came to be disputed later, notably by Gadamer (1976). He emphasizes the historical perspective of meaning and the role of an interpreter (reader of the text) in the process of understanding. Thus, the text can have many meanings rather then one meaning. Gadamer considers meaning as dependent on the context, and he sees understanding as situated, e.g. historically and culturally. Yet this approach does not imply total relativity and that any interpretation is acceptable. Gadamer suggests that understanding must be critical and reflexive, involving a dialogue with the text and awareness of our own (the reader’s) prejudices.

This hermeneutic understanding of meaning does not only apply to texts but also to objects or concepts (in a sense, anything that could be termed as a text metaphor). As Palmer (1969, p. 24) puts it

. . . meaning is a matter of context . . . . Only within a specific context is an event meaningful. . . . We may say that an object does not have significance outside of a relationship to someone, and that the relationship determines the significance. To speak of an object apart from a perceiving subject is a conceptual error caused by an inadequate realistic concept of perception and the world; but even granting this concept, does it make sense to speak of meaning and significance apart from perceiving subjects?

This is not to deny the physical existence of objects, but to accept that their meanings are dependent on perceiving subjects. While in our broader research, we are also concerned with meanings given to telehealth by different people through their actions (e.g. their involvement in projects or experiences with telehealth), this paper specifically considers meanings of telehealth, ICTs and information as they appear to us through policy documents.

## Main concepts of hermeneutics

One of the main concepts of hermeneutics developed by Gadamer is the hermeneutic circle of understanding. This expresses the need to understand the parts of a text through the understanding of the whole, while the understanding of the whole is determined by our understanding of its parts. Such understanding is based on lived experience. We start with foreunderstanding (prejudice) of the subject and, through dialogue with the subject, we move to and from the subject (the part) to the context (the whole). This process of (multiple) interpretations leads us to a better understanding of the subject in a given situation (Hoy, 1982). The concept of foreunderstanding is an important one. It implies the subjectivity of our interpretations, which are situated in our horizon. It also asserts the importance of our experience. ‘For the interpreter to “perform” the text, he must “understand” it: he must preunderstand the subject and the situation before he can enter the horizon of its meaning.’ (Palmer, 1969). This preunderstanding is then altered through dialogue with the text.

The hermeneutic approach, although not very common in information systems research, has been applied, for example by Boland (1985, 1991), Davis et al. (1992), Lee (1993, 1994) and Butler (1998). Lee (1994) applies the hermeneutic circle in the reading of a sequence of email exchanges. He illustrates how different messages are given distinct meanings by different readers, and how the readers alter the meaning of one message in the context of other messages. The hermeneutic circle takes place not only between a reader and different messages, but also between the reader and the organizational context. Lee supports the concept of a listener/reader/receiver as an active producer of meaning, rather than a passive recipient. The concept of the hermeneutic circle is also applied by Davis et al. (1992) to the process of interpretation of information system failure. He illustrates how successive passes through the circle allow the observer to reach a new meaning for the behaviour in question, and how behaviours (or more generally text passages) that seem unreasonable acquire meaning, when interpreted within a wider frame.

Boland (1991) applies a hermeneutic perspective to demonstrate that even computergenerated numeric documents can be rich in meaning to their readers. Similarly, Lee (1993) shows that email can be perceived as a medium for rich communication, despite the fact that it is text based. Both authors use concepts of distanciation, autonomization and appropriation, as developed by Ricoeur (1981). Distanciation refers to the separation, in time and distance, between a text and its author, its originally intended audience and, in some instances its originating culture and society. Autonomization signifies that the text ‘takes on a life of its own’ despite the distanciation. While the concept of appropriation indicates that, in the process of interpretation, a reader actualizes the meaning of the text for himself/herself, i.e. makes the text his/her own. Lee (1993) extends the concept of appropriation and suggests that the text and the socially constructed world behind it can appropriate the reader. This kind of appropriation results in the reader enacting the meaning for the text. Thus, the reader is not free to imagine any interpretation, but he/she is bound into the socially constructed world (or ‘non-ostensive reference’ as Ricour puts it) standing behind the text. (Lee emphasizes that socially constructed reality is not a synonym for subjective. It implies that this reality can be shared by different people and, what is more, it can be sustained by other people long after its originators are gone.) Although Ricour’s perspective is criticised by Palmer (1969) for its somewhat linear account of understanding, it adds additional insights to the process of interpretation. For example, it suggests that, although we can interpret a policy document in different ways, the text and its non-ostensive references influence these interpretations.

## Our foreknowledge

The concept of foreknowledge (prejudice) is central to hermeneutics as developed by Gadamer. Prejudice is not a negative concept and, indeed, it is necessary to the understanding of the text, but it must be questioned and made (as much as possible) explicit. Our foreknowledge includes (perhaps limited) understanding of the political situation in Britain and the

NHS, as well as familiarity with the ongoing debate about health and medicine. As this paper is concerned with ICTs, we focus on our prejudices that stem from our knowledge of the information systems discipline. Thus, we approach reading of the documents with prior understanding of information society issues, including the relevance and roles of ICTs, information systems, telehealth and the notion of information.

We believe that the information technology projects are fundamentally problematic and that a process of developing and implementing information systems does not run smoothly. There are numerous examples of information systems failures within the health domain and acknowledgement of the problems of evaluation of such systems (Anderson & Stephen, 1987; Kaplan, 1988; Mumford, 1993; Sheaff & Peel, 1995; Anderson, 1997; Raghutathi, 1997; Rindfleisch, 1997). There are also considerable challenges to introducing ICTs in medical contexts, and organizational structures and social/power relationships are particularly significant within health care organizations. As such bodies are highly diffuse, they incorporate many influential professions who are independent of management and draw on different cultures and beliefs. Members of those professions are likely to be committed to their profession as well as to the organization. Other obstacles to the implementation of ICTs in health care organizations is the lack of an information or IT culture, the difficulty of modelling complex and uncertain problems, and the sensitivity of data (privacy and confidentiality) and instances when patient safety may depend on its timeliness, accuracy and availability (Aldridge, 1995).

Likewise, we consider the notion of information as problematic. We question the perception of information as a ‘Holy Grail’ and believe that understanding and acting upon information is not a straightforward process. This is acknowledged by health policy specialists (Abel-Smith, 1994; Rogers et al., 1998) and increasingly pointed out within the information systems discipline (Boland, 1987; Introna, 1997; Macias-Chapula, 1997). In the Boland’s fantasies of information subsection, we discuss five fantasies of information as presented by Boland (1987) and then, in the Images of information subsection, we use these insights in our interpretation of policy documents.

The word telehealth contains two parts; the first ‘tele’ signifies distance and technologies that overcome it, and the second is concerned with the concept of health. Our notion of health is of a societal phenomenon, rather than purely individual or medical. Thus, we see it in the context of societal trends and the way society is evolving with ICTs. We also understand the ‘tele’ part as implying the crossing of organizational boundaries, changing structures and shifts in time, as well as roles and potentials for ICTs. We consider these within a wider debate on the nature of information society and contradicting views: one declaring that a new kind of society is emerging, post-industrial (Bell, 1973), information (Bell, 1980) or post-modern (Castells, 1989), and another seeing current developments as a continuum of the past (Beniger, 1986; Mulgan, 1991; Webster, 1995). The former indicates a major shift in society with its enormous implications based on the emergence of information technologies and services. But even those who emphasize continuity still tend to agree that substantive changes, quantitative and qualitative, are taking place. These different views on the nature of the changes are taken further in the next section.

## SCENARIOS FOR INFORMATION SOCIETY AND FANTASIES OF INFORMATION

In this section, we provide an elaboration of our prejudices based on prior academic work. By framing our foreknowledge in these terms, we are able to achieve in key areas the required ‘explicit statement’ of what we bring to our interpretative actions.

## Miles’ model of alternative scenarios for information society

Miles (1996) sees developments in ICTs as pivotal to the process of shaping our society. He points to two dimensions underlying the debate on the social implications of ICTs: depth and width. The depth perspective depicts different views on speed and the extent of change. At one extreme, ‘continuism’ stresses the continuity of trends, the limited extent of ICT-related change and social and economic innovation in society (even to deny the notion of the information society). At the other end, ‘transformism’ regards information society as a fundamentally different society, in which ICTs have an all-pervasive revolutionary potential. The other dimension, introduced by Miles, refers to contradicting views about the nature of information society, on the one hand as a place of greater democracy, decentralization, self-expression and personal choice, as proclaimed by ‘concordists’, while on the other a centralized society characterized by the threat of greater surveillance and control of political and personal activities, as envisaged by ‘antagonists’.

Views that try to synthesize these diverse beliefs, and almost all do, attempt to build on contributions from all positions and are what Miles calls ‘structuralism’. Structuralism proclaims that a diversity of actors and interests result in a multiplicity of choices and many outcomes. Thus, there is not simply one future outcome but many possible ones. Although such a framework may seem simplistic, it does illustrate a diversity of views, and it sensitizes us to different paths open (or not so open) to society. It also helps us to understand the proposed and emerging role(s) of health telematics. Thus, IT and, specifically, telehealth technologies might be deployed and used to serve the aims of social cohesion and personal freedom, or they may lead to social exclusion and a control regime for the provision of health. Here, we read and interpret those different roles of health telematics in the policy documents and consider whether they are somehow resolved (or not). We do not, however, investigate directly how telematics may change the society. We subscribe to Webster’s (1995) argument that the assumption that a technological innovation results in social change is the wrong point of departure to study technology and society. He proposes that it is misleading to separate the social realm from technology/technique (i.e. to see no influence of beliefs and values on technological developments) and then to bring those two together when describing the ‘impact’ of technology on society.

## Boland’s fantasies of information

The policy papers we study emphasize the importance of information, within the NHS and other organizations, as well as for the whole population. Information also plays an important role in the delivery of telehealth services. Furthermore, the concept of information is central in the information systems discipline. Thus, we feel it is important to focus on the images of information unfolding in the texts. We try to interpret them within a horizon of our foreknowledge, specifically referring to Boland’s fantasies of information.

Drawing on the hermeneutic tradition, Boland (1987) and Introna (1997) deconstruct the concept of information (in-formation) and emphasize the importance of meaning and lived experience. Boland (1987) identifies ‘five fantasies of information’ present in everyday assumptions about information systems: (a) information is structured data; (b) organization is information; (c) information is power; (d) information is intelligence; and finally (e) information is perfectable. These fantasies, he argues, deny the importance of dialogue in the creation of meaning and, thus, they are false representations of language and communication.

The ‘information is structured data’ fantasy decontextualizes information and denies or puts aside the notion that ‘information is really the meaning of data to a free, intentional human being’ (Boland, 1987, p. 370, author’s emphasis). Thus, it allows us to ignore the problem of interpretation. ‘[Organisation is information fantasy] . . . extends the fantasy that information is structured data and applies it to the entire organization. It turns the organization as a whole into structured data.’ (pp. 371–372). The third fantasy, ‘information is power’, distorts the idea of power relationships. As Boland (1987), following Foucault, points out ‘Properly understood, power is a relationship, not an object.’ (p. 373). Thus, it cannot be given or passed on to people by an information system. ‘It (information) neither gives nor brings power. . . . Information is found in the lived experience of the human condition and the fantasies which delude us into looking elsewhere will only lead us to search in vain.’ (p. 370). While ‘The fantasy that information is intelligence, like the first three, continues to expand the fallacy of scientism. Now we reach beyond communication, beyond control and beyond power into the realm of the organization as designable mind. The fallacy that intelligence is an object, a refined example of structured data, is especially insidious because it provides the further and now complete separation of information from the human agent. . . .This fantasy allows the organization to be totally disembodied.’ (p. 374). The last fantasy, ‘information is perfectable’, brings the first four together and uses them to delude us to believe that there exists a possibility of perfect information and a perfect future that is designable and achievable.

These fantasies, ignoring as they do hermeneutic understanding of the world, nonetheless often lie at the core of information systems design. ‘As opposed to the hermeneutic problem of continually accomplishing and re-accomplishing meaning, we are given a single, immutable meaning – without an actor, without a history, without a future.’ (Boland, 1987, p. 376).

## BRITISH HEALTH POLICIES AND HEALTH TELEMATICS

This section reports the first reading of the policy papers, highlighting the main reforms, as well as proposals referring to the application of ICTs. The white paper Working for Patients (DOH, 1989) outlined fundamental administrative reforms, mainly the establishment of an ‘internal market’ in the NHS. This involved the separation of the service into purchasers and providers of care. These two parts were required to conclude contracts with each other. General practitioners (GPs) were given an option to become fundholders, i.e. to control allocated budgets for purchasing part of the secondary care for their patients [Secondary care is specialist care, typically provided in a hospital setting or after referral from a primary or community health professional. Primary care includes family health services provided by family doctors, dentists, pharmacists, optometrists and ophthalmic medical practitioners (DOH, 1997).] In turn, consumers were given some freedom to choose their GP.

These reforms created an increased demand for administrative and managerial information systems. Purchasers required information from providers in support of contracts. For example, a Family Health Services Authority (FHSA), as the purchaser of family health services, needed to know what services were provided, if they were cost-effective, of acceptable quality and whether services were meeting patients’ needs. Likewise, a fundholding practice required a relatively sophisticated information system for monitoring and planning its provision and purchase of care and to administer budgets (Mackintosh & Shakespeare, 1995).

The white paper The Health of the Nation (DOH, 1992) signalled another and equally significant policy shift, from medicine to health. The aim was to promote health, not simply health care, and to prevent illness in order to improve the quality as well as the quantity of life. This stressed the importance of people taking responsibility for their health and suggested that a healthy population was the goal of policy. It promised individual opportunities and wider choices, facilitated by health education in schools and for the general population, emphasiz ing the need for reliable and diverse sources of information about health. The paper proposed health-promoting action in different settings. Such settings would include healthy cities, schools, hospitals, workplaces, homes and environments.

Again, information plays a central role. The paper stated that, overall, more comprehensive health monitoring information was needed both nationally and locally. Monitoring health was described as ‘developing the information base for the health of the nation’ (DOH, 1992; p. 38, section 5.3). The need for the measurement of health outcomes was also emphasized. In addition, it was envisaged that information systems supporting the flow of information from providers to purchasers would focus on the individual patient, and that details of all interventions, treatments and outcomes over time and across all service providers and agencies would become available (without breaking confidentiality rules). These initiatives, as well as the provision of health information to citizens, strongly imply the extensive use of ICTs.

The white paper The New NHS: Modern. Dependable (DOH, 1997), produced by the Labour government, discussed organizational changes for the health service, mainly the replacement of the internal market with a model of integrated care. It set out the challenges for the NHS as providing high quality treatment, working with others to improve the health of the nation and reduce health inequalities, as well as tailoring the NHS to meet the needs of individuals. The aim was expressed as to deliver better care at lower costs. New, flexible, local ways of delivering health and health care, as well as the integration of health and social care services were promised. Primary care was to benefit from greater investments and the expansion of its role, including taking the responsibility for managing resources for both primary and some secondary care. At the same time, its gatekeeping role was re-emphasized, seen as a good way of controlling the costs of health care.

Health authorities were given the task of addressing the health impact of inequalities and co-ordinating health improvement programmes for their districts across health service organizations and other agencies. Thus, in co-operation with others, they were expected to create a framework for health, based on analysis of health needs and monitored by indicators of quality health care and population-based outcomes. They were also to co-ordinate information and information technology plans across primary care, community health services and secondary care.

Another fundamental development seen within the policy was a shift towards the empowerment of patients or rather, in the new language, consumers of health services. [The term ‘empowerment’ is used in the health/political discourse, but it only appears in the health policy papers in relation to health professionals, not patients. Instead, in the policy documents, patients were promised more rights and more choices, e.g. the ability to participate and to influence the health care process and access to more information (about health in general and the NHS in particular, including the performance of different trusts).] This stems from the idea of more informed citizens but goes beyond it by indicating the power to make choices, take part in the decision-making process, and thus influence not only one’s own health but the process of health care provision as well. These ideas were described as fundamental and underlying the government’s philosophy of health.

The white paper stated that the NHS must change, so that it is able to embrace rapid changes in science and medicine as well as to harness the potential of modern information and communication technologies.

The speed of change in science and medicine and the potential of modern information and communication systems require the NHS to embrace change. A modern and dependable national health service will capture developments in modern medicine and information technology. It will be built around the needs of people, not of institutions and it will provide reliable care. (DOH, 1997, p. 2, para 1.6)

In contrast to the 1989 white paper, the role of information technologies was defined quite explicitly as supporting front-line staff in delivering benefits to patients and bringing new services to communities and into individual homes.

A modern and dependable NHS needs to be supported by accurate and up-to-date information and information technology. In recent years, information technology in the NHS has been focused on supporting the transaction processes of the internal market. This has been at the expense of realising the potential of IT to support front-line staff in delivering benefits for patients. (DOH, 1997, p. 20, para 3.15)

The paper promised some specific actions, including the connection of every GP surgery and hospital to NHSnet, the NHS’s own information superhighway, and electronic links between GPs and pharmacists. This, for example, would allow transfer of patient records, test results, online booking of appointments and receiving up-to-date specialist advice electronically. In addition, general information about health and illness would be delivered directly into homes over the Internet and emerging public access media (e.g. digital TV). A first step in this direction has been the establishment of NHS Direct, a 24-hour telephone advice line staffed by nurses. It is also worth noting that, for the first time in these health policy documents, the word telemedicine was used explicitly: ‘developing telemedicine to ensure specialist skills are available to all parts of the country’ (DOH, 1997, p. 20, para 3.15).

The 1997 white paper was followed by the consultative green paper Our Healthier Nation (DOH, 1998a), which discussed overall health and health care strategies. The government’s aim was defined as ‘To improve the health of the population as a whole’ and ‘To improve the health of the worst off in society and to narrow the health gap’ (p. 5). Fighting health inequalities was linked here to tackling all kinds of inequalities. Social exclusion was identified as both one of the causes and one of the effects of ill health or disability. Strategies for communicating health information included a new web-site, Wired for Health, a window to other credible web-sites providing health information. This would be available to all schools and colleges through the National Grid for Learning, as well as to other organizations and individuals already having access to the Internet. The proposals to tackle health inequalities, complementing the government’s plans for Health Action Zones, included a network of Healthy Living Centres across the UK. Their aim would be to reach out into communities and raise local awareness on varied health issues, for example diet, smoking and physical activities.

## Summary of the ‘first reading’ of the policy documents – from the whole to the parts

This sequence of white and green papers signals a sustained shift from medicine to health and from cure to prevention and health promotion. They convey a vision of a refocused health care system based on a primary care sector serving a population that has the informational resources to manage their own health and to participate more actively in the health care process. This vision, particularly in the Labour policy documents, is set against a broader requirement for raising the quality of care, tackling inequality, promoting social cohesion and, of course, controlling costs.

Over the decade, ICTs’ role, as envisaged in the papers, has expanded and shifted from reforming and supporting administrative and management functions within the NHS, through facilitating the collection and processing of data on the health of the population and health outcomes to enabling the delivery of information and care to communities and into homes (Sheaff & Peel, 1995; Lenagham, 1998). In recent health policy publications, all these roles are present. The goal of more integrated care within complex institutional structures puts forward a strong image of a central role for ICTs, contributing to the goals of increased efficiency, effectiveness and quality of care (e.g. by facilitating the move towards evidence-based medicine and monitoring performance), as well as assisting with the monitoring of population-based outcomes. Finally, they are depicted as supporting the provision of medical services and care (telemedicine and telecare), as well as health promotion and information (telehealth).

## ALTERNATIVE INTERPRETATIONS – CHALLENGES AND TENSIONS IN THE POLICY TEXTS

Policy documents can be interpreted in many different ways. Policy papers might be accepted as ‘straightforward’ and the messages they convey taken as facts (rather than rhetorical statements). The ‘preferred reading’ embedded within policy papers encourages us to think of policies as generally ‘good’, for example leading to a better quality of care, reducing health inequalities and resulting in a healthier population. Likewise, the preferred reading of applications/services enabled by ICTs embedded in the papers is of unproblematic technology/services that ‘work’. In subsequent readings of the papers, summarized in this section, we attempt to develop a critical understanding of the policies, highlighting challenges, tensions and possible problems inherent in them. To help us in this task, we use as analytical tools Miles’ framework (Figure 1) and Boland’s fantasies of information.

## Images of ICTs

## Transformism vs. continuism dimension

All the policy documents deliberately set out to transform the health service and to reform the NHS. Thus, they initiated the restructuring of the NHS and encouraged the employment of new technologies to support the transformation of health service provision. In the foreword to Working for Patients (DOH, 1989), Mrs Thatcher wrote:

Taken together, the proposals represent the most far-reaching reform of the National Health Service in its 40 years history.

This transformation or (as it is referred to) modernization is also strongly emphasized a decade later in the foreword by Tony Blair to The New NHS – Modern. Dependable (DOH, 1997):

The NHS needs to modernise in order to meet the demands of today’s public. This White Paper begins a process of modernisation. The NHS will start to provide new and better services to the public.

Despite the trend to reform and modernize, the fundamental assumptions upon which the NHS was built remain intact. The notion of national health service free for all at the point of delivery and based upon principles of equality and equity was confirmed by both governments:

But major tasks now face us: to bring all parts of the National Health Service up to the very high standard of the best, while maintaining the principles on which it was founded; and to prepare for the needs of the future. (DOH, 1989, foreword by the Prime Minister)

The Government has committed itself anew to the historic principle of the NHS: that if you are ill or injured there will be a national health service there to help: and access to it will be based on need and need alone – not on your ability to pay, or on who your GP happens to be or on where you live. (DOH, 1997, foreword by the Prime Minister)

Within the documents, the transformism and continuism positions are resolved in a very particular way. It appears that, although technologies are given a transformist role, this is within a ‘continuation’ framework. Thus, although the structures may be altered, and the way services are delivered may change or even services themselves may be different, the underlying values will be preserved.

## Concordism vs. antagonism

Further examination of the papers reveals that there are also tensions between concordist and antagonist positions, as in the following statements from Working for Patients:

. . . to ensure that all concerned with delivering services to the patient make the best use of the resource available to them, quality of service and value for money will be more rigorously audited. (DOH, 1989, p. 5)

Those who take decisions which involve spending money must be accountable for that spending. Equally, those who are responsible for managing the service must be able to influence the way in which its resources are used. (DOH, 1989, p. 7, para 1.16)

Delegation downwards must be matched by accountability upwards. (DOH, 1989, p. 11)

These are proposals that challenge the professional autonomy of health care workers by subjecting them to stricter managerial and financial controls. Information systems are seen as tools of control – for monitoring of practices and adherence to targets and budgets.

The text can be read not only on the basis of what it incorporates but also on what it excludes. Thus, it is indicative that, despite the emphasis on a healthier population, The Health of the Nation (DOH, 1992) did not give priority to health inequalities. The paper did not address ‘causes of the causes’ of illness, for example social circumstances such as poverty or unemployment. This emphasis on individual responsibility for health coupled with attempts to monitor and, to some extent, control the practices of health professionals reflect some of the characteristics of Miles’ antagonist scenario. The government was criticized at the time for its unwillingness to tackle the social reasons behind ill health and for an excessive emphasis on targets that become ends in themselves (Gabbay, 1998). It seems that the establishment of fundholding practices did not change GP culture evolving around caring for the sick individual, rather than promoting the health of the population. No adequate incentives were introduced to support this shift (DOH, 1998b). Thus, although The Health of the Nation strategy had ‘an important symbolic role [ . . . ] its impact on local policy-making was negligible.’ (DOH, 1998b, p. 13)

Some of these ‘antagonist’ characteristics may also be uncovered in the later policy papers (although these are not so obvious as they use less direct language). For example, there may be a conflict between different GP roles – as health promoters but, at the same time, gatekeepers to the other NHS services. Although GPs are given increased power (e.g. to set budgets and to influence health services, not just their own practices), this means additional responsibilities and the necessity to adhere to budgets that may result in cutting levels of services (Chisholm, 1998; Gabbay, 1998). The policy also makes no acknowledgement that GPs are already overworked (or see themselves as so) and have little capacity to take on their new and central role.

Overall, the 1997 paper places strong emphasis on efficiency and monitoring. This is evident in the texts below:

For the first time in the history of the NHS the Government will align clinical and financial responsibility to give all the professionals who make prescribing and referring decisions the opportunity to make financial decisions in the best interests of their patients. That will better attune local services to local needs. But the Government will set a framework of national standards and will monitor performance to ensure consistency and fairness. (DOH, 1997, para 1.22)

. . . the Government will develop a national schedule of ‘reference costs’ which will itemise what individual treatments across the NHS cost. By requiring NHS Trusts to publish and benchmark their own costs on the same basis, the new arrangements will give Health Authorities, Primary Care Groups and the NHS Executive a strong lever with which to tackle inefficiency. (DOH, 1997, para 3.11)

Thus, information systems’ role is described as ‘supporting the drive for quality and efficiency in the NHS’ (DOH, 1997, p. 20). Efficiency in its simplest form is often taken as meaning cost control. Thus, the concern is that health telematics used for this purpose may be contradictory to the ideas of social cohesion and user empowerment. Particularly, the goal of increased efficiency and, at the same time, better quality is difficult to achieve.

Some of the proposals in all the papers are ‘concordist’ in nature. For example, the emphasis on the role of primary care and health promotion sets a context within which telehealth services might be developed and delivered. The programme of healthy schools, healthy workplaces and healthy neighbourhoods de-emphasizes health promotion directed purely at individuals. Within these community settings, telehealth could be regarded as technology supporting group rather than individual behaviour and contributing to social cohesion (DOH, 1992; DOH, 1998a). In contrast to earlier papers, Our Healthier Nation adopts at times such a strongly ‘concordist’ tone:

In a modern and strong society, united by core values of fairness and compassion, it is vital that everyone gains from a national drive for better health. (DOH, 1998a, p. 9, para 1.6)

The causes of ill health do not therefore rest with individuals on their own or with Government on its own. They are shared by society. (DOH, 1998a, p. 26, para 2.36)

The paper promises extra resources for initiatives promoting good health, particularly in the community setting (e.g. Health Action Zones, Healthy Living Centres). It links bad health to social and economic causes (particularly social exclusion) and proposes to combat those ills:

Social exclusion involves not only social but also economic and psychological isolation. Although people may know what affects their health, their hardship and isolation mean that it is often difficult to act on what they know. The best way to make a start on helping them live healthier lives is to provide help and support to enable them to participate in society, and to help them improve their own economic and social circumstances. (DOH, 1998a, p. 17, para 2.7)

These proposals suggest the need for a particular type of services that may, for example, help to decrease the social exclusion of disadvantaged groups and communities. The employment of ICTs to fulfil such a goal might be seen as a reflection of concordism in Miles’ framework. These proposals are set against a vision of a new, more participative society, in which people, given rights, tools and support, take charge of their destiny.

## Meanings of telehealth unfolded in this reading

Different passages even in the same documents create (for us) contradictory meanings for ICTs that could find their way in to more than one quadrant of Miles’ framework. Trying to reconcile these conflicting meanings, we turn to the whole documents. We conclude that they essentially introduce strong managerial discourse (‘managerialism’) to the NHS culture. Thus, particular passages should be interpreted within the managerial ethos. This implies that telehealth may need to be developed in such a context responding to the goal of aligning clinical and financial responsibilities, with emphasis on efficiency and effectiveness. In such a context, telehealth also acquires meaning as a tool of control, introducing monitoring of practices or even imposing different work practices.

Yet, particularly in the recent papers (DOH, 1997; DOH, 1998a), we deduct another discourse – of social responsibility and community values. Within this context, telehealth and ICTs can be perceived as a tool applied to the goal of eradicating social exclusion and making the provision of health services more egalitarian, i.e. not dependent on people’s social class or geographical location. Those two discourses, although not necessarily mutually exclusive, are nevertheless difficult to reconcile. Thus, we turn our attention to a wider context. Behind the policy documents stands socially constructed reality (or realities), including the reality of New Labour values. Noting that conflicting meanings are present in this reality as well, we interpret the documents in the context of the striving for ‘a third way’ between societal/ community values and individual responsibilities, but also, perhaps primarily so, in the context of economic pressures facing the NHS and the welfare state.

## Images of information

This reading focuses on telehealth more directly, particularly on ‘information’ as part of telehealth. Images of information in the policy documents are teased out using Boland’s five fantasies of information. First, we provide our interpretations of selected paragraphs, and then we consider their meaning, i.e. meaning of the parts, in the context of the whole documents. To begin with, we consider the following paragraph:

providing knowledge about health, illness and best treatment practice to the public through the Internet and emerging public access media (e.g. digital TV). . . .The aim will be to create a powerful alliance between knowledgeable patients advised by knowledgeable professionals as a means of improving health and healthcare. (DOH, 1997, p. 20)

This text reflects an ‘information is structured data’ fantasy. In a sense, it goes even further, as it equates data with knowledge. It denies that data must be made meaningful to become information, and information needs to be internalized to become knowledge. Investigating how people understand, translate and act upon information, including health messages and promotions, is necessary if we are to use telehealth effectively to this end. Furthermore, even if we accept the notion of ‘data equal information and knowledge’, the idea that health information will result in people adopting healthy lifestyles has to be questioned. As Abel-Smith (1994, p. 44) points out: ‘Health knowledge is not enough to change behaviour because people become prisoners to their social and economic environment.’ To some extent, these limitations are recognized when linking health to action against social and economic deprivation and within a community or group context, as in Our Healthier Nation (DOH 1998a).

The cited paragraph also gives a sense of an ‘information is power’ fantasy when it implies that knowledge (or rather data) will be a base for a ‘powerful alliance’ between doctors and patients. Yet, it is simplistic to assume that the relationship between patients and health professionals will change (with patients gaining more power) because of telehealth applications, or even that telehealth plays any significant part in it. Nevertheless, information (and ICTs, which enable easier dissemination of information as well as access to non-governmental or non-establishment sources) might be seen as the precondition of power even if it does not necessary result in power.

There are other, perhaps more basic, problems with the vision of informed patients using new ICT-based informational resources and taking charge of their health. These include, for example, a problem of universal access to new technologies and services, and lack of skills to use them. To some extent, the papers acknowledge these problems and try to address them, e.g. through the proposal to create Healthy Living Centres with access to the Wired for Health web-site. Similarly, difficulties with health promotion are acknowledged and addressed (at different levels of sophistication) in the policy papers. The Health of the Nation recognizes the problem of inconsistent messages from different sources and (rather simplistically) proposes to remedy this through ‘clear and authoritative’ advice from the government. A deeper understanding of problems concerning health promotion is conveyed in Our Healthier Nation (DOH 1998a):

For people to be able to make an informed judgement on risk they need to be able to understand and weigh up the evidence. They need to be able to use the information provided. It is very important to communicate the right information in the right way. (p. 34, para 3.23)

Yet, even this paragraph betrays a notion of ‘information is structured data’ and ‘information is power’, as it implies that people process data rationally, consider it as ‘evidence’ and, providing conditions are met, feel empowered to act upon it. It also invokes a strong sense of the ‘information is perfectable’ fantasy, as it expresses the belief that ‘the right information and ‘a right way’ of presenting it exist.

Other fantasies are reflected in the text. Consider the following:

A key feature . . . is that doctors, nurses and other professional staff have much more information at their fingertips about the care their patients are getting. Their involvement in the development . . . has also ensured that the new systems are actively used for the benefit of patients. (DOH, 1989, p. 16, para 2.14)

In the community: patients will benefit from quicker test results, up-to-date specialist advice in the doctor’s surgery and on-line booking of out-patient appointments, when we connect every GP surgery and hospital to NHSnet, the NHS’s own information superhighway. (DOH, 1997, p. 6, para 1.12)

And in order to guarantee that patients have quicker access to local services, the Health Authority will coordinate information and information technology plans across primary care, community health services and secondary care. (DOH, 1997, p. 27, para 4.12)

Reading of the above paragraphs reveals that they succumb to ‘organisation is information and ‘information is intelligence’ fantasies. A seductive, but misleading, metaphor of ‘information at fingertips’ is employed in the first text, and all three paragraphs express a simple, deterministic link between IT and better advice for patients and quicker access to services. Thus, information is separated from the organizational context or human agency.

So far, our interpretation has focused on the parts of the text; now, following the hermeneutic circle, we turn to the whole and interpret the paragraphs in the context of the documents. As we have noted before, a strong managerial discourse runs through all the papers. This discourse emphasizes outcomes and uses simplified notions of an organization and social reality in the search for a ‘perfect’ organization. Thus, it could be said that the papers as a whole perpetuate the ‘information is perfectable’ fantasy and, by implication, all other fantasies that build up to this final one. For example, although the 1997 white paper includes paragraphs stressing the value of experience and local knowledge, nevertheless it creates an image of a future perfect organization delivering unified, seamless quality care. This is to be achieved in particular by adhering to new evidence-based and information-driven National Services Frameworks and guidelines from the National Institute for Clinical Excellence and applying strong and directive clinical governance arrangements. Thus, this perfect state is going to be reached, at least partly through the creation and dissemination of ‘perfect’ information.

Taken together, the policy papers put great emphasis on the value of information for the NHS and the population as a whole. Overall, information is seen as something that can easily be captured, understood and transformed in order to achieve a wide range of goals (e.g. more effective and efficient running of the NHS, a better informed and healthier public). In this reading then, telehealth can acquire meaning in a specific context of meanings given to information. Thus, the fantasies of information could be seen as fantasies of telehealth, removing meanings of telehealth from a local, organizational and human context. An additional (but related) fantasy that can be identified is the perception that information is medicine, which can be ‘administered’ to the population.

## POTENTIAL IMPLICATIONS OF POLICY DOCUMENTS FOR TELEHEALTH

In the Introduction to this paper, we have indicated that our research is concerned with telehealth as an emerging phenomenon, and this paper is devoted to finding meanings of telehealth in policy documents. As the documents do not discuss telehealth at length, we have sought to unfold meanings of ICTs and information as indicative of telehealth. Here, we return to our main research interest – how different intertwined factors present in policy documents shape understanding and expectations of telehealth.

In the Alternative interpretations – challenges and tensions in the policy texts section, we have highlighted the transformative role given to ICTs in the policy texts. Telehealth and telemedicine services themselves are increasingly understood as being able to reshape the way health care is delivered: remotely instead of person to person; in the home rather than in hospital; to groups rather than to individuals; and across traditional institutional boundaries. Yet this transformative role is perceived (in the UK) firmly within the boundaries of longestablished goals, particularly of providing quality care regardless of people’s social class or geographical location. Understood in this way, this role fits in well with the overall discourse of social responsibility and community values present in the later documents. This discourse implies that telehealth can develop within a framework of actions aimed at combating social exclusion, increasing social cohesion and bringing better health care to the worst off. It goes beyond a narrow clinical focus and emphasizes telehealth within a social and economic environment.

Within this discourse, telehealth acquires meaning as a tool for sharing experiences and providing help within a community (e.g. self-help groups supported by access to the Internet), helping people to stay in the community rather than in institutions (telecare), giving more choices to people regarding their care (by informing them of existing alternatives and by enabling new services) and offering specialist care locally (aided by telemedicine applications). Yet, the policy documents give rise to other expectations of telehealth. The managerial discourse directs attention to efficiency and effectiveness. This means that the role of telehealth is seen not only as providing new or improved services, but also, or perhaps most importantly, as a way of helping to contain the costs of health care.

The papers imply that these different roles can be reconciled, as one would expect, and that telehealth can provide better and, at the same time, more cost-efficient services. Yet this may not be so easily achievable. One of the discourses, probably the managerial, may exert a dominant influence on developments in telehealth. Emphasis on cost savings and efficiency for telemedicine may encourage the trend towards medicalization, with the care component becoming removed from the meaning of telehealth and telecare. Thus, we must ask if telecare’s aim will become, for example, to enable the elderly and fragile to stay independent or to cut the costs of providing home care by reducing the numbers of personal visits. Does the application of telemedicine have to lead to a better service or just a more cost-efficient one? Is the aim of telehealth, for example NHS Direct, to offer an additional service or will it lead to a reduction in surgeries’ opening times?

These are just a few questions that illustrate ambiguities of future meanings of telehealth, and these ambiguities are also present in another – professional and organizational – dimension of the texts. The trends towards a co-operation across organizational boundaries, reorganizing the NHS, redefining professional roles, e.g. giving increased responsibilities to nurses and midwives, also have implications for developments in telehealth. In this environment, within the social responsibility discourse, telehealth can be seen as able to help the primary sector to provide a wider range of services, to support professionals in their work by providing access to medical information and training and enabling communication and cooperation between different institutions. Yet, within the managerial discourse, telehealth can be given a role in controlling medical practices and limiting professional freedom (e.g. by imposing clinical practices).

We can certainly see that, as a result of the introduction of new technologies, professional roles and work practices may change in ways not welcomed by those involved and thus resisted. Some developments may have different meanings for different people, e.g. increased status for nurses may be read as undermining the position of doctors. Telehealth is often seen in an instrumental way, rather than as part of a rich organizational context. Yet telehealth needs to acquire meaning within the local work practices of all relevant people. Critical theory and feminist writings are particularly useful in drawing our attention to voices that maybe suppressed or simply ignored. For example, notions of ‘invisibility of work’ and ‘articulation of work’ highlight how work (usually done by lower status employees) goes unnoticed and is rarely incorporated into the design of technological systems (Star, 1991; Suchman, 1996).

The contradictions between different discourses in the papers can also be expected to be reflected in different views on how to evaluate telehealth applications, including what to assess and what methods and techniques to use. Some believe that telehealth should be evaluated through some form of cost–benefit analysis or through means akin to drug trials, others that it needs to be assessed on the basis of improvements in services or even improvements in the population’s health. In the case of cost–benefit analysis, the question of the unit of evaluation (the scope) also needs to be addressed. Thus, evaluation could be limited to a particular project, an organization within the NHS or, alternatively, it may include the whole NHS or perhaps a society. Also, a professional dimension has to be addressed in evaluation, including effects on work practices and staff satisfaction. Thus, the overall policy discourse has implications for the evaluation of telehealth, particularly on what is evaluated (the content). Even more importantly, it exerts influence over the initiation of projects (indirectly, by encouraging interests in certain areas, providing positive arguments for negotiating projects; and directly, through a promise of funds). Obviously, projects that can claim that they will save money and deliver a good service have a better chance of being funded.

Overall, and particularly in the latest policy documents, we do see a direct encouragement of the development of telehealth, for example by introducing NHS Direct and various services to be provided via NHSnet. Furthermore, many proposals, not directly concerned with telehealth, may result in an environment in which telehealth might flourish. This would include the emphasis on primary and local care, co-operation between organizations and a better informed public more involved in the health care process.

## CONCLUSIONS

This paper focuses on the interpretations of ICTs and information within health policy documents. Through these, we seek to develop some understanding as to how telehealth is imagined and given meaning and how different discourses present in these texts may influence the development of telehealth. To do this, a sequence of readings of policy texts is described. The work has sought to show that, while there is a preferred reading of these texts and a set of continuities in their evolving forms, there is also a set of specific, and not always consistent, images and impressions of the role of ICTs that need to be found and focused on. From this, it is then possible to return to the policy text as a whole and to develop an improved or refined understanding of the whole. Then, we project this understanding into the emerging domain of telehealth.

This acquisition of meaning surrounding the role and expectations for ICTs in the health sector, and telehealth in particular, mirrors issues discussed in relation to the information society as a whole, for example centralization vs. decentralization (a more decentralized or centralized health care system), devolution of power vs. control (devolution of power from secondary and tertiary care to primary care, from managers to doctors, from clinicians to people/patients) or, alternatively, standardization of practices (tight control of budgets, efficiency targets, monitoring, e.g. of drug-prescribing habits). These issues are clearly reflected in the documents studied here, for example the recent focus on social exclusion suggests an image of ICTs as an enabler of positive social reshaping and processes of empowerment. At the same time, we see the question of the evolving balance of professional and personal relationships as largely ignored (altering the doctor–patient relationship, establishing alternative networks of support, e.g. self-help groups over the Internet, lack of personal contact). Finally, we see little substantial discussion of issues of privacy and data protection.

The research reported here illustrates that, in order to contemplate the consequences and processes of adoption of information and communication technologies and systems in society, it is useful to think about how these technologies and systems take particular meanings. This research only considers one aspect of this, the meanings developed and exposed in health policy documents. Yet, even within such a narrow scope, the research shows the complexity of the issues involved, potential tensions and problems and choices to be made. Telehealth is not a given, even within the policy debate, it is a concept that has to acquire, develop and sustain meanings.
