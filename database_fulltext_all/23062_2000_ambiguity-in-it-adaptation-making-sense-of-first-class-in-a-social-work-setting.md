# Ambiguity in IT adaptation: making sense of First Class in a social work setting

Ola Henfridsson

Department of Informatics, Umeå University, SE-901 87 Umeå, Sweden, email ohson@informatik.umu.se

Abstract. This paper explores how people make sense of the ambiguity caused by newly introduced information technology (IT) in organizations. A better understanding of these sense-making processes might provide some basis for improving the way in which information technology is adapted in organizations. On the basis of an interpretive case study, the paper identifies how certain attention structures facilitated and restricted the meaningful construction of IT in a particular organizational context – social work. In this context, the previous coincidence of IT introductions with increasing administrative workload had considerable implications for the way in which a communications technology (First Class) was adapted. On a general level, it is concluded that making sense of ambiguity is an important ingredient in successful IT adaptation.

Keywords: Ambiguity, interpretive case study, IT adaptation, sense making, social work

## INTRODUCTION

Over the years, the primary concern of social work has been the economic and social security of disadvantaged people. Among possible means for addressing this concern, technology has played a relatively minor role in shaping today’s social services. Consider, for instance, that only during the last 10 years have computers entered Swedish social services departments. As social services have become increasingly complex to co-ordinate, however, quite a few Swedish social services departments have experienced a growing need for information infrastructures to support electronic communication. These social services departments feel that electronic communication may be a good option for handling this increasing complexity, as they perceive how a typical client investigation involves a growing number of circumstances relevant to assessing the client’s needs. To be sure, it has become increasingly important for social services departments to communicate both internally and externally to get an overview of a client’s life situation. On the basis of an interpretive case study (Walsham, 1995a; Klein & Myers, 1999), this paper explores one Swedish social services department’s attempt to introduce an electronic communication technology. The specific technology introduced was First Class, which is a client/server system developed by SoftArc, Inc; this system integrates email with group conferencing, real-time discussions and online communications. As would be expected for IT introductions in large organizations (cf. Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997), First Class was added to an installed base of applications, which included standard off-the-shelf word processors and spreadsheets, but also more social work-specific client register programs and a network of governmental databases.

As often happens when IT is introduced, First Class was initially attributed many different meanings by the staff at the research site. To be sure, there was no single meaning of First Class. In parallel, the system was attributed the role of making the social worker an active information consumer, enabling vertical integration, establishing the learning organization and supporting the relationship between the department and citizens. It turned out that this ambiguity was an important starting point for making the new technology meaningful in social work. Building on Weick’s (1979; 1995) thinking and March & Olsen’s (1976) concept of ‘attention structures’, this paper explores how the initial ambiguity about the role of First Class was a necessary component in making the new technology sensible and meaningful for those involved.

Prior research indicates that meanings and how they come about vary between situations. To be sure, even in cases where the same technology is adapted, different assumptions are formed (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994), different meanings are attributed (Lee, 1994) and different outcomes evolve (Ciborra, 1996; Orlikowski, 1996a; Orlikowski & Hofman, 1997). Despite contextual differences, however, there are some general problems seeking answers: How do sense-making processes affect the outcome of IT adaptation? How can ambiguity be managed for making the most out of IT adaptation? On a general level, the answers to these questions can generate a better understanding of how IT becomes meaningful in practical day-to-day activity. On a practical level, such an understanding might be crucial for anyone wanting to manage IT in an economy with ongoing development of new routines and transactions.

This paper is structured as follows. The second section, MAKING SENSE OF AMBIGUITY IN IT ADAPTATION, explores what the literature offers in the way of understanding how to resolve ambiguity successfully in IT adaptation. The third section, FIRST CLASS IN SOCIAL SERVICES: AMBIGUITY, ATTENTION STRUCTURES, BRACKETING AND SELF-FULFILLING PROPHECIES, presents the case study of First Class in social services and the fourth section, which is the CONCLUSION, gives an analysis of what happened, in order to present some implications for practice and research.

## MAKING SENSE OF AMBIGUITY IN IT ADAPTATION

During the last few years, increasing attention has been paid to interpretive IS research (Walsham, 1995b). One suspects that scholars as well as practitioners have begun to respond to Orlikowski & Baroudi’s (1991) call for a plurality of research perspectives. This call asserts that the interpretive perspective (like any other perspective) is valid and fruitful as long as researchers understand its implications. One such implication is the presumption that reality is a product of social action (for example, see Berger & Luckmann, 1967, Pinch & Bijker, 1987). For the IS researcher interested in understanding information systems in cultural and social contexts, this presumption directs the research focus to people’s assumptions, beliefs and desires. Pursuing this focus, this paper investigates the ambiguity in the assumptions and beliefs held by organizational actors and how this ambiguity played a significant role in the process by which First Class was adapted and readapted in a Swedish social services department. Before giving attention to the details and subtleties of the case study, this section provides a theoretical assessment of prior research useful for understanding this topic

## What is ambiguity and why does it matter?

In going through the literature, one can see that ambiguity is generally referred to as a situation of unclear nature. Seldom defined, ambiguity seems prevalent in organizational aspects, such as decision-making (Ghosh & Ray, 1997), knowledge-intensive companies (Alvesson, 1993), leadership (Denis etal., 1996) and organizational choice (March & Olsen, 1976). Considering that these aspects are important themes in the use and development of information systems, the relative absence of studies explicitly on the role of ambiguity in IT adaptation motivates a closer examination of the issue. What is ambiguity, and why does it matter?

This paper starts from Weick’s (1995, p. 92) assertion that ambiguity, unlike uncertainty, cannot be resolved by more information. The problem with ambiguity is rather that there are too many meanings associated with the same phenomenon. As a result, the co-existence of these meanings makes it difficult for organizational actors to relate the phenomenon to his or her daily working life. Orlikowski (1996b) provides an example of this in her case study of a large management consulting firm (Alpha): this firm attempted to integrate Lotus Notes into their specific organizational context. Confronted with ambiguity triggered by the new technology, Alpha employees constructed interpretations of this distinct, but still unknown, object called Notes, as a way to understand its meaningfulness in their ongoing projects and practice. ‘Big email’, ‘new version of 1-2-3’, ‘word-processing power into spreadsheets’, ‘database and ‘network’ were all initial interpretations of the groupware technology. Over time, and with more experience, the initial ambiguity was resolved into a more solid and consistent interpretation of the meaning of Notes for this firm. In a process in which persistent cognitive and structural elements were involved, Lotus Notes turned out to be an individual productivity tool, despite the initial intentions to create a system for knowledge sharing between individuals.

What can be learned from the Alpha case in terms of ambiguity and IT adaptation? Orlikowski (1996b) suggests that it is important that users understand the premises underlying groupware, so that they can more accurately interpret the value of this collaboration technology. Indeed, one way to arrive at this understanding would be better articulating the visions behind the technology introduction. Learning from Orlikowski’s case, however, the forces at play in the form of norms, and systems supporting individual behaviour, cannot easily be suspended by a collaboration technology itself. This is true whatever well-communicated visions lie behind its introduction. Any IT adaptation process seems to be guided by several organizational forces directing what organizational actors are alert to. At Alpha, the individualistic culture made consultants attend to aspects of Notes that reinforced and augmented individual effort and ability. What, however, are the mechanisms underlying attention and ambiguity in IT adaptation?

On the individual level, the practical problem for an organizational actor in IT adaptation is to figure out how a new technology can be integrated in his or her specific organizational undertakings. There are, however, cognitive limitations as to what individuals can handle in doing so. To be sure, a single organizational member cannot pay attention to all relevant facts, values, ideas, interpretations and thought trials that might be significant at any given moment. As early as 1947, Herbert Simon emphasized that individuals in organizations cannot possibly consider all possible alternatives for action (Simon, 1997). Regardless of the current validity of Simon’s model of the organization as a whole, ‘bounded rationality’ (Simon, 1955; Cyert & March, 1992) requires that the individual’s continuous perceptual stream be reduced to a manageable set of perceptions, even for the most competent professional, not least to avoid what is often labelled as ‘information overload’ (for example, see Schultze & Vandenbosch, 1998).

On a collective level, most interpretive researchers would subscribe to the view that there are certain mental models that help people to reduce the stream of perceptions to a manageable amount. As noted by many interpretive sociologists (for example, see Berger & Luckmann, 1967; Giddens, 1979, 1984; Garfinkel, 1984), these models cannot be separated from the cultural and institutional context in which they are developed and maintained. As people in organizations interact with technology, by analogy, they are socialized into certain ‘interaction orders’ (Goffman, 1983) that guide how to sort out relevant perceptions from irrelevant ones. People tacitly and unconsciously build ‘models of the world’ that direct perception to information that fits practical undertakings and projects. Concepts such as ‘theories-in-use’ (Argyris & Schön, 1996), ‘schema’ (Weick, 1995), ‘script’ (Barley, 1986), Weltanschauung (Churchman, 1971; Checkland, 1981) and ‘attention structures’ (March & Olsen, 1976) have been used to refer to such structures. This paper refers to them as ‘attention structures’, because the concept captures how mental models partially depend on external structures, such as hierarchy. Noting the hierarchical form of Swedish social services (Westerberg, 1999), one might find March & Olsen’s (1976, pp. 40–41) discussion of hierarchical attention structures useful for identifying the elements of ranking and social prestige in attention. Attention structures may specify limits on attention, where lower limits refer to what must be attended to. Of particular interest for IT in a social work setting, the concept ‘attention structures’ can reveal the tension between the virtues of social work and social workers’ attention to administrative work. This tension is illustrated and analysed in the First Class in social services subsection as an important part of how staff made sense of ambiguity about the role of First Class in their practical day-to-day activity.

## From ambiguity to meaningful IT: related literature

IT adaptation processes are anything but straightforward. As mentioned earlier, the practical problem for the single organizational actor is to figure out what a new technology means in his or her specific work practice. Where actors have different projects and undertakings, however, this also becomes a collective process in which technology is evaluated for meaning from different viewpoints. This represents the typical case of ambiguity in IT adaptation. Indeed, from an organizational perspective, the problem is not primarily to generate meanings, but to ‘choose’ tentatively the most appropriate out of a collection created and shaped by individuals and groups in the organization. This organizational ‘choice’ is not a rational decision with well-defined alternatives, but rather a process of ‘muddling through’ (Cohen et al., 1972), in which these alternatives are moulded in the realm of power games and improvization. To be sure, this ‘choice’ is tentative. Suffice it to say that, when surrounding contexts and institutions change, formerly meaningful IT can severely hamper needed transformation because of outdated meanings associated with it.

A few authors have focused on the collective creation and shaping of interpretations for understanding and enacting IT. Orlikowski & Gash (1994), for instance, showed that assumptions, beliefs and values about technology can fruitfully be classified as the technological frames of certain stakeholders. The authors argue that an understanding of people’s interaction with a technology builds on their interpretations of it. In addition, various stakeholders, such as users, designers and managers, can often have different assumptions, beliefs and values about a technology; this is critical to attempts to integrate a particular technology into organizational daily activity. Coming to terms with a technology, therefore, is much about coming to terms with stakeholders’ technological frames (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). Furthermore, they claim that it is important that the technological frames of stakeholders have a certain congruency to avoid difficulties and conflict. Developing and managing IT should therefore be directed towards managing technological frames. While the work of Orlikowski & Gash (1994) provides a useful framework with which to analyse how stakeholders’ assumptions affect how information technology is adapted in organizations, the authors tend to play down the potential source of innovation that resides in incongruencies. Rather than putting technology introductions into the context, for instance learning from unexpected breakdowns, the authors’ prescriptions for practice seem to be targeted at smoothly introducing technology according to predefined objectives only. For Orlikowski & Gash (1994), congruency tends to be the preferred condition throughout the systems lifecycle. Ciborra & Lanzara (1994), for their part, beautifully outline an interpretive vocabulary with which to understand the subtle and ambiguous reality that systems developers find themselves in. As a central part of this vocabulary, ‘formative context’ embraces the ‘. . . the preexisting institutional arrangements, cognitive frames and imageries that actors bring and routinely enact in a situation of action (p. 70). Ciborra & Lanzara (1994) suggest that this context works as a critical resource and restriction for organizational actors involved in IT adaptation. Where limited learning and invention occur, the organization is incapable of enquiring into the existing formative context. Some what unlike Orlikowski & Gash (1994), Ciborra & Lanzara (1994) view ambiguity and puzzles as unexploited sources of innovation, rather than as incongruencies that must be homogenized.

Subscribing to Ciborra & Lanzara’s (1994) view that upcoming organizational puzzles are essential ingredients in IT adaptation, the research questions remain: How do sensemaking processes affect the outcome of IT adaptation? How can ambiguity be managed for making the most out of IT adaptation? The following section explores what can be learned from assessing these questions empirically in a Swedish social services department.

## FIRST CLASS IN SOCIAL SERVICES: AMBIGUITY, ATTENTION STRUCTURES, BRACKETING AND SELF-FULFILLING PROPHECIES

This section presents a case study of First Class in a Swedish social services department. In particular, the section presents the interpretive research methodology used and the sensemaking process by which ambiguity was resolved in this department.

## Research strategy and methodology

This research can be classified broadly as an interpretive case study (Walsham, 1995a). Over a 2-year period (January 1997–December 1998), research was conducted including data collection methods, such as interviews, archival studies and observations. A total of 18 formal interviews was conducted within three job categories: managers (M), group leaders (G) and social workers (S) (see Table 1). Questions covered topics such as technology, attitudes towards change, technology diffusion, clients, IT strategy, managerial commitment, departmental culture and the information systems department. First Class had been introduced in early 1996, however, which made it necessary to reconstruct the first year of use historically.

As one of the most important principles of interpretive research (Klein & Myers, 1999), I adopted the hermeneutic circle for interpreting and analysing the data. The hermeneutic circle suggests that understanding stems from the inter-relation between parts and wholes of the phenomenon studied. To understand IT adaptation in social services, therefore, this phenomenon was put into the context of surrounding societal change in Sweden; at the same time, intimate details and microstories were assessed as important parts of understanding the whole. In other words, the iteration between parts and whole that can be found in the follow ing analysis of this case represents an attempt to be consistent with the interpretive research philosophy.

While concurring with the principle of the hermeneutic circle, the field study was divided into three phases of slightly different character. The first phase was primarily explorative. The purpose was primarily to get a general understanding of the introduction of technology and of the character of social work. This phase (January–April 1997) mainly involved five unstructured interviews (four managers and one group leader) and document review, but it also con tained informal contacts with two informants that helped me with details and contextual back grounds as to what was going on. These informal contacts provided valuable information on how to cover a wide range of nominally different opinions; such a range was one important goal of the data collection process. By allowing the data collection be controlled by the emerging theory [cf. Strauss’s (1987) discussion on ‘theoretical sampling’], I sought to minimize constraining theoretical presuppositions. As an important outcome of this phase, I had to revise some of the initial assumptions. Even though social workers were a bit reluctant early on, it turned out that the staff in general was much more positive towards First Class than expected. In addition, initial broad assumptions about First Class changing the nature of work (which I had developed on the basis of Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994 and Orlikowski, 1996a) were gradually revised in the face of a much richer and complex daily life. The second phase was primarily generative. This phase (May 1997–April 1998) mainly involved 11 semi-structured interviews (one manager, five group leaders and five social workers) and observations of daily social work. During this phase, emergent themes were generated from transcripts of interviews and available documents. This was accomplished by systematically going through the collected material searching for similarities and differences; furthermore, I sought to consolidate these espoused themes in view of observations of daily practices. One theme related to the relatively abstract vocabulary used by organizational members when they described what First Class could do for social services. Using ‘experience-distant’ concepts (cf. Geertz, 1979), such as ‘tool’, ‘democracy’ and ‘the learning organization’, there seemed to be an unmotivated major gap between these conceptions and First Class used as an email system. Another theme noted how the staff had learned and appropriated First Class in an ad hoc fashion. Most learning of functionality, such as making distribution lists and tracing the history of messages, seemed to take place in response to breakdowns in the flow of daily activity. Yet another theme related to how departmental members described their interactions with First Class, in particular what First Class meant for their practical day-to-day activities. This theme noted how First Class was enacted as a communications booster, as a way to get in touch with people easily. Even though these themes are inter-related, one might say that this paper primarily analyses the last theme. The third phase was primarily verifying. This phase (May 1998–December 1998) mainly involved several telephone calls and other types of interactions for validating and complementing the material gathered, but it also involved one semistructured interview (a manager). In addition, the gathered material was reconsidered in a two-step process. First, counterevidence was searched for, in order to test the stability of the themes. Second, all material supporting the themes was reassessed with the intention of finding the best possible labels.

My role in activities at the investigated site resembled what Walsham (1995a) calls an ‘outside observer’. This is not to say that interviews and observations did not influence the material gathered. In fact, living in a preinterpreted world, the interpretations of the researcher are based on the interpretations of the respondents (Van Maanen, 1979). As part of an attempt not to overdirect, I was careful to explain to the respondents that I had no assignment by any stakeholder in the municipality. This was also a way to encourage the study of any potential inconvenient opinions.

## The research site: a Swedish social services department

No doubt social services have been an important cornerstone of the Swedish welfare system for a long time. The Social Security Act was instituted as early as 1871 and, since then, it has been the subject of four major revisions. This act outlines the principles for social and economic security of disadvantaged people, but it also lays down conditions for how Swedish municipalities – in exchange for government subsidies – are responsible for guaranteeing a certain level of social and economic security for their residents. Organized as departments within Swedish municipalities (at the time of the study, there was a total of 289 municipalities in Sweden), the typical social services department offers social and economic support for individuals and families in need, as well as for people with different forms of physical and psychological disabilities. Responsible for the social and economic well-being of 103151 people in 1997 (5181 households were entitled to some kind of social allowance), the investigated social services department was one of the largest in Sweden.

Out of 3440 employees, there were 390 First Class users in early 1997. This figure corresponds to most people using personal computers in their work. Non-users, which are therefore excluded from this study, were employed, for instance at old people’s homes and in long-term medical care. As indicated earlier, First Class users can be divided into three job categories: social workers, group leaders and managers. Social workers spend most of their time in scheduled meetings with clients. Between meetings, they investigate client affairs to make informed decisions. Most information is kept under professional secrecy. Group leaders spend much of their time in meetings with social workers and managers. Besides supervising less experienced social workers in complex cases, they deal with budget planning and staff allocation. Managers spend most of their time interacting with other managers and group leaders. Their responsibilities mainly cover financial and staff matters. Comparatively free to plan their own work, managers are often under pressure from many stakeholders.

## First Class in social services

First Class is a client/server system developed by SoftArc Inc. that integrates email with group conferencing, real-time discussions and online communication functions. As a result of a decision by the municipal council in late 1995, the social services department (as one municipal department among others) acquired and installed First Class on all staff PCs a few months later. At the municipal level, the decision was motivated by a perceived need for an information infrastructure that could promote internal information exchange. The management of the social services department shared this view and was quite committed to the higher level decision. The department felt that developments in Swedish society had made it more complicated to make informed decisions about the economic and social security of disadvantaged people. Improved internal exchange was therefore seen as an important means of accomplishing good social work. Furthermore, introducing a communications technology was regarded as a natural step in an ongoing transformation of the Swedish public service sector. In fact, the director of the social services department related this transformation to ongoing IT efforts:

Swedish public service has a tradition of being quite rigid and slow-moving. This is a cultural problem. Today, however, we have become a continuously changing organization. This shift is a response to all new priorities we’ve been forced to make. Today, we are more mature, and we are more responsive to changes in general, and I do think these facts are useful for beneficial adaptation of IT in the department. (M5)

First Class was added to an already installed base of information technologies, including standard off-the-shelf spreadsheets and word processors, but also social work-specific client register programs and a network of governmental databases. The introduction of First Class evolved in the context of other ongoing IT projects. In different stages of introduction, workflow systems, quality systems and Internet services were either under consideration or under development. No doubt, however, the introduction of First Class was a top priority among IT projects. The director of the social services department noted:

First Class is the most significant IT project of the municipality. The system is easy to use for everyone. It affects the working practice of so many people. (M5)

The actual introduction of First Class was quite intensive and straightforward. The client software was installed on staff PCs without either training or guidance. Users were expected to make the most out of the system themselves. This ad hoc approach to IT adaptation was partly a result of the felt urgency in this matter and partly a result of the low budget for development activities in Swedish municipalities. Courses were later offered on a voluntary basis. Rather than covering the role and meaning of First Class in social work, however, these courses primarily concerned the functionality of the software.

## Early interpretations

As can be expected from an introduction of information technology with neither guidance nor training, the staff had only vague notions of First Class when the system was installed on staff PCs. A few managers had taken part in the development of the department’s IT strategy and could therefore see First Class as part of the general intention of establishing the department as a learning organization. Most of the staff, however, had no clear conception of the intended role of First Class in departmental activities. Not surprisingly, quite a few of the staff reported confusion, as they were not sure what to use the system for. At this point, there were critical voices raising a supposed general lack of planning and strategy. One group leade (G1), for instance, claimed that ‘the strategy in practice is half-done and half-thought’. Other people criticized the training strategy:

I use First Class to a much greater extent than I am trained for. I have not learned to exploit all its advantages yet. Despite this fact, I still like First Class. Once I know this technology, once I know it fully, it will be most useful. (M5)

It is a pity that we don’t get any training at the time the system is introduced. The training is provided way too late. Instead, you must test yourself; or, if you got a colleague who knows more, you can ask him or her to show you. I think this is a major drawback. (S4)

Despite the critical voices, it turned out that many staff members welcomed First Class itself as an information technology that would benefit social work in general. There were quite a few early adopters fascinated by what First Class could do for improving procedures. As can be expected in the early phases of IT adaptation processes, interpretations were quite diverse. Managers and group leaders, especially, held a wide range of interesting interpretations. One district manager (M2), for instance, viewed First Class as a way of supporting and enabling vertical integration. She said that this was the whole point of using IT in social services, while one of her group leaders (G1) proposed that First Class would require that social workers became active information consumers. Using First Class, this group leader stated, it would no longer be possible to justify your own ignorance by saying that nobody told you. To put these interpretations in context, one might note that the district manager mentioned was regarded by the IT co-ordinator (M1) as one of the most far-sighted managers in the department, while the group leader has found the general social worker irritatingly passive for a long time. Among social workers, however, there were few interpretations at that time. The relative lack of interpretations might be explained thus: while managers and group leaders were early adopters, the growth of use among social workers was more gradual. As one can see from early interpretations, there were several of them side by side. The existing ambiguity and sense making were both a potential for innovative use of First Class and a threat for its further integration into existing work practices.

## Solidifying interpretations and patterns-of-use

Many of the early meanings associated with First Class were indeed ambitious and visionary. After people had used the system for a couple months, however, interpretations solidified on a more down-to-earth basis. Even though some managers still emphasized that First Class was an important part of an ongoing learning process in the department, a commonly shared and enacted interpretation was First Class as a communications booster. It was believed that First Class was primarily a good way to get in touch with people easily. Because social workers are busily scheduled throughout a working day, First Class was considered as a convenient tool for contacting people in meetings or in client interactions. Consider these representative comments:

I’m positive. First Class has meant a lot in terms of efficiency; this is especially the case when exchanging files. With the internal paper mail system, for instance, a message can be delayed two or three days before it reaches its recipient. (M4)

I consider First Class extremely useful. . . . Our information processing is so much faster. It used to be hell to get in touch with people; you were trying to reach people by phone for days; you sent internal paper mails that got lost. Our accessibility has improved. One useful feature of First Class is that you can get confirmations that people have read your messages. (M5)

First Class is splendid. You get in touch with people. Whoever you want to reach, you get an answer. Pretty fast, also. I can reply immediately, and if I want to, I can attach a document to which I want some response. It is efficient. (G2)

I think First Class is especially good for brief interactions. There are many occasions when I cannot reach the district manager or other group leaders. In such cases, First Class is a very good alternative. Most of all, I like the opportunity to view the history of the message. I can check whether my message is read or not. I can see that my message was delivered, and I did not have to use several days for doing this over the phone. (G5)

With First Class, the information flows faster and faster. It’s good in a way. However, sometimes the tempo is out of line. . . . I think you’re always a bit reluctant in the beginning. When our group grew in numbers, the district manager told me to start mailing the information. . . . So, I use First Class for reminders and such things. It is impossible to spread the information otherwise. People are all around. So, First Class is an efficient way to spread information. (G6)

First Class makes it easier to work. The telephone isn’t the only way to reach people any more. People can be off the phone the whole day, but you can reach them anyway. (S2)

Well, I think it’s rather good. It’s easy to handle. Furthermore, it’s easy to get in touch with people. (S4)

I feel a bit closer to those people who I can reach with First Class. Consider, for example, when you work with the attendance officer. She works downtown, and she is very hard to reach over the phone. But I just have to send a First Class message, and she will contact me. I believe that the more often you talk to, or inform, each other, the closer co-operation you get. (S6)

Typically, the down-to-earth meanings associated with First Class were reflected in the way it was used by departmental members. In addition to frequent exchange of MS Word and Excel files, managers, for instance, had quite extensive contact with their group leaders. They used First Class for distributing economic and financial information, and they scheduled meetings and conducted brief interactions with group leaders and social workers. Considering this, there is little doubt that the managers’ ways of using First Class largely corresponded to the procedures and ways of organizing things that preceded the system’s introduction. Evidence supporting this observation includes the observation that email distribution lists mapped the formal organization, and that little communication across departmental borders resulted from introducing First Class. It seemed that First Class primarily intensified existing work procedures. Despite the relative absence of changes on a more substantial level, however, the managers perceived the system as an important change agent. One manager (M6), for instance, commented that she did not remember how they worked before using First Class. It felt like she had used the technology all her life, she said, giving words to the thoughts of quite a few of the respondents at the managerial level. Looking at group leaders and social workers, similar patterns were observed. However, one notable difference between social workers and the two other job categories was this: the relative difference between perceptions of First Class as a change agent and the observed change in work practices was less striking for social workers.

Despite the general affirmations of First Class as an efficient way to get in touch with people, as well as perceived qualitative changes, no substantial learning of new work routines evolved around First Class. Functions such as sending mail, exchanging files, viewing the history of messages and creating distribution lists were acquired; ultimately, however, First Class primarily intensified existing working routines. It seemed that the sense-making processes started out with Utopian visions of vertical integration and the social worker as active information consumer, but this process ended up by viewing First Class as a substitute technology for the internal paper mail system, the telephone and face-to-face interaction. Why did the visions end up in down-to-earth interpretations? Can we expect that new visions will pop up where existing interpretations do not match new requirements for conducting social work? In what follows, an attempt is made to understand this confusing picture by exploring how certain attention structures, bracketing processes and self-fulfilling prophecies affected the sensemaking process around First Class.

## Attention structures, bracketing and self-fulfilling prophecies

One interesting observation was that, among several functions of First Class, it was primarily the less fancy functions, such as sending messages and exchanging files, that were appropriated by departmental members. Indeed, these functions made most sense in the practical day-to-day activities of the social worker. Online communications and real-time discussions were obviously unattractive for promoting the social and economic security of disadvantaged people. However, the relatively minor use of fancy functions cannot explain why existing communication patterns were only intensified. For instance, a potential restructuring of communication patterns would not require online communications, but could easily emerge as a consequence only of email functions and distributions lists (for example, see Finholt & Sproull, 1990; Sproull & Kiesler, 1991). So, instead of a technology-based explanation, one might look for signs of a more social and organizational nature. In fact, the evidence suggests that there were strong and viable organizational mechanisms and routines enforcing the sense making of First Class. Not easily framed and conceptualized by the relatively complex themes of social work, there were inter-related attention structures that seemed to go beyond the influence of the fancy functions.

Considering that making a difference for disadvantaged people is at the core of social workers’ professional identity, it comes as less of a surprise that First Class was approached primarily as a communications booster. To be sure, administrative work has been viewed as a necessary evil for a long time in social services, often considered a puissance for the caring side of social work. This view was especially prominent among social workers, who have historically experienced a relative imbalance between the amount of information fed into information systems and the amount of information received from them (cf. Grudin, 1994). Among group leaders, and especially managers, administrative work has been seen to cut both ways. On the one hand, administrative duties consume time that could be used for client interaction; on the other hand, such duties are also seen as necessary components of a sound and fair welfare system. As an important consequence of seeing ‘administrative work as a necessary evil’, this view made people alert to things that decreased the administrative workload. In concrete terms, First Class (in contrast with substitute media, such as the internal paper mail system or the telephone) had important features that decreased the number of unsuccessful communicative attempts. For instance, because of its ability to transcend temporal constraints, First Class made it easy to conduct brief interactions even when the recipient was in a meeting. An email message is readily waiting for the few minutes available to social workers between client meetings, while a telephone call can require careful timing by the caller. Moreover, the email history function allowed the sender to check whether a message sent was read or not, a confirmation that seemed to compensate for the directness of the telephone. There is little doubt that these aspects of First Class were attractive to staff who had seen earlier technologies only lead to increased administrative workload, despite the fact that they wanted most of all to make a difference for disadvantaged people. In addition, knowing that social workers are obliged by law and authority to pay a minimum level of attention to administrative work, it makes even more sense that First Class was considered a blessing. In sum, the integration of First Class into practical work at the investigated social services department can be described as a bracketing process (Weick, 1979, pp. 153–157). There were certain institutionalized and historically grounded attention structures that made people alert to decreases in administrative workload. These structures bracketed out certain information from the potentially available information in people’s interaction with First Class. The selected bracketing constructed First Class as a ‘communications booster’.

Moreover, there is little doubt that departmental members felt that social work is not about technology. At the managerial level, efforts to develop the department further were attuned towards organizationally defined constructs, such as quality assurance and workflow. Never talking in terms of technology in other than abstract ways, managers constructed a very instrumental conception of IT; in this conception, the realization of a goal such as quality assurance was seen as depending only on the staff’s willingness to learn the technology. For social workers, for their part, there were urgent issues on the agenda for improving their abilities to help clients, which could not be solved with IT. In practice, although for slightly different reasons, managers, group leaders and social workers enacted a solid separation between social work and First Class. This is understandable, given that earlier information technologies at the social services department had always coincided, more or less, with new reporting procedures or routines. Despite the fact that First Class as a communications technology was different from former technologies in terms of flexibility, this factual difference was too weak a counterforce to the attention structure seeing information technology as administration. Consistent with the traditional role of technology in social work, social workers wanted First Class to be an invisible part of their practical day-to-day activity. As a result, one can observe that dealing with First Class as an administrative technology also created such technology. In practice, the enactment of First Class as something distinct from social work produced a barrier between the use of the system and social work, which was justified by the attention structure stating that information technology is equivalent to administration. As a result, a self-fulfilling prophecy was created (Weick, 1979, pp. 159–164) – enacting First Class as an administrative tool made the system into such a tool.

What was the meaning of First Class in the studied department? Indeed, First Class meant much in the early phase of introducing the system. Early interpretations expressed several visions of what might result in terms of social work. The meanings associated and attributed by the staff included making the social worker an active information consumer, enabling vertical integration, establishing the learning organization and supporting the relationship between the department and citizens. As the process of making sense of the system was influenced by strong and viable organizational attention structures, however, the initial ambiguity was relatively soon transformed into a more homogeneous interpretation in practice. Even though some of the initial visions survived on the espoused level, the actual interaction with First Class revealed a convenient tool for intensifying existing communication patterns. As outlined in this subsection, enactments, such as self-fulfilling prophecies and bracketing, were important ingredients in these transformation processes.

## Implications for the studied social services department

One might regard the adaptation of First Class in the studied social services department as a success. Exceptionally few staff members made negative comments about the system and its use in their organization. In fact, most staff looked at First Class as a valuable technology for making their everyday activity smooth and efficient. Considering interviews and written documents, there is little doubt that First Class was considered as a supportive technology for social services. Despite the general conception of First Class as an efficient and beneficial technology, however, a potential problem for the department was that there were few signs of radical learning as a result of the sense-making processes around the actual introduction.

Any fruitful meaning creation process around technology requires that the sense maker go back and forth between ambiguous and common sense conceptions of the particular technology. At the time I ended the present study, such switches between ambiguity and familiarity could be observed only on an individual level. However, on an organizational level, the ambiguous nature of First Class was rapidly resolved into an interpretation of a communications booster; this was in the interests of both the staff caring for disadvantaged clients and the existing institutional mechanisms for carrying this out. In other words, the department learned how to use First Class to intensify already existing patterns. Actual use observed at the end of this study, however, did not reflect any signs of a new phase of ambiguity. This observation might represent a problem if strategically proclaimed goals, such as renewing social services with innovative use of IT, are to be realized.

Considering the studied case with existing IS research in mind, the non-existent learning organization does not come as a surprise. In fact, a considerable amount of literature shows how IS implementations fail (Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987; Sauer, 1993) and result in unintended consequences. This research study intends to overcome the dichotomy of success/failure by revealing a case in which the outcome cannot be measured on a one-dimensional scale. Assessing the rate of success here requires emphasizing the whole range of dimensions at play in IT adaptation. Realizing the complexity of this issue, one might perhaps consider relinquishing this dichotomy in favour of other conceptualizations. One such conceptualization suggested in this paper is to view IT adaptation as a process in which IT is constructed meaningfully by sense-making people under conditions of ambiguity.

Thus, one suggestion for the management of the social services department would be to consider mobilizing the interests and alliances needed for reviving ambiguity around First Class. Perhaps such an effort could be combined with an attempt to formulate a general communications strategy in which the Internet is also considered. This, in turn, would perhaps trigger the critical and committed attitude needed for creating the social work of the future. However, the lessons from this case study show that new visions of technology need some institutional support in the form of attention structures in order to survive. In other words, innovative visions need to be bracketed in order to fit current attention structures without maintaining or reinforcing them. In this way, the vision is less likely to be dismissed as Utopian when being scrutinized in the light of deeply embedded contextual forces.

## CONCLUSION

Considerable evidence has emerged that information technology is adapted and readapted when situated in organizational practice (Suchman, 1987; Ciborra, 1996; Orlikowski, 1996a). Where this growing body of knowledge deals with the general phenomenon, one might suggest further research to assess particular aspects of it. This paper is intended to make such a particular assessment where the focus is on the role of ambiguity and sense making in IT adaptation. How do sense-making processes affect the outcome of IT adaptation? Sense-making processes, and the potentially innovative use of IT that might emerge from them, build on deeply embedded attention structures that can be both enabling and restricting for how IT is adapted. When enabling, these attention structures work as resources for people regarding IT to be meaningful and sensible for their organizational practice. In the social services department case, this enabling aspect facilitated the appropriation of First Class as a technology to lower the administrative workload. When restricting, these attention structures can lead to self-fulfilling prophecies and vicious circles. At the studied social services department, such restriction occurred when certain historically rooted attention structures had much stronger institutional support, in the form of identity and technology tradition, than the initial meanings associated with First Class.

How can ambiguity be managed for making the most out of IT adaptation? This paper assumes that fruitful IT adaptation requires transitions, back and forth, between ambiguity and common-sense usage of IT over time. During ambiguity, organizational members tend to see the technology as a distinct artifact, not yet part of their day-to-day activity and still seeking its role. While ambiguity is an equivocal situation, in which many interpretations of technology co-exist, it is also a chance to invent new innovative uses for technology. During commonsense use, on the other hand, work tasks are at the centre of attention. Technology is enacted as a meaningful, but almost unnoticed, part of day-to-day organizational activity. For making the most out of IT adaptation, an organization needs to trigger sense-making processes around their technologies. The intention of such triggering is not to find the single meaning of them, however, because meanings vary between individuals, organizations and over time. Rather, the intention is to encourage individuals to develop their ability to invent new uses of existing technologies. Indeed, there is no meaning in searching for a single meaning of technology, but there is much meaning in the sense-making processes themselves.
