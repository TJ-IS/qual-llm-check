# Trust, power and interorganizational information systems: the case of the electronic trading community TransLease

David K. Allen, David Colligan\*, Andrew Finnie\* and Thomas Kern<sup>†</sup>

Department of Information Studies, University of Sheffield, Western Bank, Sheffield, S10 2TN, UK, email: d.k.allen@sheffield.ac.uk, \*Cap Gemini UK plc, email:

david.colligan@capemini.co.uk and andrew.finnie@ capemini.co.uk, and <sup>†</sup>Department of Information and Decision Sciences, Erasmus University Rotterdam, Burg. Oudlaan 50, PO Box 1736, 3000 DR Rotterdam, The Netherlands, email: tkern@fac.fbk,eur.nl

Abstract. This paper focuses on Cap Gemini’s electronic commerce system, TransLease. TransLease is an interorganizational information system (IOS), which facilitates electronic commerce between motor vehicle leasing and repair companies. During our investigation, the system was used by approximately 1000 repair agents working for seven of the UK’s leading vehicle leasing and contract hire companies. This system was originally developed by AT&T and acquired by Cap Gemini in July 1998. At the time of acquisition, the system was seen as being of high strategic value, although it was also seen as underperforming. This paper reports the results of an action research project, which formed one element of the process by which Cap Gemini investigated the former problem. In the paper, TransLease is described as a complex electronic community, dependent upon the existence of symbiotic relationships. As such, the problems that the system users and developers experienced can be attributed to factors that impeded the mutual benefit accruing from participation in the system. The efficacy of the terms of exchange and the degree to which participants mutually benefit through electronic interaction is determined by the complex interplay of a number of relational and organizational factors. The research therefore illustrates the importance of the ‘soft’ organizational issues in IOS management and development, and suggests a conceptual model of the factors relevant in this case. At the time of this study, TransLease was still in the early stages of its life cycle, having only been available in the marketplace for approximately 18 months. During this time, through recognizing the complex problems and issues detailed in this paper, Cap Gemini accordingly redressed the way in which the system was managed and maintained. TransLease is now seen as having matured into a highly successful example of an IOS – a view reflected by its position as market leader in the industry. As this paper will show, the key to improving the existing service has been the emphasis Cap Gemini now places on managing the ‘soft’ aspects of the electronic community.

Keywords: Electronic community, electronic commerce power and politics, intraorganizational information systems

## INTRODUCTION

Cap Gemini’s TransLease is an electronic commerce system facilitating trade among the various organizations who are paid members of its private network. It uses industry-agreed standards to structure its information and procedures. In essence, TransLease links two business communities, vehicle leasing companies and repair agents (vehicle repair garages), over an electronic network in order to create an electronic trading community. By linking organizations, the resulting boundary-spanning aspect of TransLease enforces the system as an interorganizational information and communication system (IOS). The boundary-spanning aspect of interorganizational systems, as Kumar & Dissel (1996) note, entails a level of cooperation and co-ordination that stretches beyond that of traditional arms-length interorganizational relationships. The technology element of IOS-based relations thus pushes them in the direction of ‘planned and managed co-operative ventures between otherwise independent agents’, as Kumar & Dissel (1996, p. 280) suggest.

Traditionally, IOS research has focused on the economic, co-ordination and competitive benefits (e.g. Porter & Millar, 1985; Konsynski & McFarlan, 1990; Clemons & Row, 1992; Reekers & Smithson, 1996) of such systems. Economic advantages are related to lower transmission charges and reduced personnel, which translated into \$200 million annual savings at Chrysler Corporation (Mukhopadhyay et al., 1995). Co-ordination benefits arise through the tight coupling and the resulting increased speed and flexibility of exchanging information, i.e. documents (Clemons & Row, 1992; Lee et al., 1999). Finally, the competitive advantage entails, for example, reduced transaction costs, access to diversified markets and industries, leveraging key resources in new areas and economies of scale and scope (Porter & Millar, 1985; Clemons & Row, 1992). The aspect often overlooked in this, however, is the element of complexity such systems introduce into relationships that evolve between members of an IOS environment, i.e. the electronic community. In particular, the additional relational and operational uncertainties and the resulting information openness that arises with electronic access, which can potentially subject members to opportunistic threats. Such uncertainties, but also the growing interdependency and vulnerability (cf. Reekers, 1995; Hart & Saunders, 1997) that IOS enforces, has led researchers to argue recently for closer attention to the sociopolitical (Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1996; Kumar & Dissel, 1996; Reekers & Smithson, 1996; Bensaou, 1997) or relational (Hart & Saunders, 1997) perspective to IOS.

TransLease was also sold on the premise that it can potentially save the vehicle leasing and repair industry an estimated £10–14 million per annum. The system was designed to reduce the costs and the amount of time currently incurred by vehicle repairers and vehicle users when going through the process of authorizing repairs or maintenance by the leasing companies. The authorization procedure normally involved lengthy telephone calls and demanded excessive time of all staff involved, thus lengthening customer response time. This procedure took approximately 45 minutes, whereas TransLease was designed to reduce this time to less than five minutes by allowing accurate information to reach both parties at much greater speeds. It was also designed to reduce the confusion regularly experienced at the invoice stage when a significant percentage of invoices (estimated 15–20%) are contested and rejected for payment. TransLease solved this problem by using standardized data formats, which dramatically reduce labour time spent auditing invoices.

However, it soon became evident from the community participants’ perspectives and Cap Gemini that the TransLease system was not providing the mutual benefits as originally envisaged. Despite the high number of companies buying the system and ‘joining’ the electronic community, the number of invoices being sent across the network was minimal. As Cap Gemini’s pricing model demands that invoices are sent over the network in order to generate revenue, this was seen as a significant problem. Although we realize in this case that the use of the word ‘failure’ is perhaps misleading, the problems encountered were best explained using a framework developed by Lyytinen & Hirschheim (1987) who identified four types of IS ‘failure’. The first two levels of the framework, ‘correspondence’ and ‘process’ failure, were not relevant in this case (correspondence failure refers to the failure of meeting predefined design objectives; process failure refers to failures to produce a system at all, or within budget). However, as the research progressed, it was clear that TransLease was experiencing problems in terms of ‘interaction’ and ‘expectation’ among its users. ‘Interaction failure’ concerns levels of use and degrees of user satisfaction, where systems can be underused or even left totally unused. ‘Expectation failure’ is a very broad concept concerning the inability of a system to meet specific stakeholders’ expectations. Both failure issues entailed particularly responses by participants, with specific emphasis on the degree of trust, power use and collaboration, i.e. relational factors.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. The next section discusses the research approach adopted to undertake this study. It is followed by an elaboration of a conceptual model from the literature that identifies some of the key factors necessary to handle the relational aspect of interorganizational systems. This model is then used as a guideline to discuss the findings from the case analysis, before offering some suggestions for avoiding similar problems in comparable electronic communities and IOS implementations.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY – ADDRESSING THE PROBLEM

At the initiation of the research study, the main thrust of the activity by Cap Gemini was that of analysing the technical and functional aspects of TransLease. The function of this research was to complement this activity. The initial aim was therefore to investigate the ‘soft’ organi zational (i.e. relational) aspects of the system and the utilization of the system, thinking that these might provide the reasons why the system was not delivering as expected. Alternatively, it was seen that they may point to ‘underlying’ technical problems that are at the root of the problem (Sauer, 1994), potentially causing TransLease to be underused. In line with the view of Walsham (1993), this focus aimed to complement more traditional or economic issues by focusing on human interpretation and social meaning. Sauer (1994, p. 21) claims that ‘specialist literature (in the area of information systems failure) has slowly converged on a view that social and behavioural factors are more important aspects of information systems failures than the technical’. This is a view shared by Mumford (1983), Lyytinen & Hirschheim (1987), Checkland & Scholes (1990) Fulk et al. (1990), Galliers (1992), Konsynski (1992), Meier & Sprague (1992), Checkland & Holwell (1998) and O’Callaghan (1998).

Action research, as described by Baskerville & Wood-Harper (1998) and Avison et al. (1999), defined our approach to investigating the TransLease environment. In turn, the methods used were primarily qualitative in nature. Using this method, the research was holistic, in terms of it moving beyond the ‘simple’ technical and financial issues, and inductive, in terms of aiming to uncover stakeholders’ meaningful perceptions about the system.

## Research process

In line with the participatory action research approach (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998; McTaggart, 1999), the researchers used their theoretical knowledge to determine the most appropriate activities to participate in. By reflecting then on the gathered experiences and data, the researchers were able to relate events to prior theoretical knowledge in order to recommend courses of action or highlight areas that required particular attention (Cornford & Smithson, 1996). As such, this was a cyclical process enabling researchers to develop their understanding of the problem areas incrementally (Lau, 1997). Ultimately, the benefit of participating with the people involved was that the researchers could appreciate better the role of the system’s different stakeholder groups and individuals (Kirveennummi & Tuomisto, 1998), thus improving their insight and understanding of the organizational and social pressures that users felt (Cornford & Smithson, 1996). Such a ‘deep’ understanding uncovered the necessary information to recommend a course of ‘action’ (Serafeimidis & Smithson, 1998) and allowed the researchers to intervene to direct the required change process to alleviate the problems.

The meaning of the term ‘stakeholder groups’ in this context is defined by Mitroff & Linstone (1993, p. 141) as, ‘any individual, group, organisation, institution that can affect as well as be affected by an individual’s, group’s, organisation’s or institution’s policy or policies’. Burgoyne et al. (1994, p. 190) suggests that, from a theoretical point of view, stakeholders are of interest because ‘their needs, wants, desires, perceptions and conceptualisations are different’. The role of the stakeholder Cap Gemini is that of ‘conduit host’, that is the organization that provides the network hardware and software, an interorganizational system facilitator, or ‘non-participating host’ (Cash et al., 1994, p. 339). The main stakeholder groups here are Cap Gemini, vehicle repair agents (the ‘suppliers’ in the community), vehicle leasing companies (the ‘buyers’ in the community) and Company X – the company that supplies Cap Gemini with the necessary data for the system to function. An additional stakeholder is Company Y, which manufactures, supplies and maintains a management information system, known in this industry as a ‘dealer management system’ (DMS). This is currently used by a large percentage of repair agents for various business functions. TransLease has the capacity to be integrated into the Company Y system and is often sold as an integrated package. If this is the case, after agreement between the two companies, Company Y is responsible for the training of their clients.

## Data collection and analysis

Data were collected using ‘open’ qualitative interviews, participant observation and analysis of company documentation. During the participant observation, time was spent in meetings (‘workshops’) facilitated by Cap Gemini, in which various stakeholders were present to discuss any problems or issues deemed relevant to improving the TransLease service. Research time spent in this environment was highly valuable, as the dynamics of the relationships between the various stakeholder groups were observed and recorded first-hand. The data were analysed using elements of soft systems methodology (SSM) as a sense-making tool (‘rich pictures’). Checkland & Holwell (1998) advocate the use of SSM as an interpretative approach to organizational problem solving that can be used to provide a structure for action research in which desirable change and organizational learning are the aims. In order to analyse the data, the different stakeholder groups were divided into the following categories:

• Group 1: Cap Gemini plc (conduit host)

• Group 2: vehicle leasing companies (buyers)

• Group 3: repair agents using a ‘PC’ version of TransLease (suppliers)

• Group 4: repair agents accessing through a Company Y system (suppliers)

• Group 5: Company Y Ltd (manufacturer of ‘dealer management system’)

• Group 6: Company X (data provider/supplier).

## CRITERIA FOR EVALUATING TRANSLEASE

Different stakeholders may have different perceptions of success or failure in a system (Lytinen & Hirschheim, 1987; Nandhakumar, 1996; Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996). Therefore, the definition of success or otherwise of a system depends as much on the social, economic and political setting within which it operates as on its technical quality (Sauer, 1994). Thus, the focus of this research was on gathering different perceptions of the system in an attempt to gain a deeper understanding of the factors contributing towards TransLease’s underperformance. This was seen to be a particularly useful approach to research in an IOS ‘where the traditional problems of politics, management, expectations, and to some extent technical, are magnified’ (Ramanath et al., 1998). These issues centre around the fact that there are more stakeholders involved from a variety of professional and personal backgrounds. Each stakeholder may be using the system with different agendas and operating within different organizational cultures, causing them to have different perceptions of the system (Walsham, 1993; Golden & Powell, 1997; Galliers & Baets, 1998; O’Callaghan, 1998). In the case of TransLease, this was exacerbated by the large number of users, the fact that the users may not ever meet each other on a face-to-face basis and that the community relied upon a number of virtual strategic partners (Companies X and Y). This view – that IOS are eminently more complex than traditional IS – is further supported by those claiming that such systems are more multifaceted (Lytinen, 1989) and multifunctional (Hopper, 1990; Meier & Sprague, 1991; Konsynski, 1992). Thirteen factors impacting upon the success or failure of IOS were identified from a literature review; these were seen as forming a complex web of interrelated factors. These factors were used as guides in the interventions into the stakeholder organizations.

1. Effective communications. The effective management of technological change hinges on strong and effective communication by managers and users, and strong communications must also be present once the system is in operation.

2. The issue of trust between participants. A number of researchers have highlighted the issue of IOS and politics and trust. Ramanath et al. (1998) indicated that it is among the prominent political issues in IOS.

3. Operational uncertainty. The lack of trust evident in many IOS is often related to concerns over uncertainty (Hart & Saunders, 1997; Ramanath et al., 1998). This results from the extent to which the different participants should become dependent on the IOS, and whether their involvement affects their individual independence, internal operations and competitiveness.

4. Different objectives – a need for strategic alignment. De Wit & Peterson (1998) indicated that conflicting strategies between stakeholder groups can be detrimental to the success of an IOS. They identified ‘strategic alignment’ of the different groups as being a crucial prerequisite to successful IOS, and also in managing the change from a previous system to the new. 5. Changes in business processes to meet IOS needs. The importance of organizations changing their existing business processes in order to meet the needs of the new IOS has been identified by a number of researchers (Cash et al., 1994; Sauer, 1994; Lindemann & Runge, 1998; O’Callaghan, 1998).

6. Information, data standards and protocol. Standards play a critical role in the evolution of IOS, in which shared protocols establish the rules of the system. As Konsynski (1992, p. 53) states, ‘The presence of standards affects the ability of the system to provide the high levels of market-specific information necessary to “lubricate” transactions’.

7. Power relations and politics between stakeholder groups. Fredrikkson & Vigon (1996) claimed that the power relations between trading organizations are often neglected in IOS literature, and also in the development and management of IOS, creating unforeseen problems when the system is in use.

8. Cross-cultural issues. Cross-cultural issues have been raised by a number of researchers, including Kling (1992) Poulymenakou & Holmes (1996) andJarvenpaa & Shaw (1998). claimed that different organizational cultures can create divisions, resulting in tensions that may result in systems failure.

9. Resistance to change. Resistance to change is caused by a complex combination of factors that can be organizational, individual, but primarily situational (Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996).

10. Disparate expectation levels. The fact that individuals and organizations can have different expectation levels of a system can cause conflicts, tensions and varying levels of satisfaction and usage (Checkland & Scholes, 1990; Checkland, 1992; Walsham, 1993; Sauer, 1994; Wilson, 1996).

11. User involvement and participation. Kanter (1994) argued that, in order to build the trust and respect that precedes success, it is vital that stakeholders at multiple levels are involved in the development and management of an intercompany relationship.

12. Training of users. Here, two issues can be defined: first, whether any training has been given to staff at all; secondly, whether that training is of appropriate quality. Poulymenakou & Holmes (1996) also placed significance on training in environments in which computer systems are not part of the organizational culture and users may be ‘afraid’ of them.

13. Relationship management. Konsynski (1992), Kanter (1994) and Fredrikkson & Vigon (1996) focused on the importance of actively managing the relationship between the partners in an IOS.

## THE INTERORGANIZATIONAL SYSTEM TRANSLEASE

For companies using TransLease, the system validation process begins when details of any work and parts deemed necessary to complete a repair or service are entered. These details are then submitted to the system for authorization (a database stores repairer-specific validation ‘rules of trade’ set by the leasing company and agreed with the specific repairer(s)). The details are then cross-referenced against the ‘rules’ to determine whether the work can be authorized. On satisfying the rules, work is automatically authorized. However, there is an alternative route if the application is referred: the repair agent is ‘referred’ to telephone the leasing company and, after negotiation, each alters their data in order to authorize the work. Although this entails a certain amount of subjectivity, the transaction process is still carried out through a structured framework that the system provides, hence validating the communication itself, resulting in more accurate information. However, it should be noted that, despite such referrals leading to the use of additional communication media, repair agents using TransLease gain the following benefits: priority telephone call ‘pick-up’ and an electronic ‘worksheet’ instantly visible to the leasing agent and repairer, which immediately saves time and eases communication. After work has been authorized, TransLease automatically generates invoices that are delivered electronically from repair agents to lease companies. This enables invoices to be paid much quicker than under any existing paper-based system. The resulting potential for the repair agent to increase their financial liquidity is one of the key selling points. It is also the basis of Cap Gemini’s pricing model: for every invoice that is sent over the network, the repair agent and the lease company are charged a nominal fee. Therefore, the commercial viability of the system is highly dependent on achieving high transaction volumes. To ensure increased usage, parties need to collaborate and foster good working relationships.

Indeed, as the investigation progressed, it became clear that TransLease is more than a technological system distinguished by ‘wires and boxes’. In fact, it evolved into a complex elec tronic community dependent upon the existence of symbiotic relationships. The failure of the system could thus be described as being a state in which participation in using the IOS did not provide the envisaged mutual benefit to its participants. Conversely, the success of an IOS is based on relational co-operation rather than competition (Miles & Snow, 1992) to derive mutual benefits. In TransLease, this is reflected through the data and code standards, which define and enforce the terms of exchange (including ‘rules of trade’). The efficacy of the terms of exchange and the degree to which there is mutual benefit through electronic interaction are determined by the complex interplay of a number of social and organizational factors, as shown in Figure 1.

This particular combination of factors was, perhaps, created by the peculiar ‘virtual’ nature of the TransLease system, in which users may never actually meet other members of the community on a face-to-face basis. In the TransLease case, the situation was not, as with older implementation studies, one of overcoming resistance by a distinct group of users tied to one geographical location. In this large IOS, there are diverse types of users situated in different geographical locations, and the system is, to a certain extent, reliant upon ‘virtual’ contractors whom the users rely upon, but may never communicate with. The factors described in the model are the ones that the authors see as critical for the successful management of complex IOS, such as TransLease. ## Data and code standards

The quality of the data and the fact that the data and code standards (which enshrined the ‘rules of trade’) were set to the mutual benefit of both parties was seen as particularly important. Control of this aspect was at the heart of the power relations in the TransLease community. An external supplier, Company X, supplies the data used in the TransLease system. These data come in specified standard formats and, for the system to run smoothly (i.e. authorizations to comply with rules of trade), all of the participants must use the same standard data. An issue regularly raised by all groups was that different sections of data were inacc rate and not kept up to date. This was believed to have considerable impact on the percentage of first-time authorizations that repair agents were achieving. Cap Gemini had already identified poor-quality data in certain areas and was continually encouraging Company X and lease companies to improve upon it. The results were seen in regular increases in first-time authorizations occurring on the system every month. Despite this, the damaging effects of such poor-quality data were seen to reach beyond the technical process. By impinging on how repair agents actually felt about TransLease, this problem was a source of great frustration for users. Ill feelings were heightened by the poor data quality from the lease companies:

The information at the lease company end mustn’t be accurate because even when we do everything right we still get referred. They haven’t got it set up right so it’s not always our fault. That’s the most frustrating part.’ (RA Group 3: 23.07.98)

Konsynski (1992) and Nah et al. (1998) argued that, without accurate and standardized data, transactions will not be able to take place. Although this situation has now been rectified, many interviewees confirmed that data were far from being accurate and up to date. Yet, while there are clear practical benefits from having data and code standards, there are also clear political implications. Konsynski (1992) stated that, if a standard becomes pervasive among the suppliers linked by IOS, it is the buyers who are most likely to benefit. This is said to be more likely if the buyers are very powerful and can demand that suppliers participate in the IOS as well as dictating the terms of the new relationship (Konsynski, 1992; Spinardi et al., 1997). Cash et al. (1994) argued that certain types of IOS, those that they term ‘channel-oriented’, actually raise distributor costs or raise entry barriers. These discussions raise the issue of power relations between participant groups, especially those in a buyer/supplier relationship and how they may affect the use of IOS (Hart & Saunders, 1997).

## Power

The issue with data standards was that they were not accurate and were not negotiated; yet significantly, they determined the rules of trade. As such, they demonstrate the power structures within the TransLease community: all groups perceived that it was primarily the repair agents who were reliant on lease companies for their revenue, thus placing the latter in a more powerful position. This imbalance of power is emphasized by the fact that it is the lease companies who determine the rules of trade. A dominant theme for repair agent complaints was their perception of an ‘unfair’ balance of power, which meant that they felt that lease companies would tie them into a system that would reinforce and amplify existing power structures.

It is important to note that, although the lease companies determined the rules of trade, initially the repair agents were persuaded to use the system. This persuasive approach was extremely effective in overcoming any suspicions that the repair agents had of the system. The success of this approach is demonstrated by the fact that over 1000 repair agents adopted the system within 18 months. Hart & Saunders (1997) point out that the use of persuasive (or non-coercive) power has been used extremely effectively by a number of organizations in similar situations.

In the TransLease case, the repair agents experienced problems with the system related to the accuracy of the data and the stringency of the rules of trade. Their use of the system declined, and certain lease companies therefore moved to the use of coercive power. They attempted to leverage their buying power to force the repair agents to use the system:

Next time a repairer on TransLease tries to use the old process, tell them that they can’t. There are no excuses. We’ve got to make them use it. (LC Group 2: 25.06.98)

This use of coercive power reduced the level of trust between the different stakeholders. Many of the repair agents saw it as confirmation of their worst fears:

The system is so bad that we actually want to get off it, but the lease companies are not letting us. Actually, it’s not the system itself, but how the lease companies are insisting on using it. They just use the ‘rules’ to control us even more. (RA Group 4: 29.07.98)

In this way, the use of coercive power degraded the relationship between the stakeholders. It also significantly increased the level of distrust between the stakeholders and increased the distrust the repair agents had of TransLease. At the beginning of the study, Cap Gemini’s position on this issue was made clear by their disproportionate focus on meeting lease companies’ needs rather than those of the repair agent community. However, the bias towards lease companies was seen to shift as Cap Gemini realized the need to give repair agents equal attention. This change in management approach was to reinforce the concept of TransLease as a trading community. This statement typifies the shift in approach:

There is no one company or type of company that is more important for TransLease than another. Everyone is interdependent on one another. Even though lease companies are trying to bully repairers into joining, they won’t stand for poor service forever. They need help. (CG Group 1: 9.07.98)

This change in emphasis reinforced the primacy of persuasive rather than coercive power and the importance of building trust in the system and between the stakeholders.

## Trust

The issue of trust in IOS is quite clearly affected by prevailing power structures. The research shows that the lack of trust on behalf of the lease companies insinuated itself into many different operational and conceptual aspects of the system. It was seen to incite ill feeling and resentment within repair agent organizations, in turn influencing the perceptions they held and the decisions and actions they took. The political climate that TransLease operated in at the time of this study could be described as being extremely strained, with tensions running high between the lease company and repair agent community. This emanated from a prevalent and seemingly inherent fear (i.e. uncertainty) within the lease company community that repair agents constantly tried to overcharge them for work carried out. The lease companies’ other concern was that repair agents would take advantage of the fact that they cannot physically inspect a vehicle; therefore, repair agents would carry out work that was not actually necessary in order to generate more revenue. Despite this, the following statement typified the views of lease companies:

The inherent problem with the motor-trade is that all employees are on a bonus. As a result, they always try to ‘up-sell’. It’s in their culture – they always try to rip us off. They are different from us because they are only interested in short-term gains so they always try to make money every time. We trust hardly any of them. (LC Group2: 27.07.98)

It was this feature of the relationship between the two participating communities that had an overwhelming influence on how the system was initially designed and, more relevant to this study, how it was being managed and perceived by all of the stakeholder groups. Historically, there has always been little trust of repair agents, and it is not a feature particular to the relationship enabled by TransLease. In fact, certain interviewee statements and research observations indicated that mistrust was a prime motivation for lease companies to adopt TransLease – a system that they perceived would allow them more control through manipulation of the rules of trade. This situation reflected that described by Konsynski (1992): buyers (in this case the lease companies) in a strong position, who instigate the use of IOS, would usually gain a reinforcement of their power.

The lease companies’ mistrust of repair agents appeared to manifest itself in them setting stringent authorization ‘rules of trade’, causing a high percentage of referrals. Lease companies claimed that these rules would be relaxed over time, as trust was built up in the new relationship facilitated by TransLease:

Repairers need an incentive to use TransLease. They need to see that we are relaxing the rules, and we are as guilty as anyone is because we haven’t always done that. Our rules are fairly relaxed but we can go a stage further with repairers we really trust. There will always be a certain amount of mistrust though. (LC Group 2: 24.07.98)

Moreover, a widely held belief in groups 1 and 2 was that repair agents did not appreciate how strict lease company budgets are, and would therefore never change their attitude towards revenue gain. The research of Ramanath et al. (1998) indicated that mistrust among partici pating organisations in IOS is not only a common feature, but also a serious threat to the success of a given system. As Kanter (1994, p. 105) stated, ‘mistrust, once introduced, sets off a vicious cycle. It makes success harder to attain’. Kanter (1994) went on to argue that, if mistrust is prominent, co-operation between participating organizations is highly unlikely, a situation proved in the case study by Spinardi et al. (1997). The view is that success is much easier to attain if mistrust, inherent within IOS, is kept to a minimum through effective communication and management of expectations and relationships between the participants.

## Shared objectives

Walsham (1993) stated that, when the thoughts of various stakeholders in an IOS are made explicit, motives concerning the purpose and value of such systems are often contrasting. De Wit & Peterson (1998) indicated that conflicting strategies between stakeholder groups can be detrimental to the success of an IOS. They identified ‘strategic alignment’ of the different groups as being a crucial prerequisite to successful IOS. When researching these issues within the context of TransLease, the political climate discussed previously was a key factor. The fact that neither business community within the overall trading community trusted the other was seen to have a major impact in shaping the culture of the entire industry that TransLease operates in. As a result, the lack of trust coupled with the imbalance of power meant that objectives and motives for using TransLease differed between community members. Despite some evidence of power struggles between lease companies themselves, they ultimately shared common objectives in terms of what they wanted the system to achieve. As well as the efficiency gains expected, it was observed that all lease companies envisaged the system as giving them more power through the use of objective ‘rules of trade’. While these motives were a source of contention for repair agents, lease company objectives were held in common, unifying their intended direction and strategy for TransLease. The development and reinforcement of these common objectives took place during regular meetings between lease companies at which communications between parties was seen to be relatively open.

The repair agents studied also shared a common strategy and objectives for TransLease. Primarily, their aim was to improve operating efficiencies, with some consciously aiming for the system to improve their marketability to lease companies in the long-term future. The contentious issues were that objectives differed between lease companies and repair agents and that lease companies often saw it as a means of gaining more control; the logical by-product being a high referral rate. Naturally, this clashed with the objectives of repair agents, who intended that the system would mean lower referral rates. The fact that repair agents and lease companies rarely communicated together to overcome these differences only served to exacerbate the problem. Once Cap Gemini began to facilitate regular meetings between the two parties, continual progress was made in closing the gap between the disparate objectives. The issue of communication and cultural differences was seen as critical to the creation of shared objectives.

When dealing with IOS, and particularly IOS with many participants (such as TransLease), the effective management of change hinges on strong and effective communication by managers and users (Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996). This is vital if operations are to be coordinated successfully and data held within the system are to be kept up to date (Nah et al., 1998). However, in the case of TransLease, one of the key factors contributing to the ‘failure of the system was a lack of communication between participants.

TransLease uses expert support technology in order to verify whether repair agents requests are authorized. In this type of system, Nah et al. (1998) considered it necessary to communicate to the user the reason(s) why each decision was made. They argued that communicating these details will reduce frustrations and resentment. In the case of TransLease, a general consensus of repair agents’ views from groups 3 and 4 centred upon claims that, when an authorization request was referred, lease companies did not explain the reason(s) for the referral to the agent. This resulted in frustration for the repair agents, manifesting itself in disenchantment with the system and ill feeling between the two companies. Furthermore, repair agents felt that this restricted their learning process. They claimed that, if they were told the reasons, it would allow them to avoid making similar mistakes in the future, thereby increasing chances of first-time authorizations. Added confusion over this issue came to light during discussions and observations with lease companies when managers of maintenance controller teams insisted that their staff always gave such reasons, claiming that repair agents never asked. In an effort to aid repairers’ learning, several lease companies advocated that repair agents should receive a monthly copy of the repairer-specific ‘referral reasons’ report generated by Cap Gemini:

When they (repair agents) start using it, they need attention and training to help them use it better. We think that they should have a copy of the referral reasons report so that they can see where they are going wrong. They need to sit down with us and talk through these reports. (LC Group 2: 27.07.98).

The success of a system may be hindered by having stakeholder groups with different organizational cultures, who will inherently have different views on how any given system should be managed, used and developed (Kling, 1992; Davenport, 1994; Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996). Davenport (1994, p. 120) pointed to the need to solve this problem and talked of the need to change the different organizations’ cultures that do not ‘fit’ with the others: ‘this means altering the basic behaviours, attitudes, values, expectations and incentives that relate to a system . . . changing the technology only reinforces the beliefs that already exist’. However, whether it is possible to actually change an organization’s culture is debatable (Bennet, 1997).

When asked about any cross-cultural problems or issues, lease companies felt that there was a wide gulf between their organizational cultures. Views are typified here:

We have totally different cultures. Repairers are just interested in short-term gains. They don’t think strategically like us and that’s what our culture is all about. They’re not interested in repeat business, so reducing the time a customer has to wait (an intended benefit of TransLease) doesn’t bother them. (LC Group 2: 27.07.98)

It was apparent that there were cultural ‘gaps’, not only between lease company and repair agent communities, but also between the different repair agent organizations themselves, adding further complexity to the TransLease enabled community. The differing cultures of the members of the community led to different expectations and objectives for the system. Many repair agents, particularly those independent of large dealer networks, primarily intended the system to improve operational or ‘day-to-day’ efficiencies, for example saving them time spent on the telephone. Those belonging to large dealer network organizations differed slightly in that they saw TransLease providing not only improved efficiencies but also increasing their long-term competitiveness in the marketplace. The result of these differences was that users from repair agents linked to dealerships consequently had a more entrepreneurial approach, making them more receptive to change and able to adapt easier than their independent counterparts. Ultimately, this meant that different repair agents had different attitudes and beliefs about the system.

Users in the smaller independent repair agents often had no, or very little, experience of computer systems, suggesting that IT was not embedded into their organizational culture. As a result, they were found to have feelings ranging from trepidation and fear, through to excessive expectations of what the system could achieve. On an operational level, a lack of keyboard familiarity made inputting data a slow process, in many cases resulting in a lack of enthusiasm. Conversely, lease companies were far more comfortable with such systems, leading them to become frustrated by repair agents who were not as computer literate as themselves.

They need educating . . . It takes them ages to type the details in and we can’t wait that long. (LC Group 2: 24.06.98)

The creation of shared objectives was seen as critical to the success of this system. Increased communication and an awareness of the cultural differences were significant here. Related to this was the need to create a greater level of understanding between members of the community and a greater level of understanding of the system.

## Understanding

It became clear that two factors were important in the building of understanding. The first was the development of more effective training to ensure confidence and competence in the use of the systems; the second was the effective management of user expectations of the system.

The impact that a lack of training has on how much a system is accepted and used is widely discussed in the literature (Kling, 1992; Davenport, 1994). Kling’s (1992) research stressed the importance of providing training when users hold clerical positions. Significantly, it was seen that end-users at repair agent organizations are often employed in a clerical capacity, even though they lacked ‘adequate’ training. Lease company employees appeared to be adequately trained and comfortable using the system. Conversely, of all of the repair agents visited from group 3, none had received any formal training, a situation that Cap Gemini and lease companies believed to be a common occurrence throughout the community. The effects of this were observed to be a lack of confidence in using the system, increases in data entry times, persistent mistakes on data entry (resulting in referrals) and a weakened ability to learn from any ‘mistakes’ made:

We need better training and regular workshops in these early stages. This would help us because we think we’re doing everything right but maybe we’re not, and when it goes wrong we don’t know what to do. (RA Group 4: 27.07.98)

Repair agents claimed that this problem was exacerbated by the lack of feedback from maintenance controllers detailing reasons for referral. Despite this, lease companies complained that they continually had to help repair agents use the system by instructing them over the telephone. Again, this heightened tensions between the two groups. This was exacerbated in certain companies by the high turnover of clerical staff, resulting in a continuous loss of knowledgeable system workers.

Regarding expectation levels, representative feeling of both groups of repair agents was that the system was not effective because they were only achieving a maximum of around 10% first-time authorizations, therefore not gaining any of the expected benefits. Typical feelings were as follows:

We haven’t gained any benefits. In fact, it is just the opposite – we are being slowed down by the system. We are spending more time duplicating work and typing in data, only to have to phone anyway because we get referred every single time. All of the benefits are at the lease company end where they have to do less work and gain more control over us. It is just a nightmare. (RA Group 4: 29.07.98)

As a result, the feelings and attitudes of repair agents ranged from severe disappointment to anger, frustration and bitterness. However, repair agents were not alone in not having thei expectations met. Lease companies also expressed disappointment over progress to date, citing a multitude of reasons why their expectations had been met. The expectations of this group were found not to be focused solely on achieving specific first-time authorization rates, but more on a need to be making progress in all areas. These differences highlight the importance of creating realistic expectations of the system, and the subsequent management of these expectations throughout its life cycle.

## TRANSLEASE: THE CURRENT SITUATION (1998)

As discussed previously, this independent research was done in conjunction with several Cap Gemini initiatives to investigate ways of improving the TransLease service. As a result, many of the problems and issues raised in this paper have now been addressed. Most significantly, the belief that there was a strong need to consider the ‘soft’ issues concerning the system was reinforced within Cap Gemini and other stakeholder groups. This has now been engendered within Cap Gemini’s ‘intensive care programme’. The philosophy now held by Cap Gemini and that which significantly pervades the industry is that TransLease is meant to enable an electronic business community. By this, it is meant that all stakeholder groups have equal power and influence over how the system is managed. In order to help to enable this, Cap Gemini now facilitate regular meetings and ‘workshops’ between all stakeholder groups, at all levels of their respective organizations. This democratic approach has resulted in lease companies relaxing their ‘rules of trade’ as they have recognized the needs of repair agents. In addition to the focus on the soft aspects of the system, an intensive ‘data cleaning’ programme was carried out. An agreement has been made to use standard data codes (ICME codes) for TransLease. The data integrity problems have dramatically improved, and a continuous quality management scheme ensures minimal problems in this area. The result is that TransLease now enjoys unequalled success in the marketplace. The number of TransLease-enabled transactions per month continues to increase, as does the number of community members/participants. TransLease’s performance has led to it recently winning an award from the Institute of Transport Managers for ‘cost savings to the industry and product innovation’.

## HANDLING THE RELATIONAL DIMENSION TO IOS

As highlighted by TransLease, the social or relational dimensions to interorganizational systems are often neglected as the technology becomes the actual focus of the venture. However, this was shown to be a problematic, if not a completely flawed, approach to managing IOS, often resulting in low usage rates, dissatisfaction, unachieved expectations and general failure of the IOS. As Kumar & van Dissel (1996) elaborated, IOS and its software needs to be understood as a manifestation of interorganizational relationships. Management thus needs to undertake IOS ventures with the objective of developing closer ties, which has to entail formulating a co-operative relationship management strategy.

There are, however, a number of aspects that are peculiar to IOS ventures. Relational management in an IOS context requires a well-defined foundation built upon operational standards that detail precise data and code standards. As noted early on by Konsynski & McFarlan (1990, p. 119), ‘information has to be packaged for all partners by all partners, which requires the joint design of data definitions, formats, relationships and search patterns’. In essence, these standards describe the basis for common operational rules of the electronic community, and the clearer they are the easier it will be for parties to begin operationalizing their interactions (Spar & Bussgang, 1996). As the experience of TransLease highlighted, without the order that data and code standards create, business between the lease/hire and repair companies could not be conducted effectively. Rules also ensure that participants receive equal treatment under the system (Konsynski & McFarlan, 1990). To formalize these rules requires cultural awareness and understanding of each other’s business objectives in establishing an IOS. Alignment of the strategic objectives pursued then allows all participating parties to work towards achieving each other’s objectives. In TransLease, this was clearly an essential part, which ensured not only access to mutual benefits, but also commitment to investing in the technology and the relationship. In all management aspects of the IOS, open and regular communication is an integral part to ensure not only that the system remains aligned to each party’s objectives, but also to ensure the ongoing operational effectiveness of the system.

The primary focus of relational development in IOS (as shown in the TransLease case) centres on power and trust issues (Hart & Saunders, 1997). Signs of power use and abuse arise for reasons of compensating for the often lax specified data and code standards that define transaction procedures. Because of the resulting uncertainty surrounding interactions with participants in an IOS context, control is often sought by means of relying on the original means of transaction control (i.e. telephone, mail or fax). This of course is counterproductive to the innovation of an IOS system. In turn, control or power use needs to be alleviated by increasing the degree of trust in the technology and the participants. The objective has to be that parties feel sufficiently secure to rely on using the IOS system as the core vehicle for interacting with participants and thereby minimizing the use of previous used media. Without sufficient trust in the technology, regulatory structure (including codes, standards and rules of interactions) and other participants, an interorganizational system’s future can be jeopardized.

## CONCLUSION

This action research study provided one step forward in refocusing TransLease. Its significance for the collaborating organization (i.e. Cap Gemini) was that it pointed away from the technical issues that they had been focusing on towards the softer behavioural issues. This has led to a change in direction in the management of the system. Some of the changes have been explicitly identified in this paper. TransLease is now seen as ‘successful’ by the community of users as Cap Gemini continues to rebuild shared objectives, trust and understanding within the community.

The vast majority of stakeholders interviewed did not suggest that TransLease was lacking in any way in terms of its functionality. Moreover, many interviewees felt that it was an excellent system on a technical level. Indeed, the changes to the technical functionality of the system have been minimal. Stakeholders’ criticisms, concerns and doubts were centred on issues such as how the system was being used, how it was managed and how prepared participants were to use it effectively in terms of training and reshaping business processes. The issue with overwhelming influence on the system’s use and perception in the business community was that of the political climate. More succinctly, the crucial factor making up the political climate was the lack of trust between lease companies and repair agents. This was seen to have insinuated itself into many different aspects and areas of the system. Research showed that this lack of trust impacted greatly on how repair agents perceived their role in the system, making many see it in a negative light; it was seen as a system designed for lease companies and not for themselves. The lack of trust was evident on a functional level, manifesting itself in the writing of what repair agents perceived as being unreasonably stringent ‘rules of trade’. The stringency of these rules, coupled with lease companies’ inflexibility to change them, intensified ill feelings, inducing a vicious circle of repair agent frustration and disappointment. Subsequently, these negative feelings were seen to reduce enthusiasm, optimism and, in certain cases, levels of use. Ultimately, TransLease was suffering from ‘expectation failure’. Expectations of the system’s potential were found to be unrealistically high, particularly in the repair agent community. Therefore, it was necessary to try to bring the performance of the system up towards these expectations, but also lower them to a more practical level. If this was to be done, it required that communications be improved, user involvement increased, training courses better managed and greater flexibility offered by lease companies. Most importantly, the interdependence of these factors and of the two communities had to be recognized: to achieve a true electronic community, the imbalance of power within the overall business community had to be addressed to avoid lease companies continuing to enjoy an inappropriate level of power, attention and influence. Each stakeholder group needed to have equal ‘standing’ in the community, with each having a greater empathy and understanding of each other’s needs. Our research reinforces the finding of Hart & Saunders (1997, p. 29) that the persuasive use of power is ‘more effective than coercive approaches for building long-term relationships and improving interorganizational co-ordination’. This research also points to the critical importance of the role of the ‘conduit host’ in managing not only the network hardware and software, but also the relationship between the stakeholders.

In conclusion, this paper integrates research from three separate traditions, implementation research, failure studies and IOS research, in an investigation of a large and complex electronic commerce trading community. Drawing upon qualitative empirical data, a model has been developed that describes the inter-relationship between the much-neglected social and behavioural dimension in IOS. The data gathered in this ‘action research study’ reinforce the view that, as these types of ‘extended enterprise’ become more common, the importance of focusing upon the ‘softer’ organizational issues must take greater prominence in the design, management and implementation of the systems. Further research thus needs to look more carefully at the holistic management of the relationship element in interorganizational systems.
