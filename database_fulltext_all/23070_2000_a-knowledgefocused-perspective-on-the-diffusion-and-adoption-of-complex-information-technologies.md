# A knowledge-focused perspective on the diffusion and adoption of complex information technologies: the BPR example

S. Newell, J. A. Swan\* & R. D. Galliers\*\*

Nottingham Business School, Nottingham Trent University, Burton Street, Nottingham NG1 4BU, UK, email: sue.newell@ntu.ac.uk; \*Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK, email: irobjs@wbs.warwick.ac.uk, and \*\*Information Systems Department, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK, email: r.d.galliers@lse.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper presents a knowledge-focused perspective for the development of a model to explain the diffusion and adoption of complex integrating technologies. Business process re-engineering (BPR) is used as the example to illustrate the model. However, while BPR is used to illustrate our argument, the model that is developed is relevant to understanding the innovation processes surrounding any complex IT-based innovation. It is argued that the strength of this diffusion model is that it focuses not on the spread of particular technological artifacts (whether it is BPR or any other IT-based innovation), but on the spread of the ideas and knowledge underpinning the technology. In particular, the model draws attention to the ways in which technology suppliers commodify knowledge and present ‘packaged’ solutions. This creates problems for potential users who need to unpack this knowledge and integrate it with existing organizational knowledge. The diffusion and adoption of innovations is thus seen as a process of integrating knowledge across disparate communities. Such knowledge integration, however, is difficult. This can help to explain the apparent contradiction between the limited success rate of BPR and its widespread diffusion among western firms.

Keywords: Business process re-engineering, fashion setting, information technology, innovation diffusion, knowledge integration.

## INTRODUCTION

It is possible to track a number of new ideas about organizational redesign that have each been declared as the new ‘best practice’. For example, Taylor’s idea of scientific management was heralded as the most efficient way to organize production at the beginning of the century (Taylor, 1947), while a current popular prescription for ‘best practice’ is business process re-engineering (BPR) (Hammer & Champy, 1993). Such new ideas are pervasive, particularly in developing fields such as information systems (IS). For example, concepts such as decision support systems (e.g. Keen & Scott Morton, 1978), IS for competitive advantage (e.g. McFarlan, 1984; Porter & Millar, 1984) and IS outsourcing (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993; Willcocks & Lacity, 1998) have each enjoyed periods of acclaim as the definitive answer to strategic information technology (IT) problems. More recently, we have seen the emergence of such topics as electronic commerce (e.g. Choudhury, 1997), enterprise resource planning systems (e.g. Davenport, 1998) and knowledge management (e.g. Alavi & Leidner, 1999) as phenomena that excite similar levels of interest. This pervasiveness suggests that the diffusion and adoption of such new ideas are worthy of investigation and conceptualization in their own right, as understanding the mechanisms through which particular new ideas are diffused is necessary if those with relevant IS/IT expertise are to influence this process (Abrahamson, 1996). In this paper, the focus is on the diffusion and adoption of BPR, but the general principles and theoretical framework developed are considered to be relevant to any complex innovation process involving IT.

BPR was hailed as the latest breakthrough in programmed organizational change and change management. While there is lack of agreement about its precise nature (e.g. Davenport, 1993; Hammer & Champy, 1993; Harrison & Pratt, 1993; Strassmann, 1993; Van Ackere etal., 1993), the central tenet of BPR is that organizing business activities around processes rather than functions will generate major improvements in operational effectiveness, customer satisfaction and cost reduction, and so will enhance competitive advantage (Hammer, 1990; Hammer & Champy, 1993). In its various guises, BPR essentially represents a planned, rational and phased approach to the management of organizational change generating crossfunctional integration of knowledge, skills and processes. Proponents of BPR argue that implementation involves a major departure from current practices, which therefore demands radical rather than incremental change in order to ensure the ‘order-of-magnitude improvements’ necessary to compete in today’s competitive markets (Davenport, 1993). Importantly, at the heart of BPR is the idea that the creative use of IT will be the enabler for these changes (Fahy & O’Leary, 1997): ‘IT is both an enabler and implementer of process change’ (Davenport, 1993).

BPR (Davenport & Short, 1990; Hammer, 1990) has also become big business. The market for BPR services was estimated to be \$51 billion in 1995 (Davenport, 1996). Senior executives in the western world indicate that BPR is one of the most key issues they face in the late 1990s (Galliers etal., 1994; Brancheau etal., 1996; Watson et al., 1997), and interest is still keen. Since its original conception, BPR has been widely credited across both academic and business communities, and claims of BPR successes are abundant (see, for example, Knowledge and Process Management: The Journal of Corporate Transformation, incorporating Business Change and Re-engineering, vols 1–5, 1994–98, published by Wiley).

More recently, however, cracks have appeared in the BPR mien. There is now increasing recognition in the literature of the limitations of BPR, including warnings from one of its founders that the original concept was flawed (Davenport, 1996). There are also numerous reported failures of BPR, said to have arisen, in many cases, from its narrow IT focus (Watts, 1993). Thus, BPR has been described as ‘the fad that forgot people’ (Davenport, 1996).

The novelty and endurance of BPR have also been questioned (Land, 1996). BPR is seen by some as the latest in a long line of management fads (Mumford, 1994). At best, it is seen as little different, in terms of its underlying principles and methodologies, from other longestablished and well-researched and recognized approaches to organizational redesign. Mumford (1994, p. 316), for example, argued that BPR is merely a repackaging of earlier principles of socio-technical redesign: ‘Today BPR is being hailed as an entirely new approach to efficiency improvement. However, it is difficult to see how it differs from socio-technical design’. Similarly Passmore (1994) suggested that BPR incorporates many of the essential ingredi ents of socio-technical approaches including attention to the horizontal integration of technical processes and the emphasis on multifunctional and empowered work teams. At worst, BPR has been deemed to be potentially disruptive or even doomed to failure. Mumford (1994), for example, suggested that BPR is impoverished relative to socio-technical design both because it has a weaker theoretical and methodological foundation, and because it deemphasizes the value of the social system in favour of the technical system and competitive advantage. Egan (1995) took a stronger standpoint arguing, from the broader perspective of strategic management, that BPR offers nothing new, nothing tangible, nothing strategic and nothing demonstrable in terms of sustainable advantage. ‘Organisations are far more likely to “muddle through”, to adapt incrementally rather than “reinvent” themselves’ (Egan, 1995, p. 111).

Despite these criticisms, one of the major successes of BPR has been its rapid and widespread diffusion among communities of industrialists and academics both within and across nations (Newell etal., 1999). In this respect, BPR has marched ahead of socio-technical approaches. Thus, while the proponents of socio-technical approaches seem to have been unable to persuade management to take them up (Passmore, 1994), perhaps because of its emphasis on IT, BPR has been taken on board with apparent alacrity, particularly by those working in IS. Moreover, the widespread diffusion of BPR suggests that it has had a major impact, positive or otherwise, on management thinking and practice.

The purpose of this paper, then, is not to repeat or to resolve ongoing debates about the relative competitive advantages and/or novelty of BPR approaches. These debates are covered very well elsewhere (see, for example, Sauer et al., 1997). Instead, this paper aims to inform our understanding of the process of innovation diffusion. To do this, the paper first outlines relevant theoretical perspectives on innovation diffusion. These begin to explain the mechanisms that enable the relatively rapid diffusion of complex technologies such as BPR. The paper examines similarities and differences between these different perspectives and uses them as the basis for developing a knowledge-based perspective. This is used to develop a model of the diffusion process in relation to complex technologies. While the model is used to explore the diffusion of BPR, the paper argues that it is more widely applicable to understanding the diffusion and adoption of new IT-based technologies generally. In particular, it is argued that, to understand the diffusion of complex IS/IT, it is necessary to understand the ways in which the knowledge underpinning a new technology, here BPR, is packaged and bundled by the supply side in order to encourage its more rapid and widespread diffusion. While this can indeed encourage diffusion, it also makes implementation of the technology within a particular user firm more problematic, because there is a need then to unpack this knowledge and integrate it with the localized, context-specific organizational knowledge that is embedded in existing routines and practices. In the last section, differences between this knowledge-focused model of innovation and traditional models of innovation are examined in order to develop this argument still further.

## THEORETICAL PERSPECTIVES ON INNOVATION DIFFUSION

Some new technologies, such as BPR, have clearly diffused very rapidly across organizations, while other new technologies never get widely adopted. Theories of innovation and innovation diffusion seek to explore and explain why particular new technologies do diffuse quickly and widely, while others do not. Innovation and innovation diffusion have been defined and researched in many different ways, so it is important for researchers to clarify the particular perspective taken (Wolfe, 1994). This is done next. After this, the traditional perspective on innovation diffusion is reviewed. This is essentially a supplier-focused perspective (i.e. the communications perspective of Rogers, 1962, 1983, 1995). The limitations of this perspective are considered and used to overview two more recent perspectives, which build on the supplier-focused perspective: the user-focused (i.e. the decision episode framework of Clark etal., 1992); and the fashion-setting perspective (Abrahamson, 1991, 1996). These are not exhaustive, but are chosen because they offer useful insights into the diffusion of IT-based innovations such as BPR.

## Innovation and innovation diffusion

Innovation is defined here as a socially constructed process involving the development and implementation of new ideas (Van de Ven, 1986). This process can be described in terms of a number of episodes (Rogers, 1962, 1995; Newell etal., 1993). The first episode, agenda formation, relates to the acquisition and initial sharing of new ideas by organizational members. In this first episode, problems are defined, and ideas about new technologies become available within a firm. The second episode, selection, relates to the further processing of ideas within the organization, such that particular technologies are chosen to go forward for further development. The third episode, implementation, describes the process of actually introducing the selected technologies to the organization as new products, services or processes. The final episode is usage and describes the situation when the innovation has become routine (Nelson & Winter, 1982). These episodes do not represent discrete stages: the limitations of stage models of innovation, particularly in relation to complex information systems, are well known (e.g. Ettlie & Bridges, 1987; Galliers & Sutherland, 1991). Rather, they are seen as iterative, overlapping and inherently political (Pfeffer, 1981; Swan & Clark, 1992) – hence the term ‘episodes’ as opposed to ‘stages’.

There is a large body of research (e.g. Sauer, 1993) focusing on the episode of IS implementation (and IS failure). This attempts to identify the factors that explain why new, so-called best-practice technologies fail in some situations and succeed in others, and often develops prescriptive guidelines to assist firms in implementation (e.g. Avison etal., 1988). Much of this research starts from the premise that a best-practice technology has been identified, but the problem is in implementing it. Many studies of BPR, for example, tend to assume that BPR is the best way forward and look for ways to make implementation easier (Hammer & Champy, 1993). There has been relatively less attention given to processes of agenda formation and selection, and yet problems that occur here may make implementation virtually impossible. For example, in many cases, firms select new technologies that are inappropriate for their particular problems, where better alternatives exist, and where implementation is impossible without major redesign and investment (Swan & Clark, 1992). In such cases, problems occur before implementation when crucial decisions are being made about which new concepts or technologies might solve a firm’s particular problems. Critical to understanding these problems is the analysis of the diffusion of ideas about available technologies and concepts.

Diffusion refers to the communication, spread and adoption of new ideas among social communities (Rogers, 1962, 1983, 1995). Existing theoretical perspectives offer useful insights into the reasons surrounding the widespread uptake of ideas such as BPR by communities of firms. In this paper, we follow Damanpour & Evans (1984) in taking as an indicator of innovation diffusion the adoption of any product, service or process that is new to a business unit, regardless of how old (or new) it might be to another unit. However, as the lack of success of many BPR initiatives indicates (Hammer & Champy, 1993; Davenport, 1996), adoption does not always generate innovation (i.e. development and utilization of new ideas). This paper seeks to explain the factors and processes that influence diffusion and adoption of BPR, because this has received relatively little attention to date. However, we do not assume that adoption is necessarily the right choice for particular firms, nor that it is necessarily a good indicator of appropriation (successful utilization).

In terms of understanding the diffusion and adoption of new ideas, traditional models have tended to explain this from the perspective of the supplier. While the traditional perspective provides useful insights into innovation processes, it will be argued that many of its underlying assumptions can be challenged, and that there is therefore a need to develop a new perspective.

## The traditional supplier-focused perspective of the innovation process

Researchers interested in IS diffusion have relied heavily on sociological and organizationa perspectives, dominated in particular by the communications perspective of Rogers (1962, 1983, 1995 – see, for example, Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1997). Rogers’ model is aimed at helping central technology suppliers to promote more rapid diffusion of predefined bestpractice innovations to communities of potential adopters. This research has contributed to understanding about factors that predict the rate of adoption of innovation over time and space. Central factors have been highlighted as: characteristics of the innovation, such as its complexity, relative advantage, trialability, compatibility (Rogers, 1983); characteristics of potential adopters, so that those who are likely to be early adopters can be targeted in the initial diffusion process by technology suppliers; and social networks that enable the ideas to be communicated within and across communities of organizations (Wolfe, 1994). An important point here then is that it is crucial to understand the attributes of the new technology as well as the social networks through which ideas about the technology are communicated, in order to understand its diffusion and development across organizations (Wolfe, 1994). This concurs with research on technological and organizational innovation, which identifies attributes of technologies, such as radical vs. incremental (Ettlie & Bridges, 1987); altering vs. entrenching (Clark & Staunton, 1989); informating vs. automating (Zuboff, 1988); and technical vs. administrative (Damanpour, 1987), as important mediators of the innovation process. This research tradition would thus direct us to considering the characteristics of BPR on the assumption that these characteristics would help to explain why it has diffused so widely.

Using these types of categorizations BPR, with its emphasis on major or radical technical and administrative change, is perhaps best categorized as: complex; certainly incompatible with current practice; not easily observable or trialable; and as altering existing organizational practice. Yet these are just the characteristics of innovations that are assumed to delay and slow down diffusion. In other words, given the characteristics of BPR, the traditional diffusion perspective would predict that this innovation would be rather slow to diffuse. For example, according to Rogers (1995), innovations that are incompatible with existing practice will be slower to diffuse than innovations that are compatible. Yet BPR is, by definition, incompatible with existing practice, because the very essence of BPR is that it ignores ‘how things were previously done’ and starts instead with a blank piece of paper. Similarly, innovations that alter existing practices, the central objective of a BPR project, are assumed to diffuse more slowly than are innovations that do not significantly alter existing practice. Moreover, BPR cannot really be ‘tried out’ in a small part of the organization, as its focus on cross-boundary processes means that a whole range of functions and departments needs to be involved in the design and implementation of the new ways of operating. Therefore, rapid and widespread diffusion of BPR would not be expected because it is not trialable. On the face of things then, given that BPR has many of the characteristics that might be expected to retard its diffusion, its rapid diffusion among communities of firms and academics alike appears to be paradoxical. This indicates that the supplier-focused perspective needs to be developed in order to explain this apparent contradiction.

Supplier-focused models of diffusion have made an important contribution to highlighting the importance of social networks that allow communication of new ideas across organizations, in particular the links between technology suppliers and users. They also suggest that both strong and weak relationships or ‘ties’ are important for the diffusion of new ideas (Granovetter, 1973; Rogers 1983). Strong ties are close associations among firms, for example contractual links between a firm and its customers or suppliers, or between a firm and an IT supplier. These will promote rapid diffusion of ideas among organizations within a particular community. As an illustration, the strong ties among UK firms in the banking sector are a possible explanation as to why BPR has been so widely diffused in this sector (Grey & Mitev, 1994). Paradoxically, strong ties may also restrict the range of new ideas that are diffused, because they generate stronger norms about what is considered an acceptable or legitimate form of best practice (Di Maggio & Powell, 1983). An example would be the widespread adoption of EDI among suppliers of large manufacturers (such as Benetton), because this is necessary to maintain the customer–supplier relationship. The degree to which strong ties are established will vary across industrial sectors, suggesting that, in some sectors, diffusion will be more rapid than in others. The importance of sectoral influences on innovation diffusion is echoed by others. For example, Kwon & Zmud (1987) highlighted environmental factors as important for IS implementation, and Pavitt (1984) outlined sectoral patterns of innovation.

Weak ties link individuals from organizations across different sectors or communities that would not normally make contact during their day-to-day business (for example, contacts made through educational courses or professional groups). These can be equally important in the diffusion process because, through weak ties, organizations can encounter ideas that go beyond their usual ways of operating. Communications technologies, such as electronic mail and the Internet, could also play an important role in encouraging weak ties across a wider span of individuals and organizations. IS professionals have been centrally involved in the diffusion of BPR and are also likely (although not necessarily) to be active users of communications technologies. Through electronic mail, for example, there is an abundance of information about conferences and meetings at which BPR is a central theme. Therefore, it is hardly surprising that information about BPR has spread so rapidly through this community.

## Problems with the supplier-focused perspective

It was seen above that the supplier-focused perspective had a problem in explaining the rapid diffusion of BPR, given that its inherent characteristics appeared to lead to the prediction that it would diffuse slowly. Explanation is possible, however, once it is recognized that these defin ing characteristics of new technologies are not, as assumed by traditional models, given and permanent, but rather are perceived and therefore influenced by cognitive, social and political processes. In other words, the attributes of complex technologies such as BPR are not fixed and rigid, but are socially constructed. Like many other organizational technologies, BPR is best represented as a complex, multifaceted bundle of ideas that can be embedded in a variety of ways, including technical artifacts, organizational routines, languages, symbols (i.e. the three-letter acronym is a powerful symbol), equipment, decision-making structures, and so forth (Clark & Staunton, 1989). These attributes of technologies such as BPR can be unpacked and communicated selectively by social groups who have a vested interest in promoting or limiting their adoption (Rogers, 1983 refers to these as ‘change agents’). For example, research investigating suppliers of complex computer-aided production management systems (CAPM) – such as manufacturing resources planning (MRP2) – found that IS suppliers (software suppliers and consultants) presented highly simplified and codified visions of such systems, emphasizing systems implementation and technical advantages and downplaying the degree of organizational change that was often needed to implement such systems (Clark & Staunton, 1989). This improved the suppliers’ chances of selling the systems.

However, many firms, seduced by the idea of a technical fix, adopted these technologies with very little real understanding about their complexity, with a resultant high level of failure (Swan & Clark, 1992; Wilson etal., 1994).

Another problem with supplier-focused models is that they are mainly broadcaster–receiver approaches. They are based on the assumption that definitive best-practice technologies can be identified by some central supply agency, and the problem of diffusion is to broadcast information that will persuade essentially passive users to adopt them. There are two problems with this with respect to a complex IT-based innovation such as BPR. First, the assumption that there is a single technically efficient best practice is rarely the case. Usually, IT-based innovation is heavily dependent on the context of application, so the notion that there can be a universally applicable best-practice solution is flawed (Clark, 1987; Swan etal., 1996). Rather, users need to be actively involved in unpacking and redesigning technologies as presented by the supply side alongside their existing organizational and technical systems in order to develop solutions that meet their own requirements (Clark, 1987 refers to this as ‘appropriation’). Secondly, supplier-focused models emphasize mainly supplier–user relationships. However, the importance of strong and weak ties attests to the wide variety of potentially important diffusion networks, including for example links with customers and suppliers, links with professional groups and links with colleagues within one’s own organization. Supplierfocused models tend to underestimate the active role that users play in seeking and creating knowledge relevant to the innovation through this wide range of social networks (Robertson et al., 1996).

Given the problems with some of the underlying assumptions of the traditional, supplyfocused perspective of innovation, newer models have been developed that overcome some of these limitations. In particular, there has been a recognition of the active role of users in the innovation process and of seeing innovation not necessarily as a rational decision-making process for improving efficiency, but rather as the diffusion and adoption of fads and fashions, as discussed in the following sections.

## Newer perspectives: a user-focused perspective

The active role of users in innovation diffusion is discussed by, for example, Tushman & Scanlan (1981); Clark etal. (1992); MacDonald & Williams (1993); Fleck (1994); Robertson etal. (1996) and Swanson & Ramiller (1997). For example, Tushman & Scanlan (1981) highlighted the importance of ‘boundary spanning’ individuals, who are active in penetrating interorganizational networks of various types, through which they can learn about the latest technologies. Boundary spanners also need to be well connected internally in order to be in a position to assimilate this knowledge into innovation processes within their own firms (see also Ibarra, 1993). According to this view, understanding the widespread uptake of BPR must involve consideration of the different communication networks, both internal and external, through which individuals gain access to new ideas. Such internal networks can be facilitated should there be an ‘organizing vision’ – a collective cognitive view of the new technology at work in the organization (Swan & Ramiller, 1997).

Robertson etal. (1996) describe a model of the innovation diffusion process that is referred to as the decision episode framework (DEF). This model was developed to explain diffusion of IT for operations management and was based on earlier models (Rogers, 1983; Clark etal., 1992) and empirical work (e.g. Swan & Clark, 1992; Wilson etal., 1994; Swan & Newell, 1995; Robertson etal., 1996). This depicts innovation as a series of decision episodes, but highlights the important boundary-spanning role of users. Users are presented as actively involved in a variety of networks, through which ideas about a range of alternative technologies relevant for operations management (the ‘innovation pool’) are communicated. The knowledge generated through this networking may then influence innovation within the firm, provided that the individual boundary spanner is able to get involved in the innovation decision process. According to this model, there is no universally applicable best practice, but rather a range of possible design concepts that are communicated selectively via different kinds of networks. For example, technology suppliers (IT vendors and consultants) were important in the UK and North America in influencing the diffusion process by selectively promoting ‘MRP/MRP2’ technologies to manufacturing firms as the definitive best practice, despite the fact that alternatives (such as JIT) existed at the time and were being taken up elsewhere (Clark & Newell, 1993). In keeping with other models (e.g. Rogers, 1983), the DEF depicts links between technology suppliers and users as important for innovation diffusion and also recognizes that suppliers may simplify the ways in which they portray complex new concepts such as BPR in order to persuade users to adopt. However, it also highlights the roles of other kinds of network in diffusion.

One particular network that can be a rich source of new ideas is a professional association. The main aim of a professional association is to provide its members with access to information about the latest technological developments in a particular knowledge area (Lynch, 1989; Swan & Newell, 1995). Professional association networks provide a forum for the development of weak ties. Both formal knowledge transfer (for example via courses, conferences presentations and publications) and personalized or informal knowledge transfer (for example through informal discussions and social events) can occur within this network (Kunzel & Sadowsky, 1989). Our previous research has shown that professional association networks act as powerful channels and filters in the communication of new ideas between organiza tions. For example, professional operations management associations were found to be an important route for the communication of ideas about particular ITs to firms in industry, in both Europe and North America (Swan & Newell, 1995; Robertson etal., 1996; Swan etal., 1997).

Two additional findings from this work offer useful insights into diffusion mechanisms. First, at least in the UK and North America, the information disseminated through the professional associations was shaped by an active minority of IT suppliers and consultants who penetrated these networks and wrote or presented much of their formal materials. In particular, MRP/MRP2 technologies were selectively pushed by suppliers who, because of their vested interests in selling, presented the technology in a highly simplified and prescriptive way to users in industry. Thus, the professional association network reinforced supply-led notions of best practice. As information gained through the associations was also perceived by practitioners as more complete and less partial than that gleaned directly from IT suppliers, this could entice users to adopt these technologies even though they were not really appropriate for their particular manufacturing context (Robertson et al., 1996). This suggests that it is important to consider the different interests of suppliers and users, with suppliers interested mainly in adoption and users in implementation. Secondly, there were national variations in the roles of these professional associations in the diffusion process. For example, the professional association in the UK was more heavily dominated by technology suppliers and more influential in pushing particular IT solutions to industry than that in Sweden (Swan etal., 1997). This concurs with other research on innovation, which suggests that macro-level environmental factors influence the diffusion of new technologies such as BPR (e.g. Clark, 1987; Kwon & Zmud, 1987).

## Newer perspectives: fads and fashions

Recent fashions perspectives (Abrahamson, 1991, 1996) offer additional insights into the mechanisms surrounding the diffusion process, particularly recognizing both the different interests of suppliers and users and the impact of the macro-environmental context on diffusion. Fashion-setting perspectives are useful because they help to explain the rapid diffusion of technologies, which are unproven or questionable in terms of efficiency gains for users. They are therefore particularly apposite in the BPR context.

Drawing on neo-institutional theory, Abrahamson (1996) presented a general supply and demand model of management fashion setting. Management fashions are defined as ‘transitory collective beliefs that certain management techniques are at the forefront of management progress’ (Abrahamson, 1996, p. 254). These beliefs are articulated in the form of powerful rhetorics (an example would be the three-letter acronym ‘BPR’) by fashion setters – defined as ‘organisations and individuals who dedicate themselves to producing and disseminating management knowledge’ (Abrahamson, 1996, p. 256). Abrahamson (1996) includes as fashion setters management gurus, consultants, mass media business publications and business schools – in a somewhat similar line of argument to that of Land (1996). In the context of IT in particular then, technology suppliers (software vendors and consultants) and professional associations have played a key role in disseminating rhetorics about the latest bestpractice tools and techniques to managers in industry (Swan & Newell, 1995).

According to this perspective, fashions are not simply pushed onto gullible managers. Rather, managers actively look for new techniques that will help them respond to real performance gaps opened up by technical and environmental changes. This demand for management fashions is influenced by macro socio-psychological and techno-economic forces. Technical or economic changes (e.g. market slumps) open up gaps between actual and desired performance in firms (e.g. gaps in quality). Fashion setters (e.g. consultants) sense these gaps and bring them into public awareness in the form of new rhetorics about best practice (e.g. ‘total quality management’). This, combined with a socio-psychological need among managers to adopt the latest new techniques, encourages the diffusion of fashions. In contrast to Land (1996) and Mumford (1994) among others, Abrahamson (1991) maintained that fashions do not always harm organizations, but may offer advantages as powerful symbols for efficiency and legitimacy. For example, companies that claim to be ‘re-engineering’ can potentially gain an image of innovativeness that may help them to attract or retain customers (Nystrom & Starbuck, 1984). Even if fashions are technically inefficient, they may still act as mechanisms for change and organizational action by allowing companies to label, attend to, interpret and make sense of complex and uncertain problems and, from this process, appropriate solutions could be iterated. Thus: ‘fads and fashions may constitute vital processes that animate random variations from which increasingly efficient innovations can evolve (Abrahamson, 1991, p. 609).

This perspective helps to explain the diffusion of techniques such as BPR, which generate important market opportunities for a range of suppliers. For example, BPR may be favourably promoted by IT vendors because it emphasizes IT-led change and so increases the supply and demand of software; by management consultants because it emphasizes the notion of facilitating the change process; and by IS academics because BPR brings IS to the centre of the management of strategic change. The vested interests of these groups in adoption is seen in the heavily prescriptive languages they use: ‘Don’t automate, obliterate’ (Hammer, 1990). ‘Existing approaches to meeting customer needs are so functionally based that incremental change will never yield the requisite interdependence’ (Davenport, 1993, p. 4). Using IT for competitive advantage was pronounced as the best route towards achieving competitive advantage: ‘…IT for process innovation is a virtual necessity’ (Davenport, 1993, p. 5). Thus, while acknowledging potential pains involved in its implementation, BPR has nonetheless been hailed as ‘…the hottest trend in management. Done well reengineering delivers extraordinary gains in speed, productivity, and profitability’ (Stewart, 1993, p. 32). Thus, particular social groups such as technology suppliers have been actively involved in getting firms to buy into the revolutionary vision and to recognize the strategic opportunities created by BPR.

The fashions perspective also highlights the importance of national influences on diffusion. This suggests that cycles of fashions are more likely in societies where there are strong norms for progress chasing and rationality. Norms of rationality refer to collective expectations that managers will seek the most efficient tools and techniques available. Norms of progress refer to expectations that managers will seek new and improved techniques. Together, these norms create a demand for new and ever more efficient techniques. Fashion setters are then able to fill these demands by continuously defining and redefining those techniques that generate rational management progress. BPR itself is underpinned by a philosophy of planned rational change management. Therefore, it is perhaps not surprising that the original concept was developed in the US where one might expect strong norms for progress chasing to exist (Hofstede, 1980).

## A KNOWLEDGE-FOCUSED PERSPECTIVE: A FRAMEWORK FOR EXPLAINING THE DIFFUSION OF IT-BASED TECHNOLOGIES

We have drawn on these different innovation diffusion perspectives to develop a knowledgefocused framework for exploring the spread and adoption of complex information and communication technologies such as BPR. In addition, we have drawn on our own research, which has focused on exploring the ways knowledge sharing through interorganizational networking influences the innovation process (see, for example, Newell etal., 1999; Swan etal., 1999). This perspective can help to explain the rapid spread of new technologies such as BPR. This knowledge-focused perspective is presented in the form of a model of diffusion and adoption. The model is illustrated using the example of BPR. Starting from the left-hand side, the model depicts complex ideas relating to organization and technical processes that exist in the public domain. Some of these ideas may be old (e.g. motivation), while others may be relatively new (e.g. process mapping) (Mumford, 1994). Drawing on the fashions perspective, we see that ideas are bundled together and packaged in particular ways to create solutions that can be presented by technology suppliers as relatively simple, best-practice ‘fixes’, which can be applied to organizational problems in a variety of contexts. Scarbrough (1996) refers to such solutions as ‘black-boxed’ because they conceal the complexity of the underpinning knowledge. The knowledge has been commodified, so that a set of such complex ideas can be presented as a ‘thing’ – a fixed entity – that can be slotted into any organizational context. These ideas exist within the broader society, but it is consultants and software and hardware vendors who are typically pro-active in bundling this knowledge into particular packages such as BPR. Concealment of the complexity of the underpinning knowledge is necessary because, otherwise, the new technology, in this instance BPR, would have exactly those characteristics that supply-side models of the innovation process have shown inhibit diffusion – high complexity, low trialability, low compatibility, etc. (Rogers, 1995). This commodification of knowledge into simplified, prescriptive, ‘black-boxed’ solutions is depicted in the model by the ‘commodified knowledge’ boxes. BPR has been used in this paper as a particular example, but the underlying knowledge commodification process illustrated in Figure 1 is more generally applicable to ideas promoted as the latest ‘best practice’ by technology suppliers.

The model shows how these ‘black-boxed’ solutions, as suggested by the supplier-focused perspective, are communicated directly to potential users via various communication channels linking technology suppliers and users – a process of ‘supply push’. This may occur in the form of general mass broadcasts from technology suppliers to potential users, e.g. mailing fliers about BPR and its virtues to many organizations or broadcasting such a message via electronic mail or over the Internet, and through more specific, strong ties, by which technology suppliers sell their consulting services to particular user firms. These traditional market relationships are clearly important in the diffusion of new technologies such as BPR. However, the model also draws on the user-focused perspective and depicts the fact that users do not wait passively for new ideas to be broadcast by the supply side, but actively search these out through their boundary-spanning activity (MacDonald, 1995). This user-led networking activity, both formal (e.g. contractual organizational links) and personal (informal individual links), will involve connections with a variety of different organizations, for example other firms, government bodies or educational institutions, and will involve a number of individuals from the user firm. In the model, this is depicted by the figure seeking out ideas from these different institutions. However, the model depicts, in particular, the importance of professional associations as a network that boundary spanners use in this search process, as research has demonstrated that users themselves see this as perhaps the most important source of exter nal knowledge about new technologies (Newell etal., 1996).

Professional associations have as their raison d’être the diffusion of a body of knowledge to members (Lynch, 1989), whether these members be doctors, personnel managers, operations managers or IT specialists, etc. Members are mostly practitioners who can apply this knowledge within their own organizational context. Practitioners appear to value this kind of network, because the knowledge diffused is expected to be more ‘neutral’ than that being made available through technology suppliers. However, research has demonstrated that the ‘neutrality’ of this knowledge may be more imagined than real, because the dissemination activities of professional associations are often dominated by technology suppliers. These technology suppliers (the dark figures) use the professional association network to push the same packaged solutions through this network as they push through their more direct links to potential clients (the light figures) (Newell etal., 1998). The model therefore makes a direct link between the packaged solutions provided by technology suppliers and the knowledge that is disseminated through professional associations. Users seeking new ideas through a ‘pull process may therefore be as likely to encounter the ‘black-boxed’ knowledge, in much the same way as they do when knowledge is ‘pushed’ to them directly from the technology suppliers themselves.

The model focuses on one professional association link, while in reality user firm employees may be involved with a number of different professional associations, each of which will concentrate on a relatively narrow body of knowledge. Moreover, with reference to recent management fashions, such as BPR, many professional associations are likely to have something to say on the subject, but this will have a different emphasis and temporality. For example, in relation to BPR, the ideas are likely to be bundled rather differently by a professional association whose members work in IT compared with an association whose members work as human resource specialists. While the former may focus heavily on technology, the latter may focus more on the impact of BPR on organizational change and the reallocation of human resources. Moreover, the former may have started to disseminate knowledge about this particular management fashion earlier than the human resources professional association. So, while the commodification of knowledge about BPR, for example, is depicted as a fixed entity, in reality the ‘package’ will vary somewhat as diffused by different professional associations or indeed different technology suppliers. This is important because it means that this knowledge will need to be integrated at the level of the firm when considering broad new technologies such as BPR (Newell etal., 1999).

‘Packaged’ or commodified knowledge about BPR then enters the user firm through both a supply push and a user pull. It arrives on the organizational agenda as a potential solution or opportunity for change. Being presented as a rational, ‘best-practice’ solution, which is relatively unproblematic, it is then more easily selected for implementation. The problem is that, to be fully appropriated or utilized, this knowledge has to be unpacked or unbundled within the user firm because, as has been argued, complex technologies such as BPR originate as a set of complex ideas, both technical and organizational. These complex ideas need to be understood and applied within the particular context for them to be effective, and will be transmogrified in the process of implementation (as depicted by the fact that the ideas icons are now grey rather than white in the figure). The process of unpacking the ‘black-box’ of BPR is problematic (depicted by the question mark), because user firms do not necessarily contain or involve individuals who understand the relevant underlying knowledge base adequately (Swan etal., 1996).

A key to this ability to unbundle or unpack the knowledge underpinning complex technologies such as BPR relates to how the implementation project within the user firm is managed and, in particular, who is involved. There are a variety of potential problems with this unbundling process during implementation. For example, if no-one in the user firm has a particular type of knowledge essential to BPR unbundling, e.g. process analysis, change management or IS skills, the firm may have to rely on an external party – a consultant – to provide this knowledge. The problem here is that, once the project has been concluded and the consultant has exited, a core element of the underpinning knowledge of the system is no longer available internally, unless that knowledge has been passed on as part of the project process. Given that organizations are in a constant state of flux and that systems will therefore need to be modified and updated, this is likely to prove a major obstacle for the long-term success of any complex IT project.

On the other hand, the user firm may contain individuals with the knowledge necessary to appropriate a complex technology such as BPR effectively, but not involve those people, either formally on the project team or informally through seeking their expertise at particular points during the project. This highlights the importance of internal networking for successful appropriation of complex technologies such as BPR. The set of complex ideas that are brought together within the ‘black-box’ involve a number of different disciplines: not just IS, but also for example human resource management, organizational development and the management of change. Individuals with backgrounds in these different disciplines all have, potentially at least, some expertise to contribute. This knowledge, however, needs to be integrated within the user firm in the process of implementation and usage. Where internal networks, especially across functional or disciplinary boundaries, are weak, certain expertise is likely to be excluded from the decision-making processes (Newell etal., 1998). In particular, it may be that a BPR project is led and dominated by IS/IT individuals (who may see it as a purely technical project) or OD specialists (who downplay the technology), thus excluding expertise that is necessary for achieving holistic change. It is not surprising, however, that firms adopting complex technologies such as BPR often fail to recognize this need to involve individuals from different disciplines, because the ‘black-box’ strategy of the technology suppliers (Scarbrough, 1996) has deliberately concealed this in order to mask the complexity of the particular technology and make it more marketable as a ‘solution’. Nevertheless, this problem of decontextualizing and unbundling the complex ideas that underpin the particular technological solution, e.g. BPR, will undoubtedly lead in some instances to a failure to appropriate the technology, so that the ‘solution’ is discarded (as depicted by the ideas being thrown into a bin).

Finally, the model recognizes that both macrolevel and microlevel factors will affect the diffusion and adoption of a new technology. For example, at the macrolevel, there are country differences in the way professional associations operate, which influence how far there is a technology push within this network (Swan etal., 1999). Also at the macrolevel, there are industry differences between sectors in terms of the degree of interconnection between firms, which in turn influences the degree of information exchange within a sector (Spender, 1989). At the microlevel, there are firm-specific variables, such as size and strategic orientation, that influence both external (i.e. boundary spanning) and internal networking, which, this paper has argued, are crucial for understanding the diffusion and adoption of complex technologies such as BPR.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

The strength of the diffusion model presented here is that it focuses not on the spread of particular technological artifacts [whether BPR or just-in-time (JIT), total quality management (TQM), enterprise resource planning (ERP), customer relationship management (CRM) and the like], but on the spread of the ideas and knowledge underpinning a technology. This knowledge-focused perspective on innovation suggests specific lines of future research, which may prove productive for those in IS interested in the diffusion of new technologies, whether this be to encourage their more rapid diffusion or simply to understand the process better. Viewing diffusion in terms of knowledge flows changes our understanding of the processes involved. The important point is that the knowledge needed for innovation is widely distributed and has to be integrated within the user firm through a process of negotiation and sensemaking (Weick, 1990). While technology suppliers do play a role in the dissemination of new ideas, as highlighted by supplier-focused perspectives, user organization members also actively search for and evaluate ideas ‘out there’ that might be relevant to some problem or opportunity. This is highlighted by user-focused perspectives. However, whether through the supplier ‘push’ or the user ‘pull’ networking activity, what tends to be ‘found’ is highly commodified and objectified knowledge (Scarbrough, 1996), as emphasized by the fashion perspective. This knowledge packaging by technology suppliers means that successful appropriation by a user firm depends on unpacking and ‘making sense’ of the various ideas contained within the ‘package’ and integrating this with locally situated knowledge. Thus, solutions such as BPR, while presented as generic and portable ‘best practices’, actually need to be reconfigured and transformed to fit the firm-specific context (Clark & Staunton, 1989). This will involve integrating the largely codified or explicit knowledge diffused through external networking activity with the tacit, organizational knowledge, which resides locally, embedded in established routines and practices. In other words, objectified knowledge diffused through interorganizational networks needs to be sedimented into firm-specific solutions (Hislop etal., 1997). The adoption of a new technology can thus be seen as a dynamic process of interaction, in which the diffusion of knowledge shapes the firm’s utilization of knowledge and vice versa.

This knowledge-focused perspective of the innovation process clearly resonates with recent interest in the topic of knowledge management and knowledge-intensive work and workers (e.g. Drucker, 1993). The problem with some of this literature is that it ignores the inherent ‘stickiness’ of knowledge and assumes a relatively straightforward transfer of knowledge from a sender to a receiver. Highly commodified knowledge can indeed be relatively easily transferred but, unfortunately, such commodified knowledge is difficult to unpack and so makes modification and contextualization difficult. Research explicitly exploring such issues would be useful. Preliminary results from our research suggest that there may well be national differences in the extent to which technology suppliers engage in this process of knowledge commodification. For example, Swan etal. (1999) found that Swedish firms had been much more likely to develop in-house bespoke manufacturing control systems, while British firms had been more likely to adopt standardized manufacturing resource planning (MRPII) packages. This was related to the less developed consultancy and software industry in Sweden, which meant that firms were less likely to be confronted by highly commodified MRPII solutions. Interestingly, and supporting the argument that the unpacking of commodified knowledge is problematic, the Swedish firms rated their bespoke systems as being more successful than did the British firms that had adopted standard packages.

The knowledge-focused perspective on innovation highlights how the diffusion and adoption of new technologies involves the development and utilization of large amounts of specialist knowledge and expertise. While, as argued above, this involves the integration of external and internal knowledge, it is also important to recognize that unbundling the commodified knowledge will potentially involve a variety of internal experts. Crucially, where IT cuts across departmental and geographical boundaries within an organization, as with BPR, there is a need to integrate the dispersed internal organizational knowledge that is implicated. This emphasizes that innovation processes need increasingly to be seen as interactive, as they involve the integration of knowledge across disparate social communities. Sharing knowledge, especially between individuals from different backgrounds who have rather different world views and values, is difficult and time consuming. Nonaka (1994) introduced the term redundancy to illustrate how multifunctional teams engaged in knowledge creation have to share more knowledge than may be strictly necessary in order to allow those involved to appreciate the meaning and relevance of others’ ideas. Where firms do not allow for this redundancy during interactive innovation processes, there may well be problems (Swan etal., 1999). Little research to date has focused on exploring these processes of knowledge unbundling, but the knowledge-focused perspective suggests that such research is vital to understanding increasingly interactive innovation processes.

In conclusion, this paper has explored the ways in which the processes involved in the diffusion of new complex technologies such as BPR create the conditions that make its appropriation problematic. Crucially, it presents the innovation process as a knowledge integration problem. This approach contrasts with the more typical implementation analysis, which sees the problems residing more exclusively within the user firm. The latter type of analysis has an inherent pro-innovation bias – BPR, or other complex technologies, can be effective – the problem resides within the user firm, which has simply not been able to implement it well. This is rejected by the analysis presented in this paper. Complex technologies such as BPR cannot be conceived as a singular ‘best-practice’ technology, wholly applicable to all firms in all situations. Such technologies are a combination of knowledge and ideas, bundled together to create a new ‘fashion’, which is selectively presented to potential adopters by technology suppliers who conceal its underlying complexity. This commodified knowledge needs to be unbundled and integrated with firm-specific knowledge if the technology is to be appropriated successfully. Understanding the innovation process therefore depends crucially on exploring these processes of knowledge bundling and knowledge unbundling and reintegration. Based on such an analysis, what is more surprising perhaps than the high failure rate in the adoption of complex technologies such as BPR is the fact that there are a few firms that do succeed!
