# Transforming information systems development through computer-aided systems engineering (CASE): lessons from practice

Tom Butler

National University of Ireland, University College Cork, Ireland, email: tbutler@afis.ucc.ie

Abstract. In dealing with the challenges posed by the ongoing problem of developing and integrating an evermore complex and diverse range of information systems in a timely manner, practitioners continue to grapple with important issues such as increasing developer productivity and bringing quality improvements to the process and product of systems development. Many organizations have adopted computer-aided systems engineering (CASE) tools with such outcomes in mind. With few exceptions, previous research into the phenomenon of CASE adoption and use has been survey based in the main, and has resulted in some confusion over the benefits to be derived from the use of CASE tools within the systems development process. This paper extends previous work as part of an interpretive, case-based research strategy to examine the adoption and use of integrated CASE (I-CASE) in a single organization. Such an approach facilitates a deeper understanding of the impact that CASE exerts on the process and product of systems development. Based on the experience of practitioners in the organization studied, a set of recommendations is presented. The findings of this study also lend support to the view that an I-CASE development platform does indeed have the potential to exert a positive impact on the development process and its product.

Keywords: computer-aided systems engineering (CASE), rapid application development (RAD), organizational change, systems development process

## INTRODUCTION

Significant challenges face information systems practitioners as they enter the first decade of the new millennium. True, certain aspects of this predicament are traceable to the ‘software crisis’ of the 1960s and 70s – the Y2K problem was, for example, one hangover from this era– however, the bulk of today’s problems present their own unique challenges for organizations endeavouring to develop new and ever more complex information systems. Radical improvements in the productivity and quality of the systems development process are deemed necessary to meet these challenges; furthermore, in the face of an increasing scarcity of developers, computer-aided systems engineering (CASE) is considered by many to be the ‘silver bullet’ that will help resolve the new ‘software crisis’ in organizations that grapple with the task of developing bespoke systems. Off-the-shelf applications (Bansler & Havn, 1994), outsourcing (McFarlan & Nolan, 1995) and enterprise resource planning systems (ERP) (Deloitte & Touche, 1997) are presented as alternative solutions to this problem. However, Holland etal. (1999) argue that organizations will still need to look beyond generic solutions, such as ERP, if they are to leverage information technology (IT) for competitive advantage. It is also the case that organizations operating in particular industry segments will have idiosyncratic information system (IS) needs that can only be met by the development of ‘bespoke’ systems.

Over the past two decades, researchers have argued that information systems development methodologies (ISDM) help practitioners improve both the process and product of systems development (Blum, 1994), whereas others have questioned whether the methodology movement has achieved these goals (for example, see Fitzgerald, 1994). CASE tools provide automated support for systems development methodologies (Folkes & Stubenvoll, 1994). As with the methods they automate, arguments have been made that CASE tools increase developer productivity and bring efficiency and quality improvements to the development process and its product (Banker & Kauffman, 1991; Parkinson, 1990). Nevertheless, these claims have not been entirely vindicated, and questions have been raised as to the benefits that accrue from the adoption of CASE in organizations (Kemerer, 1992; Sumner & Ryan, 1994).

Researchers argue that the development process is not well understood (Lewis, 1994; Butler & Fitzgerald, 1997); unfortunately, the same is said of CASE-enabled systems development (Guinan etal., 1997). Certainly, the mixed findings of extant research on CASE adoption and use support this argument. Much of this research has been survey based and falls prey to criticisms such as those levelled at similar research conducted on other aspects of systems development (for examples, see Markus & Robey, 1988; Orlikowski, 1993; Cavaye, 1995). Qualitative interpretive research approaches are therefore indicated as appropriate to capture the ‘rich picture’ of systems development in organizations (Cavaye, 1995; Myers, 1995; cf. Butler & Fitzgerald, 1997; Butler, 1998a,b). Accordingly, this study adopts an interpretive, casebased research strategy and uses and extends the model and framework of ‘CASE adoption and use’ proposed by Orlikowski (1993) to guide both the research endeavour and the presentation of its findings. This approach facilitates the attainment of the research objective, which is to investigate the organizational content, contexts and processes surrounding the successful implementation and use of CASE in organizations.

After the brief literature review in the next section, the research objective is outlined and research questions posed. The interpretive research approach used to attain this objective and to answer these questions is then described. The case report is presented and an analysis of the findings follows in which an extended version of Orlikowski’s (1993) framework is used with associated narratives to provide in-depth descriptions of the salient issues found to be pertinent to CASE adoption and use in the organization studied. The final section then discusses the study’s findings and conclusions and ends with several recommendations for research and practice.

## A REVIEW OF RESEARCH ON THE INTRODUCTION AND BENEFITS OF CASE FOR INFORMATION SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT

The challenges that confront practitioners in the development of organizational information systems have prompted many to seek means to improve the productivity, quality and rigour of the systems development process and its product. CASE tools have long been considered to be a potential vehicle for easing systems development-related problems and bottlenecks (Banker & Kauffman, 1991; Orlikowski, 1993). A recent IFIP (WG 8.1) working conference on the systems development process emphasized the importance of CASE tools and delineated the contribution they can make to the development process (Prakash etal., 1993). Accordingly, it was pointed out that the development process has become strongly influenced by the use of CASE (Györkös & Rozman, 1993). Are these claims for the importance and utility of CASE in the development process valid? The following brief review of research on CASE offers mixed results and suggests that such claims appear to have little basis in fact.

Designing large-scale complex information systems that are free of problems demands a systematic approach (Song & Osterweil, 1994). Wood-Harper etal. (1985) argue that the systematic analysis and design of an information system requires a systems development methodology. The role of CASE is to give automated support to such a methodology (Folkes & Stubenvoll, 1994). According to Jayaratna (1994), over 1000 brand name methodologies are in use all over the world. Correspondingly, since the introduction of CASE in the mid-1980s, the number of CASE tools has proliferated; in 1990, for example, Parkinson (1991) estimated that there were in excess of 1000 in existence. As with claims for the methodologies which they automate, Aaen etal. (1992) report that improved standardization, more efficient systems development procedures and improved systems quality resulted from CASE use. Banker & Kauffman (1991) found that CASE tools increase developer productivity (see also Norman & Nunamaker, 1989; Orlikowski, 1993; Finlay & Mitchell, 1994; Dahanayake et al., 1996). In addition, CASE use is reported to have a positive influence on both the development function within organizations, by providing productivity and quality enhancements to the development process, and on individual developers, by increasing their effectiveness and engendering the development of positive attitudes (Finlay & Mitchell, 1994). Other touted benefits of CASE include its ability to facilitate software re-engineering and reuse and in its provision of an adaptable and consistent development platform for developers (Norman & Nunamaker, 1989; Iivari, 1996).

Although CASE has promised much, research has shown that it has failed to deliver on its promises. Folkes & Stubenvoll (1992), for example, indicate that the reported benefits of CASE are not being realized; they cite the Butler Cox Report to illustrate that the introduction of

CASE in organizations has led to a reduction in the quality of systems produced. Recent research also lends support to the view that practitioners are disillusioned with CASE; Kemerer (1992) reports that 70% of CASE tools are not used 1 year after their introduction and only 5% are widely used, but not to their full capacity. Recent studies indicate that the situation may not be as bad as Kemerer suggests; nevertheless, this body of research indicates that about one-third of all organizations who adopt CASE abandon its use within 2years of acquiring it (Isoda etal., 1995; Rader etal., 1995). Possible reasons for this are indicated by Sumner & Ryan (1994), who, on the basis of a survey of CASE users, argue that development activities such as logical modelling, prototyping, generating design documentation, creating modular program design specifications and design training are not made easier by the use of CASE tools. These findings led Sumner & Ryan (1994, p. 19) to suggest that ‘CASE is simply another technology which automates a series of isolated systems design tasks, without addressing the underlying need for improving the systems design process.’ Obviously, these conflicting conclusions in research on the topic make it difficult to decide whether CASE has had an overall beneficial or detrimental effect on the structure and quality of the development process and its product. Guinan etal. (1997) provide some insights as to why this may be so. They argue that the use of CASE in itself makes very little difference, related factors such as training, project size, design quality and the integrated use of CASE tools all contribute to the success of CASE adoption and use. In addition, Lorin (1996) outlines several obstacles to the adoption and implementation of CASE, namely CASE tool scalability, organizational problems, developer skills and competencies, up-front cost and proof of payback; these factors are argued by Lorin (1996) to mitigate against the successful adoption and use of CASE tools. ## Research rationale, objective and questions

It is clear that in order to resolve the contradictory findings of previous research a comprehensive and unequivocal description of the organizational content, contexts and processes that lead to the successful implementation and use of CASE is required. This task is confounded by the manner in which CASE as an information technology in itself has evolved since the early 1990s; hence, it may be that problems and deficiencies of a technical nature with earlier CASE products may not now exist. There is also the relative effectiveness of upper and lower CASE in solving ‘wicked problems’ (Post etal., 1999); Lorin (1996) argues that the payback from lower CASE tools do not scale in the short term, and that the payback from upper CASE tools, which focus on analysis, design and detailed design, may be much greater – but here again there is the problem of delayed payback. With few exceptions (for examples, see Banker & Kauffman, 1991; Orlikowski, 1993; Finlay & Mitchell, 1994; Post etal., 1999), there is little evidence in previous research of an effort to differentiate between different types of CASE tools [e.g. upper, lower and integrated CASE (I-CASE)], to say nothing of identifying tool functionality, particular vendors and so on. Taken together, then, all this is suggestive of the need for further in-depth empirical research. Orlikowski (1993) argued that empirical research of a qualitative nature was required to contribute to a deeper understanding of the phenomenon and to verify her grounded theory-based CASE research framework – to a large extent, this call remains unanswered. Although Orlikowski (1993) did not explicitly call for such, a constructivist approach to research captures holistically the content, context and process of the socially constructed reality that is organizational life (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Butler, 1998a,b); this study adopts such an approach.

It has been suggested that studies of the organizational consequences of information technology should not rely on simple causal models; rather, theoretical models that capture the social and technical complexity of the phenomenon and which emphasize ‘empirical fidelity are advocated (Markus & Robey, 1988; Robey & Azevedo, 1994). Chervany (1973) argues that empirical investigations of IS-related problems require a research framework that aids in the identification and examination of a phenomenon’s various dimensions and provides a structure for correlating and synthesizing the results of independent research. Furthermore, Teng & Galletta (1991) maintain that a young field, such as IS, requires ‘pretheory’ frameworks to guide research activities while en route to theory development. Although these arguments provide explicit support for the use of a framework such as that proposed by Orlikowsk (1993), a philosophical argument that is congruent with this study’s ontological and epistemological stance is also forthcoming. For example, constructivist philosophers such as

Heidegger (1976) and Gadamer (1975) comment on the importance of preunderstanding in guiding the investigation of social phenomena. In a research context, this preunderstanding allows a researcher to form hypotheses or expectations regarding the phenomenon so that questions may be posed and a dialogue entered into with the object in order to confirm, modify or refute such expectations (Butler, 1998a,b). Orlikowski’s (1993) model of the process and context of CASE adoption and use in organizations contributes to this researcher’s preunderstanding of the phenomenon as it captures suitably the complex socially constructed nature of CASE adoption and use.

This study’s primary objective is to investigate the organizational content, contexts and processes surrounding the successful implementation and use of CASE in one organization and to document the issues that lead to this success. Here, success is defined in terms of the continued and widespread productive use of the technology in the organization concerned. Given this study’s primary objective, and considering the model of Orlikowski (1993) and the findings of previous research, the three following research questions (RQ) act to guide this investigation.

• RQ1. What are the critical issues that IS managers and practitioners must pay particular attention to in the adoption, implementation and use of CASE?

• RQ2. Does the use of CASE have an overall positive or negative effect on the process and product of systems development?

• RQ3. What types of CASE technologies are capable of delivering these benefits?

The following section proposes a constructivist research approach for the study of the phenomenon.

## A CONSTRUCTIVIST RESEARCH APPROACH

Systems development is, in essence, a multidimensional change process that presents itself simultaneously within several related social environments or contexts – as a reality it is socially constructed (Visala, 1991; cf. Orlikowski, 1993). Furthermore, these environments give rise to a complex web of social conditions and factors that shape and influence the process and its product (Kling & Scacchi, 1982). The fundamental perspective of constructivist philosophy posits that reality is socially constructed (Berger & Luckmann, 1966; Connolly & Keutner, 1988); hence, research into social phenomena should be interpretivist in orientation so that contextualized meaning can be revealed in order to enable socially based phenomena to be understood (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). This paper on the adoption and use of CASE in Telecom Éireann is informed by the constructivist research paradigm of Guba & Lincoln (1994). Accordingly, it uses a hermeneutic research method, in conjunction with the qualitative research techniques advocated by the constructivist paradigm.

Hermeneutics is defined as the theory or philosophy of the interpretation of meaning (Bleicher, 1980). Interpretation here involves entering into the interpretative norms of a community; while meaning here operates and is to be found within the historical contexts of the interpreter and interpreted (Gadamer, 1975; Butler, 1998a). Following Connolly & Keutner (1988), Butler (1998a,b) argues that the philosophies of Heidegger (1976) and Gadamer (1975) are constructivist; Guba & Lincoln (1994) have also appropriated this label for their research paradigm in the social sciences. As Table 2 illustrates, the constructivist ontological and epistemological perspectives of Guba & Lincoln (1994) accord well with the phenomenological hermeneutics of Heidegger (1976) and Gadamer (1975). However, while Guba & Lincoln (1994) advocate the use of the hermeneutic method, they fail to offer a description of an interpretive research method that draws on the tenets of hermeneutic philosophy. This study applies the hermeneutic method articulated by Butler (1998a,b), in that it incorporates the hermeneutic ‘circle of understanding’ and the Socratic, Hegelian and reductionist/analytical dialectics as its prime methodological techniques. The basic form of communication among social actors is the personal narrative (Bruner, 1990); hence, Ricoeur (1981) argues that social action and situations can be read as texts. ## Constructivism and phenomenological hermeneutics in the IS field

Boland (1985) was one of the first within the IS field to specifically advocate phenomenological hermeneutics as a valid interpretive approach for research on the phenomenon of information systems development. Myers (1995), Westrup (1994) and Visala (1991) have also recommended that hermeneutic philosophy inform research in this area, whereas Lee (1993, 1994) has championed the use of hermeneutics in broader research contexts within the field. Therefore, following research that began with Boland (1985) and Winograd & Flores (1986), and more recently with Introna (1997), this study adopted the phenomenological perspective of Heidegger (1976) and integrated it with the hermeneutic philosophy of Gadamer (1975) in order to provide a philosophical basis for its constructivist research approach. ## Implementation of the Research Strategy

In keeping with the tenets of the constructivist paradigm, a qualitative case-based research strategy was adopted; this approach involved an exploratory single-case study (Stake, 1994). Purposeful sampling was used throughout (Marshall & Rossman, 1989; Patton, 1990). The case design used has been described by Yin (1989) as ‘posthoc longitudinal research’. The investigation of the selected case was conducted through the use of individual interview and documentary sources over an initial period of 1month in 1996, with a subsequent follow-up study of the company-wide implementation of the I-CASE workbench some 2years later. Some 19 interviews were conducted with IS managers, IT professionals in the organization’s quality unit, the project manager of the rapid application development (RAD) team, and the analysts, programmers and user representative who constituted the team who used the I-CASE workbench to develop the system concerned. The general interview guide approach was chosen as being the most appropriate for this particular study – here, a semistructured interview strategy was chosen (Patton, 1990) – and each interview was up to 2hours in length. Additional data collection methods used included documentary evidence and informal participant observation and discussion. During the data analysis phase, the hermeneutic techniques of the ‘circle of understanding’ and the Socratic, Hegelian and reductionist/analytic dialectics were operationalized using the data analysis methods of content and constant comparative analysis (see Patton, 1990; Calloway & Ariv, 1991). Elements of Orlikowski’s (1993) model were used as ‘seed categories’ to prime the hermeneutic ‘preunderstanding of the phenomenon in order to identify and understand the ‘whole-part’ relationships that emerged from the interview transcripts and other documentary sources. The technique of data reduction and display was also used to meta-analyse the qualitative data (see Miles & Huberman, 1994). Finally, the case report approach was used to write up the research findings.

Being a member of the organization chosen for study, the author was what Bødker & Pedersen (1991) have termed a ‘cultural insider’. Hence, as a member of the general business constituency and an IT professional, he was intimate with several of the subuniverses of reality that comprised the overall institutional reality (see Berger & Luckmann, 1966). This provided ‘human instrument’ of Lincoln & Guba (1985) with valuable insights into the organization’s culture and climate, and this greatly aided in the interpretation of the case.

## A CASE STUDY OF THE INTRODUCTION OF CASE FOR RAPID APPLICATION DEVELOPMENT IN TELECOM ÉIREANN

The organization chosen for study is Telecom Éireann (now eircom), the Republic of Ireland’s national telecommunications company (telco). It is a state-owned company with two minority shareholders: Telia, a Swedish telco, and KPN, a Dutch telco. Telecom Éireann provides universal telecommunications services within Ireland, and presently enjoys a monopoly in many of its service areas. There are over 10 companies in the Telecom Group, the majority of whom are wholly owned subsidiaries. As of 1999, Telecom employed approximately 11500 staff. In 1994–5, the company formulated its plans to transform its core operational business processes under the umbrella of the planned Operational Support System (OSS). Telecom’s IS function was charged with the development of an ambitious portfolio of information systems which were to constitute the OSS, one of these systems is the Generic Appointment System (GAS).

## An overview of the generic appointment system

The Generic Appointment System grew out of a business need in one key area of the Telecom Éireann’s operations – its telephone repair service. Business managers across the organization recognized the need to introduce efficiencies into the manner in which repair service workloads were managed and associated service appointments made with customers. It was hoped that the introduction of this information system would eliminate the occurrence of unproductive visits by operational staff to customer premises when customers were absent. The GAS would also assist supervisors in their task of allocating workloads to their repair teams, which consist of operational staff. Both groups therefore had a keen interest in the development and implementation of this system as it had an impact upon some of their basic functions. The GAS also supports the operation of the company’s 10 fault-handling and repair centres. As a distributed IS, the GAS is a client–server application using eight Oracle relational data bases that serve up to 180 windows-based PC terminals in fault-handling centres around Ireland, and a further 400 in operational depots nationwide. The CASE-supported RAD approach saw development take place within the 3-month time period set for the project; however, the implementation of the first phase of the GAS took a further 6months and was completed in late 1996. The GAS project came in on time and on budget.

## The GAS development project as a pilot study for the IS function’s implementation of the information engineering facility’s I-CASE workbench

Although the business management within the company had articulated a need for a customer appointments system, the chief stimulus for the development of the GAS arose out of an internal need within the IS function. In response to the challenge posed by the development of the OSS, senior IS managers recognized that development teams needed to produce modular maintainable systems in a rapid manner. IS managers also wanted to maximize the productivity of a fixed developer resource and skillset and to maintain product quality. Although the IS function had a brief but unproductive experience with structured systems and design method (SSADM), the return to ad hoc development practices was equally unsatisfactory. That said, the GAS project manager reported that because of a range of development-related issues support grew for the acquisition and use of a ‘common/generic development environment that we could use, cradle to grave, to give a total life cycle coverage.’ Subsequent to a rigorous vetting procedure by the IS function’s quality team, a computer-aided systems engineering (CASE) workbench called the information engineering facility (IEF) was acquired from Texas Instruments to meet this requirement.

IEF is an integrated CASE (I-CASE) workbench that operationalizes and automates the associated information engineering methodology (Martin, 1990) using a set of tightly integrated tools. Essentially, it provides architectural (planning), conceptual (structured design modelling), external (prototyping), implementation (procedure writing, code generation and hardware) and execution (integration of the product into hardware, target data bases, existing applications and data networks) layers that map onto the information engineering (IE)

methodology (Texas Instruments, 1992). Besides the productivity improvements promised by IEF, IS function managers also viewed the introduction of IEF as a means to improve the quality of the development process and product and to quickly re-engineer and maintain development applications. Although initial tests of IEF were favourable, IS managers were mindful of the function’s previous experience with SSADM; a rigorous test of IEF’s capabilities and suitability to purpose was deemed necessary in order to assess its potential fully. There was also the question of the cost of the investment, which was estimated at £250000 for the planned company-wide implementation of IEF. Hence, a decision was made to use IEF to develop rapidly an information system from the existing backlog of projects using a minimum of licenced IEF-based workstations. A senior IS manager, who was responsible for the bulk of systems development at the time and who championed CASE use, selected ‘a small, noncritical project’, the generic appointments system, to test fully the systems development capabilities of IEF (from conceptual to execution layers) within a real development environment and with a minimum of cost. A 3-month deadline was set for the development of the project’s core requirements within one operational area; full corporate-wide implementation would then be effected at a later date.

## ANALYSIS OF THE RESEARCH FINDINGS

This study uses Orlikowski’s (1993) Process Model of Organisational Change Around CASE Tools to provide insights into and report on the present interpretivist study on the introduction, adoption and use of CASE in Telecom Éireann. Orlikowski’s model (see Figure 1) describes the complex web of influences between process and context in CASE adoption and use and captures the effect that such factors have on the various participants in the adoption and use process. For example, the external environmental, organizational and IS contexts are all argued to influence IS managers’ attitudes toward and expectations of CASE. These individuals in turn formulate IS function policy on the adoption and use of CASE with reference to the aforementioned contexts. Orlikowski (1993) maintains that this leads to significant process and context change around CASE adoption and use. The outcomes of CASE adoption and use are, in turn, dependent on the preceding actions, policies and practices and, consequently, exert a considerable influence on the social contexts mentioned. Table A1 in Appendix A arrays the model’s descriptive categories into a framework that provides a synopsis of the research findings under each of the categories indicated by the model. Extended narratives are utilized to describe particular aspects of the introduction of I-CASE referenced in Tables A1 and 3 that have been found to be relevant in this organization and which help to answer the research questions posed.

## Critical issues in the adoption and use process in Telecom Éireann (RQ1)

Table3 provides an overview of the issues that help answer research question 1. The following narratives explore these issues under the general headings of project team selection, the development approach used, group commitment and buy-in, and vendor support in the adoption and use process.

## Selection and training of the rapid application development team

A development project team was chosen with the special needs of this development endeavour in mind, i.e. team members were selected on the basis of perceived disposition towards issues of demarcation and previously observed commitment to conflict avoidance. As the GAS was to be integrated with the existing Fault Handling System (FHS), two experienced analysts with prior experience in development of the FHS were chosen, along with a programmer who had worked on a related project. Both analysts possessed experience and skills in such diverse areas as traditional systems analysis and design, programming, computer hardware and technical aspects of network communications. As with all IS development within the organization, a user project manager and a user representative were appointed to participate in the development of this IS; these individuals had a wide range of experience in the operational areas encompassed by the proposed system. User groups provided pools of experienced users, from the operational areas of interest, to participate with the development team so as to generate, refine and validate the user requirements and system prototypes. The impact of this participative approach to development, which involved a combination of both joint application development (JAD) and participatory design (PD), was commented on by the development project manager, who stated that ‘the appointments system development was really driven by the user group.’ Thus, the careful selection of team members allowed IS managers to eliminate the possibility that the pilot testing of what was an extremely expensive investment would not fail as a result of poor selection of the project team. Once the team was selected, 3 weeks of on-site training took place with a consultant from the I-CASE vendor, Texas Instruments, who remained on site for the 3-month project. Intensive hands-on training was afforded to all team members, including the user representative; nevertheless, steep learning curves (see Lorin, 1996) were encountered and continued to mark the team’s learning experience with IEF.

## On the rapid application development (RAD) approach used

The development approach adopted was consistent with extant RAD approaches described in the literature, namely a RAD approach uses small autonomous teams in prototypingenabled joint application development with user participation (Subramanian & Zarnich, 1996). The IEF development environment supports a structured approach to development built around the systems development life cycle (SDLC) approach. However, the GAS project team adopted a customized RAD approach for the development of the appointments system. Basically, prototyping was the primary mechanism used to refine user requirements and to design system functionality; developers omitted certain design procedures embodied within IEF deemed not to be relevant. This prototyping approach worked well with IEF and was approved of by the IEF consultant, who was a member of the project team. However, the project manager experienced some difficulties with the project management of the I-CASE-enabled RAD approach, namely ‘a structured approach to project management along the lines of the SDLC didn’t work . . . it didn’t match what [the developers] were doing in reality on the ground’ . . . ‘We found on the GAS that anything that took longer than one week to track was too hard to control. We need to break down the task into smaller modules, but we found that that was not possible.’ Hence, the enhanced creativity that the I-CASE tools brought to the development process caused project management headaches. Essentially, the project manager found it difficult to estimate and manage development activities that were evolutionary and creative in nature – here, the difficulty centred mainly on the use of user-led prototyping.

## The development of a consensus approach to CASE use

According to all members of the development team, the project was well managed, coordinated and controlled; a strong positive relationship was in evidence between the development project manager and the development team. The project manager realized that because existing development-related roles were heavily demarcated within the IS function, there was the potential for intrateam conflict to develop between analysts and programmers on the one hand and between the participating user and developers on the other. Before the commencement of training on IEF, the project manager met with the proposed team to develop a consensus approach to CASE use. Whereas the analysts and programmers had their own areas of speciality, and were expected to contribute accordingly, there was to be no specialization in development-related activities associated with the use of IEF; as the project manager put it, ‘the difficulty was going to be that if team members are working on the same project, doing the same development tasks and getting different pay . . . and if objections are raised and normal procedures had to be adhered to, then, for example, analysts would have to write a spec for the programmers, etc.; however, analysts could write pseudo-code in IEF faster . . . I couldn’t ask them to do that.’ The consensus approach applied to the user representative also because he was a highly experienced technician and was therefore expected to participate fully with the developers in almost all aspects of systems development. In fact, it was he who was responsible for the technical implementation of the GAS. Hence, as one team member put it, ‘we developers came together and agreed a common approach to the system’s development . . . no “us and them”, either within the team or between developers and users . . . nevertheless, it must be said that IEF has the potential for conflict.’ It was clear that the consensus approach adopted by the development project manager and his team created a development climate and subculture that effectively mitigated any problems that may have arisen because of ‘demarcation’ issues between analysts and programmers when using IEF, as such issues marked much of the existing organizational culture and climate in relation to staff practices.

## On the importance of vendor support

The need for comprehensive vendor support also figured highly in this project. Training aside, vendor assistance was required throughout this project in both support and troubleshooting functions; for example, a joint vendor–developer approach was taken in the analysis and design stage because of the difficulty developers had in translating the results of the requirements analysis interviews into the data and process models required by the I-CASE platform. The client–server architecture used was also new to developers and problems existed with: (i) the integration of the data base server for the GAS and the existing fault handling system; (ii) the transmission protocols supported by the CASE environment; and (iii) difficulties with optimizing client–server performance. The solutions to these problems warranted a significant level of vendor support as the I-CASE workbench’s support for proprietary standards was less than satisfactory and needed an experienced hand to ‘make things work like it said in the manual.’ One developer commented on this; ‘the signalling protocols were obstacles, in that one was DEC’s, the other IEF’s, at the end of the day we had 3–4weeks of very technical assistance from TI to get the systems to work together . . . now that we are aware of the type of problems that can arise, we can cut down on the time spent resolving them in future by getting vendor advice in advance.’ It is clear that the significant technical obstacles that arose would not have been difficult to overcome for novice CASE users without vendor assistance.

## Assessing the benefits: the impact and outcome of I-CASE adoption and use (RQ2)

As the analysis on the consequences for adopting and using CASE tools in Table A1 indicates, the introduction and use of IEF I-CASE environment elicited positive reactions from developers, IS managers and users alike. Similarly, the promise of increased developer productivity and process and product improvements within the context of a RAD-based development project were also delivered upon in the GAS project. For example, one developer stated that ‘the IEF development environment was absolutely superb, there was enormous savings in terms of coding, and programme management was enabled’; yet another stated that CASE ‘afforded better communication with the users through its modelling and prototyping facilities’ and the resultant system ‘was more maintainable, better documented, better developed because of the support IEF offered.’ This developer also stated that ‘[IEF was] very powerful and versatile in the area [of data and process modelling] as models are built with an integrity that was maintained throughout.’ The importance of such a structured, yet flexible, development tool was proven when developers accomplished a major change to the functionality of the GAS in a record time – ‘it was a credit to the power of IEF that the changes demanded by the unions were accomplished in just one day.’ Nevertheless, one developer pointed out that although ‘the CASE workbench went from systems analysis to code generation really well, however, [it was a weakness that] the requirements analysis and its documentation were not addressed or integrated into the CASE workbench.’ This statement points to a potential weakness of CASE, one not easily resolved, as the project manager stated, because the translation of written requirements into modelling conventions has always plagued analysts – ‘no tool is going to do that for you, it’s all down to the experience of the analyst and his ability to interpret what users’ requirements are.

In 1998, some 2 years after the initial study, a follow-up investigation revealed that IEF had been adopted as the IS function’s application development environment (ADE) of choice fo in-house ‘bespoke’ systems development within the organization; and for the OSS in particular. Thus, the initial management goal of introducing a function-wide development environment was said to realized. IS managers interviewed stated that the subsequent roll-out of IEF across the IS function was facilitated by the lessons learned from the adoption and use IEF on the GAS project. In addition, two analysts on the GAS/IEF project were promoted to the position of project manager as part of a strategy to integrate their knowledge and experience from the use of IEF in the pilot project to the development of new systems within the organization using the I-CASE workbench. An IS function manager involved in the selection and introduction of IEF stated that, ‘without IEF or COOL:Gen, or whatever they are calling it now – by the way, it’s not called CASE anymore, now it’s application development environments or ADEs – re-engineering the fault handling system and integrating the GAS fully into the new version of the [FHS] could have been a monumental task.’ He added that ‘if we hadn’t gone down the IEF road when we did, we would have been in a right mess. With the shortage of staff here and the number of new systems we have to implement, the transformation of the company would have been slowed down considerably and IT would have had to shoulder the blame. This IT professional pointed out that because of idiosyncratic business requirements the IS function had to build proprietary systems, and would be using COOL:Gen, Sterling Software’s new IEF-based ADE, for these projects. He also indicated that other approaches to application development and procurement were adopted, VIZ ‘we’ve also gone to Telia and KPN to transfer in some of their systems, and we’ll be using SAP and other canned software for support activities such as logistics and financial reporting.’

IS managers and developers in Telecom are of the opinion that the tight integration of IEF’s tools and facilities has led to maintenance costs being trimmed significantly as a result of the capabilities of IEF to cascade changes made at early stages of the development life cycle through to the finished product. Developers in the GAS project stated that IEF’s test features also added greatly to the relative ease with which post-development changes were made to the system. In other development projects, including the evolution of the GAS, IEF’s capabilities have been argued to have a considerable impact upon lowering post-development costs. It was also reported by IS managers that the important productivity gains which accrued through CASE in the GAS project (where a fully developed system was produced within a 3- month period) were replicated in subsequent projects. So too were the improvements in user–developer communication experienced during the development of the GAS. Overall, IT professionals were of the opinion that CASE helped them produce systems of a higher quality and which require less maintenance; they also said that it allowed systems to evolve and to be re-engineered to meet changing business needs.

## I-CASE tools that increase developer productivity and associated benefits (RQ3)

The effectiveness of the structured methodology embodied in the IEF CASE workbench, coupled with its prototyping ability, won the praise of all members of the development team: ‘the methodology does enforce a rigorous approach to development, it was really good.’ In the previous section, IS managers and developers attested to the benefits that accrued from CASE use on the GAS project. Although the data and process modelling tools won special praise, the prototyping techniques supported by IEF were universally acclaimed as being critical to the success of the GAS development project. For example, IEF’s prototyping capabilities proved to be invaluable as, in the words of one developer, ‘the effort put into the requirements analysis was too restricted . . . the prototyping facility allowed us to compensate for this . . . [it] gave users a sense of presence and ownership of the system . . . they could actually see the changes they recommended.’ According to the user representative, who actively used the CASE tools, ‘what was perceived as a requirement, quite often was not, prototyping drove [the actual requirements] out . . . what we started out with and ended up with was totally different’. However, as another developer commented, ‘the prototype couldn’t functionally do anything . . . it was very good in a cognitive sense, but we had to describe how the data flows would take place’; this was perceived as being a drawback, but not significant so as to detract from the overall effectiveness of the prototyping approach adopted. The scripting tool that allowed developers to write pseudo-code and the code generator which integrates the output from this tool and the data modelling, process modelling, and prototyping tools were also considered to be key components of IEF. To avoid the undesirable consequences of developers handcrafting generated code, and thus threatening the integrity of a particular software version and losing the benefits of CASE in terms of documentation and version control, the project manager put a policy in place to the effect that any and all changes would be made before the code generation stage. Despite the positive comments on the capabilities of IEF’s code generator, developers pointed out that the code generated might not be optimal for all applications. A case in point here was that while IEF was being used by an Israeli software vendor to develop a mainframe-based application for Telecom Éireann developers had to handcraft the code because database performance factors mandated high-speed access and retrieval of data, something that ${ \mathsf { I E F } } { \mathsf { s } }$ code generator could not guarantee. Thus, developers and IS managers were all too aware that the structure and efficiency of the code output from IEF’s code generator might not be suitable for all applications. The technical problems that arose in the GAS project also called into question ${ \mathsf { I E F s } }$ ability to support proprietary network protocols and different database management systems. On a positive note, IEF’s test and debug tool won special praise from experienced developers, who noted how quickly problems were identified and resolved.

In the final analysis, it was IEF’s tool integration that was commented on by developers and IS managers; tight integration of tools that implemented the IE methodology was, according to practitioners, IEF’s major strength.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

Previous research has cast doubt on the benefits of CASE use. The present interpretivist investigation joins several recent studies – both quantitative and qualitative – in arguing that CASE has the potential to improve developer productivity and the quality of both the process and product of systems development (for examples, see Norman & Nunamaker, 1989; Finlay & Mitchell, 1994; Dahanayake etal., 1996; Iivari, 1996). It was seen that a multitude of contextual factors exerts both positive and negative influences on the ability of developers to realize these benefits. For example, in accordance with Bruner’s (1990) observations on the formation and influence of social actors’ subjective ‘intentional states’, it was observed that IS manager and developer attitudes towards and commitment to the use of I-CASE were shaped by cultural influences emanating from the immediate IS context (for example, the project management style that incorporated a consensus approach to I-CASE use by analysts, programmer and user representative), from the organizational context (for example, the participative policy on decision making and the use of JAD and PD approaches, previous experience with SSADM), and from the environmental context (for example, the support and advice obtained from the I-CASE vendor). This accords well with model proposed by Orlikowsk (1993). One point worth noting, however, is that because the development team was specially selected to participate in what was, in essence, a pilot study on I-CASE adoption and use, it is difficult to predict the impact that other less well-disposed or skilled developers would have had in a project such as this. Following Orlikowski (1993), it is not unreasonable to surmise, though, that developer attitudes and cultural influences within an organization would be significant determinants in achieving the benefits associated with CASE use. This proved to be the case as IEF and later COOL:Gen (IEF’s newest variant) were formally adopted by the IS function for use in all ‘bespoke’ in-house development; it was clear, also, that the CASE implementation process benefited from the lessons learned in the pilot project as IS managers used them to mitigate potential negative contextual influences. In addition, the benefits attributed to CASE use in the pilot project supported what was a significant investment in IEF (over £250000, according to one IS manager) by the IS function in 1996–7; this investment was expanded significantly when the IS function decided to migrate to COOL:Gen.

## Beyond Orlikowski’s model of CASE adoption and use

Although the model proposed by Orlikowski (1993) provides an adequate framework from which to conceptualize CASE adoption and use, this study illustrates that it requires extension (see Table3) in order to describe explicitly important characteristics of the phenomenon. In response to the research questions posed, previous sections have highlighted several salient issues found to be critical for CASE adoption and use. It was seen, for example, that the prototyping tools were central to the successful use of the I-CASE workbench within the context of the RAD development approach adopted (cf. Agresti, 1986). Team selection and make-up were also important. Developers found that the tools provided to implement the IEbased data and process modelling were easy to use and well integrated with the other CASE tools in the workbench. The flexibility and ease of use of the scripting tool and code generator drew special praise from developers and end-users alike; so too did the testing and trace tool (cf. Post etal., 1999). However, on the down side, developers found that translating the analysis findings to CASE modelling conventions problematic and would have preferred CASE support in this area also. Finally, there were several technical issues with the CASE-developed end-product that required significant problem solving on behalf of both analysts and CASE vendor alike. All in all, the web of relationships that relates context to process, and which are mapped by Orlikowski’s model, were very much in evidence. The analytical framework presented in Tables3 and A1, coupled with the sections which a nswer the research questions, reveal that significant reciprocal influences existed between the external, organizational and IS contexts and social actors involved in the process of CASE adoption and use. Nevertheless, in the present paper, these issues are described with a finer degree of granularity than in the original study by Orlikowski (1993), this should render the study’s findings more accessible to practitioners and researchers.

## Radical and incremental change around CASE

It is clear that in Telecom Éireann the nature of change associated with CASE was radical in terms of the pilot project, but incremental in terms IEF’s roll-out across the division responsible for bespoke IS development. Unlike the rationale for CASE adoption in Orlikowski’s two organizations, IS managers in Telecom intended to radically change the process by which systems were developed, reduce costs and increase productivity, but only in specific areas. Other elements of Telecom’s IT strategy also addressed the productivity problem, namely through the outsourcing of IS development projects, the use of customizable application software, and the introduction of ERP products such as Systemanalyse and Programmentwicklung (SAP). One of the major concerns of IS managers in Telecom here was to ensure that post-development modifications and maintenance activities for in-house developed ‘bespoke’ systems would be performed rapidly and with a minimum of effort; this applied also to outsourced applications developed using IEF.

The well-articulated fears that technological tools such as CASE (see Cooley, 1987) would result in the deskilling of developers did not appear to have an effect on the CASE-related attitudes of IS managers, analysts or programmers in this company. It was clear from events in Telecom Éireann that developers were keen to ease the burden of pressures put on them by the organizational requirement to develop an ambitious portfolio of complex systems in a rapid manner; they seemed unconcerned by a possible loss of skills. Rather, programmers, whose skills appeared to be most under threat, welcomed the opportunity to broaden their development-related knowledge and competencies and perform analysis and design activities – this was true of analysts also, who saw an opportunity to participate throughout the development process and exercise more control over the attributes of the eventual product. In many respects, one of the unintended consequences of the adoption and use of CASE was to have developers focus more on the area of analysis and design which, according to Jarke & Pohl (1993) and Jayaratna (1990), is the source of many of the problems associated with the development process. Nevertheless, a down side for developers is the likelihood that their mobility across the IT industry could be limited if they are tied to a particular CASE tool and if they have been unable to develop a broader skills base. However, because of the limitations of CASE code generators to produce tight efficient code, the demand for programming skills is unlikely to decline, as is the demand for IT professionals, which was not as strong in the early 1990s when much previous qualitative research on the topic was conducted.

## Limitations and future research

One of the obvious limitations of this study is that it is confined to one case; however, Stake (1994), Mintzberg (1979) and Lincoln & Guba (1985) point out that much can be learned from a single case, especially when refining theory or in helping to establish the limits of generalizability. Constructivists do not use the latter concept; instead, they focus on transferring the findings to other social contexts. Accordingly, the transferability of this study’s research findings and conclusions is argued to be dependent on the ability of the researcher as a human instrument to capture the socially constructed reality of the organization under study. While the present case and its findings attempts to represent this ‘reality’, care needs to be exercised when interpreting and transferring this study’s results. That said, further empirical research is required to validate the extended framework and to indicate areas where alteration is required when it is applied in specific research contexts.

One final point of note is that the translation of requirements statements into data and process models is one area that CASE does not deal with effectively (a similar claim could be made in respect of object-oriented models). This is an interpretive process and calls on the experience and skill of seasoned analysts. It is worthwhile to suggest that case-based reasoning (CBR) technologies could be integrated into an I-CASE product to provide a knowledge base for the identification of data entities, processes and so on (for an overview of the use of CBR technologies in organizational contexts, see Davenport & Prusak, 1998). This would allow CASE to support what is a complex interpretive process.

In conclusion, then, the ‘rich picture’ offered in previous sections of this paper provides insights into the complex network of contextual influences at work in the adoption and use of CASE for systems development. Simplistic models of CASE adoption and use have not, in the past, fully captured the phenomenon in all its richness. The extended CASE research framework, as presented herein, provides an appropriate point of departure for future studies and helps contribute towards a cumulative body of knowledge on the phenomenon.

## Biography

Tom Butler is a College Lecturer in Management Information Systems at University College Cork (UCC). Formerly a telecommunications engineer with Telecom Éireann (now eircom), Tom has a wealth of practical experience in the IT industry. He holds an MSc in Information Systems from the National University of Ireland and is currently completing his PhD at UCC. His research interests encompass the design, development and implementation of IS and include such topics as the use of CASE, user participation, organizational change and the role of IT competencies in the provision of IT infrastructures. He is also interested in the application of interpretive methods for research in IS. His research has been published and presented at a number of international conferences and he has several book and journal publications.
