# Exploring the impact of formal training in ISD methods on the cognitive structure of novice information systems developers

Jungwoo Lee & Duane P. Truex\*

Department of Management, College of Business, University of Nevada, Las Vegas, Las Vegas, NV 89154–6009, USA, email: jungwoo@acm.org, and \*Department of Computer Information Systems, J. Mack. Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, PO Box 4015, Atlanta, Georgia 30302-4015, USA, and Leverhulme Fellow, Information Systems Research Centre, IS Institute, University of Salford, UK, email: dtruex@gsu.edu

Abstract. Hundreds of information systems development (ISD) methods exist at the present time, covering many different perspectives on the problem of systems development. There is a fairly consistent assumption running although debate on ISD methods; namely, that systems development is a cognitively intensive activity. But despite considerable study systems development remains a poorly understood activity. There is also evidence that, in practice, systems development projects are being approached from phenomenological pragmatism rather than from formally proposed teleological prescriptions, or ISD methods. That is, in practice ISD methods do not appear to be widely followed. This raises a question as to whether part of the reason lies in the training in formal ISD methods. Or phrased differently: what impact does formal development methods training have upon the cognitive development of systems developers. This research investigates one aspect of the relationship between the methodical training and the cognitive structure of novice systems developers, i.e. changes in a developer’s cognitive complexity. The study suggests a cognitive growth cycle experienced by systems developers that underlies an informed method usage cycle.

Keywords: Amethodical systems development, cognitive structure, development teams, methodical systems development, personal construct theory, repertory grid technique

## INTRODUCTION

As long as people have been building computerized information systems, there have been proposals as to how systems should be built. Hundreds of information systems development (ISD) methods exist at the present time (Hirschheim et al., 1995), covering many different perspectives of the problem of systems development. Even so, the field has not reached a consensus as to what constitutes a good development method or even how these development methods are actually being applied. Moreover, there is evidence that, in practice, systems development projects are being approached from phenomenological pragmatism, deviating from theoretically proposed teleological prescriptions (Introna, 1996; Fitzgerald, 1997). Thus, development methods, especially formalized ones, are sometimes criticized as being a ‘fetish of techniques’ (Wastell, 1996), just the ‘faking’ of a rational process (Parnas & Clements, 1986; Glass, 1994), or in the artificial imposition of unwanted restrictions on the design process (Russo & Stolterman, 1998).

This inability of formal methods to provide clear, consistent, universal and practical prescriptions for building successful information systems may be one of the reasons why the field still suffers from the ‘software crisis’ (Veldwijk et al., 1994) and makes it difficult to enforce the use of formal development methods within the developer community. The problem is further confounded because the field is experiencing a dramatic shift into object oriented, visual, and graphical programming environments and systems developers may soon be facing environments where systems are assembled from components delivered on the Internet (van den Goor et al., 1992; van Slooten & Brinkkemper, 1993; Welke, 1994; van Slooten, 1995). Clearly these changes present even greater challenges to formal ISD methods. The gap between the process and product of development (Floyd, 1987) and the discrepancy between theory and practice (Glass, 1995) remains and threatens to grow larger. Systems will be developed, some successfully but many as dramatic failures, unless we are provided with a clearer and deeper understanding as to how they are built.

There is a fairly consistent assumption running although debate on the alternative forms of ISD methods; namely, that systems development is a cognitively intensive activity performed by systems developers using tools and techniques (Brooks, 1986). Yet, one of the unanswered questions with regard to development methods is the impact of ISD methods knowledge has on the cognitive development of systems developers? We know little about the influence of methods training on systems developers’ thought processes. What are the most salient characteristics of systems developers who have been trained in a given ISD method? Are trained developers really different from non-trained developers, such as programmers who have not been trained in formal ISD methods? If so, how are they different? Do the thought processes of formally trained developers vary from those developers who do not have formal methods training? These questions are of critical importance in understanding as to how systems are built.

This research attempts to address these issues by investigating the relationship between training in ISD methods and changes in the cognitive structure of novice systems developers, i.e. changes in a developer’s cognitive complexity. This paper reports on an experiment designed to address this issue. The next section provides a brief theoretical background. It is followed by sections describing the experimental design and development of the instrument; the conduct of the experiment, and; the research findings. Finally, the paper concludes with a discussion of the results.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## Development methods and their emergent antitheses

While formal ISD approaches remain in the mainstream, a few researchers theorize that the underlying assumptions of formal methods may be flawed and may actually inhibit successful systems development in contemporary settings. Critics of formal ISD suggest that shortcomings of methods are inherent in the underlying philosophy of formal methods themselves. They argue it is because these methodical approaches are derived from the deterministic instrumental rationality in modern science (Truex & Klein, 1991; Hirschheim & Klein, 1992a; Wynekoop & Russo, 1993; Hirschheim et al., 1995; Introna, 1996; Truex et al., 1997). Because the environment is assumed to have a limited or ‘bounded’ set of conditions, applicable development methods are also assumed to have a bounded set of method components. These components may be assembled and reassembled for the situation at hand, based on preestablished principles (Kumar & Welke, 1992; Hirschheim etal., 1995). In reality, the limited or bounded set assumption is often violated. Because the organization and its environment are dynamic, development approaches need to allow dynamic adjustments such that the system always reflects the requisite variety needed to control dynamic system states (Ashby, 1957). An information system developed with rationalistic and teleological methods, with the systems requirements frozen early in the process, will naturally suppress the organization’s emergent behaviour (Kraft & Truex, 1994; Truex et al., 1999).

## Mental model theory and personal construct theory

Brooks (1987) suggests that the essence of systems development is a creative process, augmented by the intelligence of competent systems developers. This position is echoed by many others in the literature (Gladden, 1982; Giddings, 1984; Naur, 1985; Parnas & Clements, 1986; Glass, 1988; Jepsen et al., 1989; Couger, 1990; Sugiyama & Horowitz, 1991; Couger, 1993; Glass, 1994; Berry, 1995). Successful, creative developers clearly rise to this challenge. But most systems do not spontaneously arise from the creative energies of systems developers; design is a creative process informed by sets of cognitive frames, or structures formed through experience and perhaps formal training. Systems development is also a set of cognitive activities which systems developers perform for the purpose of transforming systems requirements into actual information systems, with the help of various tools and techniques in the process. Systems development is a complex and cognitively demanding activity. The crux of life-cycle approaches, and of structured design in general, is that methods allow the ‘off-loading’ of cognitive complexity. At the core of this process is the systems developer’s cognitive structure. Thus, the cognitive structure of the systems developer is among the most critical features in systems development. One might even argue that this cognitive structure governs the whole of the development process. If this is correct then understanding and influencing the developer’s cognitive structure is important in influencing the creation of an information system. Are those structures easily adjusted and if changed does it matter to the eventual implementation of information systems. What is the impact if any when we change a developer’s cognitive structure? To answer these important questions, two different but related theories are brought to this research: mental model theory and personal construct theory.

Mental model theory in cognitive science tells us that: (1) problem solvers build mental representations of a problem situation, rather than strictly applying rules or propositions, and that; (2) this mental representation is the basis for simulating and testing solution alternatives (Johnson-Laird, 1983). Therefore, it can be inferred that the process of systems development depends upon the developers’ ability to formulate a mental model quickly, correctly, and appropriately, and then to simulate and forecast likely outcomes against this mental model. Applying this theory to systems development process, one can then restate the systems development process as a series of cognitive activities whose purpose is to transform situational requirements into the form of an implemented information system. These activities occur regardless of whether systems developers are using methodical or alternative approaches. The importance of the developer’s mental model is also well recognized in the systems development area (Welke, 1983; Hirschheim & Klein, 1992b; Kendall & Kendall, 1993; Kendall & Kendall, 1994). However, the task of measuring mental models has been a challenging one, because these models often take multiple forms. These models are dynamic in nature, inherently latent, polymorphic, and situation specific. Moreover they are incomplete and evolve continuously based upon new inputs and feedback (Norman, 1983). They are often vaguely defined (Rouse & Morris, 1986) and there is no single agreed-upon measure of a mental model. Because individual mental models are difficult to observe directly, many researchers choose to base their conclusions on inferences from performance. They use think-aloud protocols, interviews, or multivariate statistical techniques to infer these mental models. Unfortunately there have been no extant instruments to aid in the capture of these models. Thus, developing an instrument to identify and describe a developer’s mental model has become a necessary element of this research.

To capture mental models, this research extends the theory of mental models to include personal construct theory (PCT). While mental model theory posits that each person builds and maintains mental models, it offers little guidance as to how they are built or what they look like. PCT addresses this gap in our understanding by showing that an individual’s perception of the world can be represented in the form of personal constructs against which one may evaluate current and anticipate future events (Kelly, 1955; Kelly, 1969). For a given and specific problem domain, critical elements (e.g. components of that domain) may be identified. The elements may then be presented to an individual who, by describing comparative aspects of these elements, provides insight into their own personal constructs in that problem domain. Thus, identifying and measuring these personal constructs may approximate a given mental model.

This research represents an extension of Kelly’s work and covers the individual cognitive process of systems development activities. The model is presented in Figure 1, and more fully described in the research method section that follows.

## RESEARCH DESIGN

This research employs an experimental design that examines the cognitive structures of novice systems developers trained on formal development methods vs. novice developers who approach the development problem without formal training in formal development methods. This section details the research design including instrument development and experimental procedure.

## Choice of measurement technique

First, to capture mental models, three major cognitive elicitation techniques were explored: verbal protocol analysis (Ericsson & Simon, 1984), cognitive mapping (Carley & Palmquist, 1992) and the repertory grid technique (Bannister & Mair, 1968). The think-aloud (verbal) protocol analysis is criticized for not predicting performance (Rowe, 1994). At best, it is known to provide what subjects think but not how they think. The cognitive mapping technique seemed promising, but the final outcome is a complex web with crossing lines among different concepts and as the number of concepts grows, the cognitive map becomes exponentially complicated. Moreover these two techniques are developed and best suited for idiographic analysis. On the other hand, the repertory grid technique is amenable to both idiographic and nomothetic analysis, simpler to administer, and directly linked to PCT. Hence, for this research, the repertory grid technique was adopted (Kelly, 1955; Bannister & Mair, 1968) and developed into an instrument for measuring the cognitive structure of systems developers. Moreover the technique had been used in related work in systems development. In that work (Hunter, 1997) the repertory grid was employed to gather interview data from systems analysts.

## Instrument building: the repertory grid

In a nutshell, a repertory grid is a matrix consisting of elements across the top and constructs on the vertical axis. Figure 2 presents a typical repertory grid used in a clinical session. In this example the elements are possible roles a person or others in his environment might hold. The constructs are represented as bi-polar adjectives in the right-most column. When administered a subject scores the roles on the bi-polar scale. Next the score is tallied for later comparison and analysis. In this research an initial challenge was encountered in identifying the appropriate elements and constructs for the instrument.

At the initial phase of this research project, a set of desired system components were identified from a specific business case and these system components were used as elements of the domain of systems development. Following the literature, 11 elements were selected (Easterby-Smith, 1980). Ten elements are framed over a common general system model, e.g. the input, processing and output components of an information system. An additional element was added to cover underlying database structure. The elements are summarized in Table 1.

Ten constructs were derived from an extensive literature review of the ontological and epistemological debate between methodical development approaches and their antitheses. These constructs were then converted into question form as shown in Table 2.

As a linking mechanism between the 10 constructs and 11 elements, the repertory grid used a rating scale of one to 10 for each construct. The subjects would mark the relative applicability of each construct for each element on the scale. In this system a lower number refers to more methodical stance while 10 refers to extreme antimethodical stance. Refer to the Appendix A for the instrument as developed.

## Experiment

An urban university in the southeastern United States was selected as a site. The subjects were students all of whom were computer information systems majors. The issue of using students as surrogates for professional developers invariably arises with controlled laboratory studies in an educational setting. The question has been examined and discussed in the literature. For example, this concern was expressed by Gordon et al. (1986). There is however, support for the use of student subjects as professional surrogates.

For example, in an Academy of Management Review study (Greenberg et al., 1987), it is argued that organizational researchers should not dismiss the findings of studies using student samples. Two points are addressed in understanding this issue: (1) the relative usefulness of student and non-student samples, and (2) the interpretation of between-subject differences. Another study (Remus, 1986) examined the practice of using graduate and undergraduate business students commonly as subjects in laboratory research on managerial decision making. It explored the question of whether such research results may or may not be transferable to real business situations. The study was conducted to assess the suitability of using business students as surrogates for managers in decision-making situations. In it second-level line managers and students (with no managerial experience) enrolled in a masters-level business programme were enlisted to solve a scheduling problem. Both student and manager groups had common educational levels and no previous courses or experience in production scheduling. Analysis of the costs and erraticism of decision making revealed no significant differences between the student and manager groups, suggesting that research in management decision making using student subjects is not necessarily questionable.

Lynch et al. (1982) gives a detailed discussion of the issues affecting the external validity of experiments in consumer research. Their list includes (1) statistical generalizability based on sampling procedures, (2) conceptual replicability dependent upon applicability of results to other subpopulations of subjects, and (3) realism dependent upon the realistic use of tasks, stimuli, and research settings. The degree of generalizability of research results will ultimately be based on the degree of validity of the implicit and explicit behaviour models that are examined. We note for instance that in both theoretical and applied consumer researches, internal, construct, and external validity are of major importance.

They also propose that external validity is not required for progress in theoretical research; it is neither practical nor possible to demand external validity in any given experiment, which cannot alone support or prove a theory. For them progress results from the refutation of predictions, through which new hypotheses and theories are developed.

Accordingly, we would ask that the reader set aside a concern about the use of the student subjects in favour of considering the intriguing questions that arose in this research. By joining the discourse on ISD methods use we would hope that it entices others to examine the question of the cyclicality of the cognitive and methods use cycles and to consider ways to improve upon the research approach.

The urban university chosen for this study was selected because it had a relatively strict sequence of course prerequisites in the training of programmers and of systems developers. Specifically, students in the information systems programme were required to take programming classes before they could take systems development courses. In this programme, the acquisition of programming skills preceded formal training in ISD methods. Moreover the student’s systems development course programme was divided into two academic classes in sequence, one covered systems analysis and the other covered systems design. Using a ‘natural experiment’ scheme (Babbie, 1992), four measurement points were selected as shown in Figure 3.

The ideal research design would have been a within-subject longitudinal experiment, track ing students in an IS major throughout the degree programme, while measuring their cognitive changes during that course of study. However, limited time and resources did not allow for a full longitudinal approach. As a result the next best possible option was chosen, i.e. a natural experiment, taking advantage of the prerequisite sequence, where the experiment was administered as a ‘between-subject’ comparison. In order to compensate for the weakness of the natural experiment, two cautionary measures were taken. First, two groups of students were recruited: students in programming classes and students in systems design classes. The instruments were administered to both groups at the beginning and end of each class. This experiment was not completely a ‘within-subjects’ design, as they are not being tracked longitudinally. Rather it was a partial within-subjects design in the sense that the first two data points were collected from different sections of students from the same class – a programming class – and the remaining two data points were collected from students in the other class – a systems design class. This subgrouping of within-subject measurement provides partially longitudinal and indirect validation of the instrument because it provides two data points before the treatment and two data points after the treatment. During the experiment, demographic and background information was also collected and used as a statistical control to ensure that only proper samples were included in each group.

## ANALYSIS

The subject’s demographics are reported in Table 3. A total of 82 students participated, but responses from eight of them were incomplete and unusable for various reasons. Therefore 74 data points are included in this analysis: 12 (before) and 17 (after) in programming classes and 27 (before) and 18 (after) in systems design class. In order to examine for sampling bias, t-tests with the unequal variance assumption were conducted on the demographic variables of age and work experience. Each group was compared with others in terms of mean age and mean work experience in years. None of these demographic variables indicated any statistically significant difference among these groups, as can be seen in Table 3. This suggested that there were no significant mean differences among group demographics. Therefore, we proceeded under the assumption that the demographic differences were not going to be a source of variance in further analysis.

## Intensity score: cognitive complexity

The cognitive intensity score (Adams-Webber, 1979) is one of the best-known measures of cognitive structure associated with the repertory grid technique. The intensity score is based on Kelly’s argument (Kelly, 1955) concerning the relationship between the size of the correlation obtained on a grid and the notion of tightness-looseness of thinking. A construct used in a ‘tight’ way leads to more accurate predictions of the subject’s behaviour than constructs used in a ‘loose’ way. Bannister (1960) further developed this idea of tightness-looseness and coined the term the ‘Intensity Score’, and Bieri et al. (1966) developed it further and defined the cognitive complexity as ‘. . . the capacity to construe social behaviour in a multidimensional way. A more cognitively complex person has available a more differentiated system of dimensions for perceiving others’ behaviour than does a less cognitively complex individual . . .’ (Bieri et al., 1966, p. 185)

## DISCUSSION

Information systems development is a complex cognitive activity in which an individual developer’s cognitive structure plays an important role. As ‘systems development methods’ are designed to be cognitive and procedural aids to systems developers the developer’s cognitive structure recursively interacts with these development methods. That is, a developer’s cognitive structure governs how the developer is using methods and tools in terms of the level of usage and the degree of deviation from suggested guidelines. The developer’s cognitive structure also adapts itself throughout the process of methods use. If the literature on the management of ISD is to be believed then this interaction is a determinant in the quality of the system that is ultimately produced.

This study deals with only one side of this interaction: namely, how does the cognitive structure change before and after formal training on systems development methods? This research was designed to measure the changes in cognitive structure using ISD methods training as the experimental manipulation. The research results suggest that novice developers who completed training on development methods may actually simplify or loosen their cognitive structure when compared with people who did not have explicit ISD methods training.

In interpreting this result, two factors in complexity theory need to be considered: cognitive complexity and task complexity. In the literature, it has been established that a cognitively complex person may not perform well in every situation. What is often more important is the fit between cognitive complexity and the complexity of the task. Thus, a cognitively ‘simple person may perform better than a cognitively ‘complex’ person performing a task in a relatively simple environment where parameters and conditions are clearly predefined. The virtue of a cognitively complex person is evident only when the environment requires a person to deal with complex conditions and a high level of uncertainty.

If we presume systems development to be a complex task, this research suggests that the teaching of methods themselves is not sufficient in the training of novice systems developers. For if methods training ‘simplifies’ cognitive structure then one might infer that, training in the use of ISD methods may somehow work against the goal of training competent systems developers to work in a complex environment performing complex tasks.

A further note of clarification is required. The previous observation may seem to imply that simplifying a developer’s cognitive structure is bad. But Kelly (1955) suggests that the apparent dichotomy of loose thinking or tight thinking and complexity or simplicity – by itself is not (in Kelly’s terms) ‘pathological’. Kelly (1955) simply pointed out that in some contexts the person who could deal with social relationships in a relatively differentiated way, that is by moving freely between both ways of thinking, might be more successful than the person who is more tightly constrained in his cognitive processes. Kelly also made the observation from his clinical experience that schizophrenia is evident only when loose thinking does not become tightened at all. That is, the inability to move cyclically between ‘loosening’ and ‘tightening one’s cognitive structure is an indication of trouble. This theory of differentiated structure is directly applicable in the IS development arena. The theory is applicable because the complexity of systems development, coupled with the diversity of stakeholders, and rapid tech nological change requires successful developers to remain open-minded and flexible in accepting other viewpoints, while at the same time being well grounded in one or more conceptual frameworks.

The essence of Kelly’s argument is that a healthy and balanced person will loosen, then tighten, and then loosen thinking in a cyclical manner. In learning terms the cycle may be expressed as one where we conceptualize and then make the concepts concrete, or one of learning then consolidating, learning-consolidating and so on. In discussions with colleagues and in our review of the literature it had been suggested that cyclical patterns were observed in other instructional and professional settings. In a series of private interviews, Dr Lars Matthiassen, of Aalborg University, Denmark mentioned that he has observed this pattern in young developers. Fitzgerald (1997) also suggested such cyclical patterns exist in the professional development community.

Fitzgerald suggests a relationship between a developer’s experience and methodology use. He posited a U-shaped curve as shown in Figure 6, illustrating how the usage of methodology decreases initially, but soars as developers gain more experience and become able to derive a sensible methodology tailored to a specific development environment. As point of accuracy, and for the sake of posterity, we note that in the preproduction manuscript version from which this curve was taken there was a legend accompanying the curve that had been omitted in print. The middle legend should have read ‘. . . prescriptions touted as universally applicable’.

## Implications of the research findings

For us, Fitzgerald’s U-shaped curve represents a larger picture – an interpretation of how systems developers view methods with increasing experience of methods use. Our analysis of novice IS developer’s cognitive intensity scores extends Fitzgerald’s hypothesized general U-shaped curve. Moreover this research provides evidence that this curve may be a collection of small oscillating curves depicting smaller cycles of cognitive loosening and tightening of the IS developer’s thinking. Two overlapping curves illustrating this point are presented in Figure 7.

As developers gain experience their cognitive structure is subject to iterative cycles of loosening and tightening. It appears that this cycle is generally in sync with Fitzgerald’s hypothesized pattern of method usage shown in Figure 6. If our assumption is correct, the cognitive cycle may influence or even govern this method usage cycle. If this is true, by shortening the smaller cycles of cognitive change, we may be able to more quickly help developers reach the mature phase of informed methods use.

Generalizing to the training of systems developers, educational institutes need to focus less on methodical textbook prescriptions, and provide for more hands-on projects in which students can feel and learn amethodical aspects of systems development. To confirm the overall shape of the methodology usage curve and overlapping nature of cognitive intensity curve, further field studies will, of course, be necessary.

## CONCLUSION

As this is a controlled laboratory experiment using student subjects, there are limitations to our findings. First, untrained (hence amethodical) novice developers may not be the same as the trained developers who choose to build systems ‘amethodically’ (Truex & Klein, 1991; Truex et al., 1999). The trained professional’s cognitive structure may differ greatly from novices. This needs to be further investigated. Another limitation is that as this research deals with novice groups, e.g. students who had had little practical experience, differences in their cognitive structures may have been too small to be easily detected by statistical analysis. Studies on professional developers may detect larger differences. Finally, we have to recognize that all programming languages have embedded methodical assumptions. As the subjects were developing in/with visual programming languages these embedded methodological assumptions may compress or camouflage the differences we hoped to measure.

Having freely acknowledged the study’s limitations and the need for further investigation of these questions, we still believe that we have shed light on the nature of an IS developer’s cognitive structure and provided a means and a starting point for further research. We have created a research instrument, collected data and modified an analysis technique. Taken together these contributions will allow us to investigate the cognitive structure and cognitive cycle that may lead to mature methods use. To summarize, this research has: (1) provided a baseline understanding of system developers’ cognitive structure; (2) produced a validated instrument which measures aspects of developer’s cognitive structure and can be further generalized for use in studies of practitioners and experts across different domains, and finally;

(3) introduced empirical evidence on how IS methods are influencing developers’ cognitive structures. It also has suggested implications for the training of systems developers.
