# Impact of the quality of information products on information system users’ job satisfaction: an empirical investigation

Kailash Joshi & \*Arun Rai

School of Business Administration, CCB215, University of Missouri, 8001 Natural Bridge Road, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA, email: joshi@umsl.edu, and \*J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA, email: arunrai@gsu.edu

Abstract. In recent years, researchers and practitioners have paid increasing attention to the effects of information systems on work-related outcomes. The purpose of the reported research is to investigate the influence of information systems on work-related outcomes. A model is derived from prior research that considers direct effects of quality of information product on job satisfaction along with indirect effects through role conflict and role ambiguity. Results from a field study of 368 users indicated that quality of information product has a positive relationship with job satisfaction. However, role ambiguity and role conflict mediated this relationship. A causal path model identified that quality of information product influenced job satisfaction through role conflict and role ambiguity. Role conflict and role ambiguity fully mediated the relationship between quality of information product and job satisfaction. The results identify the role of information systems in influencing users’ work related outcomes. In view of the importance of job satisfaction in motivating employees, IS designers and researchers should pay greater attention to the need for designing quality systems that not only meet their primary information delivery objectives, but also take into account the task and organizational design issues that may influence the work environment of users.

Keywords: Information product, information systems, job satisfaction, management of computing, organizational impacts, quality of role conflict, role ambiguity

## INTRODUCTION

Over the past four decades, information technology (IT) has transformed work and organizations. Consequently, understanding the nature of impacts that IT creates in organizations has been a focus of several MIS research streams (Kling & Scacchi, 1982; Kraut, Dumais & Koch, 1989; Agarwal & Tanniru, 1992; DeTienne, 1994; Lucas & Baroudi, 1994; Ryker & Nath, 1995). The consequences of IT implementation at the organizational and individual level, specifically with respect to impacts on structure, design, users’ social experience, job-related characteristics, and quality of work life have been examined by researchers. One stream focuses on the consequences at the organizational level and considers issues such as organizational structure and design (Lucas & Baroudi, 1994; Robey & Boudreau, 1999). Another stream of research examines issues at the individual level in terms of the impact of information systems on users’ social experience, particularly on the nature of their jobs and quality of their work lives (Turner, 1984; Irving etal., 1986; Blackler, 1988; Koch, 1989).

This research provides a useful lens to understand and manage IT’s role in shaping future organizations and work environments. However, researchers examining IT impacts have reported contradictory findings. In a recent essay, Robey & Boudreau (1999) discuss some of these contradictory findings of studies dealing with consequences of IT, particularly in relation to IT’s role in transforming organizational designs. Past research into the impact of IT on individual users’ quality of work life issues has also reported contradictory findings (Attewell & Rule, 1984). Additionally, in recent times there has been limited research attention to studies on quality of work life issues of information technology users. In our review of recent IS literature, we found studies mainly focusing on the job satisfaction and other job related outcomes for IS professionals (e.g. King & Sethi, 1997).

Information Technology is now playing a key role in organizing the work place and in determining work-related outcomes for information system users. In recent times this role has been further enhanced due to the deployment of newer technologies and concepts, such as multimedia systems, Internet and Intranets, web browser-based interfaces, enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, supply chain management, and electronic commerce. The business model of several organizations now depends on a 24¥7 availability of core information technology services and applications (Weill & Broadbent, 1997). Given the growing pervasiveness of information technology and the increasing dependence of organizational work on IT, it is critical to develop a solid theoretical and practical understanding of IT impacts on users’ quality of work life.

The importance of job-related outcomes, such as job satisfaction, is highlighted in the organizational behaviour literature (Robbins, 1993; Wagner & Hollenbeck, 1998; Greenberg & Baron, 2000). Job-related outcomes need to be addressed in IS research in tandem with properties of information systems and user’s work environment. Specifically, there is a need for research that theoretically develops and empirically tests the relationships between properties of information systems, users’ work environment, and job-related outcomes. Such understanding can contribute to the development of suitable guidelines for system designers and improve consideration of issues related to the quality of users’ work life in the design process.

In this paper, we theoretically develop and empirically test a causal model of the relationship between information systems characteristics and users’ job-related outcomes. Specifically, this study investigates the effects of quality of information systems products on users’ job satisfaction. Concepts from a socio-technical systems perspective are used to identify possible intervening variables that may moderate or mediate this relationship (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977a, b; Mumford & Weir, 1979; Bostrom, 1980a; Pava, 1985).

The organization of the paper is as follows. In the next section, a causal model of the impact of the quality of information product on job satisfaction is proposed based on the STS framework and the work of Leavitt (1965). The following section discusses the research methodology. Results from the field study are presented next. The final section discusses the results and their implications for IS designers and researchers.

## IMPACT OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS ON WORK OUTCOMES

Prior research in the area of impact of computers has been mainly concerned with the impact of introduction computer technology on (a) work-related outcomes such as productivity (see e.g. Kraut etal., 1989), job dimensions (Ryker & Nath, 1995) and individual performance (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995), (b) organizational attributes such as extent of centralization and decentralization (see e.g. Blau etal., 1976; Agarwal & Tanniru, 1992), and broader societal issues such as deskilling of workers, telecommuting (see e.g. Attewell, 1983) and increased monitoring (DeTienne, 1994). Most of the studies assessing the impact of computers on work-related outcomes have focused on how the introduction of computerized systems have affected the workplace in a single organization (Attewell, 1983; Irving etal., 1986; Weber etal., 1988).

Results from the prior studies in this area indicate that information systems do affect work outcomes, but the results are mixed. A model that explores how IS affects organizational work systems and work outcomes should be useful in developing a better understanding of this complex phenomenon. Such a model could contribute towards a better understanding of the impacts of information systems in organizations.

## A MODEL OF IS IMPACT ON WORK OUTCOMES

Leavitt (1965) proposed a model of organizations that includes structure, people, tasks and technology. The model provides a useful framework to help identify the interactive nature of variables that define the organization (see Figure 1(a)). For our purposes the model clarifies the interplay between technology and other organizational variables such as tasks and structure, which can affect work-related outcomes for people. The Leavitt model has been widely used in IS literature as a basis to study the impact of technology, for example, on structure (Lucas & Baroudi, 1994) and individual performance on the basis of task-technology fit (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). The Leavitt model suggests that information technology may impact users’ work-related outcomes directly as well as through its impact on the task and structure related organizational variables.

Researchers in the socio-technical systems (STS) perspective have further elaborated Leavitt’s work. The STS framework views a work system as an open socio-technical system.

Each work system is considered to be made up of two interacting subsystems: the technical and the social (Bostrom, 1978). The technical subsystem involves processes, tasks and technologies needed to transform inputs to outputs. The social subsystem involves issues such as attributes of people (e.g. attitudes, skills and values), roles they play, reward systems, and the authority structure. Any design or redesign of a work system must consider the tow subsystems in an integrated manner (Bostrom, 1980b).

STS perspective recognizes different design mechanisms that integrate the variables in the technical and the social subsystems (see Figure 2). For example, job design involves assigning people to tasks that need to be performed. This assignment can impact both task performance and quality of work-life outcomes. Among other design mechanisms (e.g. work organization, reward system), information systems (communication and control system) also plays an important role in structuring the work environment and in determining the task accomplishment and quality of work-life outcomes.

Relationships between these broad factors in the model derived for this study are identified in Figure 1(b). The model in Figure 1(b) represents a general framework, where each block can be viewed as consisting of several variables. This study explores certain aspects of the effects of IS by focusing on relationships between few selected variables in the blocks. In formulating the specific model to be investigated in this study, two recommendations made by prior researchers were kept in mind. The first recommendation was to include in the model only those variables and relationships that have theoretical or logical support (Heise, 1975; Asher, 1981). The second suggestion was that the number of variables included in the model be kept to a minimum (Young, 1977).

Because information can be considered as the primary end product of IS, a variable characterizing the outputs of IS namely, ‘quality of information products’ (QIP) was selected for the IS block. For work-related outcomes, block job satisfaction was selected, as satisfied workers is one of the major organizational goals, which can be influenced by information system. The nature and causes of job satisfaction have been of immense interest to organizational researchers over the past three decades (Locke, 1976). Based on prior research, role ambiguity and role conflict were selected for the task and the organizational systems block. Role conflict and role ambiguity have been widely recognized as the major sources of stress emerging from the work environment. STS perspective also recognizes that an IS can influence the role variables for users of information systems. A discussion of these four variables and their relationships follows.

## Quality of information products

One of the major ways in which IS can influence job satisfaction is the quality of output produced by the IS. QIP has been well established as a key technical systems factor (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Ives etal., 1983). QIP measures the following dimensions relating to the output from information systems:

1 Timeliness of output information;

2 Currency (up to dateness) of the output information;

3 Reliability of output information;

4 Relevancy of output information (to intended function);

5 Volume of output information;

6 Accuracy of output information;

7 Precision of output information;

8 Completeness of output information.

A user who experiences poor quality of information products, in the form of untimely reports, unreliable data, missing data, conflicting data, or erroneous data, is likely to be dissatisfied. Extra work may be required of the user to secure the needed information and perform tasks normally not assigned to him/her such as error corrections and manual calculations (Bostrom, 1980b). The situation may be further complicated by the user’s lack of knowledge as to what should be carried out in such situations. The user may be forced into making decisions without the required information. As a result, poor QIP can cause decreased job satisfaction, while good QIP can result in enhanced job satisfaction. It is possible that the influence of QIP on job satisfaction (JS) is mediated by some other variables. Job satisfaction has been shown to be influenced by many factors in the task and organization work environment (Locke, 1976). Some of these factors such as pay or supervisory behaviour and leadership may not be influenced by QIP in any significant manner. However, some other characteristics of the work environment such as nature of the task, cognitive effort involved in performing the job, and clarity of assignment may be significantly influenced by QIP. Thus, QIP may also influence job satisfaction indirectly through such factors. Previous research identifies two such potential intervening factors, role conflict and role ambiguity (Mumford & Pettigrew, 1975; Bostrom, 1980b).

## Role ambiguity and role conflict

Role ambiguity and role conflict are important constructs in the work environment as evidenced in the organizational behaviour (OB) literature (Schaubroeck etal., 1989). By including such constructs in the IS area we can draw upon the results from prior OB studies and attempt to integrate the findings from the OB literature to build better theories and models. Role ambiguity and role conflict variables have been found to be associated with communication in organization (Kahn etal., 1964), the nature of tasks (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Miller & Rice, 1967; Adams, 1976), and organizational structure (Kahn etal., 1964; House & Rizzo, 1972).

Kahn et al. (1964) have identified role ambiguity and role conflict as the major stressors in the work environment. Stress may occur at two levels: role conflict (i.e. conflicting job demands) and role ambiguity (i.e. lack of clarity about the task). Role conflict exists when an individual is torn by the conflicting demands of other members in the organization. This results from (a) doing tasks that are not perceived to be part of the job, (b) by being involved with a job that conflicts with personal values or beliefs, or (c) the inability to meet various expectations or demands. Role ambiguity arises when an employee does not have adequate information in order to carry out the task; or does not understand or realize the expectations associated with that particular job. Unclear goals and objectives result in job dissatisfaction, lack of selfconfidence and other dysfunctional consequences (Kahn etal., 1964; Schaubroeck etal., 1989).

Role ambiguity (RA) and role conflict (RC) are related to an array of dysfunctional consequences such as job dissatisfaction, tension and anxiety, and lowered organizational commitment (Beehr & Newman, 1978; Van Sell etal., 1981; Fisher & Gitelson, 1983). According to Rizzo etal. (1970), RC and RA are related to job dissatisfaction and inappropriate organizational behaviour. Tension, job dissatisfaction, and turnover have repeatedly been demonstrated as correlates of RA and RC (Gupta & Beehr, 1979; Breaugh, 1980; Ivancevich etal., 1983; Churchill etal., 1985). Kemery etal. (1985) empirically tested a causal model which hypothesized that RA and RC adversely affected job satisfaction levels and found support for it. Bedeian & Armenakis (1981) found similar results. Schaubroeck etal. (1989) further explored the nature of these relationships. ## Quality of information product, role ambiguity and role conflict

Bostrom (1980b) found that poor quality of information product results in increased role conflict and role ambiguity. Mumford and Pettigrew’s research (1975) also points out the role of IS in influencing RC and RA. According to them, properly designed information systems play a major role in reducing uncertainty and in clarifying the content and boundaries of the new roles expected from organizational participants. ‘Right information at the right time at the right place’ can help in reducing the uncertainty and ambiguity involved in the tasks to be performed. A comparative report may help a worker in deciding between conflicting work requirements. Consider a supervisor, a classic ‘man in the middle’ experiencing high levels of role conflict, who has to negotiate a pay raise for subordinates with superiors. The supervisor may have to appear fair to both the sides. A good assessment of subordinate performance figures along with an analysis of the effects of various pay raise levels may enable him/her to effectively deal with the situation.

In recent literature it has been reported that the use of a third party as an information source was significantly related to an increase in role conflict (Holder, 1996). Poor quality of information is likely to force users to seek information and guidance from relevant others, causing a potential increase in role conflict. For example, a buyer may experience increased levels of role conflict and ambiguity when the current inventory status and past consumption is not accurately reflected in the information provided. The buyer may experience increased levels of role ambiguity in the face of undefined expectations on the course of action to be taken, as well as increased role conflict in planning for future purchases given that she/he may be required to maintain low inventories and yet avoid stockouts. She/he may be forced to go to the warehouse for a physical confirmation of stock levels even although it may not be his/her area of responsibility. Furthermore, such efforts are likely to create conflict in the allocation of his/her valuable time for activities that are not his/her direct responsibility. It has also been recognized that poor quality information systems may create information overload, leading to stress and analysis paralysis, leading to more stress (McCune, 1998). Such stress is likely to be reflected in increased RC and RA.

## The structural model

Combining the relationships 1–5, a structural model as shown in Figure 3 can be formulated. The model in Figure 3 shows the relationship between quality of information product and job satisfaction. One direct effect and two indirect effects are posited. QIP can directly influence job satisfaction. At the same time, increasing levels of QIP can also lead to decreasing levels of RA and RC and vice versa. Lower levels of RA and RC will result in higher levels of job satisfaction and vice versa. Thus, QIP can indirectly influence JS through its effect on RA and RC.

## THE EMPIRICAL STUDY

## Data collection and sample

A cross-sectional survey was conducted to empirically test the structural model. Previous studies in this area have generally focused upon users in clerical positions. Users of information system products from a wide range of supervisory, non-supervisory, and professional positions were selected to participate in this study. These users receive information outputs and rely on the information provided by IS in their day-to-day activities.

Several large organizations participated in the study. The organizations included manufacturing firms, governmental agencies, and educational institutions. Data were also collected from full-time employees enrolled in evening and executive management programmes. These employees represented a large cross-section of organizations. In all 700 surveys were distributed out of which 368 were returned, with a response rate of 53%.

Demographic information on the users is provided in Tables1 and 2. Average experience of the users was 8 years, indicating that the users were sufficiently experienced. Most of the users belonged to supervisory (n=142, 39%) and middle management (n=134, 36%) levels. Forty-one users were upper-level managers. Other users included staff in non-supervisory and clerical positions. Various functional areas were represented in the sample.

## Measurement instrument

Existing instruments were used in the survey. Quality of information product was measured using items from Ives etal. instrument (1983). Items for QIP were selected based on the results of the factor analysis reported in Ives etal. (1983). Most of the studies examining RA and RC have employed questionnaire measures developed by Rizzo etal. (1970), consisting of six role ambiguity items and eight role conflict items. Dougherthy & Pritchard (1985) conclude that these measures capture somewhat global perceptions and can use be used for employees in any type of job. Their psychometric properties have been found to be adequate (Rizzo etal., 1970; Schuler etal., 1977). Job satisfaction was measured using items from the Job Diagnostic Survey of Hackman & Oldham (1975). Summary statistics for the four variables under investigation are presented in Table3.

## Analysis methodology

The entire data set had 360 usable observations that had no missing values, which were randomly separated into two approximately equal subsamples. We divided our dataset into two subsamples so that the analysis conducted with the first subsample could provide insights on opportunities for model refinement. The revised model was then subsequently tested using the second subsample. Similar approaches are used in research where the primary focus is on theory building, as opposed to testing well-established theories (Grant & Higgins, 1991). Our study falls into the theory building category, as we are unaware of previous work that has examined the interrelationships between the constructs identified here under one model.

We used the partial least square (PLS) method of structural modelling to test the research model. PLS is a second-generation, multivariate technique used to estimate the parameters of a causal models with latent constructs. The PLS procedure has been gaining significant interest and use among information system researchers because its ability to model latent constructs under conditions of non-normality and small to medium sample sizes (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Chin & Gopal, 1995). The technique allows researchers to specify the relationships among constructs of interest and the measures associated with each construct. The PLS technique conducts a simultaneous analysis of (i) how well the multiple measures associated with a latent construct, in fact, relate to the latent constructs, and (ii) whether or not there is evidence for the hypothesized relationships among the latent constructs. The use of multiple measures for each construct increases the accuracy of the estimates for path coefficients between constructs, which are typically biased downward because of measurement error when techniques such as multiple regression and traditional path analysis are used. Iterative regressions are used by PLS to estimate the coefficients for each path in the original model (Pedhazur, 1982). These regressions produce estimates of indicator loading on the constructs allowing researchers to test measurement properties within the context of a theoretical model.

The PLS analyses were performed using PLS-GRAPH. In PLS, latent constructs can be modelled as reflective or formative. Indicators of reflective constructs are viewed as affected by the same underlying construct and are measures that covary to the extent that they measure this underlying construct. In our model, all four latent constructs are reflective in that their respective indicators are expected to covary. See Chin (1998) for more details on the distinction between formative and reflective constructs.

## Convergent validity and reliability

Convergent validity of all constructs was adequate, as shown in Table4. A factor loading 0.707 means that 50% of the variance in the indicator item is explained by the latent construct. We are using measures that have been previously tested in earlier studies. Accordingly, we need to be cautious in deleting the indicator items from the model, as the meaning of the construct should not be compromised in the process. We identified a total of two indicator items that had loadings less than 0.50. Two of these items belonged to the role conflict construct, while one of the items was an indicator for the QIP construct. We addressed these problems in our revision of the model prior to conducting analysis with the second subsample. Using the second subsample, the factor loadings of all items on their respective constructs was above 0.60. Reliability coefficients (Cronbach alphas) were above 0.80 for all constructs, indicating high reliability for the measures used in the study.

## Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity between constructs was very good. To test for discriminant validity, the squared correlation between constructs, which is their shared variance, should be less than the average variance extracted for a construct (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Discriminant validity exists between constructs if measures of a construct explain more variance of that construct than do other constructs. In Table 5, discriminant validity is checked by comparing the diagonal elements, which represent average variance extracted by the indicator items for a construct, with the off-diagonal elements, which represents the shared variance between constructs. ## Relationships between constructs

The PLS method does not directly provide significance tests and estimates of confidence intervals for path coefficients. In order to estimate the significance of path coefficients, a jackknifing technique was used to generate 37 samples for the first subsample and 35 samples for the second subsample. Our PLS analysis approach, including the use of jack-knifing, freed us from making any assumptions about normality. Similar analytical strategies have been used by other IS researchers such as Grant & Higgins (1991) to test structural models.

Our PLS analysis using the first subsample revealed that the only statistically insignificant relationship was the direct effect of QIP on job satisfaction (Figure 4). The other four paths were significant. Thus, our analysis with the first subsample revealed two major weaknesses in the model, which triggered us to make two sets of changes to our model. First, we deleted the three measurement items with low loadings. As discussed earlier, these deletions did not adversely impact the validity or the reliability of any of the measures. Second, we dropped the insignificant path between QIP and job satisfaction.

The $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 }$ value shown in Figures 4 and 5 indicate the variance explained in each of the intervening and dependent constructs. Low explained variance in mediating constructs is not necessarily bad. Role ambiguity and role conflict are complex concepts and many more constructs, in addition to QIP, are needed to explain them completely. After we revised the model, the overall ${ \mathsf { R } } ^ { 2 }$ for job satisfaction marginally improves. Importantly, this absolute marginal improvement in the predictive ability of the revised model is, in fact, a substantial relative improvement, as the revised model has one lesser path.

As pointed out by Grant & Higgins (1991), paths in structural models should not be analysed individually. Each construct assumes meaning in the context of the entire network of constructs and relationships represented in the model. Furthermore, path coefficients should not be interpreted in an absolute sense in terms of the resultant consequences of a ‘one unit change’ in a latent construct. Latent constructs, such as QIP, RC and RA, are composites of multiple measures. In addition, these latent constructs are correlated, albeit in a weak manner, making inferences of consequences of a unit change somewhat problematic.

Our analysis strongly supports rejection of four of the five null hypotheses, namely $\mathsf { H } _ { 2 \mathsf { a } }$ H , $\mathsf { H } _ { 4 \mathsf { a } }$ and ${ \mathsf { H } } _ { 5 \mathsf { a } } .$ . We can reject each of these null hypotheses with a high degree of confidence, as the probability of committing a type I error is less than 5% in each case. Evidence emerging from our data analysis does not allow us to reject the null hypotheses, $\mathsf { H } _ { 1 \mathsf { a } } ,$ which states that there is no direct significant relationship between quality of information products and job satisfaction. Thus, we conclude from our analysis that there is a direct effect of information product quality on role conflict and role ambiguity, and a direct effect of role conflict and role ambiguity on job satisfaction. It is through its direct impact on the two role-related variables that information product quality indirectly impacts job satisfaction.

The most important inference that we can make from the path coefficients is that quality of information product does not directly impact the job satisfaction of end-users of information systems. Instead, QIP has a substantial indirect effect on job satisfaction because of its direct impact on role conflict and role ambiguity, each of which, in turn, impacts job satisfaction. As expected, RA and RC were found to have a substantial effect on job satisfaction.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

A model was proposed that identified the likely direct and indirect influences of the QIP on JS. In addition to the direct influence of QIP on JS, it was proposed that QIP is also likely to influence RC and RA, identified as major stressors in the work environment. Finally, RC and RA were also posited to influence job satisfaction based upon organizational behaviour literature.

## Summary of results

The data analysis provided some interesting results. The relationships proposed in the paper were supported by the results. QIP was found to have a positive correlation with JS as proposed. However, the PLS analysis suggested that the direct influence of QIP on JS is not significant. The relationship between QIP and JS was fully mediated by variables from the task and organization system (RC and RA). The generalizability of these results was supported by matching results from the holdout sample analysis.

The model explains 32% variance in users’ job satisfaction. This appears reasonable given the large universe of factors that can influence job satisfaction. The magnitude of causal influences estimated by the analysis also appears to be reasonable, given that several other factors in a work environment can influence RC, RA and JS besides the information system.

## Implications of the research

Management of stress and job dissatisfaction have important consequences for organizations, particularly in terms of medical health care costs, absenteeism, turnover, low organizational commitment, and work-place violence. Job dissatisfaction and stress may also affect employee performance. Furthermore, employee dissatisfaction promotes customer turnover and investor turnover. Well-managed firms are able to convert employee satisfaction into customer and investor satisfaction and loyalty (Hollenbeck, 1998). It becomes critical to develop a rich theoretical understand of how work environments can be structured to enhance job satisfaction. From our standpoint, the growing pervasiveness of IT and their potential to structure work environments make it critical to develop a rich theoretical and practical understanding of IT impacts on users’ work life. Unlike studies that primarily focus on the adoption of a specific IT innovation in studying its impacts, this study examines the relationships between characteristics of information products produced by information systems and users’ job-related outcomes.

The model developed and tested includes both direct and mediated effects of information product quality on job outcomes. Based on their review of organizational consequences of IT, Robey & Boudreau (1999) note the importance of including such important contingency variables to elaborate theories. Such theory development helps resolve contradictions and leads to a richer understanding of causal relationships between information technology and organizational variables. Our model includes role conflict and role ambiguity as mediating variables and the empirical test demonstrates their usefulness in elaborating the process through which quality of information impacts employees’ job satisfaction.

Our model identifies the impact of information product quality on important work-related stressors, namely role conflict and role ambiguity. The results suggest that poor quality of information enhances work-related stressors, thereby setting the stage for several dysfunctional consequences. Exposure to work-related stress over time can lead to diminished task performance, emotional exhaustion, depersonalization (callous and cynical attitudes about caree and work), and reduced personal accomplishment. Burnout induced by work-related stress can manifest itself in physical symptoms (e.g. headaches), dysfunctional behavioural changes (e.g. increased irritability), and reduced work performance (e.g. reduced efficiency, lack of initiative and interest in working, increased rigidity of thought and inflexibility) (Greenberg & Baron, 2000).

Our study has direct implications for IS designers. Designers should embrace a sociotechnical systems perspective of IT deployment. The empirical results provided indicate that IS design influences the social subsystem of the work system. In addition to their traditional focus on the technical subsystem involving tasks and technology, IS designers should pay increased attention to the social subsystem of the work system. In particular, IS designers should pay greater attention to minimizing users’ stress levels induced by inadequate quality of information provided by applications and systems.

System designers should evaluate the likely impact of information product quality attributes on role conflict and role ambiguity by considering task characteristics, and conceivably individual characteristics. It becomes important for system designers to develop a deep contextual understanding of the work environment and demands of the tasks of their clients. The challenge for the designer is to deliver quality systems design and information delivery arrangements that meet the needs of the users’ task environment. Designers can incorporate specific steps to (i) determine the nature and magnitude of stressors currently faced in work settings by users, and (ii) how these stressors can be exacerbated by deficiencies in information quality produced by information systems.

Our study also has implications for the design and implementation of operational and bench marking procedures. Processing controls, integrity enforcement, data validation checks, and regular auditing of systems should be established to ensure the accuracy and consistency of information provided to users. Beyond the initial design, mechanisms should be put in place to undertake continuous improvement so as to better meet users’ requirements. Policies encouraging refinement of requirements and system modification based on users’ experiences while using the system should be developed. Such policies send a clear signal that management and designers are sensitive to and interested in matching information product quality with the needs of the user’s work environment.

User surveys of RC and RA may be conducted before and after implementing a new system to help assess the impacts of the new system. Such surveys should be part of regular post implementation audits to assess the success of a new system or major enhancements. Benchmarking information quality perceptions, role conflict, role ambiguity and job satisfaction across user and task domain can be useful to detect relatively dysfunctional applications that enhance stressors and reduce job satisfaction. Such benchmarking data can provide a basis to analytically prioritize improvement efforts and allocate resources for information product quality enhancement.

## Limitations and directions for future research

Some limitations of the study should be kept in mind in interpreting the results of this study. The data were collected from organizations in the Midwestern United States. Thus, limits to generalizability should be acknowledged, even although given the theoretical nature of relationships considered in the study, they are likely to hold in other populations as well. Results of the study are based on self-reported data; thus the possibility of a bias exists. Better controls and a time sequence can provide a higher degree of confidence in the results compared with a cross-sectional field study. For the sake of parsimony we have focused on a limited set of variables to examine the influence of information systems on the work-related outcomes for end users. However, a significant role of variables outside the model can not be ruled out.

In future research, the issues considered here can also be investigated in an experimental setting, which can provide researchers an ability to manipulate the independent variables in a controlled setting. Researchers should also consider other variables that may be relevant in this context to develop better models of the role of information systems in influencing the quality of work-life-related outcomes for users. Researchers should also examine the influence of new information technologies and implementation practices on users. For example, introduction of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems may cause stress for users. Thus, in our quest for technically superior solutions, we should not overlook the important goal of fostering higher job satisfaction among users.
