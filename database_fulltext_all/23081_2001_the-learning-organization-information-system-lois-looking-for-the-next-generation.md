# The learning organization information system (LOIS): looking for the next generation

Adrian Williamson & Constantinos Iliopoulos\*

Development, Graham Technology Plc, Glasgow, UK, email: Adrian.Williamson@gtnet.com, and \*Department of Computing and Information Systems, University of Paisley, UK, email: C.Iliopoulos@paisley.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper explores the notion that the next generation of information systems will focus on supporting organizational learning. The paper suggests that the increasingly successful automation of procedural work will lead to pressure on organizations to improve performance through enhanced support for knowledge work. A set of outline requirements for the learning organization information system (LOIS) is then proposed using recent research findings from computer supported co-operative working and organizational learning. The computerized on-line journal from this research is described. This journal provides transparent capture of episodes of work and it is argued that the general principles established could support LOIS by helping to provide a richly defined organizational memory. The journal supports collaborative working through the use of groupware, which manages the sharing of, and learning from, journal contents. This can facilitate the retention of not only data and information, but also the inquiry process that produced them. The paper concludes that LOIS will be a selforganizing system, focussing on knowledge work, learning and using advanced technologies drawn from ubiquitous computing. A view of a system that moves towards this aim is presented. Future topics for research are identified, and a natural language approach to knowledge asset management is discussed briefly. In closing, it is argued that LOIS is an important future vision for organizations operating in the information age.

Keywords: Computer-supported co-operative work, groupware, human computer interaction, information systems, knowledge asset management, organizational learning, transparency

## INTRODUCTION

The excitement brought about by the Internet and the corresponding changes in organizational behaviour prompt speculation about what the future generations of information systems will look like. Rapid advances in business technology have revolutionized procedural work and its associated communication work (Drucker, 1966). Consequently organizations are now looking for improvements in effectiveness for the remaining group of workers, the knowledge workers. Knowledge work is characteristically subjective, eclectic, individual, context specific and often one-off making it traditionally the most difficult to support with technology. In spite of these difficulties, recent work in the field of computer-supported co-operative working (CSCW) (Williamson & Jenkins, 1997) and advances in supporting technologies such as ubiquitous computing (Weiser, 1993) and wearable computing (Rhodes, 1997; Starner et al., 1997) suggest that much more flexible ways of working are just around the corner. Workers will become increasingly able to undertake the more interesting, challenging and empowered roles characteristic of knowledge work. This movement towards responsible, empowered employees is advocated in approaches such as learning organizations (LOs) (Senge, 1990), although it has assumed other guises such as business process re-engineering (Hammer & Champy, 1994). Additionally, the process of inquiry and learning inherent in knowledge work further bind it to organizational learning. Once the view is adopted that new systems will be focussed on knowledge work, then the difficulties involved with technology support can be examined. In this context knowledge work in the form of design activity has been supported in the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC)-funded Designers Using Co-operative Knowledge (DUCK) and Safety Critical Integrated Design Support (SCIDS) projects (Jenkins et al., 1997; Turner & Turner, 1997). This research has suggested a number of ways in which the use of technology can be better matched to knowledge work. The results from these projects are described in the section on the learning organization information system (LOIS). We conclude that insight into new ways of working within business might be gained from analogy with this support for engineering design. The conclusion is that future information systems will focus strongly on knowledge work, organizational learning and leading-edge technologies capable of assisting them.

Here, we present a future vision of information systems informed by these projects and reflection on the topics above. Our analysis has led to the production of an outline set of requirements. These characterize the next generation of information systems as LOIS. Our analysis suggests that such systems will:

• support structured and unstructured dialogue and negotiation between colleagues;

• support reflection and creative synthesis of information and knowledge and thus integrate working and learning;

• help reveal assumptions and beliefs;

• be ubiquitous;

• document information and knowledge as it builds up, e.g. by electronic journals;

• help make recorded information and knowledge retrievable, and individuals with information and knowledge accessible;

• create opportunities for intelligent (e.g. progressive) archiving and retrieval of organizational information and knowledge (including forgetting);

• provide electronic playpens for scenario simulations and surfacing of tacit knowledge.

We comment that the notion that emerges resembles strongly the classical history paradigm. There is the idea of learning from past events, the collection of data and repeated reinterpretation of its meaning, significance and impact for new generations.

The remainder of the paper is laid out as follows. In the next section, we introduce LOIS as a scheme to create, support and operate a form of corporate memory focussed on learning. In the section on LOIS outline requirements, we examine the justification for the outline requirements proposed above, providing pointers to the relevant topics. The work from engineering design is described in the section on LOIS, and analogies drawn with the more general processes typical of knowledge work. This leads to the conclusions, which present the elements of functionality that might support such a LOIS. Finally, we cover future work, drawing attention to the relevance of knowledge asset management and features of natural language that could contribute to the creation and operation of LOIS.

## INTRODUCING LOIS

In this section, we consider LOIS as a scheme to operate a form of corporate memory, gathering and distributing data, information and knowledge across the organization. In such learning environments, information systems are geared to improve the interactions between knowledge seekers (Drucker, 1966; Kidd, 1994) and the various forms of information providers and knowledge creators. Two important characteristics of this genre of systems are the reduction of uncertainty and the capturing of learning episodes in some form of electronic history. Four basic processes are identified in knowledge asset management (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995): develop new knowledge, secure new and existing knowledge, distribute knowledge and combine available knowledge. However, the main goal of organizations is not to be geared solely towards knowledge development, storage and distribution, but also to improve the organizations’ chances for success and survival by continuously adapting to the external environment. Organizations need to store and remember information and knowledge to help learning and adaptation. LOIS may facilitate this by making it easier to access and use information and knowledge developed in other parts of the organization.

Although LOIS may provide an internal learning framework, information and knowledge can also increase through acquisition from outside the organization. Moreover, useful and potentially actionable information and knowledge originates on the periphery of the ‘formal’ organization. LOIS should be able not only to acquire information and knowledge from outwith the organization but also capture its internal formal and informal aspects. Informality can act as a creative playpen for individuals and groups in which innovative combinations of existing knowledge occur and new knowledge can be created. It could also act as an interface in which ‘tacit’ knowledge becomes explicit and shareable. LOIS could increase social participation and interaction within organizations. By utilizing LOIS we stand a better chance of increasing shared understanding within the enterprise, and thus better foster learning.

If LOIS is able to gather information and knowledge it can contribute towards creating an organizational memory (Lamming et al., 1994; Conklin & Young, 1996). It can make recorded knowledge retrievable or make individuals with knowledge accessible. An organization’s past knowledge can live in a CSCW, which could make the organization more accessible and understandable to its members. In turn, LOIS can increase the decision making and problem solving options for their users, allowing ready re-use of existing information and knowledge.

As a CSCW environment, LOIS can help facilitate and provide the right context for dialogue as perceived by Senge (1990) to help individuals and groups become observers of their own thinking. LOIS could facilitate adequate quantity and quality of dialogue and thereby foster learning; for example, by supporting anonymity and a range of interaction. A team approach is often necessary to tackle problems effectively. For example, much of the work carried out in information-age organizations involves more unstructured, messy and wicked problems in which the usefulness of numeric data to solve these problems lessens. For example, discharging mental health patients requires a co-ordinated effort with both hard scientific evidence and subjective impressions combined into a joint decision. Furthermore, these complex problems have to be addressed by distributed workgroups and the work of the groups will vary during the various phases of a work episode. Sometimes immediate feedback is required, at other times workgroups input their contribution asynchronously. Information sharing and filtering activity is again at the centre of the learning organization and its groups. A learning workgroup may then use LOIS to support the co-ordination and completion of the complex tasks involved.

As a pervasive infrastructure, LOIS can also provide the conceptual framework for the integration of information and knowledge technologies. LOIS should span the spectrum of technologies from rigid forms of information technology (e.g. databases) to systems supporting dynamic, non-structured, self-evolving knowledge networks (conceptual/cognitive mapping). Thus, LOIS should cater for the ‘single-loop learning – adaptive’ functions as well as the ‘double-loop learning – generative’ functions, i.e. the change and innovation requirements of the organization. A measurable challenge for LOIS is to provide conceptual and IT-based tools that support meaningful connectivity and navigation through these knowledge networks. Meaningful connectivity helps the organization to survive, and to gain and maintain an enduring advantage.

Overall, LOIS are necessary to help organizations and workgroups sense and make sense of the environment, foster diversity, document and remember, make decisions and solve problems in a collaborative fashion, i.e. ‘learning in action’.

## LOIS OUTLINE REQUIREMENTS

We now consider the outline requirements presented in the Introduction. Many strands of work across a variety of disciplines have contributed to these proposals. A consideration of organizational learning (Argyris & Schon, 1978; 1996) has suggested the requirements to support dialogue, support reflection, support creative synthesis and help to reveal assumptions and beliefs. Using the learning organization as a concrete example the necessity to support these can be argued as follows. First, a learning organization recognizes and exploits its information corpus in a conscious and systematic fashion thus encouraging equivocality and multiplicity of interpretations of critical episodes (Sproull & Hofmeister, 1986). Second, it is an anthropocentric entity making its organizational actors central in interpreting, leveraging and diffusing its information corpus as in Johnson & Hasher (1987). Finally, organizational learning (OL) research indicates that the ability of the LO to change and innovate is crucial (Tushman & Anderson, 1986). The LO actively explores its information corpus in order to produce new knowledge and new action and action patterns. Experiencing its own history and learning from it is an important trait: it is a complex organizational system with conscious and deliberate action and reflection mechanisms in place. Consequently, the action and reflection activity typical of LOs and knowledge work will be central to LOIS and must be catered for. Inherent within this learning activity is curiosity and processes of inquiry, which can lead to the exposition of assumptions and beliefs as well as facilitating the acquisition of knowledge.

As discussed in the previous section, the organization may find itself approaching large or complex issues where the individual capacity of those involved may easily be exceeded, and so some notion of corporate memory (Conklin & Young, 1996) and subsequent team working is required. New, ubiquitous and pervasive computer technologies (Weiser, 1993; Norman, 1998) are starting to offer potential solutions to interacting with such innovations. This research also leads to more practical questions of knowledge storage and use, covered by knowledge asset management (KAM).

Finally, the provision for ‘what-if’ scenarios and ‘electronic playpens’ arises from the need to facilitate creativity and invention within the enterprise, allowing the organization to better understand and adapt to its ever changing environment. We now discuss these issues in more detail.

## Dialogue, creative synthesis and revealing beliefs

There is widespread recognition of the importance of dialogue in all forms of learning environments and it is one of the most significant conceptual tools at our disposal (Pask, 1976). In the more specific case of learning organizations, the purpose of dialogue is to let the meaning of our words inform the people and workgroups to develop understanding, or consensus based understanding, important information, experiences, goals, personal templates, values, beliefs, etc. (Senge et al., 1994). Dialogue is both an information filter and a mechanism for bringing together seemingly disparate facets of an issue. Dialogue is not only one of the prime mechanisms involved in work but it also helps achieve collective consciousness in the LO.

It will be important not to enforce a rigid structure within LOIS. The literature on organizational learning is diverse, little overall agreement exists in current theoretical debates, and our current observations show that it is not formed on any one given theoretical base. From this we argue the practical need for a multiplicity of approaches to start understanding the dynamics of OL, rather than a prescriptive style. This suggests that the approach advocated in Lawrence & Lorsch (1967) is most appropriate. They advocate acknowledging the existence of different learning styles, and maintain that the best learning style for an organization depends on a number of contingent factors, such as culture, size, structure, industry, etc. One possible conclusion from this is that an eclectic approach could be appropriate for the OL field. This eclectic stance has solid theoretical underpinnings in that it informs the formation of a rich philosophical, cultural and technological interpretation of purpose and perspective in learning organizations. We perceive LOs not as being a static model of a particular kind of organization, but as dynamic and evolutionary in nature. A learning orientation in organizations acts as a conceptual ‘glue’ bringing a rich variety of elements with their context. This provides approaches, methodologies and tools that enable these elements to be seen as integrated, meaningful patterns. Such a varied stance will be best supported by a flexible framework as proposed for LOIS.

## The inquiry process

Inquiry is a fundamental component of learning and knowledge work and therefore LOIS will need to support it. The varied nature of the inquiry process suggests that some generic support will be needed, and consideration of existing methods may yield the nature of the requirement within LOIS. One example of a recognized inquiry approach is the appreciative inquiry method (AIM) (Stowell et al., 1991; West, 1992), a part of client-led design (CLD) (Stowell & West, 1994). AIM supports discussion where a client-led conversation with some notional supplier (typically an analyst) is facilitated by the use of system maps, CATWOE (customer actors transformation weltanshauung owners environment) diagrams (Checkland, 1981), root definitions and conceptual models. The technique has gained an increasing number of successes, most recently in health care, as described by Smith (1996). Many requirements elicitation (RE) approaches are focused on transferring descriptions from customer to supplier, typically in a specific domain, e.g. software engineering, and therefore support a scientific paradigm. AIM, by contrast, is founded on an interpretive epistemology and therefore has a wider field of application, coping with description transfer and also the more symbiotic relationship embodied in Vickers’ notion of appreciation (Vickers, 1995). We conclude that AIM is representative of a wide ranging inquiry process appropriate to LOIS, and also points to the difficulties of providing IT support for such approaches.

The mechanization of AIM as a means of improving knowledge elicitation for knowledgebased systems has been addressed (Stansfield, 1993), but tool support has proved difficult to achieve (Stansfield, 1993; West et al., 1994). Conventional computer solutions here have failed on two fronts: first interference with the task in hand through poor interfacing, and second by enforcing structure on a method which is not intended to be so highly prescriptive. The result is that users of AIM continue to use paper-based techniques as the most appropriate for the method. The new, innovative computing technologies do offer at least partial solutions to these two problems by providing more transparent, ubiquitous interfaces and consequently supporting more unstructured working. This suggests that LOIS will be able to support inquiry processes if it embodies such innovation.

## Ubiquitous computing, recording and retrieving company history

There are many new technologies emerging in the corporate marketplace. Many are relevant to LOIS but fall into the category of better support for aspects of procedural work. The revolution in communications technologies advances Drucker’s third worker type – the communication worker. Many of these technologies provide specific tools that might be used within LOIS rather than core services, although the field of information retrieval (IR) is particularly significant to the history paradigm currently proposed. For brevity, the discussion here will focus on innovations which help to support knowledge work in the LOIS framework, leaving the more deterministic and procedural activities to the established literature.

From a macro perspective, LOs have come to characterize organizations that can adapt and change. In Fiol & Lyles (1985), learning is ‘the process of improving actions through better knowledge and understanding’. Individuals and collective entities learn broadly in two ways, first by adapting, learning from experience and coping. Second by anticipating, innovating and pro-actively creating opportunities (Argyris & Schon, 1978; 1996). The main difference between OL and individual learning is that the collective entity (e.g. workgroup) does not lose its learning abilities or learned lessons as these can be retained in the organizational/ collective memory in the form of tacit knowledge [regardless of whether this is information technology (IT) based or not].

The process of sensing, responding and documenting or remembering usually exceeds the sensing abilities of individuals working for the organization. The organization as a complex system can make the task of sensing, managing and directing impossible for individuals or workgroups. However, the need to sense and make sense of the organization is of paramount importance. Support for this through ubiquitous, transparent computing technologies is now becoming feasible.

Computer technology is slowly starting to produce the advances necessary to realize some of the goals of ubiquitous computing (Weiser, 1993; Norman, 1998). This view of ‘one person, many computers’ contrasts sharply with that of the single personal computer on the desk. Ubiquitous computing views technology as a mechanic’s toolbox rather than the all-in-one solution of a Swiss Army knife. This is a better reflection of the history of man’s tools as a collection of simple items used in combination, rather than a single multipurpose device. As an example, the same history can be seen in the development of the compact cassette player. It started with portable radio size, then Hi-Fi separate, then Walkman. At first one was taken everywhere, now they are ubiquitous: people have all three, and one in their car as well. Related to this is transparency, which removes conscious effort associated with the technology from the user. A popular example is the worldwide web: the physical location and

URL (uniform resource locator) of the item is transparent when one follows a link in a hypertext document. There is also transparency associated with practice: how many of us think about the mechanics of writing when we pick up a pen and paper (unless the pen does not work!)?

Wearable computing is also moving on from the backpack to the more accessible, which allows a transparent portability, muting the call of the worker to the workstation. This has provided concepts such as augmented reality (Starner et al., 1997), and systems for augmenting human memory (Rhodes, 1997). In this latter system, a wearable computer is equipped with a small head mounted display, which can be glanced at while still allowing full eye contact with companions. Input to the system is via a chord keyboard. Although text based, and therefore requiring significant specialist skills, the notion of entering and retrieving diary data has been shown to be possible. As advances continue, then multimedia interaction may increase the transparency of the system and reduce the need for typed input, and produce a richer record of an individual’s activity. Traditional computing modalities need not always be used, and wearable audio computing can offer similar benefits, for meeting notes for example. With a suitable interface this has a number of advantages, leading to a range of systems under investigation (Roy et al., 1997).

In fact, truly wearable computing is also being realized, as described in Post & Orth (1997). The use of conductive fabrics such as silk organza has allowed microcircuits to be soldered or connected directly to clothing, and fabric keyboards using matrix and capacitive detection have been trailed. The shoe computer which transmits signals using skin as a conductor to communicate with others has also been demonstrated as long ago as 1996.

Even the personal digital assistant (PDA) allows the worker to roam as they used to with pencil and pad, rather than enforcing the straightjacket of the desktop (or even the lap top). For example, the GUIDE project (Davies et al., 1998) uses wireless, context sensitive web devices to guide visitors around the city of Lancaster. A series of transmitter cells download information to the device as the sightseer requests it. Although some activities are structured, there is also emphasis on personal tailoring within a framework of tourist tasks. The communication is two-way, allowing email from the user back to the Tourist Office or to other friends using the devices.

Wearable computing removes the need to consciously carry technology around, allowing the capture of potentially useful data without guidance from the user. Both these philosophies recognize rapidly changing needs: the solution which provides support for fixed business processes or task patterns is no longer universal. We suggest that it is technology more focused on social aspects (Carroll et al., 1994; Carroll, 1995) that will shape the information system of the future.

These technologies can provide workers with augmented memory and processing abilities. If transparent, ubiquitous computing is available then we can more readily capture outcomes and process. If wearable computing is available then we have one more unconscious facility (i.e. hindsight) for collecting potentially important data that can be reviewed, with a reduced risk of points being missed or forgotten. As we learn how to deploy and use such systems we can envisage individual knowledge workers using them to better sense and adapt to thei environment.

## Electronic playpens

The need to enable serendipitous, informal and creative activity is fundamental to facilitating effective knowledge work and learning within the organization. In many ways this is a difficult area to support, and indeed a negative view may be most effective approach. At this early juncture, we restrict our recommendations to ensuring that any resulting structure does not preclude creative play rather than attempting to channel such an unpredictable activity.

## LOIS

Our view of LOIS is first a vision for commerce and industry, and second a realizable technology. Our early ideas are therefore focused on industrial and commercial activities. At present, many of the components have been researched in isolation, the vision for LOIS is the future integration of the relevant components.

In the current technology development cycle (Somerville, 1996), there are many interfaces between clients, businesses, analysts, engineers, administrators, etc., which are difficult to manage and become sources of ambiguity and errors. Adopting a learning strategy in this environment will allow the re-use, re-evaluation, or learning from, past work activity. In particular, the analyst will have access to a richer set of information than just previous project deliverables. With transparently gathered data on outcomes and process, they will not only have information to support their immediate task, but also routes into the relevant personnel through a corporate memory provision. Providing workers with the ability to develop their own business systems will offer potential benefits in removing some interfaces, and hence sources of error.

From consideration of knowledge work, the underlying history paradigm and our previous discussion on organizational learning we argue that three factors must come together to achieve LOIS:

• business activities and procedures;

• transparent recording and prompting technology;

• use and interpretation of current and stored data for learning.

Many facets of such a system have been identified in isolation, the originality of LOIS is the holistic concept embracing a set of emerging principles. We have seen how AIM is effective, but needs new technologies as it cannot be supported with current IT. Transparent recording can be used in the design domain, but has not been assessed more widely in business. Ubiquitous technology has been tried, but not linked to corporate history systems. Some data may be stored and accessible, but it is rarely rich enough for effective learning (e.g. the continuing use of pen and paper in inquiry techniques such as AIM). It is the synthesis of the three principles which sets LOIS apart from the piecemeal solutions currently used.

The first consideration is the fundamental business processes such as identifying customer needs, and support for innovators within the enterprise (the knowledge workers). From our previous discussion we propose that AIM and systems thinking are suitable. Second, we will look at the issue of transparent recording, drawing on the design domain as an area representative of knowledge work involving learning. Finally, we will outline the issues of re-use and sharing of information in the design context, featuring the use of groupware and distributed systems, to support individual and group learning, pointing towards the viability of this LOIS vision.

The problem of suitable technology support is readily identified as the past inability of the computer to offer the sort of transparency now advocated (Weiser, 1993; Buxton, 1996; Norman, 1998), and the flexibility and performance issues raised in (Lakin et al., 1989) and (Tang, 1991). For example, an electronic version of the paper pad has been investigated in Weiser (1993), introducing the notion of ubiquitous computing. Weiser advocates many cheap electronic pads, rather than one expensive desk-based box. This can change fundamentally the way we work with computers. I could now work with electronic pads scattered across the desk, each containing a piece of the information that I am currently working with. This could also tackle the display limitations of current personal computers, by allowing a real window display to be distributed across the desk rather than dealing with a few square centimetres on an overcrowded screen. Activities such as ‘bring to front’ would be transparent: I could simply pick up the pad and move it. These pads may also be multimedia. A notes–index-based system for recording meetings has been explored (Whittaker et al., 1994), although issues of privacy arise (Bellotti, 1996). Commercially, the Palm Pilot (Hawkins, 1998) has realized some of these research results, including hand-written input.

These emerging technologies improve the richness of data available and we suggest that they will be an important part of LOIS, as they start to offer solutions to the problems of technology support for the inquiry and related research processes typical of a responsible, empowered knowledge worker in a modern organization.

## Capturing work episodes

The advantage of having the conventional work activity using paper pads computerized would be to allow the monitoring of work episodes. The system will know what information I am using on my desk at any one time, providing transparent capture of my current work environment. This facilitates the capture of a rich context for review, re-use, or learning. This starts to circumvent the traditional problems of work episode capture which is that workers do not have time to record many activities explicitly, often knowing they will not re-use the information. When a systemic view in the context of learning is adopted however, it can be seen that this work episode may be useful to someone else in the organization, although this can neither be foreseen nor used as a motivator for an extra explicit work overhead.

We would suggest that moving to such electronic pad technology will therefore allow the ready capture of work context, allowing automation of some processes crucial to supporting the requirements identified in the introduction. In spite of the advantages offered by such a technique it still only gathers explicit knowledge. Nevertheless, it could provide a route to discussions which could make important and relevant tacit knowledge explicit and hence accessible. We now consider results from recent research into supporting industrial design.

## Transparent recording in the design domain

Although the use of Weiser’s pads in this context has not been investigated, the Windows systems investigated (Jenkins et al., 1997; Turner & Turner, 1997) are still representative of this physical working pattern. By recording the Windows contents during a work episode, a rich context can be inferred without requiring the worker to spend time explicitly recording their activities. A second issue raised in Jenkins (1994) is support for ad hoc or individual working rather than enforcing IT compatible methods. To provide support for this, an electronic daybook has been suggested, which allows work episodes to be captured in any order and collated later. This activity is representative of findings from studies of messy desk owners (Williamson, 1998), where activities are largely unplanned but assembled to some purpose later.

Here we will consider the example of mechanical design as representative of more general learning activity at work. Within design, learning is an inherent characteristic and the critical review of past work is widely practised. The provision of rationale alongside final designs offers both justification and learning opportunities for those concerned, and possibly a wider audience. That designers are willing and able to use rationale in the re-use of designs is well documented, for example in Karsenty (1996), although management of the re-use process is a non-trivial problem.

## The on-line design journal

Design activity has been supported by technology in the EPSRC-funded DUCK and SCIDS projects. In SCIDS, the system encourages the on-line capture of design rationale through two mechanisms: an electronic on-line design journal (ODJ) (Turner & Turner, 1997; Turner et al., 1997) and a transparent computer work episode capture system. The work episode capture system consists of two agents working together: a protocol manager (PM) and a designer’s assistant (DA) (Williamson & Jenkins, 1997; Jenkins et al., 1997).

The ODJ provides a jotting area for text and diagrams, graphics, etc., which can be used like an engineer’s daybook. It is used by the designer to record comments about what is going on which would not be appropriate in the formal design deliverables, but which she would like to record in passing. This typically includes rationale information such as options and relevant criteria for the decisions made. The prototype ODJ, although not robust enough for commercial use, was used and accepted during the long-term trials described in Turner & Turner (1997)

The work episode capture system uses the principle that work activity can be inferred from the computer artefacts involved in the process. Workers use their computer workstation without interference, filling in pages in their ODJ as they choose, and working with applications and documents to carry out their work tasks. This use of standard software is monitored invisibly by the protocol manager agent, which logs applications and documents in use over time. This raw work history can be stored in the groupware database. The DA is intelligent enough to carry out a preliminary assignment of work episodes based upon the current user history, leaving the user to deal with exceptions only.

Where the design proceeds in a top-down fashion, the concerns can be preloaded and work assigned to these given categories. The system’s record of concerns and work database is effectively an index into the history of design stored by the ODJ and other tools, as designers can find past work by free text query and browsing of the database to form an effective aid in the recovery, recall and hence re-use, and learning from, past work.

## Information sharing

Within the ODJ information sharing is supported in two ways. First, all the pages of the notebook are available in a system wide proprietary database. Second, follow-up comments and questions, observations and criticisms can be made on overlays over the original page. The original page is available only in read-only mode once it has been filed by its originator to guarantee the validity of past design history. A number of overlays is permitted on each page and the reader can select which overlays to view. In addition, the ODJ and associated work history database will allow concerns and issues of one worker to be related automatically to the experience of other workers within the enterprise, providing a rich environment for individual and organizational learning.

One of the issues addressed by the prototype was that of motivation for both the use for recording work, and assisting re-use. It was found that following a period of familiarization the electronic notebook was accepted and judged to be effective and worth using (Turner & Turner, 1997). Significant in the introductory period was familiarization with the information visibility. The system kept individual notebooks private, but allowed the posting of public pages into group notebooks. Comments could be attached to these public pages by any reader. This approach to information security proved effective in encouraging entry, use and re-use of ODJ contents.

## Agency and agents

The notion of ‘agency’ is implicit in the preceding description of the operation and interaction of the ODJ, DA and PM. The PM and the DA are themselves agents. This is only one specific example of agents providing agency, and it will be necessary to supply more agency in line with the techniques identified previously, such as AIM or KAM. The agency may be developed using the ODJ to record the decision activities leading to the establishment of a procedure, and then instigate the procedure itself. Others can then learn about the procedure from its design history.

The agent-based approach has the further and indeed crucial advantage of supporting incremental extension (or reduction) of the work environment’s capability: additional agents may be added as and when considered appropriate. Considering this more generally as the knowledge worker’s environment, then the fundamental activity of the technology developer becomes focused on the direct support of the business through agency, rather than through indirect support systems.

## Hindsight

We have suggested previously that transparent recording of business activity may lead to better support for hindsight. Consider a design sequence for a new product. We can see that the new product is not the only potentially useful output from the process. The first time the new product sequence is enacted it might not be obvious that a ‘new product procedure’ facility will be beneficial. By the 3rd launch, someone is thinking a set process is a good idea, but much of the forming discussion will have been lost, and a conventional project may have to be created to produce it. If a historical recording is available, then a review may be all that’s required to craft the new product procedure, learning from both the initial and future uses. Significantly not all activity will be so useful, and there will be activity whose specialist context precludes its direct re-use. This dichotomy is addressed by the ODJ making a (deliberately limited but adequate) transparent record of work, rather than requiring a constant categorization and classification effort from users, which would rarely be honoured (Williamson, 1998). If the activity is never re-used, then no effort has been wasted in pursuing its refinement. In summary, LOIS gives us the opportunity to use hindsight: we can re-use past activities even if we do not recognize their value until some time in the future.

## CONCLUSIONS

The conclusion which we draw from our investigations into organizational learning, design rationale and KAM is that LOIS will be a self-organizing, integrated, semi-formal (i.e. formal and informal), action-oriented, component-oriented and distributed system. The underlying architecture will need to support this, and we propose that this might be informed from the functionality of the SCIDS project. The functionality that we suggest is shown in Figure 2.

The functionality works within the history paradigm. The ‘What’ section holds formal raw data, such as documents, emails, drawings, faxes, etc. Alongside is the store of electronic design journal notes, which are the informal entries from workers such as salesmen, designers, developers, etc. The ‘What’ store is overlaid with a transparently captured record of work, the ‘How’, which captures context from the computer systems used. This will allow future investigators to see links between the raw data items, and provides one (chronological) path through the design and development data. These sections are read only: as in the case of history, the raw evidence must not be altered or distorted. Finally, the ‘Why’ top level concerns allow a mapping of conceptual matters over the record of work and raw data space. This provides a semantic index into the historical data, similar to the ontological approaches currently under discussion in information systems and management (Patil & Russ, 1998) and work on learned journals (Buckingham Shum, 1998).

We suggest that this functionality will support key features for a LOIS as presented here. The history and rationale are captured in raw documents and a record of work, with accompanying Journal notes. The learning process is supported by allowing the retrieval of this information either as raw content, or via virtual indexes such as the time line record of work, or the design concerns. This supports the process of inquiry which is also logged, allowing learning from the inquiry process itself. KAM can be facilitated by dedicated agents as suggested in the agency and agents section.

We may also conclude that such an infrastructure will not only support the business, but associated development activity by analysts, engineers, etc. under one integrated resource. From this account we might also conclude that the role and activities of information system practitioners will change dramatically in the future. A powerful and distributed computer system will offer facilities for organizational memory. This will store both raw data and information such as procedures, decisions and heuristic solutions to organizational problems/issues. The development of a new business process or procedure will be the outcome of a series of meetings of knowledge workers, who devise the protocol to achieve the new task. They will be able to undertake a process of inquiry across the organization, gathering information or personal contacts to achieve the learning necessary to complete their new goals. Dialogues and negotiation components serve the need for collaborative inquiry. They will complement and fine tune the information exchange, knowledge sharing and thus the learning processes.

Finally, we notice the move away from formalizing the knowledge stored, towards the storage of raw data, documents, pictures, etc. wrapped in contextual notes. This removes the unbearable overhead of traditional ‘knowledge elicitation’ techniques, and replaces it with raw data accessed via a lightweight knowledge map. It is notable that this approach was adopted in Smith (1996), and it points to approaches emerging in the management literature (von Krogh & Roos, 1995; 1996). We have shown this approach to be feasible in the real work setting using an electronic notebook and agent support, and it can readily be seen that this could be applied across a range of businesses and professions.

## FUTURE WORK

Although we believe that LOIS is a significant vision of a future generation of information systems, there is still much work to be carried out in validating these principles both in theory and in practice. There are serious questions to be addressed in connection with knowledge capture and knowledge asset management within the business. There will also be far ranging implications for an organization seeking to adopt LOIS in terms of its culture, behaviour, internal structures, interfaces to its environment and indeed its motivation to do so.

To reiterate our argument from the introduction: we believe that commercial pressure to improve the effectiveness of knowledge workers within the organization will provide strong motivation for the adoption of LOIS. Increasingly knowledge workers will become the wealth creators for the company by ensuring continued product or service distinction, and ensuring competitive advantage. This is not to say that all organizations will benefit from LOIS, and indeed work on organizational learning within small to medium enterprises (SMEs) (Chaston et al., 1999) suggests that OL may actually be detrimental to some business sectors. Similarly, where the attributes of LOIS can be met by an individual or small group that can adequately monitor and sense its environment, then the overhead introduced by the proposed support technologies will not be cost effective for this enterprise either.

By its very nature, our view of LOIS inherits the learning organization culture (Senge, 1990; Senge et al., 1994) with consequences for company strategy, behaviour and resource management. These principles will need to be revisited and analysed in the light of this presentation.

As data is acquired by LOIS, so it will need access schemes and technologies to provide future retrieval in addition to the chronological and work episode access proposed above. LOIS will need a range of services to deal with both this and legacy data, dictating the provision of knowledge asset management. Early approaches to this problem used the expert system paradigm, attempting to extract knowledge from a domain expert and instantiating it in some computer-based form. Although successful for a range of deterministic hard system applications, issues such as complexity, validation, verification and, most particularly, maintenance have prevented their widespread adoption for managing the modern firm. As an illustration, consider task analysis for knowledge description (TAKD) (Diaper, 1989). This approach uses a task description hierarchy (TDH) to categorize task knowledge, and a uniqueness rule ensures that each item can be identified, and subsequently described by a knowledge representation grammar (KRG) term. The analysis works with a consistent (although user-defined) vocabulary. If we consider using such a method for knowledge management, then a number of problems arise. First, the analysis is exhaustive and therefore time consuming. Second, the knowledge instantiation it produces is inflexible, and so cannot be easily evolved. When organizational learning is being supported then information and knowledge are constantly evolving, and the sort of intransigence characterized by TAKD is a significant problem. In fact, neither the uniqueness nor the consistent vocabulary constraints are necessary when considering the complex socio-technical organization which is the modern firm.

As an example solution, POSIT (Driver & Louvieris, 1998) uses similar description terms to KRG but advocates polymorphism, ambiguity and redundancy as realistic necessities of modern knowledge management systems. In fact, these are advocated strongly to help solve the generic problem with knowledge description systems: the reliance on a single analyst’s/expert’s/implementer’s/user’s view. Within any social organization multiple views exist, and while standards (e.g. for views) may be enforced to improve efficiency, the need to provide cross-disciplinary support means that multiple views must be facilitated. The provision of polymorphism allows many (different-view) routes to the same data, while allowing ambiguity enables rapid evolution by removing the need for elaborate cross-referencing and maintenance. The users will be trusted to differentiate the ambiguities as they arise, as they would in social situations. Redundancy again allows diversity of view, rapid evolution and a rich descriptive environment. It is not that database principles are to be discarded, but that access routes should gain a new flexibility and richness. It is more widely argued (von Krogh & Roos, 1995; 1996) that natural language is a viable mechanism for KAM and facilitating these multiple views, and so it is likely that LOIS will need to support these new developments. The aim is to enrich the picture with relevant information and knowledge as well as serendipitous findings (Garrat, 1987) before the ‘confinement’ of interpretation takes place.

In spite of the large range of issues to be addressed, this LOIS approach seems a likely and fruitful way ahead. Diverse human, organizational and information system requirements can be integrated, and different modalities of learning can be supported with LOIS. The need for careful customization which takes note of contextual and emergent issues is a prime issue that needs to be addressed if LOIS is to adequately cope with the complexity of organizational life. Perhaps the most enduring sensation from viewing this LOIS philosophy is that we see the subjective discussions and objective decisions on the future of the technology and its interfaces being recorded by the technology itself for future learning. This forms the basis of a self-organizing, evolving, long-life system.
