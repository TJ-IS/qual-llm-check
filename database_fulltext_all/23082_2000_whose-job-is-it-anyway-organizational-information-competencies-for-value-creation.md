# Whose job is it anyway?: organizational information competencies for value creation

Joe Peppard, Rob Lambert and Chris Edwards

Information Systems Research Centre, Cranfield School of Management, Cranfield, Bedford MK43 0AL, UK, email: j.peppard@cranfield.ac.uk

Abstract. Research highlights that most business managers continue to be dissatisfied with the value they perceive they are deriving from their organization’s information systems investments. On examining the literature, the dominant perspective is that creating value through information systems is primarily the responsibility of the IS function. Accordingly, to address this chronic malaise, attention generally focuses on the IS function with proposed prescriptions ranging from reskilling the IS professional through re-engineering the IS function to the ultimate sanction of outsourcing. This paper examines the problem of value creation from IS investments from an organizational as opposed to an IS functional perspective. Drawing on resource-based theory, the paper argues that the effective deployment and exploitation of information should be viewed as a ‘strategic asset’. To leverage value from IS, the paper proposes that organizations must recognize and develop information competencies and that the elements of these competencies are distributed throughout the organization and not solely resident in the IS function. Through a multimethodological approach these information competencies are identified and described. The resultant competencies are then studied in an organizational context. The paper ends by drawing conclusions and articulating further research directions and opportunities

Keywords: Competitive advantage, information competencies, IS function, resource-based theory, value creation

## INTRODUCTION

A recent case study in Harvard Business Review eloquently captured the dilemma which many organizations face with regard to their information systems investments (Reimus, 1997). This fictitious case concerns an insurance company that had invested millions of dollars in a new system which had yet to be implemented and used by front-line staff. Indeed the application development, which began 3years previously, months ahead of any of their competitors, was now significantly behind schedule and any advantage which the organization may have derived had now been lost. While the dialogue in the case centres around a discussion between the Chief Executive Officer (CEO), Finance Director and Chief Information Officer (CIO) concerning the status of the project, the case opens up a whole range of issues regarding the management of information and the development of the appropriate competencies within the organization. A critical item in the debate is ‘whose job is IT anyway?’: where does responsibility for information systems and technology (IS/IT) lie and in particular who is responsible for delivering value from IS/IT investments?

The simple argument is to say that IS/IT is the responsibility of the CIO and the IS function and, by association, that the delivery of value from information investments is also the responsibility of the incumbents of this function. While appealing, practice and research has suggested that this may not be the case (Rockart, 1988; Boynton etal., 1992; Anonymous, 1995; Dutta, 1996). Technology is merely part of the value creation process, it needs to be combined with organizational change and then the resultant processes applied in the organization (Soh & Markus, 1995).

The history of IS investment in organizations is littered with disappointment. It is not that the technological systems have not been built, but that in many organizations they have consistently failed to deliver business value (Strassman, 1990; Willcocks, 1994; Ward et al., 1996). Reasons promoted for this failure include:

• lack of strategic direction given by the business to IS investment decisions (Earl, 1989; Ward & Griffiths, 1996), often reflected in a misalignment between IT strategy and processes, and business strategy and processes (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Earl, 1996a);

• inappropriate management of IS-related change (Benjamin & Levinson, 1993; Benjamin & Markus, 1997; Ward & Elvin, 1998);

• lack of a formal ‘benefits management’ approach (Farbey etal., 1993; Ward etal., 1996; Remenyi etal., 1997);

• inability to leverage existing IT infrastructures (Weill, 1993; Broadbent & Weill, 1997);

• ‘paving the cow paths’ rather than capitalizing on innovative ways to organize work that technology provides (Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1993);

• the relationship ‘gap’ between the IS function and rest of the business (Schein, 1992; Grindley, 1993; Ward & Peppard, 1996; Peppard & Ward, 1999);

• not availing of the marketplace to provide IS/IT applications and services through a strategy of selective sourcing (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994; Cross, 1995; Lacity etal., 1995, 1996; Earl, 1996). Indeed, we would argue that the disappointing results which many organizations have achieved through outsourcing is due to the treatment of IT as something which can be abdicated to third-party vendors to manage, without explicitly considering the wider organizational role in leveraging value from IT. See also Strassman (1995) for a more disparaging argument.

Recognizing the imperative to generate value from IS, organizations often engage in an examination of their IS function and many have looked towards the re-engineering of IS processes (Clark etal., 1997; Boar, 1998; Brown & Magill, 1998). However, such re-engineering effort generally only addresses the supply of technology into the business: the IS function becomes better at building and operating applications. Yet, these applications may only be contributing marginally to the achievement of organizational goals and objectives as the focus is on building and operating applications and technology rather than delivering significant business benefit.

Although information technology provides a powerful vehicle for processing information, the focus on technology has often shifted the emphasis away from the real issue of exploiting information for value creation to the delivery of technology. In general, information technology has no inherent value in itself; For example, just having PCs on employees’ desks does not confer any value to the organization. This value must be unlocked and it is only business managers and users who can ensure that this occurs. In short, the ‘T’ of IT has become the focus of attention rather than the ‘I’. Unfortunately, information is a Cinderella with few champions while the ‘T’ has lots of mercenaries (the technology supply industry, IS functions and outsourcing vendors, to name but a few). Yet the irony is that information is a factor of production (Bell, 1981) while technology is a cost of doing business. Organizations must redress the balance in favour of the ‘I’ if value is to be created.

The objective of this paper is to identify and describe those information competencies that are necessary to create value. We use the words information competence to capture all aspects of the management of information in an organization, including assessing the role of information in an industry, the exploration of the potential impact of technology, the identification of competitive opportunities through to the design of systems, the deployment of information technologies and the realization of business benefits from these systems. The paper concludes that creating value from information is an organizational wide issue and not a responsibility that can be assigned solely to the IS function. Information competencies span across the organization.

The paper begins by examining the concept of competence as portrayed in the general management literature. The IS literature is then surveyed for specific guidance in relation to information competencies and a framework synthesising the prior research is developed and presented. Following this, the research design and process is outlined and a set of information competencies are identified and defined. These competencies are then studied in the context of an organizational setting. The paper finishes with conclusions and identifies future research opportunities.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

As the central objective of this research is to identify and describe those competencies necessary to create value from information investments, in this section the concept of competence as it is addressed in the general management literature is first explored. Then, focusing specifically on value creation through IS and information competencies, there is an examination of the IS literature, critiquing empirical and conceptual studies in this area. Finally, a framework, which provides an initial basis for positioning and understanding information competencies is developed and presented.

## The concept of ‘competence’ in the management literature

There has been a burgeoning literature in economics, industrial organization, marketing and strategic management providing theoretical and empirical research to understand the concept of competence. This literature falls broadly into two camps. First are those researchers who have considered the concept of competence at the level of individual skills and knowledge (cf. Klemp, 1980; Boyatzis, 1982; Woodruffe, 1991). Second are the researchers who focus on competence at an organizational level and have explored the concept of competence as a basis for organizational competitive advantage (cf. Wernerfelt, 1984; Winter, 1987; Prahalad & Hamel, 1990; Barney, 1991; Amit & Schoemaker, 1993; Peteraf, 1993; Andreu, 1994; Ciborra, 1994; Hamel & Heene, 1994; Collis & Montgomery, 1995; McGrath etal., 1995). This second view is the one to which this paper subscribes.

Captured in a theoretical perspective known as resource-based theory, the central tenet is that is that unique organizational resources of both tangible and intangible nature are the real source of competitive advantage. With resource-based theory, organizations are viewed as a collection of resources that are heterogeneously distributed within and across industries. Accordingly, what makes the performance of an organization distinctive is the unique blend of the resources it possesses (Barney, 1991; Rumelt, 1991; Collis & Montgomery, 1995). A firm’s resources include not only its physical assets such as plant and location but also its competencies. Much of this research suggests that the ability to leverage distinctive internal competencies relative to environmental situations ultimately effects the performance of the business (Barney, 1991; Ginsberg & Venkatraman, 1995).

There has been some debate in the strategy literature as to the distinction between between ‘competence’ and ‘capability’. Prahalad & Hamel (1990; Hamel & Prahalad, 1994) who popularized the concept of ‘core competence’ see the two as one and the same. Stalk etal. (1992), however, make a clear distinction between the two, portraying capability as a higher level construct. This paper views organizational capability at this highest organizing level and as being directed towards the strategic purpose of the organization. Specific capabilities are achieved through the unique combination of organizational competencies. From this perspective, organizational competencies are a subset of capabilities and are concerned with acquiring, developing, managing and deploying resources of all types. Such competencies therefore comprise a blend of personal skills, knowledge and experience, roles and organizational processes (McGrath etal., 1995).

## Information competencies in the literature

Exploring competencies in the context of the management of information is a relatively recent development in the evolution of the IS discipline. Culnan (1986; 1987; Culnan & Swanson, 1986) in her co-citation analysis of IS makes no reference to the concept of competencies as a subfield which constitutes MIS research. Swanson & Ramiller (1993) reviewed the nature of manuscripts submitted to Information Systems Research and none fall within the thematic area of ‘competence’. Indeed, the MIS Quarterly keyword classification scheme (Barki etal., 1993) does not explicitly refer to competence although it would probably fall under category D. Organizational Environment.

Throughout the 1980s there was a steady stream of articles and papers on the theme of IT and competitive advantage (McFarlan, 1984; Rockart & Scott Morton, 1984; Cash & Kon synski, 1985; Porter & Miller, 1985; Earl, 1987). In general, these articles were predominately descriptive and illustrated how companies were applying technology in ways that gave them a competitive advantage in the market place. Recently, the sustainability of IT as a source of competitive advantage has been examined by a number of authors (Lemons, 1986; Floyd & Woolridge, 1990; Feeny & Ives, 1990; Keen, 1993; Kettinger etal., 1994; Mata etal., 1995; Dvorak etal., 1997; Powel & Dent-Micallef, 1997) with the overall conclusion that technical wizardry does not generate sustainable competitive advantage. In fact, today information technology is a commodity product that is available to all. Consequently, the general conclusion and recommendation from this body of research is that organizations should focus less on technology per se and more on the management of information. In the language of resourcebased theory, there are currently few ‘structural impediments’ to purchasing technology; but organizations require competencies to leverage value from their information investments.

Within the context of resource-based theory, the management of information (and consequently IS) is a ‘strategic asset’ (Dierickx & Cool, 1989) which demands development and cultivation. The importance of developing competencies that allow organizations to successfully take advantage of information in their specific context has been noted even if these competencies have not yet been articulated (Sauer & Yetton, 1997).

The concept of competence in the IS literature is predominantly focused upon individual competence in the form of IS skill sets rather than treated as an organizational construct. The focus has been on the technology supply side and individuals’ skills, emphasising the requirement for IS professionals to have not just ‘technical’ skills but also ‘business’ and ‘interpersonal’ skills (Palmer & Ottley, 1990; Skyrme, 1992; Lee etal., 1995; Todd etal., 1995; Cross etal., 1997). More recently, ‘change agentry’ as a skill for IS professionals has been proposed (Markus & Benjamin, 1996). The implication of this literature stream is that the solution to the ‘problem’ with IT can be solved by equipping IS specialists with additional skills. The inference is that the inability to deliver value from information arises from shortcomings in the IS func tion and among IS professionals.

Sambamurthy & Zmud (1994, 1997) were the first researchers who explicitly looked at information competencies (they use the term ‘IT management competencies’) from an organizational perspective. In their research they identified seven categories of IT management competencies: business deployment, external networks, line technology leadership, process adaptiveness, IT planning, IT infrastructure, and data centre utility. Each of these categories contained a number of competencies, giving 29 in total. While providing an innovative perspective, three main criticisms can be levelled at this research. First, the definitions of their competencies lack rigour. For example, one competence they cite is labelled ‘existence of elec tronic links with the organization’s customers’; we would argue that this is not a competence but is the outcome of having a particular competence. Second, the interrelatedness of these competencies has not been illustrated; how do they ‘fit together’ and what is their relationship? Third, their list of competencies makes no explicit reference to the exploitation of information; this is how value is created (McGrath etal., 1995; Soh & Markus, 1995).

Ross etal. (1996) contend that to apply IT to enhance competitiveness lies in the development of an effective IT capability. Their research suggests that this capability derives from careful management of three key IT assets: a highly competent IT human resource, a reusable technology base, and a strong partnering relationship between IT and business management. This research does not explicitly address the detail of the IT capability, rather it provides glimpses of possible effectiveness.

Feeny & Willcocks (1998) have addressed the issue of IS competencies specifically from an IS functional perspective. They have identified nine ‘core IT capabilities’: IS/IT leadership, business system thinking, relationship building, architecture planning, making technology work, informed buying, contract facilitation, contract monitoring and vendor development. However, no explicit reference is made to those competencies required within the business as a whole in order to create value through the exploitation of information.

These studies have proved to be useful platforms to ground our research and we have built upon them. However, there are two aspects in particular which we sought to address in the research reported in this paper. First, to focus not just on the technology supply side competencies but to explicitly describe a set of organizational wide information competencies which would also incorporate this technology supply side view. Second, to develop a framework depicting the relationship between these competencies illustrating how they relate to each other.

## An initial competence positioning framework

To construct an initial framework for positioning information competencies, three domains of research were discerned from the literature. The early IS literature tended to focus upon ways to improvement the management of the ‘IT department’ to ensure the effective supply of information systems to the business (cf. Nolan, 1973). Issues such as structured systems analysis and information elicitation techniques were exhaustively discussed. These issues of information supply have continued to be researched right up to the present day; for example outsourcing of information technology has been a topical issue throughout the 1990s (Cross, 1995; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; McFarlan & Nolan, 1995; Earl, 1996b).

Practitioners and researchers soon recognized that the efficient supply of technology was not sufficient; the requirement for alignment of the organization’s IS/IT strategy with the business’s underlying goals and objectives was apparent (Porter & Miller, 1985; Earl, 1989; 1993; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Peppard, 1993; Ward & Griffiths, 1996). Approaches were developed which sought to identify opportunities for competitive advantage in the marketplace from IT developments (Wiseman, 1985; Earl, 1989) as well as others that provide mechanisms for classifying and prioritising IS investments (Ward & Griffiths, 1996). The subject area now known as ‘IS strategy’ dominated the IS literature of the late 1980s. Yet, even organizations that developed IS strategies often failed to derive significant value from their investments; and it was recognized that while an IS/IT strategy may be a necessary condition it was clearly not sufficient.

There was a significant body of research during the 1990s that investigated why organizations with an IS strategy and efficient technology supply still did not create value. The research area of information exploitation was beginning to emerge, concerned with issues such as productivity (Brynjolfsson, 1993); process inefficiencies and ineffectiveness (Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1993); and ‘value for money’ and benefits (Farbey etal., 1993; Willcocks, 1994). It is only through exploitation that value is created (McGrath etal., 1995), corresponding to ‘the IT use process’ as defined by Soh & Markus (1995).

Building on this literature, an initial framework is proposed which suggests that organiza tional information competencies fall into three broad domains (see Figure 1):

• Information strategy competencies: evaluating strategic information and technology based opportunities as part of business strategy formulation and translation of these into resource supply requirements.

• IS/IT supply competencies: sourcing IS/IT supply resources, developing and implementing information systems and technology.

• Information exploitation competencies: defining the required information and information systems and the ongoing exploitation of information to realise business benefits.

This categorization, which was shortened to strategy, supply and exploit within the research project, extends the traditional business strategy-IT strategy linkage to explicitly incorporate the exploitation of information. The framework was used to guide the conduct of the early phases of the research (see below).

## RESEARCH DESIGN

While the research objective was clear, the novelty of the topic under study for the IS discipline meant that many of the questions were unknown and would evolve over the duration of the research project. While this presented many challenges for the research design it also ensured that the research process would have to be ground in the ‘real world’. The organizations participating in the research were large and complex and were committed to the project agreeing full access and participation; a number provided funding. They recognized the opportunities provided by the concept of competencies and sought to explore further the application of the concept to the creation of value through information, systems and technology.

The research design was divided into two stages. The first stage was to identify, describe and validate the set of information competencies for value creation in an organization. The second stage involved an in-depth investigation of these competencies within a single organization setting. The research project was undertaken over an 18-month period.

Within stage 1 there were three phases. Phase 1 was the conduct of a literature review of the research area and the construction of an initial research framework (already described above). Phase 2 involved further exploration and analysis of this framework in ‘communities of inquiry’ both within each participating organization and also across participating organizations. This was to ensure a wide debate and understanding of the issues with regard to information competencies and the identification and definition of a complete set of competencies. Two case studies were also undertaken to provide input into this process.

For ease of elaboration a third phase is shown as part of the overall design. In reality, this was an extension of phase 2 as the research highlighted that further refinement and analysis was going to be required if the competencies defined were going to be ‘useful’. We did not expect at the outset of the research process that there would be a distinction between ‘macro’ and ‘micro’ competencies. This was one of the benefits of using communities of inquiry as a research method (see below).

## Research methods

Given the focus of the first stage of the research, a single research approach was deemed inappropriate. Despite the available advice for selecting an appropriate research methodology (cf. Antill, 1985; Franz & Robey, 1987; Galliers, 1991) the nature of the problem suggested that it would be more appropriately addressed by adopting a multimethodological approach. Driving this view was a recognition that the often conceptual and intangible nature of competencies could possibly make their identification and definition difficult. It was postulated that by using a number of research methods a more coherent and complete set of competencies would be identified. Two methods were selected as appropriate: communities of inquiry (Heron, 1996) or focus groups (Greenbaum, 1998) and case studies (Lee, 1989; Smith, 1990; Yin, 1994; Myers, 1997). Figure 2 illustrates the structure of the stage 1 research process.

The communities of inquiry were used as a mechanism for a co-operative inquiry (Heron, 1971) process between the researchers and practitioners. Co-operative inquiry is a collaborative, experiential, reflexive and action-oriented process (Park, 1999) with the researchers and the research subjects forming a ‘community of inquiry’ and engage as equal partners in the inquiry process. This process cycles between creating four types of knowledge: experiential, practical, presentational and propositional knowledge (Heron, 1996).

The researchers and participants acted as both co-researchers and co-subjects (Heron, 1971). Subjects within each of the focus groups were drawn from the participating organizations, with each focus group lasting from half-a-day to 2 days. All participants contributed to all aspects of the research process in generating ideas, collecting, analysing and interpreting the data and concluding from the findings (Reason, 1994). Co-operative inquiry is committed to the exploration of concerns and interests by a group of people who are part of the activity being researched. The fundamental aim of this method is the development of improved propositional knowledge (Heron, 1988; Reason, 1999).

The two case studies, which were undertaken by the researchers, functioned as an input into the deliberations of the communities of inquiry. In-depth interviews were held with key informants in the two case companies. The semi-structured interviews were guided by the initial research framework with questions keyed to this framework.

Action research was employed for the conduct of the stage 2 case study. This method represents an interventionist approach to the acquisition of scientific knowledge with foundations in the postpositivist tradition. The researchers were in a ‘helping role’ within the organization being studied (cf. Schein, 1987; p. 11). To achieve scientific rigor, the five-phase cyclical process, as articulated by Susman & Evered (1978) and elaborated upon in relation to IS research by Baskerville & Wood-Harper (1996; 1998), was adhered to.

## Guiding paradigms and epistemology foundations

Research methods should be assess within the canons and principles of the guiding paradigm. Each of the research methods was guided by a particular research paradigm, had a different ontology and epistemology (see Table1).

The communities of inquiry were conducted under the auspices of the participatory paradigm. This paradigm articulates the philosophical and epistemological principles for participatory forms of inquiry (Reason & Rowan, 1981; Heron & Reason, 1997). The ontology of the participatory worldview is in the unity of the natural and social world. The outer world is portrayed as objectively given and subjectively represented. Humans are seen as an integral part of this world and, most importantly, they are self-determining. Thus, from an epistemological standpoint, social reality is not pregiven or deterministically predefined but co-created. For an exposition of the application of co-operative inquiry and the participatory paradigm to IS research see Breau & Peppard, 2000.

The stage 1 case studies were conducted under the constructivist paradigm. The relativist view of reality in constructivism proposes that realities, as multiple mental construction are local and specific because they are ground in humans’ social and experiential contexts (Guba & Lincoln, 1994). The relativist concept of reality leads to a transactional and subjectivist view of epistemology. Knowledge is created, not discovered by the mind, because the scientist and the subject of investigation actively interact in the research process (Schwandt, 1994).

In contrast to the stage 1 case studies, the stage 2 case study was conducted using an action research approach (Checkland, 1991; Wood-Harper, 1985, 1992; Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996; 1998). Action research aims to contribute both to practical problem solutions and the advancement of social science theory. Action research is not fully participatory because it is still largely the researcher who defines the topic, issues and problems from the perceptions of the practitioners in their particular context (Reason, 1994). It aims to build descriptions and explanations within the practice context itself. The inquiry structure of theoretical framing, data collection, and evaluation constitutes a cycle of continuous interaction between theory and practice that ensures that action research is both rigorous and relevant (Checkland, 1991; Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996).

## Validity of the research process

The guiding paradigm for each of the methods has been discussed in the previous section and it is these paradigms which provide a commensurate theory of validation (Kuhn, 1962). This validity aspect of the research process addresses the plausibility and credibility of the evidence produced within its scientific paradigm. In the context of this research, in particular stage 1, it sought to ensure that the competencies identified accurately represented a comprehensive and complete set.

The nature of co-operative inquiry is the open, direct and collaborative conduct of the research process with the social actors in the field (Reason, 1988). Co-operative inquiry strategies share the fundamental idea that reliability of the research outcomes is ensured through the grounding of the data in real action and in contexts that are relevant to the real world participants (Pettigrew, 1990; Eden & Huxham, 1996).

Participatory research cycles between action and reflection, exploring experiences from diverse perspectives, developing diverse ideas and interpretations, and testing different forms of action. The participants, in actively creating, modifying and testing these knowledge types in an iterative research process, not only own but validate the outcomes of the research. This cyclical research model is indeed the strongest validity check of knowledge claims in participatory research. Validity is enhanced in creating and enacting the findings through those participating in the inquiry. The quality of participatory research is improved further through the commitment to critical subjectivity, which means that the research participants critically examine their beliefs and theories in the inquiry process.

The outcomes of discussions within the communities of inquiry were sent to participants to ensure that they accurately reflected discussions and outcomes thus increasing validity (Silverman, 1997). Respondent validation of data (Reason & Rowan, 1981), referring back to the subjects with tentative outcomes, also increased the validity of the data generated throughout the research process. These outcomes were then refined in the light of their reactions. In essence, those involved in the research process were asked to ensure that the competencies corroborated to their experiences. The competencies were also linked back to the research literature, again providing validity at a theoretical level (see Table 2).

The tactics for the validity of the case studies as recommended by Yin (1994) and Eisenhardt (1989) such as using multiple sources of evidence, were adhered to during the conduct of the phase 1 case studies. A data collection protocol, keyed to the initial competence framework, was developed. The semi-structured interviews, used for the development of these cases, were grounded in the literature. The case descriptions were reviewed by the case organizations to ensure that they accurately represented the situation that existed in their organization.

## Phase 2

In order to explore the 3 competence domains as illustrated in Figure 2 and to understand the issues faced by participating organizations more clearly, case study research was undertaken in John Brown Engineering and the UK’s National Health Service (NHS) while communities of inquiry were established with subjects from the CCTA, GlaxoWellcome, Scottish Equitable, National Westminster Bank, and Zeneca. These subjects were a mixture of business and IS professionals.

While this phase confirmed the possibility of categorizing competencies using the three domains, it also stressed the significance of the interplay between these three categories – and highlighted that competencies were also required to address this interplay. This led to the construction of an enhanced framework for mapping competencies, explicitly showing the linkages between strategy, supply and exploitation. This is illustrated in Figure3.

The linkage between the strategy and supply competencies highlights the need to identify and justify the development of future IT supply resources, i.e. the development of future IT infrastructure including people, hardware and technologies. Historically, such developments have been piecemeal, often limited by a yearly budgeting cycle or only making investments when applications are developed. The longer term nature of IT infrastructure development can only be justified when linked to future strategic business vision (Broadbent & Weill, 1997).

## Phase 3

As the community of inquiry discussions progressed, they revealed that the descriptions developed above were useful in understanding the broad competencies required in an organization, but that they were at ‘too high a level of abstraction’ to be useful in both evaluation and assessment. While these six ‘macro’ competencies do provide a mechanism for reporting an overall competence profile for an organization, for assessment purposes the community of inquiry concluded that these macro competencies would require further refinement by decomposing them into constituent ‘micro competencies’.

In the same co-operative environment, the inquiry community identified, developed and subsequently refined the macro competencies into constituent ‘micro’ competencies. The identification of micro competencies was undertaken by analysis and debate within the focus groups. Table4 lists the micro competencies, positioning them within the domains of the six macro competencies.

While the process of competency identification and description was inductive we wished to test the theoretical validity of these micro competencies against the literature. We used Rockart etal.¢s (1996) imperatives for the IT organization in order to assess whether there might be gaps between their prescriptions and the competencies which we identified. We also plotted Brancheau etal.¢s (1996) study of IS management issues against the macro competencies to ensure that the competencies would deal with the management issues raised in their study. Reassuringly competencies existed to address all of these issues.

Finally, to demonstrate the relevance of these 25 micro competencies to contemporary IS issues, we reviewed research studies and conceptual papers and mapped these onto the 25 microcompetencies. Table 4 lists out the micro competencies together with relevant literature sources.

## A STUDY ASSESSING INFORMATION COMPETENCIES IN AN ORGANIZATION

The research project to date had identified six macro competencies and their interrelationships, which was subsequently divided into 25 micro competencies. This section of the paper describes the application of the micro competencies in order to assess an organization’s information competence of. A multinational organization agreed to participate in this stage of the research process. The specific name and details of this organization are irrelevant for the purposes of this paper; indeed the sensitive nature of both the discussions and findings also preclude its publication. The objective of stage 2 of the research process was to assess the robustness of the competencies as defined. From the perspective of the case study organization, the research sought to:

• raise awareness of information competencies and expose relevant issues;

• determine the existence or otherwise of each of the micro competencies;

• assess current performance in each of the micro competence areas as perceived by organizational actors;

• define those competencies which need to be either improved or developed by the organization;

• gain commitment for change from key managers.

The first instrument facilitated a review of each micro competence in order to determine the required level of performance to support the business objectives. The outcome of this debate was a shared vision for each micro competence of the required performance and its criticality to the delivery of business strategy. Appendix A illustrates sample questions for the micro competencies within a macro competence.

A second instrument was developed to help assess the actual performance in relation to each micro competence, as perceived by the organizational actors. These questions were developed initially from the literature and then refined through a combination of an expert panel and by discussions with participating organizations. In total, over 300 questions were determined. Appendix B illustrates a sample of questions used to assess one of the micro competencies.

Both instruments were used in workshops facilitating discussion and debate between participants. This allowed them to develop a shared vision for each micro competence and its criticality in achieving business objectives. Additionally, participants were able to discuss their views on current performance and also reasons for any shortfall to the required level of performance. By encouraging any differences in opinion to be explored and resolved enhanced understanding and commitment to the assessment was secured. The participants themselves presented and discussed the results of their analysis with their peers.

In summary, the micro competencies were used by the organization as a lens to assess performance. The workshops also allowed the research team to assess the reaction to and understanding of the micro competencies and supporting instruments by the participants.

To improve the communication of the results for each competence ‘radar charts’ were developed permitting a visual representation of current performance in relation to the shared vision of the required level of performance. Figure 4 depicts an example used to illustrate overall performance at the micro competence level. The outer line is the consensus view of the required performance level. The shaded area represents the evaluation of current level of performance for each of the micro competencies. These charts proved to be a powerful communication mechanism and the participants repeatedly returned to them to anchor their discussions.

## Conduct of the workshops

Four workshops together with a feedback meeting were held to review the organization’s performance. These were conducted away from the organization’s premises to ensure full commitment of the participants and to minimize interruptions. The CIO, IT Strategy Planning Manager and Head of the Business Division agreed to involve as many business and IS/IT people as possible.

The first workshop was attended by 30 business managers and six IS/IT managers from across the division. The second workshop was attended by 20 IS/IT managers and four business managers. The third workshop involved the Head of the Division together with his direct reports. The fourth workshop was a feedback session to the participants at the first two workshops. Finally, there was a presentation of the findings and discussion of the conclusions at a meeting attended by 70 people, including board members, most of whom had participated in one or another of the previous sessions.

Each of the workshops was led by one of the researchers who facilitated the discussion. At the first two workshops, the approach was explained and six syndicate groups, each containing a mix of IS/IT and business managers with facilitators, debated each of the macro competencies, together with their component micro competencies. Subsequently each syndicate group presented, for discussion, their assessment of the ability of the organization to perform these micro competencies. While it is not possible to discuss in this paper every point in detail of the deliberations within these syndicate groups and plenary sessions, a great number of issues were raised, and many deficiencies in the organization’s information competencies were uncovered. Interestingly, the conclusions of the first two workshops were very similar despite the different backgrounds of the participants.

The third session was successful in engaging senior management in the review; the pictorial representation, using radar charts, forcefully brought home weaknesses within particular information competencies. ## CONCLUSIONS, COMMENTS AND IMPLICATIONS FOR FURTHER RESEARCH

In this paper we have developed the notion of competence within the context of the management of information for business value creation. We have grounded our discussions in the perspective of the resource based theory and have taken the stance that the effective management of information is a ‘strategic asset.’ Using a combination of research methods, 25 microc ompetencies located within six macro-competence areas have been identified and positioned around a framework ground in the literature but extended through early empirical research.

Adopting a competence perspective in relation to creating value from information investments has provided significant insights that may be summarized as follows:

• An almost exclusive focus on the IS function has been the traditional solution to problems of securing value from information investments. This study indicates that a broader organization-wide perspective is required.

• The research indicates that to create value from information investments it is important to understand and then develop information competencies.

• Information competencies are organization wide, potentially having elements in both the business as well as in the IS function.

• Research in the stage 2 study organization suggests that poor information competencies can result from inadequate recognition and unclear management responsibility.

• An informed discussion between business and IT managers, based around the competence framework can provide a shared understanding and insights into the development of an organization’s information competencies.

• The evidence from the stage 2 study organization suggests that organizations can develop action plans to improve their information competence profile.

We concur with Feeny & Willcocks (1997) who note, in relation to information competence, that ‘in this emerging field there is as yet little general agreement on the labelling or definition of the building blocks, or even on the level at which a competence is most appropriately identified’ (p. 467). We have attempted to advance the contribution of those scholars who have been exploring these issues.

Over the past decade, there have been a number of articles strongly recommending that business managers take more responsibility for IT management (Palmer & Ottley, 1990; Boynton etal., 1992; Anonymous, 1995; Dutta, 1996; Rockart, 1988). Such prescriptions have recommended that ‘the line takes leadership’ (Rockart, 1988) and more recently pleas from a number of scholars and practitioners for the ‘end of delegation’ of information decisions to IS specialists (Anonymous, 1995). Yet these exhortations have largely fallen on stony ground, possibly because they lack specificity. In this paper have contributed to this discussion through an examination of information competencies.

The title of this paper proposes a provocative but crucial question (the title is also a play on words and could also be ‘Whose job is IT anyway’). While responsibility for IS management is a ‘grey area of management’ (Griffiths, 1994) we conclude that it is important to view information competencies from an organizational rather than an IS functional perspective, and that the competencies necessary to deliver value are likely to transcend functional boundaries. Some component elements of each competence may reside in the business while others may reside in the IS function. Yet all elements must exist in a holistic context if a particular competence is to exist. From this organization-wide perspective, the folly therefore of assigning responsibility to the IS function for information value creation becomes obvious.

The important contribution which business managers make to each of the individual information competencies should be recognized. The stage 2 study highlighted that situations where the micro competencies resided primarily in the IS function performance was assessed more positively and often highly regarded within the organization. This is probably because the CIO (or equivalent) had direct responsibility for this competence, including its structuring and resourcing. Where the micro competencies resided primarily in the business, such as those competencies necessary for the exploitation of information, performance was rated low as individual in the business did not see their role as encompassing the exploitation of information. This would suggest that clarification of roles and responsibilities would assist in max imising value creation and represents an interesting research opportunity.

Significantly, the research has highlighted that the IS function’s ability to build relationships with the business is not a competence, in itself, which is in contrast to the assertions of a number of scholars (cf. Ross etal., 1996). Dialogue within the focus groups suggests that building and fostering relationships with business managers and users is an element present in many of the competencies. However, as the study indicates, the ability to build relationships with suppliers and vendors is a competence. The research also supports Feeny & Willcocks (1998) findings that project management is not a competence, but pervades a number of the competencies.

The micro competencies developed in this paper require further study in order to identify their component elements. There are also likely to be tacit aspects to be dealt with, for example, the close relationship between business and IT management that may infuse particular competencies. Is a particular cultural climate conducive to the existence of certain competencies? For example, Zmud etal. (1986) have described the notion of an ‘information economy’ within a company while more recently Davenport and Prusak (1997) has used the term ‘information ecology’ to capture the cultural conditions necessary for effective exploitation of information.

Given our embryonic understanding of micro competencies defining action plans in general could be problematic. McGrath etal. (1995) have proposed a model illustrating that organizational competencies not only derive from personal skills and knowledge (what they refer to as components) but also through effective organizational processes (what they refer to as deft ness) that allow these individual skills to be deployed. While this paper has identified the information competencies for value creation, the challenge is to address the components and deftness dimensions. A crucial research question is therefore how do organizations translate these information competencies into individual skills, knowledge and behavioural characteristics? How can human resources and organizational development programmes help in the creation and maintenance of these competencies? In the recruitment of staff and in defining employee contracts, how do these competencies translate into job descriptions? All of these questions require further research.

## Biographies

Joe Peppard is Senior Research Fellow at the Information Systems Research Centre, Cranfield School of Management. He has held a number of visiting academic positions and has published widely in the information system field. His current research and consulting focuses on how organizations can leverage value and benefits from information technology. He is a Director of Managed Solutions Corporation, a leading solutions vendor to the financial services industry.

Robert Lambert is Senior Lecturer in Management Information Systems at the Cranfield School of Management. Before joining Cranfield, Rob worked in industry for 12 years. He is a former Director of the Cranfield General Management Programme for Specialists and the Cranfield Senior Management Programme. He is interested in the organizational competencies required for IS to enable corporate objectives

Chris Edwards is Professor of Management Information Systems at Cranfield School of Management. Prior to joining academia, Chris worked in industry for 12 years developing business systems. During that time he qualified as a Chartered Management Accountant and hence specialized in financial information systems. He joined the

Scottish Business School as a lecturer in 1971. After receiving a PhD he moved to Carnegie-Mellon University, Pittsburgh, USA and then to the chair at Cranfield. His interests presently lie in IT-enabled business change, particularly in the role required of very senior organizational management to successfully action such change.

## APPENDIX A

## Sample questions for assessing future requirements within macro competence exploit and monitor

EXPLOIT: The ability to develop and apply exploration plan and monitor value creation

What criteria should be used to judge the success of IT in your organization?

Prompts: When projects are delivered on time to budget and specification? When business managers identify and realize benefits? When the organization realises benefits corporate wide and gains strategic advantage?

With regard to business systems implementation, what should be the focus when implementing new systems solutions?

Prompts: Training users in the new application? Training users in the new business process including the IT aspects? Involving users throughout the life cycle of the project enabling them to contribute to the design and development of the business processes?

What should be the primary purpose of information in your organization?

Prompts: To provide reliable, accurate and timely input to support business decisions? To allow the business to understand and maximize the use of business resources? To understand and exploit customer and market trends?

How should IT investment be understood and justified? Prompts: IT resource should be costed in isolation? Both IT and business resource costed together? Full costs of organizational change included? Benefits to be quantified and measured?
