# Reflections on information systems practice, education and research: 10 years of the Information Systems Journal

David Avison\*, Guy Fitzgerald<sup>†</sup> & Philip Powell<sup>‡</sup>

\*ESSEC Business School, 95021 Cergy-Pontoise, France, email: avison@essec.fr, <sup>†</sup>Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, UK, email: guy.fitzgerald@brunel.ac.uk, and <sup>‡</sup>School of Management, University of Bath, Claverton Down, Bath, UK, email: mnspp@management.bath.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper celebrates the 10-year anniversary of the Information Systems Journal (ISJ) and the Editors reflect on the papers that have been published over that period and the changes that have occurred in the discipline of information systems. In the opening paper of ISJ, we suggested that the ‘launch of a new journal in information systems prompts thought and debate concerning the state of the subject area and some contemplation of its past and future’. We discussed three areas of this ‘not-yet-established discipline’: practice, education and research. In this follow-up paper, we forgo our convention of ISJ editors not publishing in the Journal. We examine the issues raised in the first paper and consider what has happened in the intervening years as charted in the ISJ. The overview is necessarily selective, probably Anglocentric (with, perhaps, a slight Francophile tinge), sometimes downright opinionated, as well as over-estimating, perhaps, the contribution of one particular IS journal.

Keywords: Information systems education, information systems practice, infor mation systems research

## I N T RO D U C T I O N

This paper celebrates the 10-year anniversary of the Information Systems Journal (henceforth ISJ). In the mid 1980s, David Avison and Guy Fitzgerald, from their respective UK bases in departments of computer science and management, contemplated the state of information systems (IS). It was essentially the field of applied computing, still known by some as DP (data processing). The perspective was different to that of computer science at the time, and we took the view that we, in IS, stood with our backs to the machine and looked outward towards the world at large, whereas computer science stood in much the same place but looked in. For us, the context was broad and included important issues beyond the technology, including business, organizational and social impacts. We clearly felt that the impact of technology was not just concerned with efficiency, i.e. that it allowed existing activities to be undertaken more quickly and cheaply, but that it was a significant change agent with an ‘enabling’ quality. This wider perspective meant that IS had to embrace a number of associated disciplines and their research approaches, some of which were some considerable distance from the generally quantitative and positivistic methods of computer science. Whether we were right or wrong was open to debate but we felt that the existing institutions were not particularly supportive of this way of thinking and, to take one example, the existing journals did not provide suitable outlets for research adopting this IS view of the world. Neither did they embrace the associated pluralistic research methods. Thus, we adopted a number of strategies to try and change this situation, with the help of the arrogance and blindness of relative youth, and one of these strategies was to establish the ISJ, which was launched at ICIS in December 1990, with the first issue dated January 1991.

So, 10 years later we find ourselves (together with Philip Powell, ISJ’s Managing Editor) again contemplating the field of IS. In that first issue we took the opportunity to write up some of our ‘contemplations of IS’ and as we stated the ‘launch of a new journal in information systems prompts thought and debate concerning the state of the subject area and some contemplation of its past and future’. We discussed three areas of this ‘not-yet-established discipline’: practice, education and research. We now re-examine the issues raised in this first paper and consider what has happened in the intervening years as charted in the ISJ. The overview is necessarily selective, probably Anglocentric, given our backgrounds (with, perhaps, a slight Francophile tinge of late), and sometimes downright opinionated. Nevertheless we hope it makes a contribution to the continuing debate about the discipline of IS.

## THE DEBATE

In our original paper (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1991), we first asked why a debate on information systems practice, education and research might be useful and suggested general opportunities and threats. We were not explicit, but the threats we had in mind included the loss of information systems in the compulsory part of some MBA programmes in the US, the generally small and disparate nature of IS groups in academia, the lack of impact of IS academia on practice and the lack of consensus as to the IS curricula. Yet we also saw many opportunities and strengths. These included the first ICIS outside the US (in Copenhagen), the birth of a number of journals (including the ISJ) and the appearance of some university departments called ‘departments of information systems’.

We also reflected that similar debates in the disciplines of anthropology, English and sociology, had – at least partly – been dogmatic in nature and destructive as a result. Indeed, there are many colleagues who regard such debate as similar to ‘navel gazing’ – interesting, perhaps, but not very useful. Yet others see it as important but ‘yesterday’s question’ and perhaps largely resolved. We do not share this belief. The issues of what curriculum we should teach, what and how we should research the subject and how we can have a greater impact on practice, are not resolved, and, in any case, such debate is at the heart of any growing and living discipline.

It is surely not in dispute that information systems has a number of foundation disciplines (see Backhouse et al., 1991; Truex & Baskerville, 1998), nor is it unique in that respect. We suggested that applied psychology, computer science, economics, ergonomics, ethics, linguistics, mathematics, semiotics, sociology and systems thinking were the main, but not the only, foundation disciplines. We have published papers with their roots in other disciplines that reflect on the information systems context. From a sociological perspective, for example, Bloomfield (1992) looks at the social practices of systems developers and Silverman (1998) examines research methods. Indeed, it can be said that the output of the ISJ reflects and justifies the need for, and strength of, being interdisciplinary.

In 1993 the ISJ had its first special issue, that is, an issue devoted to a particular topic that the editors identified as especially important and relevant to the IS community. Special issues are edited by guest editors who are leaders in their field and who take responsibility for obtaining the papers and putting together an informative and coherent issue on the special topic (fully refereed in the normal way, of course). The first special issue (3.1) was a fine exemplar, being edited by Eden, Jackson, Rosenhead and Tomlinson. It explored the links between oper ational research and information systems and included papers by Checkland & Holwell, Espejo, Walsham and Finlay. The issues discussed, in the area of information management and organizational structure and processes, impinge on both ‘disciplines’ and it is excellent that these leading researchers avoid a narrow disciplinary view. The ISJ continues to welcome proposals for special issues (see Editorial of issue 9.4).

In the original paper, we suggested that information systems has a rich multidisciplinary nature, in which ‘human factors’ are at least as important as ‘technological factors’. Since that time, this view has become ‘established thinking’. Indeed, organizational and social factors may take precedence over technological ones. For example, technology is rarely either the limiting factor in information systems design or is it frequently the cause of IS failure. Factors, such as poor strategy, poor communications, poor control, poor training and user resistance, are much more likely to be behind an IS failure (see, for example, Grover & Teng, 1994; Haga & Zviran, 1994; Mahmood, 1994; Sillince & Frost, 1994; Stowell & West, 1994) or, indeed, their inverse, in terms of success (Powell, 1992; O’Connor, 1993; Lacity & Feeny, 1996). Peppard et al. (2000) looked at value creation arising from IS investments from an organizational strategic perspective. Yetton et al. (2000) looked at the performance of the development project itself and Changchit et al. (1998) also assessed information systems evaluation in their study of 24 projects in 13 organizations. In this context, the paper by Braa & Ogrim (1995) is interesting because of the emphasis on technical quality and documentation in the ISO9001 standard. They conclude that such a standard may well act to the detriment of information systems quality.

In its 10-year life, the ISJ has published 157 high quality papers in 40 issues (the early issues had three, four or five papers, before we standardized on four per issue) with an acceptance rate now of around 12% of submissions. Each paper was reviewed by at least two referees, plus an associate editor, and an editor or managing editor (see the editorial of 9.2 for a statement of our refereeing policy). Very few papers reached publication without going through at least two rounds of changes. Despite the time this takes, the results are impressive as the published papers demonstrate.

ISJ is an international journal, though it is unsurprising that a UK-based publication attracts many UK-authored papers. The 157 papers we have published are the fruits of 282 authors’ labours. Of these 265 are academics and 17 practitioners. In the first half of the ISJ’s life, 47% of authors were UK based, 29% from North America, and around 12% from each of the rest of Europe and Asia-Pacific. As the journal became established, more submissions from outside the UK emerged. In the most recent five issues, 29% of papers are from North America, 16% from Asia-Pacific, 17% from continental Europe and 38% from the UK. In terms of gender, only 21% of our authors are female.

IS is multidisciplinary and this is reflected in the ‘homes’ of the authors. Around a quarter of authors are based in information systems departments (or those with IS somewhere in the title). A similar number come from computer science departments. Five per cent are practitioners while 40% come from business or management schools. The remaining 7% are spread among sociology, anthropology, statistics, design, psychology, management science, engineering and medicine. People, it seems, are working together more these days. In the ISJ’s first 5 years, 42% of papers were single authored whereas more recently this has dropped to 26%. Although, as stated above, we believe that this multidisciplinarity is a positive factor it at the same time causes problems for IS in achieving coherence and establishing itself as a discipline. The wide range of departments and names can cause confusion and result in a lack of focus.

It is difficult to classify papers by topic or research method. But of the 157 papers, 24 were predominantly case studies, 17 dependent on surveys, and 69 were discursive. Espoused methods range through grounded theory, ethnography, laboratory tests, action research, data crunching, surveys and theory extension.

These 157 papers that have survived the ordeal by refereeing are a rich seam upon which to base reflections on IS practice, IS education and IS research.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS PRACTICE

Notwithstanding our view that human factors are at least as important as technological factors, we suggested that information systems started as a subject with the introduction of the computer. There can, of course, be IS without computers. The grapevine, for example, is a powerful information system. Furthermore, if technology is used, it does not have to be sophisticated. But, in practice, information systems are almost invariably computerized. The rejoinder is simply that people will be involved as much as computers, and that not all parts of the information system will be automated.

But the progress of technology, in terms of huge increases in memory and processing power and decreases in physical size, along with reductions in cost has, if anything, accelerated. We mentioned the potential of communications, networks, satellites, CAD/CAM, robotics, video and lasers, many of which have impacted very positively in the intervening decade. Nevertheless, some promised technologies, such as speech recognition and machine translation, seem ‘nearly there’ (as they were 10 years ago). Armstrong (1991) discusses the impact of one technology, CD-ROM, including its limitations, and provides one of the few examples of a paper written by a practitioner as sole author. Another is Kelleher (1995), which confirms that the classic waterfall model of information systems development is inappropriate to the needs of industry. Unfortunately most papers submitted by practitioners fell foul of our stringent refereeing process. This causes us some concern given our belief in the importance of practice and we hope in the future to find a way of maintaining academic and research standards without losing practitioner papers.

Computer applications have changed. The original accounting activities that were computerized did not change the nature of the business and the way of doing things. Typically, existing applications were simply ‘computerized’ and developed in isolation. With database use and more power, some consolidation was possible. Decision support systems, which dipped into the operational databases to provide management information, evolved. Later came enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems that can provide an organization-wide, integrated view of information systems and consume or take in the organization’s legacy systems. Of course, there are down sides, including the necessity sometimes of changing the business to suit the system (Iivari, 1992; amongst many others, argues that the information system should be made to fit the organization), and the overheads and potential negative effects on competitive advantage, of such a generalized system. More important is the largely negative impact on employee morale and, indeed, on employment generally. Lyytinen et al. (1998) investigated the impacts of a next-generation technology platform. Hsiao & Ormerod (1998) looked at how organizations can best cope with IT-enabled strategic change. Lyytinen et al. (1998) suggested requirements for research and practice in the ‘brave new world’ of distributed computing and the web. Other innovations include data warehousing [Loeb et al. (1998) show its impact at IBM and workflow management (Barros & ter Hofstede, 1998)].

Perhaps having an even more dramatic impact on employee morale and employment has been the business process re-engineering (BPR) movement, which, although not necessarily driven by IT and IS, was almost invariably supported by IT and IS changes (sometimes via ERP systems). BPR has proved to be largely unsuccessful despite (or because of) the ‘hype’, and it is no consolation for many to hear from its protagonists that ‘we’re sorry – we forgot the people’. There have been many other fads during the 1990s to add to BPR and ERP, including executive information systems, group decision support systems, outsourcing, learning organizations and knowledge management and, of course, electronic commerce and electronic business. Other movements have included that from centralization and mainframes to client server (and back to mainframes and centralization).

After (almost) every fad there is something left which does offer benefits for practice. However, it seems inevitable that after hyperbole, enthusiasm and implementation, come many disappointments. Only consultants seem to gain, as they recommend one ‘silver bullet’ and then another to put right the failure of the first. Articles in quality journals, such as the ISJ, might be used by organizations to temper the claims of the most enthusiastic consultants. Westrup (1999) argues that in some cases changes in terminology reflect only marginal progress in the underlying technology. He illustrates the change through the object-oriented techniques of the 1990s with the SOP (Study Organize Plan) approach of the 1960s. Perhaps the changes from management information systems to decision support systems to executive information systems, for example, were only marginal as well.

But the growth in the use of PCs and the Internet has been staggering, not least because they are now used in the home. When we used the ARPA network at the then Thames Polytechnic in the early 1970s, with teletypes to print our communications, we did not realize where this would lead. The Internet, the worldwide web and distributed computing in general has been one of the major technical advances of the period, yet as ever it is not just a technical matter but raises important social and organizational issues. In our view, the issues of distributed computing are right at the centre of IS as they exemplify the change and enabling impacts of technology with impacts on organizations and society.

In our original article of 1991, we saw ‘practice’ to mean organizations. It is at least potentially liberating that people are using the Internet and other computer applications in the home. Perhaps this experience can lead to more effective and pleasurable use of IT and IS in all aspects of life. IT applications need not be dehumanizing and destructive, but they certainly can be so.

Along with use in homes, information systems are important in small businesses as well as large ones, and this is often neglected in journals. However, ISJ has published a number of papers with SMEs as the context. Chang & Powell (1998) looked at SMEs in relation to BPR, Martinsons (1991) looked at knowledge-based systems and Ngwenyama & Klein (1994) attempted to define the universe of discourse for knowledge acquisition. Mao & Benbasat (1998) looked at contextualized analysis to knowledge. Newell et al. (2000) linked BPR and knowledge management. Jordan’s (1994) research suggests that large organizations with hierarchical structures present an atypical organizational structure for information systems. Post et al. (1994) also considered both small and large firms in their investigation of IT evaluation. Puuronen & Savolainen (1997) investigated the use of another new technology and compares the use of mobile information systems in the UK, France, Italy and Finland. They found major differences that they see as being due to cultural factors.

In the original article we devoted much space to information systems development. Wynekoop & Russo (1997) provided a useful overview of IS development methodologies. We argued that the traditional life cycle or waterfall model had many problems and suggested that alternative approaches had much to commend them, and mentioned, in particular, ETHICS, Multiview and Soft Systems Methodology (SSM). Although these have their protagonists [Mumford (1991) provided one example of the use of ETHICS at Digital; Watson & Wood-Harper (1995) discussed the use of Multiview; and Checkland & Holwell (1993) looked at SSM at Shell Oil and the British National Health Service] they are not commonly used in practice. Indeed, in her later paper Mumford (1997) asks ‘why isn’t participant systems design more popular?’. Chatzoglou & Macaulay (1996) argue that this is due to attitudes of the people involved or the organizational constraints in which they operate.

Fitzgerald (1996; 1997) argues from his field studies that the adoption of methodologies does not necessarily address the problems inherent in systems development and therefore methodologies are not widely adopted. This is confirmed by Sauer & Lau (1997). Wastell (1996) sees their use, when they are used, as a social defence and though they may help in ‘doing the thing right’ they do not guide their users into ‘doing the right thing’. In other words, the use of methodologies may well not improve systems development. Further, the use of tools (for example, CASE tools) does not necessarily improve the situation nor the resulting applications, as van Hofstede & Verhoe (1996), King (1996), Mathiassen & Sorensen (1996), Butler (2000) and Day (2000) demonstrate. Of course, it is possible to ‘avoid’ the problem through outsourcing development activity. But, as Lacity & Wilcocks (1997), Currie & Willcocks (1998) and Hancox & Hackney (2000) point out, this may not always be successful. Willcocks & Lacity (1999) looked at the issue of risk and outsourcing in detail.

Another special edition (3.3) edited by Mingers looked at soft systems (a topic that crops up relatively regularly in ISJ). Gregory (1993) suggested a Wittgensteinian approach to soft systems and information systems; Lewis (1993) attempted to link SSM with data-oriented IS development in a way similar to Multiview; Doyle et al. (1993) linked soft systems and systems engineering; and Galliers (1993) linked soft approaches with business process redesign. The later papers of Savage & Mingers (1996) and Liang et al. (1998) linked SSM with Jackson’s system development approach and object-oriented analysis respectively.

This reassessment of IS development provides evidence to support our view that research should be used to re-examine common ‘wisdom’ as well as make new discoveries. Olfman & Bostrom (1991) Stowell (1991), Moynihan & Connor (1991), Koh & Heng (1996), Flynn & Jazi (1998) and Taylor et al. (1998) extol the virtues of end-user design and development and foresee the changing role of systems analysts and users. Joshi & Rai (2000) look at IS quality on the basis of users’ job satisfaction.

Of course, such change can bring problems and challenges, and Holsapple et al. (1991) suggested negotiation support systems that can facilitate such changes as well as those due to the impact of technology itself. Lilley (1992) argued that one ‘new technology’, executive information systems (EIS), are ‘disabling executive creativity’ and suggested an alternative framework for executive computer support. Espejo (1993) also looked at the limitations of EIS. McBride (1997) studied an organization where an executive information system was seen initially as a success but a failure in the long run. However, Seeley & Targett’s (1997) study revealed that senior executives (particularly younger ones) are making more extensive use of computers with a wider range of software. Finlay (1993), unusually, looked at information systems aimed at the lone manager/decision maker. Of course, information systems are about change and Ward & Elvin (1999) is one of a number of papers that dealt with IT-enabled process change.

Ramprasad et al. (1993) suggested the use of mental models and cognitive dissonance to increase the effectiveness of executive IS. Galliers & Sutherland (1991) and Mason & Willcocks (1991) also questioned conventional wisdom as they reassessed Nolan’s stages of growth model. Damsgaard & Scheepers (2000) proposed a four-stage model or perspective, but this time for Intranet implementation and management. Grint (1995) saw computers as solutions looking for problems rather than solutions to pre-existing problems. Such a challenge may come from using non-conventional techniques to understand the organization and Walsham (1993) used metaphors to this effect. Melao & Pidd (2000) also used metaphors (and viewpoints) for the understanding of business processes. Straub & Angell (1992) sug gested asking fundamental questions, in this case, on the nature of ‘system’. Another possibility is to use a framework in another domain, such as Wood & Wood-Harper’s (1993) use of Mitroff’s framework in IS.

Issue 9.4 on virtual team-working represents the most recent special edition of the Journal which was edited by John Sillince. Furst et al. (1999) considered virtual team effectiveness and suggested that unless organizations recognize the differences between face-to-face teams and virtual teams, the latter are unlikely to be successful. Warkentin & Beranek (1999) looked at training and raise issues of trust, commitment and frank expression in virtual teams. Jackson (1999) looked at different types of virtual team, and Vickery et al. (1999) considered virtual roles and positions, and suggested that they may create non-traditional commitment structures.

Finally, in the original paper we looked at the status of IT managers, suggesting they were too often seen as low level and isolated. ‘As IT becomes more and more influential in determining the success or otherwise of an organization, then the responsibility for its direction, planning and control must be taken by the most senior management.’ We would largely stick by this statement with one change, that ‘IT’ is replaced by ‘IS’. We see the strategic management of information systems (which encompasses IT) as vital to organizations (see Andreu et al., 1991; Beaumont & Walters, 1991), and we see information management to be a key aspect of this. IT managers, IS managers and even CIOs are, 10 years on, still not frequently found on the boards of firms, though the e-business revolution seems to be having more of an impact on this than any prior force. One solution suggested by Earl & Skyrme (1992) is for the boards of firms to include at least some ‘hybrid managers’ who understand the IS/IT issues, as well as those of at least one other management area.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS EDUCATION

At the time of writing the first article, information systems academics in the UK were mainly found in computer science departments. This reflected a technology-driven view of the world. Furthermore, they were mainly found in the polytechnics (since becoming ‘new’ universities). More recently IS academics are to be found equally spread in management departments as well as computing departments and in the vast majority of universities, indeed, in the US they are mainly to be found in business departments. This is reflected by 40% of ISJ authors being from management departments. Furthermore, there are now a number of departments of information systems, many outgrowing computing departments (just as most of these, in turn, sprang from departments of mathematics).

Information Systems is now much more established as an academic discipline, and it is rare to find a university without some IS academics, even if many groups are still very small and isolated. One recent answer to this isolation has been the establishment of international support groups (in particular, the Association of Information Systems) and national groups [for example, the UK Academy for Information Systems (UKAIS) and the Association of Information Management (AIM) in France]. Such groups have provided an invaluable service, supporting conferences, PhD consortia, regional meetings, newsletters and web sites. They have also acted as pressure groups for IS academics. Relevant conferences, such as ICIS, ECIS and IFIP8.2, have become very successful. It is interesting in this respect that the UKAIS was partly formed as a reaction to the British Computer Society seeking Chartered Engineering status, thereby even further emphasizing its technological roots, whereas IS as a discipline has been broadening in scope away from technology alone.

There are a number of degree programmes specializing in information systems. In the first paper, we contrasted sharply the proposed curricula in the US and the UK. We argued that the former emphasized technology and the latter presented a more balanced curriculum highlighting social and organizational issues as well as technical ones. This is no longer valid, as US curricula are much more balanced than they were. At the postgraduate level, there are still a number of conversion courses and, in the US, ‘hybrid’ MBAs that reflect the demand for hybrid managers are popular. It is also the case that today MBA students are very interested in IS as a result of the impact of the web on business. They now see IS as highly relevant whereas, as mentioned earlier, the loss of IS from MBA programmes was seen as a distinct threat in 1991. Indeed, students are now pushing hard for more IS/web-type courses. The challenge now is for IS to provide the appropriate type of course.

Today, the subject is taught using lectures, seminars, tutorials, laboratory work, case studies and group work, and the use of action learning, considered in our earlier paper as quite a novel approach, is now by no means unusual on IS courses. We have published a number of papers reflecting teaching and learning issues. West (1992), for example, looked at management training in her research on knowledge elicitation. Davis & Bostrom (1994) discussed software training and interface design to help users learn and use software effectively and suggest that ‘fun’ is the key factor. Olfman & Mandviwalla (1995) in a related study looked at the impact of end-user software training manuals for group decision support systems. Shayo et al. (1999), Warkentin & Beranek (1999) and Lee & Truex (2000) also discussed training issues. Lyytinen & Robey (1999) looked at learning and educational barriers. Stolterman (1999) looked at education and training issues related to the design of information systems and Jiang et al. (1999) at skill requirements for systems analysts. The paper of Smithson & Hirschheim (1992) discussed the use of novel ‘playlets’ in university courses, in the form of scripted dialogues, to discuss the potential of end-user computing and the importance of the user–computer interface.

Sometimes in information systems research, students are used for surveys, interviews or as a ‘laboratory context’. The ISJ has been somewhat reluctant to publish such research being more interested in the ‘real world’ as a context for research. Nevertheless, Hughes (1994) suggested students using function point analysis as a way of measuring the functionality of information systems and Conger et al. (1995) correctly regarded student views as important in the context of ethical issues. However, they also interviewed practitioners and the comparative views are particularly interesting. Walsham’s (1996) paper also looked at ethical issues and it provided a critique of the ACM code of ethics.

It is interesting that this section on information systems teaching is the smallest of the three in terms of articles in the ISJ. However, this should not be seen as indicative of its relative unimportance. It simply reflects our view that teaching should itself reflect current practice and research. Our courses cannot be relevant if they do not discuss practice. All the issues discussed in the previous section therefore are also issues to be included in the curriculum. Otherwise our courses in IS are not relevant. Similarly, the curriculum needs to reflect up-todate research, which should itself feed into class discussions so that students are not only informed by textbook material. There have been many submissions on IS teaching, lots of the ‘here’s how we revised our IS courses’ variety. Few have got through initial editorial screening, even fewer got into print. Many are very insular reflecting perhaps that IS teaching practices are not well-disseminated throughout the profession. Many of these ‘innovations’ are standard practice elsewhere or so localized as to be of little interest to an international journal audience. For example, many reflected the need to get some sort of accreditation from a national professional body.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS RESEARCH

Research in IS at the time of the publication of the ISJ in 1991 mainly concerned information systems development (in particular control and methodological aspects), managing organizational change, economic aspects, productivity tools, database modelling (and modelling in general), personal computing, office technology, competitive implications, expert systems and the impact of IT on the nature of work.

Of these, the ‘harder’ aspects, such as design methodologies, productivity tools and office technology, and also research looking at potential gains using IT, such as economic aspects and competitive implications, along with research looking for ‘solutions’ to perennial problems, such as productivity tools, database modelling, personal computing, and expert systems were particularly ‘popular’.

The ISJ, while publishing some papers that place these advances in a social, human and organizational context [such as Kaasboll (1993) paper using object-oriented methods sup porting human–computer interaction and Heintz & Acar (1994) which also considers objectorientation, but this time in the context of group decision support systems], has pioneered the publication of IS research emphasizing social, human and organizational aspects of information systems. The paper in the first issue by Lyytinen et al. (1991) typified this trend and also brought in several contributions of researchers outside the information systems domain. Indeed the paper by Straub & Angell (1991) in the same issue provided an exposé of the limitations of technological systems in organizations and the vital importance of good management practice in interpreting computer output. A particularly interesting aspect of Crnkovic & Holstein’s (1995) research is the context in which it took place, in this case two countries in eastern and central Europe undergoing great change.

Ten years ago we lamented the poor impact of academia on practice. The most important development since that time has been the growth of the Internet and the web. The Internet itself is a development of the US Department of Defence Advanced Projects Research Agency and the web sprang for the work of Tim Berners-Lee at CERN, again a research institution.

The fact that the web is available free and with sensible open protocols is largely due to the fact that it was not developed at a commercial institution.

The lamentation regarding research funding is also much less valid now. In the UK, both the ESRC (Economics and Social Research Funding Council) and the EPSRC (Engineering and Physical Science Research Funding Council) fund IS research, indeed, one recent and successful initiative concerning systems engineering and business processes seems to have been directed at joint research between software engineering and information systems researchers (see http://www.seisn.reading.ac.uk). Many of the acknowledgements in the papers published in the ISJ also recognize contributions from government and private funding agencies.

We did mention the European Esprit programme as potentially helpful, and the paper by Loucopoulos et al. (1991), on models and tools supporting database development, came out of this initiative. However, it is indicative that this paper, along with other earlier publications, Halpin & Orlowska (1992) which was critical of entity-relationship modelling, Smeaton (1992) on hypertext and information retrieval, Oei et al. (1994) on evolving Swatman & Swatman (1992) on formal specification using EDI as an application area, are perhaps the most technical of any we have published.

Databases are mainly seen as technical artefacts and research tends to emphasize these aspects, but it need not be so. Beynon-Davies (1992) discussed the sociology, semiotics and pedagogy of database work. Rao et al. (1995) and Li & Williams (1999) looked at EDI, the precursor to business-to-business electronic commerce, but stressed the social and organi zational factors rather than the technical ones.

But we are not only interested in what we research. We are also interested in how we research. Because we are concerned with people and organizations as well technology, our research approach is rarely purely ‘scientific’. We cannot repeat our experiments unless they concern technology alone. Colomb & Orlowska (1995) also challenged the assumption that it is possible to suggest general solutions based on any research.

Previously, much IS research that was published was based on the interpretation of questionnaire/surveys. This approach is often used in the sciences and can rely on statistical techniques, but the results are usually interesting and relevant only when the writer begins to interpret the information through subjective means. Such research can be very valuable. Berg eron & Raymond (1992) and Vandenbosch & Higgins (1995), for example, both used a survey to evaluate the impact of executive information systems on managers. Castner & Ferguson (2000) used the approach to consider software diffusion, Yetton et al. (2000) used the approach to assess development project performance and Willcocks (1992) evaluated IT investments based on a survey of 50 organizations. Drury & Farhoomand (1997) discussed the use and misuse of survey research.

King & Kluger (1993) used a laboratory experiment to test the influence of rhetorical schemes designated ‘emotional’ and ‘logical’ on innovation decisions, and Wastell (1996) useed laboratory simulation. Flynn & Warhurst (1994) combined open-ended questionnaires and interviews in their study of systems analysts and Jones (1994) used semi-structured interviews with users of Apple Macintosh computers to elicitate attitudes to PC use. Interviews are often used in IS research and Hunter (1997) showed how repertory grids can be used to gather interview data. Darke & Shanks (1997) suggested that these repertory grids should be used in a mix of techniques including brainstorming, cognitive mapping and rich pictures when doing investigatory work.

However, the ISJ has pioneered alternative approaches to IS research, including case studies (see below), action research (see below), hermeneutics (Myers, 1995; Klecun-Dabrowska, 2000), ethnography (Harvey, 1997; Nandhakumar & Jones, 1997; Silverman, 1998; Seltsikas, 1999), critical thinking (Jackson, 1992; Mingers, 1995; Warren & Adman, 1999), agency theory (Karake, 1995; Hancox & Hackney, 2000), speech act theory (Ngwenyama & Klein, 1994, Janson & Woo, 1996; van Reijswoud et al., 1999), structuration theory (Brooks, 1997), post-modernist theory (Janson et al., 1997), grounded theory (Calloway & Ariav, 1995; Hunter & Beck, 1996), feminist theory (Harvey, 1997), personal construct theory (Lee & Truex, 2000) and phenomenological research (King, 1996).

We now highlight case studies and action research. Case studies are particularly useful and the paper by Farbey et al. (1995), based on 16 organizations, exemplifies their use in a study of IT evaluation. Another, by Wareham et al. (1998) looked at a Danish firm attempting to empower their technicians through a computer control system. Darke et al. (1998) showed how the case study can combine rigour, relevance and pragmatism. Henfridsson (2000) used an interpretive case study to explore the way that IT systems and artefacts are used, viewed, perceived and adapted as a product of social action. Beynon-Davies et al. (2000) employed an in-depth case study to investigate a rapid application development (RAD) project at British Telecommunications. Cavaye (1996) is a particularly frequently cited paper that discussed the different forms that case study research can take. Silverman (1998) suggested possible problems in the use of case studies and draws attention to the potential of using video rather than the more conventional interviews in case study research.

We suggested in our original paper that action research was particularly open to criticism because it recognizes that the researcher is not impartial and, worse, it is sometimes seen as lacking rigour, indeed, being no more than consultancy. We were brave enough to include a paper in that first issue (Mansell, 1991) extolling the virtue of this research approach with its emphasis on applying knowledge to practice. Wood-Harper (1992) added to this debate, and Baskerville (1993), Reponen (1994), Vidgen (1997), Kock & McQueen (1998), Davison & Vogel (2000) and Allen et al. (2000) provided further examples of papers based on action research. Remenyi & Williams (1996) suggested that there is room in IS for many research approaches, both quantitative and qualitative. The essential aspect is that the research method is used appropriately. Truex & Baskerville (1998) is a well-cited, indeed, an exemplar paper that is theoretical, an example of what is sometimes termed ‘conceptual study’.

We hope that the Journal is also challenging. Wand & Weber (1993), for example, ‘on the ontological expressiveness of information systems analysis and design grammars’ received two very positive reviews and one negative review (all three by leading IS academics). This seemed to us likely to suggest a far more interesting paper than one that receives three moderately favourable reviews. The arguments of the third reviewer hinged on the philosophical basis of the paper and suggested that it is impossible to develop a grammar for expressing real-world phenomena. Such debates ought to be aired. This work was developed further in Wand & Weber (1995) and Weber & Zhang (1996). The paper by Nordbotten & Crosby (1999) on graphic models was also controversial and came with a commentary by Venable (1999) and a reply by the original authors.

Most startling of all is the change in policy of MIS Quarterly (rarely challenged in its status as the leading journal in IS) during the 10 years of the ISJ. In September 1989 it suggested that a paper ‘in the Research and Theory category should . . . be based on a set of welldefined hypotheses, unbiased and reproducible . . . often [involving] the collection of considerable quantitative data through such means as laboratory experiments or survey instruments. The data are then subjected to statistical analysis’. In the March 1993 issue of MISQ, there was a broadening of this stance as they welcomed ‘research based on positivist, interpretive, or integrated approaches [though] we remain strong in our commitment to hypothesis testing and quantitative data analysis’. The present editor, Allen Lee, has bravely further opened the methods of acceptable research (but not without opposition, as recipients on the ISWorld list serve looking at the various messages between Allen and his opponents will be all too aware). We like to think that the contribution of the ISJ over the past 10 years has contributed in small part to bringing about such positive changes.

## CONCLUSIONS

This follow-up paper presents a much more positive view of information systems practice, teaching and research than the very tentative original article. The subject is far more coherent, widespread and influential. IS has matured and is more at ease with itself with a more mature infrastructure. It is now generally accepted that the information systems world is one where human, social and organizational factors are as important as the technological. Curricula are much broader than the early curricula, which emphasized technological aspects. IS is well established in management schools and also in computing departments, and IS now has some departments to call its own. Those management schools, for example, that do not have an IS group look somewhat limited in these days of e-business. They must lose many potential students and practitioner clients as a result. IS academics have many international and national associations and conferences of excellent repute. The many IS journals, some well established and others established after the ISJ, are much more open to a wider view of ‘acceptable’ research, much of which relates to ‘softer’ issues, often using qualitative research approaches. Plurality of method is now no longer frowned upon. Research funding, from government and private funding bodies, is much more widely available. IS is commercially important and recognized as such. Many of the ‘hot issues’ in information systems are also ‘hot issues’ in management generally, so the impact of IS on practice (and vice versa) is also much more prominent. Some CIOs are now becoming CEOs. Amethodological systems development is just as prevalent now as it was 10 years ago. But now at least we know this to be the case, we have alternatives, we understand some of those alternatives, and further we cannot kid ourselves that the lifecycle model is a panacea. We may be beyond method and perhaps beyond tools. Yet, IS research should have a greater impact on practice. Perhaps weaned on the many reports of IS failures, information systems academics are wary and cynical of quick fixes such as BPR – but we were not listened to. We trust that the next 10 years will be equally positive and that the ISJ again reflects these changes.

It is dangerous to speculate on the future, especially in a field as fast changing as information systems. But it is interesting to brain-storm about the sort of terms that may apply to education, practice and research in IS in the next decade. Looking at the practice of IS, terms such as mobility, ubiquity, interconnection, virtual, networked, skills, IS not IT, globalization and polarization come to mind. For education, global, harmonization, accreditation, professionalism, and supply and demand arise. In IS research, maturity, virtual, plurality, interdisciplinary, culture, and electronic are terms we expect to see. Technology may change but the mindset and the context often does not.

The ISJ has published 157 excellent papers about information systems. It has received many hundreds more papers that unfortunately did not make it, but hopefully found a home elsewhere. This decade of publication has only been possible via a combined effort of the IS community acting as authors, associate editors, reviewers, editorial board, publishers and printers, advisors and readers. We would like to thank them all for being part of this contribution to the world of IS and hope that you will join us in the exploration of the next decade.

## Biographies

David Avison is now Professor of Information Systems at ESSEC Business School, near Paris, France after 9 years as Professor at Southampton University. He is also visiting Professor at Brunel University, UK; University Technology, Sydney, Australia; and University of Dunedin, New Zealand. As well as being joint editor of the Information Systems Journal, he is joint editor of the McGraw-Hill series of texts in Information Systems (also with Guy Fitzgerald). So far, 17 books are to his credit (plus one translation from the French) as well as a large number of papers in learned journals, edited texts and conference papers. He is Chair of the International Federation of Information Processing (IFIP) 8.2 group on the impact of IS/IT on organizations and was past President of the UK Academy for Information Systems. He has been joint programme chair of the research papers stream of the ICJS Conference in Atlanta, programme chair of the IFIP conference in Amsterdam, panels chair for the European Conference in Information Systems at Copenhagen and publicity chair for the entity-relationship conference in Paris and chair of several other UK and European conferences.

Guy Fitzgerald is Professor of Information Systems at Brunel University. Prior to this he was the Cable and Wireless Professor of Business Information Systems at Birkbeck College, University of London. He has also held posts at Warwick and Oxford Universities and worked in the computer industry with companies such as British Telecom, Mitsbishi and CACI Inc, International. His research interests are concerned with information systems and infor mation management and he has published widely in these areas. His main research has been concerned with systems development methodologies and the effective development of systems. He is author, with David Avison, of a book entitled Information Systems Development: Methodologies, Techniques and Tools. In the area of information management he has addressed issues of strategy and has undertaken an in-depth study of IT outsourcing in the UK, with Leslie Willcocks. He is founder and editor of the Information Systems Journal and is editor of the IS series of books from McGraw-Hill (both jointly with David Avison).

Philip Powell is Professor of Information Management at the University of Bath. He was formerly, Professor of Information Systems, University of London and Director of the Information Systems Research Unit at Warwick Business School. Prior to becoming an academic he worked in insurance, accounting and computing. His main interests are IS strategy and planning particularly for small businesses and in healthcare, IS evaluation, issues of flexibility in and from IS, interorganization systems, and e-commerce. He is the author of four books on information systems and financial modelling. He has published numerous book chapters and his work has appeared in over 60 refereed international journals. He is Managing Editor of the Information Systems Journal, Book Reviews Editor of the Journal of Strategic Information Systems, an Associate Editor of OR Insight and on a number of other editorial boards. He is an elected Board Member of the UK Academy for Information Systems. He serves on the Alliance for IS Skills steering group.
