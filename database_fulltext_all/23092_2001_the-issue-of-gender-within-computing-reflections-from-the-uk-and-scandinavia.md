# The issue of gender within computing: reflections from the UK and Scandinavia

Maxine Robertson\*, Sue Newell<sup>†</sup>, Jacky Swan\*, Lars Mathiassen<sup>‡</sup> & Gro Bjerknes<sup>§</sup>

\*Warwick Business School, Warwick University, Coventry, CV4 7AL, UK, email: Maxine.Robertson@warwick.ac.uk, irobjs@wbs.warwick.ac.uk; <sup>†</sup>Management School, Royal Holloway University of London, Egham, TW20 0EX, UK, email:

Susan.Newell@rhul.ac.uk; <sup>‡</sup>Aalborg University, Frederik Bajers vej 7E, DK-9220 Aalborg East, Denmark, email: larsm@cs.auc.dk; <sup>§</sup>Avenir ASA, Norway; email: gb@avenir.no

Abstract. This paper explores some of the reasons that may underlie the gender segregation and declining levels of female participation within the field of computing in Europe during the 1990s in both the professional (industrial) and academic spheres. The interrelationships between three areas – communicative processes, social networks and legitimizing claims to knowledge overlaid by gendered-power relations – are used to analyse and explain the existing situation. The paper draws upon statistical data to explore the extent of gender segregation and then focuses on the authors’ own experiences within the UK and Scandinavia in order to explore some of the underlying causes. While direct discrimination does still occur, the paper suggests that indirect, deep-rooted discrimination is the major reason for the situation that currently exists. Drawing upon our own experiences in academia and business and acknowledging the importance of the institutional context, the paper offers a number of recommendations as to how the current situation may be improved. We suggest first that consideration is given to the pedagogical design and marketing of computing courses so that individuals are initially attracted to computing from far more diverse backgrounds, approaches and interests than at present. Second, we suggest that those with influence in the field reflect upon the constitution and behaviours of the informal networks in which they are involved and seek to include female researchers more actively here. Finally we suggest that consideration is given in more general terms to how the field may become more gender neutral and, thus, more inclusive in the future. Masculine discourses and ‘hard’ skills have dominated within computing for too long and contribute significantly to the declining participation of women within computing.

Keywords: Gender, information systems, information technology

## INTRODUCTION

Gender – ‘patterned, socially produced, distinctions between female and male, feminine and masculine’ (Acker, 1992; p. 250) – is a key concept for understanding the degree of male and female participation within any field. This paper seeks to explore and develop an understanding of some the reasons that may underlie (i) the gender segregation that currently exists within the broad, interrelated fields of information systems (IS), information technology (IT) and computer science (CS) and (ii) the declining levels of female participation within computing in Europe during the 1990s. Information systems, information technology and computer science represent three closely related academic fields within what is referred to here as the computing discipline. These three fields and the computing profession more generally are very clearly gender segregated, with a dominance of men at all levels. The male dominance generally is nothing new. As Booth (1999) notes, while the first software ever written for a machine was produced by a woman – Ada Lovelace – in 1840, it is a male, Charles Babbage, inventor of the difference calculating engine, who is generally accepted as the founding father of computing. Booth concludes: ‘And that, in microcosm, has been how the IT industry developed over the next 160 years – a combination of rapid technical advance leading to skills crises while half the nation’s workforce has been routinely overlooked’ (p. 47).

More recently this picture has changed somewhat with increasing numbers of women in computing; however, they still remain a minority. Thus, participation rates for women in the IT sector have fluctuated somewhere between 19% and 22% as a whole during the 1990s [while overall female participation rates in the UK labour force since the early 1990s has never fallen below 45% (Panteli et al., 1999)]. In addition, women in this broad area continue to be segregated both horizontally and vertically, both in academia and in business. In terms of horizontal segregation, women are found working much more often in what are considered to be ‘softer’ aspects of the profession, for example, in sales and marketing and support functions such as the help desk and customer service. These areas require good interpersonal skills and women tend to be gender stereotyped as almost naturally having such skills (Wilson, 1999). Men, however, continue to dominate in technical areas such as analyst/ programming (Panteli et al., 1999). In terms of vertical segregation computing is still dominated by men, and the imbalance becomes more striking on the higher rungs of the corporate hierarchy (Candee, 1997). Candee provides a number of examples to illustrate this vertical segregation. For example, in the Society for Information Management, a US organization of senior IT executives, only 195 of its 2700 members were women in 1997. In the UK in 1996, women constituted only 11.7% of the management posts in IT. However, women formed the majority of help-desk assistants (62%) and IT administrators (64%). This segregation is reflected in a continued wage differential. For example, the average salary of women in the IT industry in 1996 in the UK was only £26 026 compared with men at £29 870. In addition, while 39% of men earned £30 000 only 27% of women earned over this amount (Panteli et al., 1999).

This male dominance in business areas of computing is similarly reflected in academia. For example, at ECIS (The European Conference on Information Systems) in 1998, of the 300 participants, only 35 (approximately) (this figure is approximate as some names were ambiguous) were women. Moreover, female participation rates on computer science courses across Europe at all levels (undergraduate, Masters and PhD), considered by many to be a necessary prerequisite to enter the field of computing, have actually declined quite dramatically through the 1980s and 1990s (Lander & Adam, 1997; Panteli et al., 1999). Thus, the numbers of women enrolling for computing courses fell from a high of 28% in 1978 to 13% in 1985 and down to around 9% in 1998 (Booth, 1999). In other words the computing discipline continues to attract mainly men on to educational courses and subsequently in to more senior technical positions in industry.

This situation appears to be quite distinctive to computing and appears to be the core of the problem. For example, while in computing there has been a decline in the proportion of women, in most other academic fields women’s representation has increased and is becoming more reflective of overall population patterns. For example, across all academic disciplines the numbers of women now constitute almost one-half of the undergraduate population in the UK. This highlights the fact that female participation rates on University level courses have increased fairly dramatically in the last 10–20 years. Importantly, many traditionally male-dominated academic disciplines such as medicine and veterinary science are witnessing significant increases in female participation such that female rates are gradually overtaking male participation rates (Grundy, 1996). Clearly, as seen, the opposite trend is occurring in the computing discipline. In response to this situation it is becoming widely acknowledged (notably by both the UK and Scandinavian governments) that female participation within all of the scientific disciplines, but particularly within computing needs, to be encouraged if skill requirements into the new millennium are to be met (DeBare, 1999; Panteli et al., 1999). Therefore, attempts to understand the reasons for what appears to be declining levels of female participation currently within these fields within Europe are relevant and timely.

Obviously there are a multitude of factors that could be considered to help explain this continued segregation. The specific focus here, however, is on the interrelationships between communicative processes, social networks and legitimizing claims to knowledge overlaid by gendered-power relations. These interrelationships we also see to be influenced by the institutional context. These are considered by reflecting upon the authors’ own experiences in Scandinavia and the UK. Drawing also upon existing literature that has considered the issue of gender and computing, this paper aims to develop a critical insight into the processes through which female participation in computing at all levels is discouraged and hindered.

## UNDERSTANDING THE PERPETUATION OF GENDER SEGREGATION WITHIN COMPUTING

The gendered pattern of computing could be discounted as simply a reflection of the broader pattern of horizontal and vertical segregation in employment rather than anything specific to computing (Hirsch & Jackson, 1990). Yet if it is discounted in this way it effectively absolves the professional computing community from any responsibility to take action to promote change. The paper, therefore, aims through reasoned and supported argument (rather than polemic) to promote debate within the field concerning ways to stimulate the necessary changes if we are to encourage more women to enter the field at all levels and across all sub-areas.

In this section, we consider a number of explanations for gender segregation within computing. These range from overt forms of discrimination to more covert mechanisms that serve to preclude women from the profession. These covert forms, we argue, may be more significant and insidious and are not necessarily addressed by current programmes that aim to increase female participation in the field.

The observed segregation of men and women within the computing profession is partly the result of blatant gender bias. For example, DiDio (1997) looked at the continued problems of job discrimination in the computer industry. She interviewed a number of women working in Silicon Valley and found numerous examples of women still grappling with problems of being assigned menial tasks, whereas their male colleagues with similar backgrounds were given ‘choice’ assignments. Dido coined the term ‘techno-hazing’ to describe this phenomenon of gender bias. Techno-hazing refers to the bias that results from the assumption that women cannot be technically competent and so, even when qualified, they are given jobs that are more menial than their male peers, or are supervised more closely.

Blatant discrimination is visible and there is, therefore, the potential for it to be addressed with fairly direct action such as legislation. Indeed, many companies and universities have Equal Opportunities policies which, in many instances, attempt to go further than simply complying with the legislation. For example, many require their employees across the organization to take sexual harassment and sensitivity classes. However, while such initiatives can help to combat direct discrimination they do little to eradicate the majority of discrimination because this is much more indirect, subtle and insidious. This indirect gender bias is not really seen or acknowledged and is, therefore, more difficult to address. As Meyerson & Fletcher (2000) observe gender inequity across industry has not vanished, but has simply gone underground, perpetuated ‘in a plethora of work practices and cultural norms that only appear unbiased’ (p. 127). Thus, gender discrimination accounting for the segregation that has been observed earlier, does not necessarily occur because of direct discrimination, rather it occurs because of the deep-seated beliefs about jobs, tasks, roles and people which are communicated and reinforced continuously within organizations and which perpetuate the continued inequality and power differences (Wilson, 1997). Below, we consider three processes in particular, which we see as important in perpetuating this ‘unseen’ bias: (1) The marketing and pedagogic design of IT/IS/CS courses; (2) The exclusion of women from important and influential networks; (3) The difficulty in gaining legitimacy as an expert in fields such as IT and IS. The paper will conclude by considering the interrelationships between these three processes and more general processes of communication which serve to reinforce and perpetuate the gender segregation that currently exists.

## Communicative processes – the marketing and pedagogic design of IT/IS/CS courses

The most optimistic statistics produced by the Higher Education Statistical Agency (Siann, 1997) show that females in the UK make up only 19% of students majoring in CS at university. In countries such as Denmark and Sweden the picture is even worse. For example, one of the authors reports that female participation rates within his department (of Computer Science) at the University of Aalborg in Denmark were less than 5% up until a few years ago. Research by Salminen-Karlsson (1997) in Sweden concurs with this highlighting that female participation rates on CS courses are only around 5–7%. It also needs to be acknowledged here that often it is within departments of computer science that not only CS but also IS and IT are taught. This small minority of women taking university courses also find that the courses on the whole are taught by men (Wilson, 1999). Thus, there is clearly a gender bias in students recruited on to university courses, which obviously filters through subsequently into the job market. Fewer female graduates translates into fewer IT professionals in both industry and academia.

At least part of the problem of recruiting female graduates relates to the content of courses and how they are presented and taught. This concurs with a more general image of the computing profession, which exists within society. As Booth (1999) comments ‘few women yet see IT as a desirable profession, which is a reflection of its techie image’. If we first consider computing discourse, attention needs to be given to the way in which courses are promoted to the potential student population. We, therefore, need to focus upon the social construction of the computing domain. That is the way the domain has been constructed through social processes, interaction and language. For example, Turkle (1988) highlights that while the computer itself displays no gender bias computer culture and the socially constructed discourse that surrounds computing is not equally neutral. Wilson (1999) suggests that there is a legacy in computer culture of masculine images of competition, sport and violence, enhanced with a discourse that resorts to using such phrases as killing or aborting programs. In addition, words such as workbenches, tool kits, drives, engines and so forth are commonplace day-to-day jargon in computing and these words all have masculine connotations. How then can we begin to address the dominant, socially constructed masculine world of computing in which students of both genders on computer courses perceive computing to be a man’s job? (Wilson, 1997).

In order to begin to address this situation an obvious starting point would appear to be the pedagogical design and marketing of computer-related courses in order to encourage more women into the field. Over time, and by promoting greater diversity (including a critical mass of women) in the field, there may be then the opportunity to address the dominant masculine computing culture. However, as Salminen-Karlsson (1997) notes this is not a simple matter. She studied attempts by three of Sweden’s five technical universities to reform their computer engineering courses in order (it was suggested), to specifically attract more women. The initial results appeared promising in that female participation on all of the reformed courses did increase. However, Salminen-Karlsson identified that the reforms instigated by the dominant male elites in these technical universities actually had little to do with a genuine, committed desire to increase the numbers of women on these courses. There was in fact a fundamental unwillingness on the part of the reform teams to challenge the masculine culture or normativity of the male at the institutional level. Gender issues were not actually given due regard during the planning of the reform process precisely because of the low hierarchical status of the women involved in the reform teams. Hence, the women involved had very little influence on decision making and there was a very low awareness of gender issues by the teams despite the rhetoric about attracting more women. This study highlights the tokenism that can occur even when the under-representation of women on computing courses is raised as a specific issue. In these Swedish institutions, the increase in female participation seems to have occurred solely because of the pedagogical changes that were introduced which broadened the scope of study. These happened more by accident than from a real understanding of the gender issues involved. Nevertheless it is such pedagogical reform of computing courses, stimulating an interest and awareness in the field across a far more diverse range of individuals (regardless of gender) than at present that is likely to be the most successful strategy for encouraging change in the gender mix. Through this strategy diversity will be encouraged and a critical mass of women will develop. This will then help to challenge the dominant masculine discourses that currently exist.

This can be illustrated with an example from one of the authors. Lars Mathiassen has been professor at the Department of Computer Science at Aalborg University in Denmark for the past 12 years. He and the rest of his department were particularly concerned with declining female participation rates from around 15% in the 1980s to less than 5% during the 1990s. In order to address what the predominantly male department initially considered to be a gender issue, they decided to offer a special version of CS education in which female students would only work together during the first years of study and integrate with the male students later. This initiative, labelled ‘Computer Science for Women’, was launched in 1997. Not surprisingly perhaps, it did not motivate increasing numbers of women to apply for CS courses as it was simply reinforcing stereotypical perceptions of gender segregation. In addition, members of the department also found that most of the students (male and female) who were taking CS courses at the university at this time were really only effective as technical problem solvers and they tended to be solely preoccupied with technical issues. Their approach to interpreting complex real world situations was to simplify and reduce issues in order to explicitly formulate and commonly agree upon problems that could serve as starting points for purely technical interventions. These students were often ignoring the organizational implications of their technical fixes. These observations then, together with the failure of the ‘Computer Science for Women’ initiative, highlighted the fact that in order to attract students of both genders with more diverse and eclectic interests, and not just technical interests and backgrounds, a radical redesign of CS education was needed.

To achieve this a new science education was developed called Informatics. This was grounded in well-known educational elements, but combined in an innovative and timely fashion and based on the overall model outlined in Figure 1 (Mathiassen & Stage, 1999).

According to this model the commencement of the programme begins with ‘a design-oriented study’ (see for example Ehn, 1988; Madsen, 1989; and Stolterman, 1992).

Then, following on from this, two different disciplines are studied – one focusing on IT and another concentrating on IT use (see for example Andersen, 1990; Dahlbom, 1996; and Dahlbom & Mathiassen, 1997). Finally, these elements are merged and combined into one, in-depth study of the design of IT use. When launched in 1998 this new form of study was an immediate success in terms of attracting a more diverse range of individuals. Many designoriented students of both genders signed up for the initiative and 25% of them were female. This pattern was also repeated in 1999. If this pattern continues, quite soon there will be significant numbers of women studying Infomatics at Aalborg University.

There are at least two lessons from this experience. First, if we want to attract highly diverse students with interests that go beyond technical aspects of the field then, regardless of gender, we need to rethink and redesign the whole concept of what constitutes computing courses. In particular, we need to go beyond the languages of science and engineering. In this case, re-labelling the education ‘Informatics’ and changing its content accordingly appeared to have a positive communicative effect. In contrast, simply trying to attract more women onto existing courses and repackaging existing courses specifically for women does not work and may be deemed by some as overtly discriminatory. Second, the experiences presented here from Aalborg University suggest that serious attempts to attract people interested in the social and organizational aspects of technology by conscious radical redesign of computing courses is likely to increase the percentage of female students on such courses per se. Thus, where courses are redesigned to incorporate a multiplicity of skills and not just ‘hard’ technical skills, female recruitment increases. This occurs regardless of whether females are targeted in a marketing campaign.

As a further example in the UK, Salford University’s Information Systems Institute (ISI) achieves a higher than average female intake (although still only 30%) by structuring its courses so it can address graduates who want to develop both IT and business skills. Perhaps even more significantly, one of the most successful Masters level courses in the UK run by the London School of Economics not only attracts significant numbers of students from all over the world but interestingly attracts high proportions of women. The course is an MSc in the Analysis, Design and Management of Information Systems (ADMIS). Since 1994 the course has run with at least 140 students each year of which, on average, 41% are women. The Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council undertook a review of the course in 1998 and decided that its combination of technical and managerial issues associated with IS should serve as a model for other institutions to copy. What is noticeable about this course is that it is designed in such a way that no formal computing experience is required although students are expected to have a basic familiarity with desktop productivity tools (or a willingness to learn how to use them in their own time). In addition, there is no requirement to enter the course with programming knowledge. Any programming skills that are required are taught on the programme. Options within the course allow students to specialize in political, economic, or technical aspects of IS depending on student’s particular interests. At the London School of Economics then, the emphasis is on attracting students from a variety of backgrounds regardless of gender, and the pedagogical design of the course appears to have achieved this. It could also be argued that the combination of IT and management skills that are developed on this course make the course very attractive to employers and hence to students regardless of gender.

These examples from Scandinavia and the UK highlight the way in which the marketing and communication of IT/IS/CS courses to potential students and their pedagogical design are crucial if we are to attract a more diverse range of individuals (including women) into computing-related subjects.

## Social networks – The exclusion of women from social networks

While female student numbers are small and appear to be falling, there is an even greater problem in terms of women actually working in the profession – in industry and academia. Female academics are few and far between in computing departments. For example, an analysis of staff ratios in computing departments across all of what are known as the ‘old’ universities in the UK revealed that women constituted only 13.4% of lower level teaching and research posts, falling to 6% at senior levels. In addition, 18% of computing departments actually had no women academics at all (Grundy, 1996). As it is these very departments that are responsible for the pedagogical design and marketing of computing courses, the lack of women (particularly in positions of influence where they can affect change) must be considered a critical issue.

Developing this analysis further, if there are so few female academics within the field then it follows that their presence and influence within important powerful networks within the international academic community will also be minimal. The European Conference on Information Systems, for example, is a major event within the IS community, and those individuals involved in the organization of this academic forum could therefore be perceived as particularly influential within the field. The poor representation of women within this network at all levels is, however, evident, as demonstrated in Table 1.

Belonging to networks (both formal and informal) has long been recognized as important for career progression. Networking informally, for example, allows people to develop an ‘intelligence or communication base’, which may help them to penetrate the formal positions. Indeed, in research by Coe (1992), which included data from a large sample of female managers in the UK, the ‘old boys network’ was identified as the single biggest barrier to their career progression, ahead of domestic juggling and stereotypes. These informal networks tend to be strong among those who work together, which, as seen, within the field of computing happen mainly to be men. Such informal communication can and does, therefore, perpetuate the status and power inequalities within organizations (Coe, 1992).

Within the computing profession, women are poorly represented within both powerful formal networks (e.g. institutional hierarchies) and informal forums (e.g. editorial boards, programme committees.). It follows then that women will have a generally low level of influence. This makes it difficult, if not impossible, currently for women to effect any changes to the current status quo and masculine culture of computing. The example of the low female representation on the ECIS organizing committee highlights this point and can be interpreted as an illustrative example of the way in which exclusionary informal communication processes occurring within social networks dominated by men sustain opaque power structures within the computing discipline. These are even more difficult to challenge than the more transparent institutionalized power structures that constitute hierarchies in universities and industry.

The opaque power structures are in part formed through the informal networks that operate across institutions and countries. These structures are very powerful in that they work by reference only, i.e. one cannot apply to become a member, one has to be (informally) invited to participate. It is largely these networks that control editorial boards, programme committees and appointment panels in academia. The power is opaque insofar as it is taboo, particularly within academia, to ask why people have been chosen to hold particular roles or participate in different tasks or decision making. In this way, the informal networks to some extent decide upon the acceptance of papers for publication at refereed conferences, in refereed journals and formal positions. We are not suggesting here that academia is corrupt. Extremely good papers and candidates will usually be acknowledged and recognized, as will very weak ones, and the ‘blind’ review process does ensure some level of impartiality. However, significantly it is when we enter the ‘grey zone’ that the opaque power manifest in these networks characterized by informal communication flows operate in favour of their own members, who are predominantly male. For example, editorial boards are more likely to accept mediocre papers from ‘known stars’ who are typically male (or at least invite them to resubmit) than from junior researchers (which will include most female academics). In addition, the notion of what constitutes a ‘good’ paper is necessarily subjective, and those involved in the review process will tend to select like-minded papers as good.

From personal experience in both academia and business as a computer professional in Scandinavia, one of the authors suggests that this situation is somewhat unique to academia (and obviously not confined to the field of computing). (This suggestion is based on anecdotal evidence which, while supported by some literature, will need to be more rigorously tested by research.) In industry, while informal power structures often do exist concurrently with the formal hierarchy, it is considered perfectly legitimate to question particular decisions and to expect answers. So there exists at least the possibility to examine and discuss the reasoning behind decisions within a business context. This opportunity, however, simply does not exist within the male-dominated field of computing in academia. Hence, a situation exists whereby women actually encounter a ‘glass door’ on entry to the international computing community rather than encountering the better known ‘glass ceiling’ as their careers progress (Hemenway, 1995; Meyerson & Fletcher, 2000). So a PhD degree is the generally acknowledged ‘entrance ticket’ to these informal networks and our experiences suggest that when a man has successfully completed a PhD, he will normally become a member of at least one informal network. From there, he will be recommended to other networks, and to positions where he becomes visible within the international community, e.g. program committees and editorial boards. He will also be taught quite informally by more experienced members of the network the ‘proper’ strategic behaviour that should be adopted. In this way the networks and network relations become self-reinforcing, sustaining particular behaviours, including the exclusion of women. This ‘indeterminate knowledge’ – knowledge that is not discussed in formal settings – is extremely problematic for women to acquire for as we have shown it is very difficult for women to become members of such networks in the first place or even to question their existence. Thus, the socialization processes of men and women in the field are quite different. This means that women are required to expend enormous effort on self-promotion and can only learn the hard way, whereas the majority of men can rely on existing (largely informal) relations to market themselves. Women, on the other hand, have to engage in what is referred to as ‘cold sales’ – marketing themselves directly to those with whom they may have had no previous contact.

In addition, although collaborative and collegiate relations are considered to characterize academia, an underlying value is competition. This is articulated succinctly by R. C. Lewontin, professor in biology: ‘Research is a kind of competitive and aggressive business, a contest man against man [sic] where knowledge creation is an additional (extra) product (Løvlie, 1998). Of course competition is paramount in industry, but most contemporary organizations recognize that a degree of co-operation is imperative for delivering good quality to the customer. Importantly, therefore, industry has acknowledged that too much competition is counter productive. This is not so in academia. Competition is an underlying characteristic of academic work at both an individual and institutional level. The minority of women in computing departments are, therefore, in the unenviable position of being ‘outsiders’ in a highly competitive environment. Moreover, it is not ‘culturally’ acceptable as an academic to discuss even informally the unequal opportunities that exist in academia where the whole identity and ethos of the institutional context is premised on collegiality, mentoring and patronage. This, in combination with the opaque power structures of academia, means that women are quite simply massively disadvantaged. We are suggesting, therefore, that largely through processes of informal communication women encounter significantly more problems progressing within the academic community of computing than men.

While there are no easy answers to this (and this obviously does not only apply to computing) we suggest that members of editorial boards and program committees may wish to reflect upon their membership and seek out and include active female researchers. We are not advocating tokenism here. Rather we are suggesting that decisions should be made by the most powerful people in these informal networks (a very small minority of whom we acknowledge are women) to consciously sponsor active female researchers, treating them so that they are not included under sufferance but are genuinely involved in decision making. We suggest that it is only by expanding these networks and avoiding tokenism that the self-reinforcing patterns of behaviour within these informal networks which help sustain the masculine culture of computing may begin to be questioned and addressed.

## Legitimizing claims to knowledge – gaining legitimacy as a female expert in fields such as IT and IS

The preceding discussion suggests that the successful completion of computing courses by women is only the beginning. Once in the field, women immediately begin to realize the extent to which the opaque power structures sustained through informal networks, particularly within academia, control progression within the field despite a supposedly egalitarian and objective base characterized by peer review. Moreover, once women have actually entered the field as academics and active researchers they then often encounter further problems associated with patterns of communication between genders that can and do make some aspects of IS/IT research problematic. In presenting oneself as a researcher within the field one is asking others to accept that one has valuable and valid knowledge and expertize in the area. Yet, it is clear that actually using knowledge in any given context is problematic because: ‘Knowledge is a slippery and elusive concept, and every discipline has its own secret realization of it’ (Scarbrough & Burrell, 1996; p. 178).

Alvesson (1993) takes this further by arguing that the essential feature of knowledge and knowledge work, of which academic research is an obvious example, is its ambiguity. He identifies different aspects of ambiguity, for example, in terms of knowledge itself, of the significance of knowledge and of the results claimed to be contingent on knowledge. He concludes that the key to success is to create the impression that both the work itself and the individual performing that work are knowledgeable: ‘It is extremely important for those claiming to be knowledge intensive to nurture an image of them being so. . .. being perceived as an expert is more crucial than being one’ (Alvesson, 1993; p. 1004). Knowledge workers need, therefore, to project the image of ‘knowing’ in order to reduce the inherent ambiguity of knowledgeintensive work such as research, particularly fieldwork. Yet the experiences of some of the authors suggests that this ability to ‘project the image of knowing’ is in some situations very difficult for women in technical areas of computing, such as IT and IS, because within particular institutional contexts women are simply not expected to be knowledgeable about these technical areas of expertise (Burris, 1996).

The problems of legitimation when attempting to conduct IT-related research as a female researcher across Sweden and the UK were found by one of the authors to be particularly severe in the UK compared with Sweden. The research project involved approximately 40 face-to-face interviews with industrial practitioners in the UK and 20 in Sweden. Those interviewed included production managers and directors, management consultants and software suppliers who were all involved in either the marketing, implementation or use of IS for manufacturing planning and control. The vast majority of interviewees (around 95%) were male. What was experienced was that within the UK the predominantly male participants in the research assumed female researchers to be novel but fundamentally ignorant concerning IT for manufacturing planning and control. Thus, even when attempting to negotiate access, let alone when trying to gather useful data, the female researcher was confronted with communicative barriers. These barriers did not apply in the same way to the male research fellow employed on the project, despite his relatively low level of knowledge about the IT that was focal in the research.

In order to overcome this hurdle it was not only necessary for the female researcher to articulate a pre-prepared battery of explanations about the benefits of the research project to the company but also to spend considerable time and energy providing a personal history of relevant research experience and results achieved to date. This approach was found to be necessary in order to shift perceptions away from ‘who is this strange woman, what could she possibly know about manufacturing control technology and how did she get hold of my name?’ to one that accepted the researcher as ‘legitimate’. It was necessary, therefore, to spend time convincing UK participants in the research that a female academic did actually posses the necessary research credentials and was, therefore, sufficiently knowledgeable to be worth (possibly) speaking to.

This was in quite distinct contrast to Sweden, where for example, the main factor in terms of agreeing to be involved in the project appeared to be whether the interviewee felt that they could offer a relevant contribution to the research (not vice versa). There appeared to be then a shift of emphasis and perceptions in Sweden in that there was an immediate assumption on the part of those interviewed that the research would be of interest and that the researcher (regardless of gender) would be competent to carry it out. These differences were probably not, it must be said, solely as a result of gender. For example, industry/academia research collaboration is far more prevalent in Sweden than the UK and there are significantly more funds available from both industry and government for research. Managers in industry in Sweden are, therefore, far more familiar with dealing and communicating with academic researchers. These differences are possibly enhanced, however, by the inherent masculinity of the discipline and profession as discussed earlier.

In this instance institutional differences in the training and educational routes of production and IT managers, and national differences in managerial attitudes towards university based research, appeared to contribute to these differences significantly. Differences between these two countries can be explained to some extent as a ‘societal effect’ (Sorge, 1991). That is, differences in the institutional and societal infrastructures and networking arrangements across countries generate differences in the social construction of knowledge and in the legitimation of claims to knowledge. The problems in the UK for women involved in IS research are, likely to be compounded by the strongly embedded notions of professionalism in UK society (as compared, for example, with Sweden) and the validation of knowledge and expertise through the predominantly male professions and professional networks. For example, in the UK, interviews highlighted that IT managers often came from the maledominated engineering professions. In contrast, broader industrial economics and finance backgrounds were found amongst Swedish IT managers. This rendered processes of communication and legitimation more problematic for a female researcher in the UK context compared with Sweden when enrolling industrial participants in the research.

## CONCLUSION

The preceding discussion suggests that, in order to address the issue of declining female participation rates in computing, consideration needs to be given to the interrelationships that exist between communicative processes, social networks and legitimising claims to knowledge overlaid by gendered-power relations. Communication is essential for understanding gendered relations because, while biological sex is a given, gender is not possessed in this sense (Newell, 1999). Rather it is interactively produced through communication (Mumby, 1996). Gendered relations of power then within organizations and academic disciplines are reproduced through processes of communication, through networks of gendered relationships and through wider legitimizing structures. Exploring the connections between communicative practices and the underlying power structures of institutional life, thus, exposes the systems of domination which women experience in their everyday lives. We believe this approach contributes significantly to our understanding of the reasons why declining numbers of women are choosing to enter the field. It also helps us understand why female representation in the field is particularly poor – in terms of the study of the field at undergraduate and postgraduate levels; research in the field as an academic; and practice in the field as a computer professional.

The institutional context will obviously affect these processes of communication and networks of gendered relations and so influence the way computing is perceived and experienced as a discipline and the way in which female workers are perceived within this discipline. The last example given above illustrates, albeit in a limited way, these institutional influences. It has also been argued that Scandinavia has worked longer and perhaps more successfully at promoting greater equality between men and women in the workplace (Newell, 1996). From this one would predict that the more egalitarian and participative context of Scandinavia would promote a somewhat different discourse compared with the UK with its history of heavily gendered professions. However, we have seen that the actual numbers of males and females represented on educational courses does not vary greatly between Scandinavia and the UK. Thus, Scandinavia cannot be regarded as ‘Utopia’ with regard to issues of gender equality. The lessons that are suggested would, therefore, appear to be broadly applicable.

It is the masculine discourse, embedded as it is in a masculine computing culture, that is the major deterrent to women entering the field. If the subject itself can be broadened to include a far broader set of skills and discursive practices and attempts made to avoid technohazing then it is suggested here that a more diverse group of people will be attracted to the field, including a critical mass of women. Indeed, it could be argued that numerical equality of the sexes is not the nub of the issue. Rather, it is the masculine discourse and presentation of the subject, which is the key concern. While this masculinization is not unique to computing, and indeed engineering is perhaps even more heavily masculinized, we are dealing here with a young discipline and its content and structure is still formative. This provides far greater opportunity, therefore, for those involved to actively mould the discipline in ways which de-emphasize masculinity. The challenge for those involved is to shape the discipline in ways which recognize the need (and business case) for creative, business and communicative/interpersonal skills as well as technical skills and knowledge. This is a much more important and difficult challenge than simply increasing the female headcount. Somewhat ironically, these changes are the very changes that are being demanded by industry in their search for the ‘hybrid’ IT person – the person who can combine computing and business knowledge (Earl, 1989).

While this change may, therefore, be seen as logical and beneficial, it is also unlikely to happen as long as the networks and the power structures within the discipline remain dominated by males who have achieved their formal positions through the traditional masculinized, socialization route. The problem, therefore, is that the majority of those currently in positions of power within the field, within both academia and industry, are those who also tend to see the technical aspects of computing as more important. They, therefore, create and sustain a discourse that perpetuates the masculine image of the field, making it extremely difficult for women to establish legitimacy and identity as experts in the field. This ‘old boys network simply perpetuates the status quo, making change impossible. Change and increasing participation generally within the field is crucial as demands for qualified, hybrid individuals within academia and industry grow. To effect change, therefore, existing networks need to be opened up by those key individuals who have the formal and informal power to do so. Female participation, influence and a far broader discourse more generally has to be encouraged and promoted. Promoting and enabling the involvement of women in the formal and informal networks is, we believe, likely to be the only effective facilitator of change.
