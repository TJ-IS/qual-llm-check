# Towards a framework for evaluating investments in data warehousing

Ailish Counihan\*, Pat Finnegan<sup>†</sup> & David Sammon<sup>†</sup>

\*Accounting and Business Studies, Waterford Institute of Technology, Ireland, email: ACounihan@wit.ie, and <sup>†</sup>Management Information Systems, University College Cork, Ireland, email: P.Finnegan@ucc.ie and DSammon@afis.ucc.ie

Abstract. Data warehousing technology offers organizations the potential for much greater exploitation of informational assets. However, the evaluation of potential investments in this technology poses problems for organizations as traditional evaluation methods are constrained when dealing with strategic IT applications. Nevertheless, many organizations are procedurally obliged to use such methods for evaluating data warehousing investments. This paper identifies five problems with using such methods in these circumstances: evaluating intangible benefits; making the relationship between IT and profitability explicit; dealing with the vanishing status quo; dealing with the extended investment time frame; and evaluating infrastructural investments. The authors studied how four organizations in the UK and Ireland attempted to overcome these problems when introducing data warehousing, and propose a framework for evaluating data warehousing investments. This framework consists of a high-level analysis of the economic environment and of the information intensity of the relationship between the organization and its customers. Based on the outcome of this analysis, the authors propose four factors that have to be managed during the evaluation process in order to ensure that the limitations of the traditional evaluation techniques do not adversely affect the evaluation process. These factors are: commitment and sponsorship; the approach to evaluation; the time scale of benefits; and the appraisal techniques used.

Keywords: data warehousing, investment evaluation, appraisal techniques, case research

## INTRODUCTION

Many organizations have introduced a data warehouse without a full cost justification, as they had an over-riding business situation to address and were able to bypass an objective business case justification for the project (Marquez, 1997). However, as the data warehouse requires significant investment (Gray & Watson, 1998) and has the potential to affect an organization’s strategic position (Barquin, 1996), it is essential to evaluate the data warehousing investment decision. Practitioners recognize that the traditional techniques place a serious constraint on IT investment (Clemons & Weber, 1990; Hochstrasser, 1990; Nixon, 1995; Saarinen, 1996; Ballantine & Stray, 1998; McAulay, 2000). As these techniques concentrate on the operational implications of the investment decision and fail to evaluate the strategic implications of the investment decision, many strategically important projects are not evaluated adequately (Iran & Love, 2001). However, this does not mean that evaluation should be abandoned and investment decisions made on ‘gut feeling’ (Weill & Olson, 1989; Grindley, 1991; Fitzgerald, 1998). There is general acceptance that the evaluation of the data warehousing investment decision should be evaluated using a business justification (Marquez, 1997). However, there are no guidelines available to identify the important factors for the data warehousing evaluation process.

This paper addresses the evaluation of investments in data warehousing technology. Four indepth case studies of leading Irish and UK companies afforded the researchers an opportunity to experience many aspects of the data warehousing evaluation processes. The findings illustrate the limited usefulness of traditional evaluation approaches, and the efforts of evaluators to bypass formal channels in order to get approval for the technology. The researchers identify the factors that need to be managed during the data warehousing evaluation process to ensure that the process is not reliant on traditional evaluation methods. The paper concludes by proposing a framework for evaluating investments in data warehousing.

## DATA WAREHOUSING AND INVESTMENT APPRAISAL

Data warehousing has emerged as a response to the problems encountered by those trying to implement decision support systems for strategic management (Kelly, 1996, 1997; Inmon et al., 1997). As organizations often maintain separate operational systems for each business function, cross-functional analysis of information contained in these databases is difficult (Inmon, 1996). The driving force behind the evolution of data warehousing is the need to gain informational, as opposed to operational, access to corporate data (Babcock, 1995). The data warehouse is built to leverage the investments already made in legacy systems, allowing business users effectively to make the transition from traditional corporate data access to informational access (Sauls, 1996; Berson & Smith, 1997).

Data warehousing provides the architecture to model, map, filter, integrate, condense and transform operational data into a separate database of meaningful information that can be accessed, analysed and acted upon (Kelly, 1997; Singh, 1998). Conceptually, data warehousing can be described under three headings, data acquisition, data management and data exploitation. Data acquisition involves extracting data from various sources and transforming it to make the data usable in the data warehouse (Man, 1996; Kelly, 1997). Data management involves managing the central repository of enterprise-wide data from relevant source systems, allowing ad hoc discovery and drill-down analysis by multiple user groups (Armstrong, 1996; Zagelow, 1997). Storage of such data is usually managed by relational databases and other special tools in such a way that data are used for effective decision support (Mattison, 1996; Sach, 1996). Data exploitation involves exploiting data to provide information for decision making (Ladley, 1997). Exploitation tools include data-mining tools such as multidimensional analysis products, neural networks and data discovery tools (Mattison, 1996) that support end users in accessing and analysing the data in the warehouse in various ways (Francett, 1994). Data exploitation ranges from supporting routine decision making to exposing hidden trends in enterprise data that can be used to exploit new business opportunities (Singh, 1998). Data warehousing architectures can also consist of a number of data marts. These are smaller warehouses that are optimized for data analysis in specific functions or processes (Kelly, 1997).

Data warehousing projects are a significant investment (Gray & Watson, 1998), but the gains can be quite substantial. An IDC study found an average ROI of over 400% in more than 60 organizations (Berson & Smith, 1997). However, statistics such as these are typically after the event and based on successful projects (Gray & Watson, 1998). The challenge facing organizations wishing to implement a data warehouse is to evaluate its return in advance of com mitting funds to implement it.

Research indicates that capital budgeting techniques are regarded as the primary accepted approach to evaluating IT investments (Thomas, 1992; Griffiths, 1994; Ballantine & Stray, 1998). As a consequence, practitioners use investment appraisal techniques as symbols to justify the investment decision once that decision has already been taken, rather than as an assessment of the value of the information technology decision (Grindley, 1991; Clarke & Stevens, 1997). Improvements made to make the traditional capital budgeting techniques more applicable to IT investment decisions (Wilcocks & Lester, 1994; Arapoglou et al., 1997) attempt to capture the business and strategic value of the technology. However, such techniques are vague and of little operational use to practitioners (Ward et al., 1996). Despite the elaborate tools that exist to help solve the problems of information systems evaluation, the gap between theory and practice con tinues to grow (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Huerta & Sanchez, 1999).

Research illustrates that traditional accounting techniques are not suitable for the data warehousing investment decision (Marquez, 1997), and that the problems associated with the use of the traditional techniques are aggravated by the huge infrastructural investment required for the data warehouse (Ross et al., 1996). The factors that make the traditional investment appraisal techniques not readily applicable to the data warehousing investment decision have been classified by the authors under five headings, as illustrated in Table 1.

It has been claimed that the key to justifying a data warehouse is to focus on the business issues and how the data warehouse can reap rewards for the business community (Kelly, 1997). It is proposed that management will fully endorse the effort and associated cost of a decision support solution as long as the data warehouse is aligned with the strategic direction of the organization (Marquez, 1997). Nevertheless, even with a clear recognition of the business benefits by senior management, organizations are still reliant on a financial evaluation. Research is consequently needed to identify the key components of the data warehousing evaluation process that have to be managed in order to overcome the constraints of traditional financial evaluation.

## THE RESEARCH APPROACH

The objective of this study is ‘to investigate the evaluation of data warehousing investments’. Evaluation in this context was defined as a process for considering investment (or further investment) in data warehousing technology. Marshall & Rossman (1989) suggested that various research strategies including a case study, a multisite case study or a field study could be used for such exploratory research. Deciding on an appropriate strategy is subsequently a question of trade-offs (Stone, 1978). Yin (1989) suggested that case studies are appropriate when the object is to study contemporary events, and where it is not necessary to control behavioural events or variables. Benbasat (1987) proposed multiple case studies as this allows for cross-analysis and the extension of theory.

A multiple case study research design was devised in order to facilitate the collection of context data on the evaluation of data warehousing investments. The rationale used was that orga nizations with experience of evaluating investments in data warehousing technology would be able to explain the factors that they considered to be critical to such evaluation. The comparison of these factors across different organizations would lead to the foundation of a framework for evaluating data warehousing investments. The case study approach is considered appropriate as it allows the researchers to probe the relationship between project variables and eval uation in more depth than research methods such as surveys and experiments. The four organizations studied were chosen purposefully to provide a breadth of implementation experience. However, these organizations represent a minority of industrial sectors, which is a lim itation of the study.

The primary data collection methods were semi-structured interviews and document anal ysis. Each organization was preselected based on their data warehousing evaluation experi ence and their willingness to co-operate. Having received an overview of the data warehousin project, the researchers, in conjunction with a contact person, identified the key decision mak ers in each organization. Each interview was semi-structured to facilitate an examination of the company’s experience in relation to the problems identified in Table 1, as well as a consideration of other aspects of the company’s data warehousing initiative(s). The interview data were triangulated with data gathered from planning and implementation documents, investment pro posals and evaluation matrices. The data were analysed using decision tree analysis and meta-matrices as recommended by Miles & Huberman (1994). This approach facilitated an exploration of the key issues within each company and an analysis of these issues across companies.

## ASSESSING DATA WAREHOUSING INVESTMENTS

An overview of the four organizations studied is provided here. The following section examines how each of the organizations evaluated their investments in data warehousing, paying particular attention to how each strove to overcome the problems with the traditional methods highlighted in Table 1.

Royal & Sun Alliance Group plc was formed from a merger in 1996 to become the largest composite insurer in the UK. It was a necessity for Royal & Sun Alliance to become more aggressive in their efforts at attracting customers as competition was eroding their customer base, and the organization decided that they should adopt a business strategy of specializing. There was general agreement that Royal & Sun Alliance could not achieve this business aim unless the organization invested in a data warehouse. The managing director carefully chose the key participants in the data warehousing steering committee as individuals who already understood the business value of such a system. However, the first two data warehousing proposals, costing £18 m and £10 m, failed to gain approval from the board as the costs were considered too high and the time scale for deliverables too long. A compromise project costing £4 m was finally put in place, as the steering group decided to keep the project below the threshold for board approval of £5 million.

Barclays plc was severely affected by the UK recession from 1989 to 1992, and declared a loss of £2.5 billion in 1992. The decision to invest in an enterprise-wide data warehouse came as a direct consequence of key business drivers and the need to improve decision making. The finance director was instrumental in the evaluation of the project. However, he was not concerned with financial appraisal. The decision-making process focused around the discretion of the key decision maker to invest in the warehouse. However, that finance director left in 1993, and the newly appointed director made changes. Since 1993, there has been a move away from separate IT justification. Now, a decision to invest in the data warehouse is based on an evaluation of its contribution to a business project.

Anglo Bank (pseudonym) is a leading UK bank providing wealth management products to personal and corporate customers. The customer cards database (CCD) warehousing initiative was driven by competitive pressure in the market. Competitors had targeted information about their customers and were using this information to tailor their products to suit the needs of individual customers. The warehouse never went through a formal evaluation process as the initial costs were within the limit that the business unit could approve. As the project matured, the team realized that the initiative was going to require more investment than was initially thought. The costs were increasing during the project, but the data warehouse never hit the appraisal limit because the decision to invest evolved and the costs were just extended. What started out as a shoestring turned out to be £15 million three years later.

Hiberno Air (pseudonym) is an airline company serving the European and American markets. The data warehousing initiative at Hiberno Air was first started in October 1996 and abandoned in January 1997. The data warehousing initiative was driven by the marketing function and was evaluated using traditional financial appraisal techniques. The market planning function was expected to carry the full cost of the data warehouse even though the base infrastructure would yield benefits in other functions and departments. The criterion for investment was that the investment had to achieve a three-year return on investment. Owing to the strategic nature of the investment decision, traditional techniques could not quantify the benefits, and the project failed the financial appraisal.

## OVERCOMING THE LIMITATIONS OF THE TRADITIONAL TECHNIQUES

Table 1 outlines the five factors that make traditional investment appraisal techniques inappli cable to the data warehousing investment decision. However, as mentioned previously, the tra ditional capital budgeting techniques are regarded as the principal means of evaluating IT investments. Table 2 provides a summary of how the problems associated with the traditional capital budgeting techniques were overcome by three of the organizations studied. Hiberno Air is excluded as it did not overcome these problems. The company failed to deal effectively with the problems of the traditional techniques outlined in Table 1. Consequently, the data ware housing project failed the evaluation process and was abandoned.

## Evaluating intangible benefits

The three financial services organizations had an insatiable appetite for information relating to customer behaviour. The business value of intangibles was therefore paramount. At Hiberno Air, the intangible benefits of the data warehousing investment were not taken into account during the evaluation process, as there was strict adherence to financial criteria, resulting in project abandonment.

The principal intangible benefit to Royal & Sun Alliance of investment in a data warehouse was to ensure a more proactive role for information management. The project manager explained that ‘without the proper management of information, the organization would get cru cified in the market place’. The data warehouse allowed Royal & SunAlliance to understand their cost drivers and identify business opportunities. It was, according to the project manager, seen as ‘a survival issue’. However, the evaluation process had to consider this as an intan gible. According to the management information manager, ‘the real problem is actually building something in the evaluation of the investment which isolates the value of management information.. . . It is just such a core activity for us as we don’t make anything; all we do is make promises and make up prices’. In order to address the situation, the participants in the steering group were hand picked. According to the management information project manager, they were people ‘who have the right roles and responsibilities in the organisation. These people already understand some of the wider issues and can influence a peer group’.

At Barclays, the group finance director used his discretion to make the investment in the initial data warehousing project. His over-riding concern was that he believed that the support functions were giving him summary data that were not based on facts. He believed that improved data analysis would make him more comfortable answering to market analysts and shareholders. The second phase of the data warehousing project also focused on gaining a thorough understanding of customer behaviour. However, such an important intangible could not be evaluated using financial appraisal. According to the IT director, the evaluation was undertaken from the perspective of strategic benefit: ‘before the data warehouse, people looked at analysis as a high cost luxury. It is now seen as an essential element of the way we do our business and the latest investment case did not quote any financial benefits at all of the warehouse’.

The issue of positioning the data warehousing project as a vehicle for cultural change was evident across the organizations studied. The projects were positioned to make personnel more demanding as regards their use of the information available in the organization. Consequently, cultural change was seen as a factor for consideration in the evaluation process. The project champions managed to get executive support by illustrating how the data warehouse could change the culture and enable company personnel to be more creative in their use of management information. Such executive support ensured that strategic intangibles did not have to feature on a financial appraisal, but were considered a strong motivator for introducing the project.

## Making the relationship between IT and profitability explici

At Royal & Sun Alliance, Anglo Bank and Barclays, the analysis of customer behaviour patterns was vital for commercial survival, and the data warehousing projects were initiated in order to respond to competitive pressures. Consequently, organizational profitability had to be considered as part of the evaluation process. Hiberno Air was not operating in such an intensely com petitive environment, so the data warehousing project was not seen to impact on profitability. Barclays, Anglo Bank and Royal & Sun Alliance needed commitment and sponsorship from key personnel to emphasize the profitability impact of exploiting the intense relationship between the organization and the customer.

As Royal & Sun Alliance could not quantify the profitability implications (in monetary terms) from becoming more heavily involved in customer relationship management, the traditional capital budgeting techniques could not be relied upon for evaluation. The project manager believed that the evaluation process had a greater chance of success if the participants responsible for the investment were viewed as the ‘appropriate’ people. It was critical that the steering group would be able to influence their peers, thus reducing the need for financial appraisal. However, the first case for the data warehouse investment failed, as the initiative did not have the level of commitment necessary for an enterprise-wide solution.

The first data warehouse implementation at Anglo Bank was the card services data warehouse. The Card Services Division saw new entrants coming in with better information on their customers and knew that, if they did not react in a proactive manner, then Anglo Bank was going to lose a percentage of their customer base. However, the project did not have ‘the level of group-wide commitment that would have been desirable’ according to the IT director. Therefore, it was felt that justification would be easier on a unit (rather than group-wide) basis. As high-level commitment was not secured, the data warehouse was funded on an incremental basis, with costs continually extended. The project never hit the appraisal limit, and all the investment was categorised as incremental cost.

The finance director at Barclays knew that a financial appraisal would not adequately capture, in monetary terms, the relationship between the investment decision and its effect on profitability. As the project had such high-level sponsorship, the emphasis was taken away from financial appraisal and focused on strategic necessity as a surrogate for profitability.

## Dealing with the vanishing status quo

For Royal & Sun Alliance, Anglo Bank and Barclays, data warehousing technology was seen as the enabler that would ensure organizational survival. It was considered that continuation of the status quo was not a viable alternative. Hiberno Air was not as competitively constrained, as other factors such as price could be used to compete effectively. Hiberno Air consequently considered the status quo as a viable option within the evaluation process. The other organizations used the vanishing status quo as an argument within the evaluation process and ensured that senior executives viewed the data warehouse project as the only alternative.

After the tariff system that sheltered insurance companies was withdrawn, Royal & Sun Alliance could no longer rely on a constant stream of loyal customers. Consequently, a specialization strategy of targeting customers was adopted. An enterprise-wide data warehouse was seen as the most viable option of implementing this in the late 1990s. The underlying competitive necessities ensured that an appropriate level of commitment was secured. Securing this commitment was seen as being far superior to the mechanics of the evaluation process.

The group finance director at Barclays believed that data existed in the organization that would allow the organization to manage the banking losses from the 1989–92 recession in a proactive manner. In particular, he was concerned about the £2.5 billion bad loans write-off and was prepared to invest regardless of the cost, as he saw the data warehouse as the only means available for ensuring commercial survival and success.

Unlike at Royal & Sun Alliance and Barclays, a high-level commitment to the data warehousing project was not forthcoming at Anglo Bank. The project was positioned to ‘empower the organization to analyse the data to allow us to target market our customers’, the group finance director explained. However, the key decision maker was at business unit level. Nevertheless, senior executives at group level recognized the importance of the project. Although the project did not have huge commitment from group management, the project did have agreement from head office.

## Dealing with the extended investment time frame

Strict adherence to a short time frame reduces the chances of a positive outcome to the evaluation process. At Hiberno Air, strict adherence to a 3-year return on investment resulted in the proposal being rejected. The other organizations attempted to tailor the evaluation process to adhere to deliverables within short time horizons, as group management was more comfortable with shorter time frames. Barclays adopted a two-tiered evaluation process to ensure quick deliverables. Royal & Sun Alliance broke the enterprise-wide data warehouse into discrete data marts and chose four for the initial phase to ensure short-term deliverables. Anglo Bank used an informal, incremental approach to evaluation, so time frame was not a consideration.

At Royal & Sun Alliance, the initial data warehouse project considered an enterprise-wide data warehouse with separate data marts for designated functions. The steering group understood the importance of taking a long-term approach to the investment. However, top management was inclined to take a short-term view. The key decision makers went through a process of agreeing how best to present the case for investment. The proposal was for 14 marts, ranging from ‘high difficulty high cost’ to ‘low difficulty low cost’. The four marts chosen for first-phase evaluation were all in critical areas. The principal reason for proposing these was to ensure enough payback in the short term to fund the infrastructural costs. However, the length of time taken to recoup the benefits (four years) was considered by the board to be too long. The steering group came up with an alternative solution, which was also rejected. On the basis of being deliverable in one year, the board, accepted the third solution. This solution was scaleable and would eventually evolve towards the initial proposal.

At Anglo Bank, the time frame was not a major consideration because of the evaluation approach taken. According to the group finance project director, ‘the evaluation process was a type of shoestring approach with very little formal evaluation’. The costs were increased during the project, but the data warehouse never hit the appraisal limit because the decision to invest evolved. In effect, there was no real decision point to spend £15 million. Given the recognition of the strategic benefits of the data warehouse, this unstructured approach reduced the need to look at benefits and costs in a particular time frame.

The decision-making process for data warehouse evaluation at Barclays focused around relatively short-term horizons. Although group management maintained a long-term view, the investment process was broken into short-term deliverables. The evaluation process was done at two levels. The base infrastructural costs were evaluated at group level, whereas the business units put forward their own case for usage of the warehouse. Group management was very keen to work on projects, which were typically between three and six months long, and have approved very few warehouse projects that run for more than six months. They acknowledged that each component will be contributing to the overall strategic benefit of the warehouse, but insisted that each phase has meaningful deliverables in its own right.

## Evaluating infrastructural investments

The traditional approach to investment appraisal justifies an application on its own merits and is useful for the cost saving/cost displacement investments. As data warehousing requires such a huge infrastructural investment, the traditional approach to investment appraisal was found not to be applicable, as was evident with Hiberno Air. Here, the marketing department was expected to carry the full cost of the data warehouse even though the base infrastructur would yield benefits in other departments throughout the organization.

The other data warehousing projects studied were evaluated using either an application approach or an enterprise-wide approach. The application approach is incremental and involves evaluation at a business unit level and also at an enterprise-wide level. Enterprise wide justification requires commitment from senior executives and an appreciation of the full benefits of organizational integration. Investment in the data warehouse at Barclays was at the discretion of the group finance director and was not reliant on a financial appraisal. Initially, Royal & Sun Alliance chose four data marts for phase one in an effort to recover the base infrastructural investment in the short term. However, the board only gave its approval when a scaled-down project using existing infrastructures was proposed. Anglo Bank evaluated the data warehousing initiative on an application basis but without formal financial appraisal.

At Anglo Bank, the key decision maker was the head of the card services business unit and, thus, evaluation was at the application level and incremental. However, the group level recognized the importance and value that an enterprise-wide data warehouse would bring. Nevertheless, as the enterprise-wide solution crossed organizational boundaries, it became difficult for the organization to get a high-level common sponsor. Consequently, the data warehousing initiatives have been sponsored by specific business units ‘with a little nod and wink to the side to contemporaries’, according to the group finance director. There was, however, an alignment process that tried to ensure that the business areas were pursuing the overall business objectives of the organization.

The enterprise-wide data warehouse at Barclays was run as a central cost on a chargeable basis with a mandate to recover costs fully. This resulted in the investment being splintered around the different business areas, depending on their usage. The justification for increased capacity was done on a case-by-case basis at group level. The business units submitted their business case for expected usage of the warehouse, and the case for increased investment in the infrastructure was considered centrally.

## SYNTHESIS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

From analysing how the organizations studied overcame the limitations of the traditional evaluation approach, as shown in Table 2, the authors have extracted six critical factors in relation to data warehousing investment evaluation. The relationship between the problems and how the organizations overcame the limitations (critical factors) is summarized in the matrix presented in Table 3.

The authors propose that evaluators need to conduct a high-level analysis of the organization in the context of the economic environment and the information intensity of the relationship with customers. The evaluation process must address the other four factors when the highlevel analysis indicates a need for data warehousing.

The authors’ recommendations are illustrated in Figure 1. It is recommended that the data warehousing investment decision commences with an economic environmental analysis to determine whether the data warehouse is a strategic necessity. If this analysis reveals that the organization is operating in an intensely competitive environment, with a requirement to focus on customers to gain a thorough understanding of customer behaviour to allow the organization to respond more proactively to competition in the market place, then the organization should proceed with an analysis of the information intensity of its relationship with the customer. If this analysis reveals that the organization is operating in an information-intensive industry sector with the need to convert large amounts of data pertaining to customers into information for analysis, then a data warehouse may be required. If these characteristics are not evident, the evaluation process should not proceed any further as it is unlikely that the organization will be able to justify the investment.

Figure 1 also presents proposals for managing the evaluation process given the existence of the characteristics from the high-level analysis. In order to manage the evaluation process, it is necessary to secure the appropriate level of commitment and sponsorship for the project. For an enterprise-wide data warehouse, commitment should be at group level. If the organization is adopting an incremental or application approach to the data warehouse project, then commitment at the business unit level is required with co-operation from group level management.

Once commitment is secured, the key participants should be chosen such that they understand the implications of the investment decision. It is a critical prerequisite for an enterprise wide data warehouse that the key participants are at group level. A business unit initiative will not lead to an enterprise-wide data warehouse.

The approach taken to evaluating the data warehouse is either an application/incremental approach or enterprise-wide evaluation. An application approach involves evaluation and implementation at business unit level. However, co-operation across business units is necessary, as data may be needed from other business areas. The evaluation process should involve the business unit manager presenting the business case for a data warehouse required to meet the specific requirements of that business unit. A two-tiered evaluation process is necessary for an enterprise-wide data warehouse. The base infrastructural investment required for the warehouse should be evaluated at group level, whereas the business units can evaluate their own applications.

It is recommended that the data warehouse be evaluated within the constraints of short time horizons, while also attempting to ensure that the longer term benefits of the investment are evaluated. The project champions should ensure that the data warehouse yields tangible short-term deliverables as organizations are more comfortable with such an approach.

Based on the experiences of the organizations studied, the authors recommend that furthe consideration be given to three appraisal technigues within the framework proposed. This rec ommendation is based on the requirements for such techniques identified during the study, rather than the use of a particular technique by an organization. These three techniques are the case study approach, value analysis and management by maxim.

The case study approach examines the data warehouse investment as a coherent unit rather than extracting the financial aspects. It uses the success of an initial data warehousing project to judge the viability of subsequent projects. However, although it reduces the emphasis on financial appraisal, it requires the successful completion of an initial project.

The organizations studied overcame the difficulties of evaluating an enterprise-wide data warehouse by investing in data marts as prototypes. Value analysis (Keen, 1981) involves building a prototype of the proposed system, thereby allowing users to evaluate the intangible business benefits of the system on a low-cost trial basis before deciding to commit to a larger investment. Consequently, value analysis could be tailored for use within the proposed framework.

The organizations also required a technique to help with appraising the infrastructural elements. The management by maxim approach (Broadbent & Weill, 1997) provides guidelines for ensuring that infrastructure investments are aligned with the organization’s strategic and competitive positioning and could be tailored for use within the proposed framework.

## CONCLUSIONS

The authors have proposed a framework for evaluating investments in data warehousing. It is clear from our study that such an evaluation is a two-stage process that differentiates potential data warehousing adopters and then manages the evaluation process so that the benefits of the technology can be considered within the context of the organizational requirements. The framework is based on the experiences of the limited number of organizations studied, and thus requires further study. This would entail first researching the evaluation processes of a greater number of organizations across a variety of industrial sectors. Based on the outcome of this research, action research should be conducted to test the use of the framework in practice.
