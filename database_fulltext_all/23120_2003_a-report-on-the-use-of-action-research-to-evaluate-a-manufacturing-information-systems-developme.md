# A report on the use of action research to evaluate a manufacturing information systems development methodology in a company

Delvin Grant\* & Ojelanki Ngwenyama<sup>†</sup>

\*School of Commerce, DePaul University, Chicago, IL 60604, email: dgrant2@depaul.edu, and <sup>†</sup>School of Business, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, VA 23284, USA, email: okngweny@saturn.vcu.edu

Abstract. The paper reports on an action research study that evaluated the usefulness of a Manufacturing Information Systems (MIS) development methodology at a manufacturing technology company. The evaluation process is based upon a five-stage action research method. The ISD methodology, in conjunction with the action research method, was used to solve five technical and organizational problems identified in the Engineering Release Function of the company. Results of the study include reduction in cycle time, work-in-process and rework.

Keywords: action research, engineering release function, evaluating, methodol ogy, models

## INTRODUCTION

Fitzgerald (Fitzgerald, 1990), Brinkkemper and colleagues (Brinkkemper et al., 1996) and oth ers point out that most inventors do not evaluate their information systems development (ISD) methodologies and there is no generally acceptable way to systematically evaluate them. Companies do not evaluate the usefulness of methodologies before applying them and this has led to many bad experiences (cf. Davis & Bjor-Andersen, 1986). Evaluating ISD methodologies is a persistent and intractable problem for many companies and therefore warrants more research (Tolvanen et al., 1996). Moreover, ineffective ISD methodologies are an important risk category that is of major concern to software development managers (Lyytinen et al., 1996; 1998). Consequently, the problem of evaluating the effectiveness of ISD methodologies is of theoretical and practical importance (Tolvanen, 1995; Kelly & Rossi, 1998; Siau & Rossi, 1998). The company that underwent this research, like many others, had a history of adopting methodologies with little consideration for how they may satisfy the project objectives.

In this research, we report on the results of an evaluation pertaining to the usefulness of an ISD methodology in a manufacturing technology company. Action research provided the theoretical framework for the intervention into the organization and it guided the investigation and critical analysis of the problem situation. The primary objective of the research was to develop an appropriate strategy for evaluating the effectiveness of an existing ISD methodology. We were also interested in the universality of the approach to evaluating future ISD methodologies. The effectiveness of the ISD methodology is measured by its ability to solve five problems that existed in the organization. In evaluating the ISD methodology, we tried to demonstrate its usefulness in the organizational context, and to identify the factors that could contribute to its success or failure.

## WHY ACTION RESEARCH IS APPROPRIATE

The managers of the company wanted a rigorous and systematic intervention process to investigate and solve the practical problems of ISD methodology evaluation. As researchers, we were interested in improving IS practice so we were motivated to solve the problems in a manner that contributed to the existing body of knowledge about ISD methodology evaluation. To satisfy the objectives of the researchers and the company, we chose action research as the method of choice. We needed an interventionist research method that would advance actionable knowledge in ISD practice. We found action research applicable due to its practical problem solving orientation and its ability to expand the scientific knowledge (Nunamaker et al., 1991; Mathiassen, 1997; Vidgen et al., 1997; Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999). According to Argyris (Argyris, 1985), action research is a collaborative process of critical inquiry that fosters the learning of theory and practice. It has become an accepted research method (Baskerville, 1999), and has been used successfully in information systems research projects (Mumford. 1983; Benbasat, 1984; Wood-Harper, 1985; Ngwenyama & Grant, 1994; Tolvanen, 1995; Baskerville, 1996; Baskerville & Wood-Harper 1996; Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999; Grant, 1999). Nevertheless, the question of what form of generalizing is achievable in action research, is sometimes raised.

Baskerville & Lee (1999) have addressed this question at length. They discuss four types of generalizing in IS research: (1) typification; (2) learning; (3) generalizability; and (4) falsification. Typification and generalizability are forms of inductive generalizing; these are reasoning processes that begin with observations and their subsequent use to build theories. Learning and falsification are forms of deductive generalizing that begin with a theory and process; the theory is then tested against observations. According to Baskerville & Lee (1999), action research is capable of achieving two forms of deductive generalizing, which are learning and falsification. Action research provides the possibility for intervention experiments in which researchers and practitioners collaborate on applying and testing change theories (methodologies or frameworks) in organizations. In the intervention experiment, the researchers apply a change theory (methodology or framework) to a set of particulars in the organizationa (empirical) setting and observe the outcome. The possible outcomes are, the change theory is falsified or supported and becomes more entrenched (as any other theory would). In action research, as in any other form of intensive research, a claim of generality is made when the change theory is supported. The reason is that the change theory is considered to be genera and should hold for other instances (organizational situations) where the empirical circumstances are similar (Baskerville & Lee, 1999). Action research therefore, contributes to both academic and practical knowledge.

## THE ENGINEERING RELEASE FUNCTION

The action research study was conducted in the Engineering Release Function of a manufacturing technology company that specializes in making computer equipment. It focused on defining the MIS architecture that could assist in the planning, co-ordination and management of the Function. We chose the Release Function because it was self-contained and it encom passed the entire range of design and manufacturing activities of other companies. It exhibited most of the typical problems encountered when developing business and manufacturing systems in organizations. If the ISD methodology could deliver a real-world solution in this self contained area of the company, we believed that its success could be duplicated in other areas of the company. For this to happen, two factors should be present. First, the Release Function is among the most complex areas of the company. Therefore, we thought it would serve as an appropriate test case for evaluating the ISD methodology. If its success could be demonstrated in such a complex environment, then the chances of success in less complex areas of the company should be good. Second, the company had experience with planning approaches similar to the one used in the study. As a result, those involved were sympathetic to the approach and welcomed its use. If the company culture was hostile to the approach and lacked prior experience, then the chances of success would be slim.

The Function comprised six departments, D1 to D6, eight managers, and over 100 employ ees, most of whom were engineering, software and manufacturing specialists. The activities of the department included the design, engineering and manufacture of printed circuit boards. It was the primary link between research and development laboratories, and the manufacturing function. Each department was responsible for a set of activities. D1 generated, developed and assembled engineering design data that were then distributed to other departments. Engineers who worked at geographically dispersed research and development laboratories produced the design data. The data were transmitted over wide-area networks to the Engineering Release Function. D2 further refined the design data by adding manufacturing process information and instructions. D3 created the manufacturing process routings and the manufacturing data. D4 and D5 collaborated on generating the numerical control (NC) artwork data, and the manufacturing materials requirements data. The artwork data are descriptions of the various fea tures (holes, lands etc.) that are found on printed circuit boards. These artwork descriptions were produced in the manufacturing stage. D6 was responsible for ensuring the manufactur ability of the printed circuit boards.

## THE RESEARCH PROBLEM

We wanted to evaluate an existing ISD methodology based on its ability to solve five specific problems that are common to a number of manufacturing companies. We chose an ISD methodology developed by Grant (Grant, 1991) to solve the integration of manufacturing and management information systems. It comprised a process, working procedures, tools and enterprise modelling techniques for developing IS architectures for manufacturing companies. The architecture defines an iterative, loop-linear process in which seven models are developed (see Figure 1). Each model provides a different view but the seven combined models are required for the comprehensive integration of the company. The arrows down the centre of the architecture (see Figure 1) describe the main flow of activity for developing each of the seven models. The arrows on the sides describe the iterative nature of the modelling process. Because the research is not about the development of the models of the architecture, we refer the reader to Grant (1991) and Grant et al. (1992) for details.

## THE FIVE PROBLEMS OF THE FUNCTION

The objective was to improve the manufacturing process and product by solving five organizational and technical problems: (1) lack of shared understanding: (2) lack of communication and co-ordination among departments; (3) insufficient throughput and long cycle time; (4) inconsistencies in documentation; and (5) duplication of effort. The managers believed that by modelling and studying the activities, the team members and management would better understand and appreciate the problems of the Function. Before the study, no-one possessed sufficient knowledge of the entire process. Individuals were considered experts within their confined work environment but knew very little about the remaining parts of the business. Consequently, a decision was made to model the Release Function using IDEF0, an ISD methodology for performing activity analysis, system design work and project planning (ES, 1988) A project group was formed to accomplish the task.

## EVALUATING THE ISD METHODOLOGY

The effectiveness of the ISD methodology is its ability to solve the five problems. We wanted to find out if the products of our modelling exercise would provide significant support for resolv ing the problems. To achieve this objective, we analysed the effectiveness of the solutions and the contribution of the models. Our analysis, to be consistent with the AR approach (Figure 2) described earlier, will discuss the diagnosis, the action plan, the action taken, the evaluation, and the learning for each of the five problems.

The role of the action researcher was to diagnose and understand the processes, tasks, and problems of the Release Function. This was accomplished over several months with the aid of team members and other experts working in the Function. After thoroughly understanding the problems, the action researcher and the team jointly developed a plan of action to solve each problem. The plan included using Information Engineering (IE) (Martin, 1989) as the underly ing ISD philosophy for developing the MIS architecture, using IDEF0 as the Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) environment, and activity modelling for representing the Release Function. The team and management, under the guidance and supervision of the researcher, were responsible for implementing the solutions. The solutions were evaluated to measure the success of the implementation. For example, we measured cycle time before and after the intervention. We collected and evaluated data using structured questionnaires, inter views, charts and informal discussions. The learning process was accomplished primarily through discussions with team members and other experts from the Function. These discussions served as the primary means of reflection for team members that led to the improvement of the process.

## Problem 1: lack of shared understanding

## Diagnosis

No-one possessed a comprehensive understanding of the Function owing to its size and complexity. This led to the undertaking of tasks without any rationale for performing them. These tasks were part of a tradition that existed for many years, and it was difficult for some team members to break with tradition. It took several meetings to convince members about the need for change.

## Action plan

To model the Function so that its members had a better understanding of how it worked and the level of complexity. To gain departmental commitment to improve the process. A total commitment for change was never fully realized. However, with the help of managers, we achieved a sufficient level of user commitment for improving the process.

## Action taken

Develop a conceptual model of the Function to help explain how it worked. The Context Model, and the Business Model played a primary role in this regard.

## Evaluation

The primary contributions of the Context Model and the Business Model were that they helped stakeholders to challenge the assumptions regarding the flow of information between departments. Both Models served as communication vehicles for all involved by showing how the Release Function was connected to the rest of the company. The key contribution of the Context Model was that it helped everyone to see the big picture, and the information flows to and from the Function. This helped the development team to consider the impact of their actions on other systems and departments. The Business Model enhanced users understanding and it served as the vehicle for communicating the interactions that exist at the task and process level.

## Learning

The management and the team realized that developing a conceptual model of the Function was a very valuable process because it made the operations of the Function more transparent. The problems of conceptual modelling were exacerbated by the limitations of the modelling tool. IDEF0 is not an integrated CASE tool and lacks the strength and sophistication of more advanced tools.

## Problem 2: lack of communication and co-ordination among departments

## Diagnosis

A lack of communication and co-ordination existed among departments. This was due to the inadequate dissemination of information, and a clash of personalities. Conflicts in the development group were the result of departmental biases that were introduced by the participating department representatives. These problems were very evident between the design engineers and the manufacturing engineers. Designers often neglected to consider the impact of thei design choices on the manufacturability of the product. Moreover, designers and manufacturing engineers rarely communicated. Although the design choices often fostered creativity and innovation, they posed serious manufacturing problems.

## Action plan

To find a way to enhance communication and co-ordination between the departments of the Function.

## Action taken

Combine the two departments into a single unit to foster better communication, co-ordination and collaboration. Quality Circles (QCs) were formed and employees from both departments staffed them.

## Evaluation

The combined contribution of the Business Model and Responsibility Model played a significant role in highlighting the lack of information exchange vital to a particular job and how it adversely affected the operations of the departments. The Responsibility Model, when crosschecked with the Business Model, revealed inconsistencies in information flow between individuals and systems. Collectively the Models helped to challenge the assumptions regarding the actual flow of information between individuals and systems. Moreover, the Context Model helped users to challenge similar assumptions at the departmental level. As the Business Model and the Responsibility Model enhanced communication between departments, they served to corrob orate the flow of information between groups within particular departments.

## Learning

Having team members coexist and having them work on a common problem significantly reduced the departmental problems. After failed attempts to get the necessary camaraderie, we realized that additional measures were required to bring the two departments together. This led to a 1-day retreat of both departments, the purpose of which was to address the working relationship of the departments and the Function. This considerably improved relations but it did not eradicate all of the problems.

## Problem 3: insufficient throughput and cycle time

## Diagnosis

Because major systems ran in batch mode, throughput and cycle time were adversely affected. Batch processing was carried out at night to ensure that the documents were available the following morning. The unsuccessful processing of documents often resulted in increased cycle time. Throughput and cycle time were also affected by the inability to track work in process (WIP). This made it difficult to make someone directly accountable for the lack of movement of WIP. This situation resulted in long cycle times as bottlenecks accumulate in the queue. Because documents were printed and distributed manually, if one person falls behind schedule, the entire process was adversely affected.

## Action plan

To reduce the product cycle time from 12 days to 4 h.

## Action taken

Develop and implement a real-time transaction processing system for the Design Product and an on-line WIP tracking system. Allow for the automatic on-line distribution of documents to departments and individuals.

## Evaluation

The Context Model, Business Model and Responsibility Model contributed to the solution of this problem by reducing the cycle time from 12 to 3 days. By applying the Business Model, the above actions were taken which helped to decrease the cycle time. To identify all the details required for making the necessary changes, we used the Responsibility Model to identify those involved in a particular work activity and the role they played. Using the Context Model, we analysed the information flows between the Release Function and the other business units. For example, the changes that were made led to increased information flows between the Release Function and manufacturing. This increase was analysed with the help of the Context Model.

## Learning

Small competitors who produced similar products had a cycle time of 24 h. Although we had aspirations to get the cycle time down to 4 h, we realized that this was not possible. First, the product was too complex and could not be produced in 4 h. Second, the sheer size of the orga nization and management structure of the company did not lend itself to a 4-h turnaround time. We also recognized that the complexity of some products excluded small competitors from producing them, so it was unfair to benchmark against them. We had to be very selective about which companies to benchmark against and what products were appropriate fo benchmarking.

## Problem 4: inconsistencies in documentation

## Diagnosis

The pre-manufacturing product data descriptions that were downloaded to manufacturing often did not match the accompanying documents, drawings, NC data and design product descrip tions. This was as a result of changes made to the design product after it was created. For example, the orientation of the image on the panel was one of many potential problems. These problems existed because there were no established quality controls governing the dissemination of the information after changes were made. Another contributing factor was the lack of updated documents to adequately reflect the fully assembled pre-manufacturing product description.

## Action plan

To find a way to better describe the product so that design changes are consistent and up-to date.

## Action taken

Eliminate the need for unit image descriptions as the basis for describing the pre-manufacturing product to manufacturing. Instead, design the product so that all the product data are pack aged as a single logical product description.

## Evaluation

The Business Model, Database Model, and Data Model were successful in eliminating and reducing the data inconsistencies, and the data integrity problems. After studying the Business Model, it became apparent that there were inconsistencies in documentation. For example, NC data did not match the pre-manufacturing product description sent to manufacturing. However, the Data Model and the Database Model supported the claim that the entire description of the pre-manufacturing product should be sent to manufacturing as a single entity. Studying logi cally related data entities sent from a particular file-based system helped us to make the deter mination. As the pre-manufacturing product is a logically related set of data entities, it should be sent to manufacturing as a single entity. This will eliminate the problem of anomalies and help to maintain the integrity of the data. These problems were identified by analysing the Data Model and the Database Model. The analysis also made it apparent that data integrity problems pertaining to inconsistent documentation, was partially attributed to the proliferation of several file-based systems.

## Learning

The participants and the researcher recognized that the data inconsistency problems were caused by the decision to represent the product as a collection of loosely related subsets of information and the inability to manage the updated changes as a unit. The researcher recognized that people in the Function had varying and conflicting views of reality that persisted for years.

## Problem 5: duplication of effort

## Diagnosis

A considerable amount of effort was expended as data had to be re-entered into different incompatible systems. This was recognized as a serious problem requiring immediate attention as it increased the product cycle time.

## Action plan

To eliminate unwanted tasks within the Function.

## Action taken

Re-engineer the Release Function to streamline the process, eliminate unwanted tasks and procedures, and implement a data repository.

## Evaluation

The Business Model, Data Model and the Database Model were successful in reducing or eliminating the duplication of effort and data redundancy. After analysing the Business Model, it became apparent that duplication of effort often occurred at the individual and departmental level. Had we developed similar business models for other business units, we might have discovered duplication of effort within them. Therefore, potential benefits for future applications of the Business Model existed. The Data Model served to identify data entities that were entered repeatedly into the system by various users and the data integrity problems associated with that activity. The Database Model aided in identifying additional data integrity problems associated with storing the same data in several systems with incompatible file formats.

## Learning

The participants became aware of the amount of duplication of effort after they had opportu nities to review the conceptual models of the Function. The researchers recognized the valu of conceptual models for communication and information gathering.

## CONCLUDING DISCUSSION

The usefulness of an ISD methodology varies depending on the experience of the practitioner. Although novice practitioners may follow every rule of the ISD methodology, expert practitioners usually exhibit a level of ‘practitioner autonomy’ when applying a methodology. Practitioners are not bound to apply an ISD methodology as stated in the manual. They tend to fit it to their orientation by ‘casting’ the ISD methodology into the particular philosophy of systems development to which they subscribe. If practitioners are uncomfortable with some of the underlying principles of an ISD methodology, they modify it to better fit their orientations and experiences. These alterations contribute as much to the eventual success or failure of the ISD methodology as to its original design. Hence, for ISD methodologies to be successful they must be robust, well designed and flexible.

We would like to address these issues with respect to the evaluation of methodologies.

It appears that the outcome of the application of an ISD methodology depends on three fac tors: (1) the original design of the methodology; (2) the background knowledge and motivation of those applying it; and (3) the organizational climate and culture. Hence, the same ISD methodology can lead to very different outcomes depending upon whether developers apply it with a different orientation under varying organizational circumstances. Through action research, we established that the ISD methodology could be successful in the case organization. This does not mean that we can guarantee its success in other settings for the reasons discussed above. In evaluating the ISD methodology, we tried to demonstrate and test its usefulness for changing work practices under the specific organizational conditions. We also tried to identify the factors that contributed to its success or failure.

We found that the organization’s culture, power structure, climate and management philosophy were factors that contributed to the success of the ISD methodology. Because the company in this research had a history of using planning approaches, we conjecture that the established practice of planning may be partially responsible for the success of the project. Many of their executives served in the military, and the strict discipline of the ISD methodology used in the study turned out to be congruent with the company’s management and planning philosophy. Had this not been the case, the ISD methodology might have been less successful. A separate research study would be needed to examine these factors in more detail.

If the solution that emerged in this case had been less successful, the action research approach could still be useful for two reasons. First, important lessons can be learned from failed projects if the results are reported to the research community via its scholarly journals (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999). The success of action research is not only measured by its practical success, but also by its ability to add to the stock of knowledge of the research community.

Action research requires the researcher to adapt it to the situation factors present in the problem situation. Therefore, as the organizational situation changes, so does the action research methodology. If the organization climate had been hostile to the ISD methodology, the researcher would have had to consider other avenues for resolving the problems. Consequently, the course of action research cannot be predicted at the outset. Its strength lies in its ability to deal with the emergent nature of human systems.

The evaluation of the method provides some evidence to test its usefulness in other organizations, even though its success cannot be guaranteed. Those who apply it must be sensitive to the practical difficulties that may emerge due to incompatibilities between the principles underlying the ISD methodology and the contextual conditions of the case site. The study demonstrates that planning methodologies can be successful and should not be dismissed on a priori grounds. If the above viewpoint has merit, the results of this research should affect our attitudes towards ISD methodologies. The trade literature often ‘sells’ methodologies as a general solution to a class of practical problems. Nevertheless, our research indicates an ISD methodology is not a ‘product’ that can be bought and ‘applied.’ For an ISD methodology to be successful, many details of current work practices need to be reconsidered and modified. Adopting a new ISD methodology is a lifestyle change for the organization because it means embracing a new set of rules for how things are done. Because many of the current working practices and rules may have existed for many years, they may be deeply ingrained. Therefore, the introduction of an ISD methodology requires major intellectual and motivational effort on the part of those who advocate the change. Consequently, action research has a very good chance of succeeding because it enables the researcher to share the life world of the users of the ISD methodology and vice versa. Users come to see their world in a different light through the interaction with the researcher. It is the construction of shared meanings that lays the groundwork for successful change.

Despite the success of the ISD methodology used in the study, it did have some limitations. First, the scope of the problems addressed in the study was confined to the operational level of the organization. Although we believe that the ISD methodology may be used with similar success at the managerial and strategic levels of the organization, it is yet to be tested. Questions remain as to the success of the ISD methodology had it been applied simultaneously across the three management levels of the organization. Adding the managerial and strategic levels to the Release Function would have further complicated the study. For example, questions remain as to the effect of the strategic level on the other two lower levels and vice versa? Would it have been sufficient to analyse each level separately and, if not, how would we account for the increased level of interactions brought about by the addition of the manageria and strategic levels?

Second, no attempts were made to address political and social problems. This should not imply however, that political problems did not arise. It is inevitable to avoid personality conflicts in large teams. When they did arise, managers outside the confines of the case study dealt with them. Generally, the ISD methodology was ill equipped to handle the group dynamics. This sometimes led to squabbling between group members. A conversation with a manager from the Function revealed that the success of the project was compromised because of personality conflicts among team members.

One may argue that another limitation of the study is the difficulty in drawing general theoretical conclusions from a single application of the approach. Although no approach is univer sally applicable to all problem situations (Galliers, 1994), it is possible that a single or limited number of case studies may be used to identify general properties (Fleck, 1979; Gummesson, 1991; Galliers, 1994; Berg et al., 1995; Baskerville, 1996). Action research aims to solve practical problems (Baskerville, 1999) and so we used it to evaluate an ISD methodology in industry by applying it to a problem situation. Action research is an appropriate method for generating theory (Glasser & Strauss, 1967; Kjellen & Soderman, 1980) while expanding scientific knowledge (Baskerville, 1999). According to Gummesson (Gummesson, 1991), applying the approach to one problem at a time will increase the stock of theoretical knowledge on the subject. This is accomplished by identifying important factors that contribute to the success or fail ure of the proposed approach. The stock of existing knowledge increases when the results are published in established journals. It then becomes the basis for further investigation and for for mulating testable hypotheses (Baskerville, 1996).

We hope that the level of generalizing achieved from this action research study and the lessons learnt will motivate others to evaluate methodologies of their choosing. We encourage others to evaluate the approach vis-à-vis theoretical sampling (Glasser & Straus, 1967; Baskerville, 1999) by applying it to cases that represent different aspects of reality (Toulmin & Gustavsen, 1996). This should provide opportunities for identifying additional factors and lessons learned or for falsification of the theory. With time, the accumulation of a body of knowl edge may allow additional theoretical conclusions to be drawn.

However, we did identify some important lessons that could inform future research on the problem of ISD methodology evaluation. For those who are interested in applying the approach, we recommend choosing problem situations with characteristics similar to the ones discussed in the case. Once the theory becomes more entrenched, the approach should then be applied to a wider domain of problem situations. These may include cases where the company culture is hostile to the approach or when company experience with the approach is limited.

Despite the limitations, the data collected from the study provide some objective measures of success. WIP was reduced by about 75%. This was determined by using the WIP tracking system that was implemented to monitor the flow of work. We conducted formal and informal surveys to determine the benefits of the project to users and team members. In addition to the improved performance of the Function, was the amount of organizational learning that took place. One team member replied, ‘It is amazing what I have learned about the process that I have been involved with for so many years. I thought I understood how it worked but I really didn’t.’ Rework was reduced by about 30%. We reduced product cycle time from 12 days to 3 days but were never able to achieve our stated objective of 4 h. In retrospect, we realized that the stated objective of 4 h was overly ambitious given the complexity of the environment and the product.

## Biographies

Delvin Grant is Associate Professor of Management Information Systems in the College of Commerce at DePaul University in Chicago, IL. He is the Program Administrator for the undergraduate programme in MIS. He received his PhD in Management Information Systems and Industrial

Engineering, and MBA from the State University of New York-Binghamton. His bachelor’s degree is in Mechanical Engineering from New York Institute of Technology, He has held visiting professorships at the University of Gothenburg in Sweden and the University of Jyvaskyla in Finland. He has published in a variety of journals including Information, Technology and People, International Journal of Computer Integrated Manufacturing, Total Quality Management Journal, Journal of Computers & Industrial Engineering, Quality Management in Higher Education, Journal of Computers in Industry, and Communications of the ACM.

Ojelanki Ngwenyama is Professor of Information Systems and Director of the International Scholars Program at Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, Virginia, USA. He is also Extraordinary Professor in the Department of Informatics, Faculty of Management Sciences and Member of the Board of Directors of the School of Infor mation Technology, University of Pretoria; Docen (Research Professor) at University of Jyväskylä Finland and Visiting Research Professor at Aalborg University, Denmark. His research papers have been published in a wide range of international journals. In 1997, he and Allen Lee won the MISQ Best Paper Award. Ojelanki is an Asso ciate Editor for MISQ; a member of the Editorial Board o the Journal of the Association of Information Systems, and a member of the Advisory Board of Scandinavian Journal of Information System. His recent book, Learning To Improve: Software Process Improvement In Practice (with Lars Mathiassen and Jan Pries-Heie), is published by Add ison Wesley Press, 2001.
