# The user–developer communication process: a critical case study

Michael J. Gallivan & Mark Keil

Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, 35 Broad Street, Atlanta, GA 30303, email: mgallivan@gsu.edu and mkeil@gsu.edu

Abstract. Although user participation in systems development is widely believed to have positive impacts on user acceptance, it does not guarantee success and there is still much that we do not know about how and why user participation sometimes delivers positive benefits, but not always. Much of the prior research on user participation assumes that user–developer communication will ensure that the resulting system will be designed to meet users’ needs and will be accepted by them. The nature and quality of the communication between users and developers, however, remains an understudied aspect of user participation. In this paper, we focus on the user–developer communication process. We propose a process model that delineates four stages of communication between users and software developers, and we argue that these stages must occur for user participation to lead to effective outcomes. To illustrate our model, we apply it to analyse a ‘critical case study’ of a software project that failed despite high levels of user involvement. We show that when ‘communication lapses’ occurred in several of the user–devel oper communication stages, developers failed to be informed regarding the underlying reasons that users avoided the system. Based on the insights from this case study, we advise researchers and practitioners how to leverage the potential benefits of user participation, rather than take them for granted.

Keywords: IT project failure, process models, software development, software project management, user–developer communication, user participation

## INTRODUCTION

The information systems (IS) literature suggests that user participation in software develop ment is beneficial because it improves the requirements determination process, leads to greater buy-in, and keeps users informed about progress (Newman & Sabherwal, 1996; Hunton & Beeler, 1997), leading to higher levels of user satisfaction, system quality, and system usage (Hwang & Thorn, 1999). Despite the pervasiveness of these beliefs, researchers have repeatedly noted the lack of consistent empirical results in this area. Although there is growing recognition that the benefits of user participation are moderated by various contingency factors (McKeen et al., 1994), there is still much that we do not know about how and why user participation sometimes delivers positive benefits, but not always. Most research models that investigate user participation implicitly assume that when users participate in system development, communication between users and software developers that is necessary for clear requirements definition will occur. Moreover, these models often assume that user–developer communication will ensure that the resulting system will be designed to meet users needs and will be accepted by them.

In this paper, we challenge these assumptions and, in doing so, we propose a process model that delineates four stages of communication between users and software developers. We contend that all four stages must occur for user participation to lead to effective outcomes. To illustrate our model, we apply it to analyse a ‘critical case study’ (Yin, 1994) of a software project that failed despite high levels of user involvement. Despite considerable input from users during initial design and development and, also despite the fact that the Project Manager continued to solicit user feedback after deployment, even orchestrating an expensive ‘redeployment effort to improve the system, the target users continued to avoid the system.

Despite the appearance that the Project Manager and the developers did everything right in terms of ensuring high levels of user participation, the resulting system never achieved true acceptance, because several essential stages of user–developer communication failed to occur in an effective manner. Through our analysis of this critical case study, we highlight the fact that an ineffective communication process can negate any benefits that might otherwise accrue from high levels of user participation. The primary contribution of our research is to integrate two previously disparate research streams (research on user participation and project team co-ordination) to generate a process model for understanding why effective communication may or may not occur.

Our study suggests that project managers and software developers must look beyond the information content that users provide. They must also question what information users may not readily volunteer, and try to create an environment where users feel free to share their concerns, no matter how critical or sensitive those judgements may be. Effective user–developer communication cannot occur when users withhold negative feedback about a system, either because they perceive such views to be ‘undiscussable’ (Argyris, 1994) or because prior history has shown that such negative feedback will be ignored. We identify ‘communication lapses’ that may occur at each of the four stages of our model, and we provide suggestions for researchers and practitioners to better understand the user–developer communication process that must be effectively managed.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

User participation in system development has been one of the most widely researched topics in the IS literature, and is widely viewed as instrumental in the successful design and implementation of information systems. Yet, there is much that we do not know about user participation and, as we will argue below, it is dangerous to assume that user participation necessarily leads to successful project outcomes. One researcher offered the following colour ful characterization of the relationship between user participation and project outcomes:

‘It is intuitively appealing that user participation leads to system success. However, the empirical evidence on the merits of user participation has as much clarity as would a law of gravity stating that a falling object may sometimes come down, occasionally go up, and peri odically drift to the side’ (Saleem, 1996, p. 145).

During the past decade, our knowledge regarding the effects of user participation on system development outcomes has improved dramatically due to several conceptual and methodolog ical advances. These advances include: formulating clear and consistent definitions of terms (Barki & Hartwick, 1989; 1994); specifying moderating and mediating variables that determine when and how user participation produces its effects (Hartwick & Barki, 1994; McKeen et al., 1994; Hunton & Beeler, 1997) developing process models to identify events that occur when users participate in development (Robey & Farrow, 1982); and using longitudinal field studies to actually observe these processes in action (Newman & Robey, 1992; Robey, 1994).

Despite the greater consistency in findings that have been achieved through identification of moderating and mediating variables (Figure 1), we argue that greater understanding of why user participation is valuable in some scenarios and not in others will require formulating and testing process theories that capture the communication that occurs between users and developers. We believe that this entails observing and understanding what happens when users and developers collaborate on software projects. Although we note the existence of an emerging lit erature on software project team co-ordination, these findings have not been integrated with our understanding of user participation. Moreover, researchers active in this emerging research stream on software project team co-ordination have used variance models and crosssectional surveys to understand this phenomenon. We believe that further theory development requires that we integrate these variance models of project team co-ordination and associated cross-sectional studies with our current process models of user participation. By merging these separate research traditions, we formulate a process model of user–developer communication that can help account for anomalous findings that cannot be explained by either research tradition alone.

## The critical role of user–developer communication

To justify the critical role of user–developer communication in shaping the events and outcomes of user participation, we begin by emphasizing user–developer communication as a common, but often implicit, theme in most models of user participation. We then highlight a common thread that runs throughout the literature on software project co-ordination, namely that informal, interpersonal communication is most important in achieving successful results. Finally, we present a framework of user–developer communication that explicates, rather than assumes, how effective communication occurs, and we use it to analyse results from a longitudinal case study of a failed software implementation effort.

Of the various process models of user participation, all assume that communication between users and developers does occur, but there is a surprising lack of specificity in describing the user–developer communication process. Starting with Robey and colleagues’ early work on conflict models of participation (Robey & Farrow, 1982; Robey et al., 1989), studies identified the role of user influence (i.e. power) in software development and how, under certain conditions, user influence may lead to conflict and, if properly managed, to conflict–resolution. Subsequently, Newman & Noble (1990) proposed a ‘learning process model’ to explain the events that occur during user participation. They highlight the importance of two-way communication as a precursor to learning, and they argued that ‘reciprocal learning’ must occur for participation to add value (i.e. users learn from developers and vice versa), rather than just ‘one-way learning.’ More recently, Hunton & Beeler (1997) examined the role of users having ‘instrumental voice’ (i.e. influence) during system design and its impact on their use of and satisfaction with the resulting system. Users who provided input into the design, but who lacked instrumental voice, were less satisfied with the resulting system than their peers who had real influence throughout the design process. Finally, Majchrzak & Beath (2000) proposed a detailed model suggesting that several processes must occur for user participation to lead to successful outcomes. Specifically, they argue that participation must trigger negotiation, collaborative learning, and cognitive elaboration, in order for positive outcomes to ensue. In short, developers and users must communicate with and learn from each other, in the process elaborating their own cognitive schemes as they negotiate critical decisions related to IS design and implementation.

What is noteworthy about all of these process models is the central role of communication, although such communication is often taken for granted (Hartwick & Barki, 2001). We define communication as the process by which one party transmits information to another party, regardless of whether the information is received at the other end, and regardless of the fidelity of the received message to the original source. Researchers examine both political aspects of communication (conflict, influence, instrumental voice and negotiation), as well as various outcomes that communication may produce (one-way learning, reciprocal learning and cognitive elaboration). These models either specify or assume that communication between users and developers occurs and that it must trigger some form of learning, conflict, or change for participation to deliver true benefits. Although several of these models focus on political processes that accompany communication, they all assume that the most fundamental communication process, transmitting feedback from users to developers, occurs without problem. We question this basic assumption and, as we shall demonstrate later, we believe that important information may not always be conveyed to developers under certain conditions, even when there are many opportunities for users to communicate with developers

## Informal, interpersonal communication in software project teams

Recently, scholars have begun to examine communication and co-ordination processes within large software project teams. Although these studies were not designed to evaluate the role of user participation, their insights are important nonetheless. For example, based on surveys and field studies of 85 software project teams, Kraut & Streeter (1995) found that informal com munication was as critical for achieving effective project outcomes as formal communication. They concluded that informal, interpersonal modes of communication were statistically related to successful project outcomes, but were not used as frequently as their value would indicate.

Faraj & Sproull (2000) have also shown that certain forms of communication are associated with more effective software development teams. In a study of 69 software teams, they showed that the mere presence of expertise on software project teams was not sufficient for successful project, outcomes unless specific communication processes were in place to leverage such expertise. Their analysis demonstrated that achieving effective project outcomes requires more than just having expertise on software project teams: there must also be sufficient inter personal communication among team members to produce ‘an emergent process of informal interactions and joint problem solving to bring [this] expertise to bear’ (Faraj & Sproull, 2000, p. 1557).

Taken together, these recent findings underscore the fact that certain forms of communication (i.e. informal, interpersonal communication) are more valuable than others, and that the total volume of communication within a project team may be a poor proxy to assess whether or not critical information exchange processes have occurred (Guinan & Faraj, 1998).

## Causes of ineffective user–developer communication

As early as 1977, Edstrom proposed that ineffective communication between users and ‘EDP specialists’ would lead to user dissatisfaction with the resulting system. Only a few studies however, have examined the factors that can lead to ineffective communication between users and developers. In a laboratory experiment prompted by Edstrom’s predictions, DeBrander and colleagues (DeBrabander & Edstrom, 1977; DeBrabander & Thiers, 1984) showed that power asymmetry between developers and users, where developers had ‘sanctionary power’ over users, would make users less inclined to use a system that they had jointly designed with developers, despite their agreement to use it. These authors argued that such power asymmetry distorts free and open user–developer communication, and results in only ‘apparent agreement’ that users will use the system as designed, rather than true ‘mutual agreement. When developers had sanctionary power over users, users would ‘go through the motions’ of agreeing on the system solution, but their actual post-implementation behaviour did not adhere to this agreement. When the researchers manipulated the experimental conditions so that users and developers had equal power, users did actually use the system in the agreed-upon manner. DeBrabander & Thiers (1984) concluded that ineffective communication occurs when developers have sanctionary power over users, causing users’ behaviour, after completion of the jointly designed system, to diverge from what users had previously agreed to do.

Although this experiment showed that power inequities could lead to ineffective communication and system non-use, one question remains: does ineffective communication occur because users provide incorrect information to developers, withhold certain information from developers (i.e. their misgivings about the proposed system), or because developers fail to disclose critical information to users? Despite the contrived research setting in Debrabander & Thiers’ study ( Debrabander & Thiers, 1984), their results validate our claim that, under certain conditions, effective user–developer communication does not occur, either because information is not transmitted between the two groups or because information that is transmitted is somehow misleading or distorted.

## Formulating a process model of user–developer communication

Prior research on user participation, project team co-ordination, and user–developer communication, suggests that potential synergies can be gained by applying a process perspective to model user–developer communication. Our process model, which we introduce below, was inductively derived from the case study that follows rather than defined a priori, and yet, it reflects many key themes found in the literature. Although it is common practice to defer the presentation of a research framework that was inductively derived from a field study until after describing the study (Orlikowski, 1993; Montealegre & Keil, 2000), here we briefly introduce the broad contours of our framework. We offer this brief skeletal view of the framework, in part, as an analytical aid to the reader, to help anticipate events that follow in the case study, and to foreshadow how we will categorize and interpret these events in our subsequent analysis.

Figure 2 shows these basic contours of our model of the user–developer communication process. Like other process models, our model reflects a number of necessary but not sufficient conditions that must occur for a particular outcome to ensue (Markus & Robey, 1988; Soh & Markus, 1995). We briefly introduce the model and its four stages here; however, a more complete description of why these four stages are important and what contextual conditions may prevent them from occurring is deferred until after our presentation of the critical case study. There, we will analyse why each of these four stages is critical for successful project out comes, and explain why various ‘communication lapses’ occurred, which rendered several stages of user–developer communication ineffective. We will argue that, despite the many opportunities for users to offer feedback, where such communication lapses did occur in one or more of these stages of our model, then the presumed value of user participation may have been negated. As depicted in Figure 2, the four stages of the user–developer communication process are:

Figure 2. Four-stage model of user-to-developer feedback

1. Users become conscious of messages to communicate to developers (e.g. requirements suggestions, complaints and other feedback).

2. Users transmit messages to developers through one or more communication channels.

3. Developers receive and interpret messages from users.

4. Developers set priorities and take action, based on their interpretation of messages.

The first two stages focus on users’ cognitions and communication behaviour, whereas the latter stages examine the cognitions and behaviour of software developers. These four stages are shaped by various forces, which comprise the context in which user–developer commu nication occurs. As we shall see later, these contextual forces include the design concept for the software, and the development environment. In addition, as shown in our preliminary process model, we anticipate that there may be multiple channels by which users may communicate their requirements, concerns, and other feedback to software developers. Among these are communication channels that are available only to some users – those selected representatives who participate on the project team – and other communication channels that are available to all potential users, whether or not they directly participate on the project team. Some communication channels are two-way (e.g. project team meetings), supporting interactive dialogue between users and developers, whereas other channels are one-way only (e.g. user surveys), as they permit information to flow in one direction only (from users to developers), but not interactively. Thus, different channels linking users and developers have different levels of communication breadth (the number of users with access to the channel) and communication depth (the channel’s richness), reflecting constructs from media richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986).

We inductively developed this process model based on our analysis of what went wrong during a very large software development project in which users participated substantially throughout the project’s lifecycle but, nevertheless, failed to provide key information to developers, which was necessary to create a system that users would accept and use. Despite the many communication opportunities available to users, the most critical feedback that they needed to transmit to developers, their negative evaluation of the system and the reasons they perceived it as not supporting them in their work, never reached the developers. The developers’ resulting failure to understand why users avoided the system permitted the firm to undertake an expensive redesign and redeployment of the system, one that, in hindsight, tinkered with many details of the system’s user interface, technology infrastructure, and implementation process, but failed to address users’ chief concerns about the system, a lack of ‘task/technology fit’ (Goodhue, 1995).

Next, we describe our research approach and introduce the case study and later, in the Discussion, we use an expanded version of our communication process model to make sense of this failure to communicate, despite high levels of user participation in the project and the many communication opportunities available to users.

## RESEARCH APPROACH

As the initial goal for this research was to understand the development and evolution of a large software project, we used a longitudinal case study approach, as recommended by other researchers who have studied the process of user participation in IS development (Newman & Noble, 1990; Newman & Robey, 1992; Robey, 1994). The particular case we examine involves an expert system project called CONFIG, which was undertaken by a firm called CompuSys (pseudonyms are used for both the company name and the project name to preserve anonymity). One reason for choosing this particular project to illustrate our model was that various aspects of CONFIG and its chequered history have been documented elsewhere (Markus & Keil, 1994; Keil, 1995; Keil et al., 1995). The second author had already conducted longitudina research on the CONFIG case, and we were able to supplement this original data and analysis with additional survey data that was collected between 2 to 6 vears after CONEIG's initia implementation. This paper presents our analysis of several new rounds of both qualitative and quantitative data that we collected from users. These data suggest several new insights about the CONFIG project, including the reason for its failure and problems in managing user participation on software projects. These insights were not available from prior analyses, which mostly focused on the system’s lack of task/technology fit (Markus & Keil, 1994) or managers escalation of commitment to the CONFIG project (Keil, 1995). Most of this additional survey data that we describe below has not previously been analysed or published.

## THE STORY OF CONFIG

The CONFIG project began in 1980 and was terminated in 1992. Since prior research (Keil, 1995; Markus & Keil, 1994) has examined this case in detail, we provide only a short synopsis here, focusing our attention on the barriers to use reported in the five surveys. CONFIG was conceived as an expert system to help the field sales force from a high-tech manufacturing firm (here labelled CompuSys) to produce error-free equipment configurations, before providing a price quote to a potential customer. Interestingly, the development of CONFIG was housed in manufacturing, which was the champion and the key beneficiary of CONFIG, whereas sales representatives (hereafter labelled ‘sales reps’) were the target users.

CONFIG’s project manager decided early in the design process to use a participative design approach. A ‘user design group’ (UDG) was organized with the goal of having users participate in CONFIG’s design and development. The UDG met 13 times over the lifecycle of CONFIG, with more frequent meetings during the early phases of design and occasional meetings thereafter. The response of the participating users to the design process was quite positive as indicated by the following remarks from two different users:

‘I think it is one of the most innovative aspects of the whole project. I’m very impressed. It gives him [a sales rep] the feeling that he really has a say in what’s going on within the whole project . . . The way it’s working, you feel like you are truly a designer of the system.

‘You feel that when the system finally . . . reaches a point in the design that it can be distributed to the field at large, that it’s a tool that will be well accepted by the field because their own peers helped design it.’

In addition to the UDG meetings, a special on-line user comment and bug reporting feature was included in the system to permit users to enter their feedback about system bugs and enhancement requests, thus providing another channel for user-to-developer feedback. After nearly 3 years of development, the initial version of CONFIG was deployed to the field sales force. Usage of CONFIG among the sales reps was voluntary and began slowly; many used it only for their most complex customer orders, preferring to manually configure more routine or simple orders, rather than use CONFIG for all orders. After the initial implementation, members of the UDG and system developers continued to meet periodically, where they identified needed fixes and sought to eliminate barriers to CONFIG’s usage. In addition to these periodic meetings between users and developers, CompuSys also retained an external consultant to conduct a series of telephone surveys with the sales reps. She interviewed approximately 100 sales reps by telephone during each of three years (1984–86), using a structured interview protocol.

The results of the three telephone surveys (see Table 2) show that inadequate training or access to the system were early barriers to adoption, but by 1986, the most common reasons for non-adoption or limited use by adopters were that CONFIG was ‘difficult to learn’ or plagued by ‘slow response time.’ As sales reps’ initial adoption of CONFIG increased between 1984 and 1986, so did negative word-of-mouth about its problems. By 1986, more than 33% of users who had previously tried CONFIG indicated that they had discontinued using it.

## The redeployment of a new-and-improved CONFIG

In 1989, after a period of declining use, the developers undertook a redesign and redeployment effort to address the users’ chief concerns. Five regional sales offices agreed to participate as pilot sites for the redeployment effort. To resolve the poor response time problems, Manufacturing agreed to provide dedicated hardware, guaranteeing sufficient processing power to ensure fast response time. To address the many complaints that the system was cumbersome, a new user interface was designed to make the system more intuitive, modelled after a menudriven office automation package that was already familiar to the sales reps. In redeploying CONFIG, the project team also paid attention to many details of how the system was reintroduced to the sales reps. An Application Support Specialist and a System Support Specialist were deployed at each pilot site to help with user training/support and technical support.

In nearly every respect, CONFIG’s redesign and redeployment was a ‘textbook case’ of good system implementation. Based on our own ‘predeployment’ survey, administered before redeployment and designed to measure perceptions of the ‘old CONFIG’, the redeployment initiative was clearly focused on the problems that users most frequently identified as barriers to use.

## CASE ANALYSIS

Despite the fact that developers addressed seven of the sales reps’ most frequent complaints about CONFIG during redeployment, this expensive effort led to little change where it really counted: users’ overall evaluation of CONFIG and their actual system usage. This is a surprising result and even a paradoxical one, in light of the many improvements that the sales reps noticed after redeployment (such as increased system availability, timeliness, reliability, etc.), as shown in Table 4. Although we hesitate to say that the CONFIG project would have fared better without such a high level of user participation, it does appear that the ways in which user participation was managed had much to do with the negative outcomes that occurred. Ironically, although there were numerous communication channels established for user– developer feedback, they ultimately proved ineffective. As we will show, it was the combination of weak signals from the users, substantial ‘noise’ citing various incidental problems with CONFIG, and selective perception on the part of the developers that led to failure of the redeployment initiative.

Previously, we introduced the broad contours of our process model (Figure 2), which iden tified four communication stages, along with multiple channels for users to communicate with developers. Based on the insights from our case study, we present a more detailed version of the process model, identifying the many communication channels, as well as problems that may have hampered their effective utilization. In general, the deeper channels supported two-way transmission of information (e.g. face-to-face project team meetings), whereas the shallower channels supported only one-way transmission from users to developers. Despite the greater communication depth of the twoway channels (face-to-face meetings), they were restricted to a small subset of the overal sales rep population, whereas the more shallow (one-way) channels were, in principle, open to all target users of CONFIG. ## Stage 1: users become conscious of messages to transmit to developers

In Stage 1, we consider whether and how users become aware of certain information, which they may later communicate to developers. During CONFIG’s initial deployment, the content of these messages was fairly broad, general requirements for how a system could support them in the configuration task. After implementation, however, the messages that users formulated became more specific, such as their likes and dislikes about CONFIG, or desired changes that might enable them to use it more easily or more frequently. One lapse of communication that may have occurred, even at this early stage, was users’ inability to become conscious of certain issues, either because they were unaware of some issues as being problems, or they saw no need to communicate something that seemed obvious to them. If particular factors related to using CONFIG were below the users’ threshold of consciousness, they would be unable to articulate them as problems.

In this regard, it is noteworthy that CONFIG’s redeployment effort focused on making technical changes to CONFIG and carefully managing the details of the pilot implementation, but it did not consider issues of ‘task/technology fit’ (Goodhue, 1995), that is, whether CONFIG was a valuable tool with concrete benefits for users. As the objectives and scope of CONFIG redeployment were framed as ‘fixing CONFIG’s technical problems’, users would more likely be conscious of CONFIG’s various technical problems than they would be aware of more fundamental issues of job design, such as whether CONFIG’s purpose was congruent with their job duties and with the new sales culture that senior management advocated. In other words, there was a bias toward adapting the technology rather than the organization to address misalignments between them (Leonard-Barton, 1988). Given this bias, the issue of whether sales reps should be responsible for configuring customer orders and whether configuration accuracy should be part of their performance evaluation process was never even discussed. Thus, few sales reps ever overtly challenged the basic assumption that sales reps should be respon sible for configuring customer orders and even fewer mentioned the lack of organizational incentives to use CONFIG. In short, issues of task/technology fit and the possibility of adapting the organization to resolve misalignments (through changes in job duties or incentives) neve reached consciousness for most sales reps.

The reasons why, in hindsight, sales reps should have questioned whether using CONFIG was relevant to their jobs are numerous. Sales reps were not penalized in any way when they configured customer orders improperly. Using CONFIG required extra time and effort by the sales reps, but using the system was not linked to their performance evaluations. In contrast, the benefits of having sales reps use CONFIG accrued to the manufacturing department, which was able to produce and ship accurately configured customer orders and avoid the need for subsequent rework. This misalignment of incentives, whereby the sales reps had to bear the cost of using CONFIG while manufacturing reaped the benefits, appears to be an obvious reason why sales reps avoided using the system. What we find puzzling is how rarely this misalignment of incentives was mentioned. The reasons for such a lack of overt complaints are unclear, although we believe that, for many sales reps, Stage 1 of the process model (‘Users become conscious of messages to transmit to developers’) may not have occurred, at leas with regard to issues of task/technology fit.

The few experienced ‘configurers’ who were represented on the UDG and RUAG were unlikely to be conscious of the incentive misalignment problem precisely because most of them were configuration experts. These expert configurers, who comprised the majority of both user groups (UDG and RUAG), enjoyed configuring sales orders and would not have required othe incentives to use CONFIG. These experts had direct face-to-face contact with developers in project meetings, and would very likely have ensured that CONFIG was designed to meet their expert level needs. Neither our recorded observations of the UDG meetings (channel 1) or RUAG meetings (channel 4), nor any other sources documenting CONFIG’s design and development (Mumford & MacDonald, 1989) mentioned such concerns about CONFIG’s fit with the job context.

## Stage 2: users transmit messages to developers

After users formulate their ideas, they may decide to transmit them to developers through one or more channels. Yet, for sales reps to transmit messages to developers, three conditions must be met. Sales reps must (1) be aware of some information to communicate; (2) be aware of an opportunity to communicate the information; and (3) actively choose to communicate the message through a communication channel. Whereas Stage 1 described how sales reps become aware of certain messages to transmit, or not, Stage 2 considers how users recognize opportunities to communicate information, and whether and how they decide to transmit this information (or decide not to transmit it).

At CompuSys, there was no shortage of channels for transmitting complaints or suggestions about how to improve CONFIG, as depicted by the five channels in Figure 3. Given the various channels available, most sales reps had the opportunity to provide feedback about CONFIG to developers. If they neglected to communicate certain concerns about CONFIG, it seems unlikely that a lack of opportunity was to blame, although we acknowledge that only some of the channels were available to all sales reps. Two criteria determine whether a communication channel is available for transmitting certain information: its breadth (the number of users with access to the channel) and its depth (the channel’s richness for transmitting a range of verbal and non-verbal information). Whereas we already mentioned some limitations to channel breadth (channels 1 and 4 were not available to all sales reps), here we consider limitations to the channels’ depth. Table 5 summarizes the breadth and depth dimensions that characterize the five channels. We classify the channel’s depth as high if it supports a range of verbal and non-verbal communication (e.g. face-to-face meetings), moderate if it supports some nonverbal cues, such as tone of voice (e.g. telephone conversations), and low if it supports only text information (e.g. e-mail and written surveys).

Although the face-to-face meetings were high in depth (supporting rich communication), they were very low in breadth. Of the remaining channels, which were, in principle, high in breadth (i.e. available to all sales reps), most of these had either low breadth in actual practice, low depth or a combination thereof. Consider the on-line bug reporting feature. Beyond the fact that the communication depth enabled by this text-based interface was low, sales reps had to be already signed on to and using CONFIG, to transmit messages through this channel. This effectively served to screen out all non-users of CONFIG, thus limiting the channel’s actual breadth to a small base of CONFIG users. Moreover, the fact that these sales reps were already using the system suggests that their perceptions of CONFIG would likely differ from those of non-users. Based on Table 5, it is noteworthy that no communication channel was high in both breadth and depth. With one exception, the communication channels that were high in breadth were low in depth, and vice versa. In other words, the communication channels were either capable of transmitting rich information from just a few potential message senders, or able to transmit lean information from a broader base of potential senders.

The only exception to this pattern was the annual telephone surveys conducted by the external consultant, which we consider to be high in breadth and moderate in depth. Although the consultant’s phone interviews were structured, they were interactive, able to communicate some non-verbal cues, but not as rich as face-to-face meetings (Daft & Lengel, 1986). In ou opinion, these interviews provided the greatest total bandwidth of any communication channel, considering both the available breadth and depth. This suggests that the phone interviews held the greatest potential for revealing sales reps’ perceptions of CONFIG and their actual reasons for failing to use it. Given the types of concerns that sales reps expressed during these inter views (slow response time, lack of user training, etc.), it does not appear that many sales reps communicated their true reasons for not using CONFIG. This may be the case for either of two reasons: the reps avoided sharing their true reasons for not using CONFIG, or the information they did share was somehow distorted between the time they sent it (responded to the survey) and when developers received it. We consider the first of these possibilities here (that users withheld information from developers); the latter possibility, we address in Stage 3.

If users were aware of their underlying reasons for not using CONFIG, a lack of fit with their job duties and incentives, results of the telephone surveys and pre-redeployment written survey do not indicate it. This may have been related to how both sets of survey questions were framed for the sales reps to answer. In the telephone and written interviews, sales reps were never asked to list all the reasons why they did not use CONFIG (or all the reasons why they did not use it more often). Instead, these interviews asked sales reps to report ‘the one thing above all that keeps you from using CONFIG’, and ‘the one thing that you would like to see changed about CONFIG.’ It appears that the framing of these questions prompted the users to identify just one factor that they disliked about CONFIG and also encouraged them to consider only technical problems, rather than broader sociotechnical issues, such as job design or performance incentives. Despite being asked many times for their opinions about CONFIG and any barriers to use, the vast majority of sales reps reported technical problems with the system. Once these problems were resolved, however, this did not alter their system usage. Very few users raised more sensitive issues, such as questioning why they should use a complex system like CONFIG in the first place. Even if sales reps did become conscious that their failure to use CONFIG was related to a lack of task/technology fit or a lack of incentives to use it, few ever reported this fact in the various surveys.

Users may perceive certain beliefs that they hold or messages that they might (in an ideal world) wish to communicate to be undiscussable (Argyris, 1994). Argyris coined this term to refer to issues that managers prefer to remain unspoken and unchallenged. The concept of undiscussability extends not just to the issue that must remain unspoken (e.g. ‘Why do we have to use this system if it doesn't benefit us?’). but also to the fact that no one will acknowledge that the taboo topic exists (e.g. ‘Why does nobody raise the issue that sales reps have no real incentive to use CONFIG?’). Like its better-known counterpart, groupthink (Janis, 1972), the problem of undiscussability has been shown to lead to poor communication and decisionmaking in organizations. Clemons et al. (1995), for example, showed that undiscussable issues were responsible for thwarting the benefits of business process reengineering. At CompuSys, we believe that undiscussability is a partial explanation for why sales reps so rarely challenged the rationale for having to use CONFIG. Most reps simply perceived this direct challenge to the legitimacy of using CONFIG as too sensitive an issue to voice.

The other alternative, which allowed sales reps to ‘save face’ during these interviews, was simply to communicate other messages regarding what they disliked about CONFIG, which could mask their true reasons for not using it. After all, the sales reps were asked to identify only one barrier to use of CONFIG or one attribute of the system that should be changed. They could save face by disguising their actual reasons for resisting CONFIG, instead citing other real, but incidental technical problems with the system. Although these reported problems may have been authentic and may have exacerbated reps’ dislike of CONFIG, there was no explicit guarantee that if developers fixed these technical problems that the Reps would embrace the system.

Sales reps thus provided socially acceptable criticisms of CONFIG’s speed, functionality, and user interface without having to reveal that they were fundamentally averse to the idea of having to spend time performing complex configurations on a computer. In effect, even if sales reps were aware that their underlying reason for avoiding CONFIG was that they were opposed to the ‘change idea’ implicit in CompuSys’ decision to deploy CONFIG (Markus & Benjamin, 1997), they could criticize other, technical aspects of the system, without having to mention their fundamental challenge to using the system. Indeed, such behaviour may easily serve as a delaying tactic for change-resistant users, who can avoid disclosing their true reasons for rejecting a system, while providing developers with more culturally appropriate problems and excuses for not using the system. By identifying other, incidental system problems, the sales reps could keep the developers busy with fixing technical problems which, once resolved, would not make any real difference to their system usage. We believe that these behaviours, which Keen (1981) labelled as ‘counter-implementation tactics’, help to explain why CONFIG survived so long without engendering obvious user acceptance or overt resistance. Markus & Benjamin (1997) explain how subtle yet devious such behaviour can be:

‘IT is a package of ideas about how people should work differently. When users understand perfectly the change idea embedded in a technology but do not like it, they don’t have to confront the issue directly. Instead of criticizing the change plan (a potentially embarrassing challenge to those who believe in it), they can merely criticize the IT itself . . . [T]hey blame the technology for their failure to do what they don’t want to do . . . Better yet, instead of panning the technology, opposed users can offer constructive, convincing suggestions about how to make it better. This deludes technology initiators and builders into believing that users have overlooked the change agenda or have finally accepted it. Nothing could be farther from the truth.

So far, we have shown why communication lapses may occur between sales reps and developers if Reps lack awareness of particular messages (Stage 1: users become conscious of messages to transmit), or if they recognize certain information to transmit, but are unaware of opportunities to do so, or consciously avoid verbalizing their thoughts (Stage 2: users transmit messages to developers). If even a few users were willing and able to reveal their true reason for not using CONFIG, it would seem reasonable that developers would become aware of the lack of task/technology fit or lack of incentives to use the system. In Stages 3 and 4 of our model (below), we address why this ‘reasonable’ outcome failed to occur. We examine why messages that are transmitted to developers may not be received or may not achieve thei intended results.

## Stage 3: developers receive and interpret message

Stage 3 considers how developers do or do not receive the various messages sent by devel opers, including various filters that operate to distort or screen out particular messages Although there are various types of cognitive filters, such as interpretative schemes, mental models, and differences in the use of language by different social groups, the primary filter we consider is where an intermediary is positioned between the message sender and receiver, who may distort or screen out messages. Although such intermediaries may add value by aggregating and prioritizing messages, or by clarifying ambiguous meanings, they can also distort communication, whether consciously or unwittingly. Prior research on user–developer communication has identified problems that such ‘go-betweens and mediators’ (Grudin, 1991) can interject into communication. In examining such communication, Keil & Carmel (1995) found that ‘intermediaries can intentionally or unintentionally filter and distort messages . . [and] may not have a complete understanding of customer needs’.

For several communication channels depicted in Figure 3, one or more intermediaries were positioned between sales reps and developers, each reducing the likelihood that a message transmitted by the sender user would be received by developers exactly in its original form. Although each intermediary existed because it added value in other ways, the precise influ ence of these intermediaries on the fidelity of user–developer communication is unknown. The severity of this problem with intermediaries ‘filtering’ out important information cannot be ascertained. Table 6 identifies the intermediaries who had the potential to modify or screen out the contents of selected communication channels. For the telephone surveys, the respondents raw, spoken comments were not recorded and directly transmitted to developers. Instead, two intermediaries, the consultant who conducted the interviews and the CONFIG ‘Program Office’, both served as filters. Although we previously identified the telephone interviews as the channel with the greatest potential bandwidth (based on a combination of breadth and depth), each intermediary altered the fidelity of the original messages (to an unknown degree), before transmitting them down the line. Specifically, the consultant who conducted the telephone interviews coded respondents’ answers, aggregated, and then counted them, generating reports that incorporated his understanding of the problems, but as interpreted through himself as a first-level filter. These coded, summarized reports were not themselves directly sent to developers; instead, they were routed to the CONFIG ‘Program Office’, where project managers reviewed and interpreted the messages, and decided what information should be relayed to CONFIG’s developers.

In addition to considering the bandwidth of each channel (i.e. its breadth and depth), it is also important to acknowledge the fidelity with which the senders’ information is transmitted to the final recipient (developers). As we did not interview the various intermediaries and are, thus, unable to evaluate the degree to which they may have deliberately or unwittingly distorted the information, we simply note that the fidelity of a communication channel with one intermediary is ‘moderate’, and a channel with two or more intermediaries is ‘low fidelity.’ We acknowledge that this is a somewhat coarse method to characterize the channel fidelity, however, the basic principle that fidelity is inversely proportional to the number of intermediaries is supported by prior research (Grudin, 1991; Keil & Carmel, 1995). For this reason, we perceive the fidelity of the telephone surveys as low (two levels of intermediaries), whereas the fidelity of the written surveys was moderate (one intermediary).

## Stage 4: developers set priorities and take action

In the model’s final stage, developers aggregate the information that they do receive and pri oritize tasks to perform, according to priorities determined by the project manager, or by thei own, implicit cognitive schemes for prioritizing them. One possible approach that developers may have followed for prioritizing the needed changes to CONFIG would be to rely on a frequency count of how many times a particular problem had been reported across the population of sales reps. For example, if 25% of sales reps reported that the slow system response time was the most important barrier that prevented them from using CONFIG, but only 2% reported that they avoided CONFIG because they thought that ‘sales should be selling, not spending valuable time inside the office [working] on a system’, then one method that developers could use to prioritize changes to CONFIG would be directly based on the frequency count, regard less of whether fixing certain problems would cause sales reps to change their usage behaviour.

In the human decision-making literature, researchers have identified various cognitive biases that influence judgement and decision-making. For the most part, such biases are by-products of what Simon (1957) called ‘bounded rationality’, due to the natural limits on people’s attention span and information-processing ability. To cope with the threat of information overload, decision-makers use various heuristics to simplify their information-processing tasks. Such heuristics may include selectively seeking information that supports their pre-existing beliefs, whereas disregarding any contradictory information (Langer, 1983). As helpful as such heu ristics may seem, they can lead to an ‘overconfidence bias’ (Einhorn & Hogarth, 1978; Clemen, 1996), whereby decision-makers perceive greater certainty in their decisions than is warranted by the quality of the information itself. Sage (1981) attributes this overconfidence bias to three related factors: (1) redundancy in the information that decision-makers compile; (2) selective perception of evidence that confirms their preconceptions; and (3) the desire for self-fulfilling prophecies. The overconfidence bias triggers a failure to seek, or outright rejection of, disconfirming evidence. One detailed review of the overconfidence bias argued that:

‘The extent to which information is repetitious and redundant adds to overconfidence . . . With all these processes: selectivity, repetition, redundancy, the stress is on ‘quantity’ of information – as the accumulation of information increases, confidence goes up without an accompanying increase in predictive accuracy.’ Rai, Stubbart & Paper (1994), p. 97.

At CompuSys, sales reps frequently reported a handful of common problems, while so rarely mentioning what we consider to be the real problems underlying non-use of CONFIG; a lack of task/technology fit and misalignment of incentives. As a result, it appears that developers were confident that they were addressing the most important problems preventing use of CON-FIG, simply because they were addressing the concerns most frequently reported in the sur veys. Although this would appear sensible, given the fact that developers could not be expected to sense and respond to problems that sales reps failed to report, in hindsight, we know that frequency counts were not a good proxy for the severity of a problem. We know this because once these widely reported problems were resolved, little change in system usage occurred, and a statistically significant decline in sales reps’ perception of CONFIG’s usefulness was noted.

Our data reveal that at least a few sales reps were aware that the problems underlying the lack of incentive to use CONFIG were not being addressed. For example, one sales rep, who was critical of developers’ priorities during the redesign and redeployment of CONFIG, commented:

‘They tried to mask the problems with ‘we’ll make it faster, give you local access to system hardware’, etc., but they ignored the core issue.

By focusing on the most frequently reported problems, CONFIG’s project manager and developers gave themselves false assurance that they were addressing the most critical barriers to use of CONFIG. Thus, the final communication lapse was in developers inability to ‘read between the lines’ of the complaints that reached them or question whether altering many technical aspects of CONFIG’s user interface and infrastructure would truly lead to acceptance and widespread use.

One important contextual factor that also shaped developers’ behaviour was what Vaughan (1996) refers to as the ‘culture of production and the production of culture.’ When CONFIG was initially implemented and found to be ‘stable’ from a technical standpoint, it was supported by approximately a dozen maintenance programmers in a ‘production mode.’ The fact that it was regarded as a ‘production system’, meant that CONFIG was treated like any other working system, developers identified critical changes and implemented them according to a quarterly (90- day) release cycle. The incentives of working according to a ‘production system’ mentality meant that developers wanted to make as many changes as they could for the next release, but that working on any single major change, particularly one that would take more than 90 days, was automatically a low priority. In addition, given the fact that the maintenance developers needed to update CONFIG’s database that tracked all CompuSys’ hardware products and the expert systems logic regarding how to configure them, these updates consumed a large portion of the maintenance effort. Thus, the amount of changes that developers could make to each new quarterly release of CONFIG was the difference between the total man-hours of programmer time available minus the number of man-hours spent updating CONFIG’s product database and expert system logic. With the limited residual time available, the maintenance programmers perceived that making many small systems changes showed greater progress than making one or a few large changes; in any case, a single change that might require more than 90 days automatically became moot, because it could not fit into the quarterly release cycle. The one exception to this ‘culture of production’ was the redeployment initiative, when additional manpower was made available for the maintenance programmers, but both before and subsequent to redeployment, the culture of production was in effect.

In addition to the culture of production, we observed the ‘production of culture’ (Vaughan, 1996) within CompuSys’ software development group. Specifically, the integration of CONFIG (which belonged to the Manufacturing division) with the company’s price quotation system (which belonged to the Sales division) was viewed as a ‘non-goal’ from the very beginning o the project. This design decision stemmed partly from an inadequate understanding of the sales process (which required tight integration between the configuration and price-quotation systems), and partly from an organizational structure and culture that discouraged cooperation among autonomous subunits. Over time, this decision to avoid integrating CONFIG with the price-quotation system led to the production of a culture that stubbornly regarded CONFIG as a viable standalone tool, and never revisited the possibility of making large-scale technical adaptations to integrate the two systems.

## Summary of process model and communication lapses

Our process model specifies four communication stages that are necessary for developers to understand users’ needs and to appropriately prioritize issues requiring resolution. The above discussion has identified reasons why communication lapses occurred at each stage. As this is a process model, it represents the necessary but not sufficient conditions for certain outcomes to occur (Markus & Robey, 1988; Soh & Markus, 1995). Thus, if even one serious lapse prevented some communication stage from occurring, then this could justify why the presumed benefits of high user participation were not realized. Instead of just identifying one communi cation lapse, however, we identified several lapses that may have prevented clear and open user–developer communication. Sales reps may not have been aware of the reasons they failed to use CONFIG (communication lapse in Stage 1) or perhaps they were not aware of opportunities to explain the problem to developers, or they withheld the true reasons, and sub stituted other, less politically sensitive explanations for non-use (communication lapses in Stage 2). If sales reps did bother to openly transmit their negative beliefs about CONFIG, this information may not have reached the developers, due to the filtering effect of intermediaries who could have distorted this information as they aggregated and prioritized certain messages (lapse in Stage 3). Finally, even if developers received all the messages that described sales reps’ concerns about and reasons for avoiding CONFIG, these problems may not have been recognized and acted upon, due to the sheer number of other, incidental technical problems with CONFIG, as well as the overconfidence bias and production mentality that shaped how developers prioritized problems and which ones they chose to resolve (lapses in Stage 4).

Taken as a whole, the many communication lapses resulted in what we characterize as a problem of low signal-to-noise ratio. The real signal (messages) that needed to be communicated to developers regarding the reasons for sales reps’ non-use of CONFIG was rarely transmitted. Surrounding and obscuring this true signal, however, was considerable noise, other problems reported to developers which, although real, were only incidental to users’ real reasons for not using CONFIG. Due to the considerable amount of noise that was transmitted and due to the fact that the redeployment focused on remedying these most frequently reported problems, CompuSys expended much effort to redesign and redeploy CONFIG, yet they overlooked the reality that many sales reps perceived that performing complex on-line configura tions was inconsistent with their role as sales professionals and, in any event, they lacked incentives to do so. Although we do not mean to trivialize the many valid complaints that sales reps reported with regard to using CONFIG, many of these technical problems functioned as noise in the communication channels between users and developers. This noise ultimately served to confuse and mislead developers, because their efforts to resolve these problems did nothing to confront sales reps’ opposition to the ‘change idea’ underlying CONFIG or their avoidance of the system. Whereas sales reps complained the loudest about the slow response time, the need for more training, the difficult user interface, etc., nevertheless after these problems were resolved through the expensive redeployment, their system usage changed very little.

Although we do not challenge the widespread notion that user participation is a necessary condition for successful IS implementation, nevertheless, we conclude that users seemed to lead developers astray, in terms of directing them to focus on improvements to CONFIG’s user interface and system response time, rather than on more fundamental problems that needed to be addressed for the sales reps to accept and embrace the system. In their failure to surface the more fundamental issues of task/technology fit (Goodhue, 1995), misalignment of incentives (Markus & Keil, 1994), or even the unspoken assumptions about whether users or developers really controlled the software design process (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994), the sales reps were sending the developers off on a ‘wild goose chase’ by encouraging them to focus on problems that could be remedied through technical fixes, without targeting the undiscussable issues that just a few sales reps could articulate (such as the comment mentioned above: ‘Show me how it saves time! I’d rather deliver product quotes and sell the benefits of our solution . . . I’d rather worry about solving customer problems than dealing with configurations’).

As this case study of the failed CONFIG system has shown, even when user input is encouraged both early in the IS development process and on an ongoing basis, it may be easy for developers to focus on the most obvious concerns, in this case, the problems that were reported most frequently, whereas these may not be the most critical barriers to system usage. Sometimes the underlying problem may be difficult to detect. Getting users to articulate their concerns may require that members of software project teams have greater sensitivity to issues of organizational design, politics and reward structures. They need to be more deliberate in considering how to create an open communication environment where users lack inhibitions to share their most critical, undiscussable thoughts about a proposed system or the context in which it is to be used. Although we can offer no ‘magic bullet’ to remedy these problems, it is important to include users on the design team who have insight into organizationa culture and dynamics. For example, Davenport (1996) advocated having ‘social systems analysts’ as members of project teams, which he defined as people who can ‘shadow managers and workers to determine actual information needs and likely usage of planned systems [and] participate in system design to fit the system to organizational structure, culture and behaviour. This is similar to DeBrabander & Thiers’ (1984) proposed solution to resolve the ‘power asymmetry’ between developers and users, which they blamed as the source of ineffective communication. They advised managers to interject a third-party user advocate into the system design process, an ‘active question elicitor’, who could encourage users to ask more questions and thereby induce them to assume a stronger negotiating position in designing a system that more fully met their needs. This notion is consistent with an ongoing program of research in the social psychology literature, where Nemeth and colleagues have shown that minority group members (i.e. those holding a minority opinion) are often reluctant to share their views publicly and instead, they exercise their influence at a more subtle or latent level. If these minority views can be surfaced, through the role of active question elicitor, then they can stimulate divergent thinking. Such divergent thinking can lead to identification of novel alternatives, which the majority group may then consider and adopt (Nemeth, 1980). For example, Nemeth & Wachtler (1983) showed that, when members who hold minority opinions can voice their ideas, this can trigger the majority members to critically evaluate not only the alternate suggestions raised by the minority members but, more importantly, the majority members’ own deeply held beliefs. Thus, the opportunity to consider alternative viewpoints, perhaps by surfacing undiscussable issues, may allow employees to reassess their own deeply held beliefs (e.g. ‘doing configurations is a necessary part of the sales job, but we just need to improve this CONFIG system to make it easier’).

Such persons who understand the change management implications of a new system design, whether we label them as ‘social systems analysts’ or ‘active question elicitors’, could do much to open up the lines of communication between users and developers. In effect, such user advocates may help to raise the level of influence (Robey & Farrow, 1982) that users have throughout the project’s lifecycle and, although such increased influence on the part of users may trigger more conflict, this could minimize the risk of users ‘going through the motions’ of agreeing on a system design (DeBrabander & Thiers, 1984) that they have no intention of using. Creating the conditions for real user influence appears to be a critical step to leveraging the benefits of user participation, despite the likelihood of increased user–developer conflict that may arise. Avoiding conflict is not always possible or desirable, particularly if it means having to endure ineffective user–developer communication (Hartwick & Barki, 1994). In the case of CONFIG, it would have been better for users to raise more issues and problems with CON-FIG early on, perhaps even challenging the very assumption of why sales reps should perform the configuration task. Although voicing such an ‘undiscussable’ issue may engender conflict with their managers and developers, at least doing so would surface the issues. This conflict could then be acknowledged and managed, rather than having the type of scenario that we observed at CompuSys where users avoided open conflict, as well as clear communication.

## Implications for research and practice

One implication for researchers studying the influence of users on system and product designs is that it may be insufficient to record data simply on the level of user participation in the design process. In addition to examining the level of participation and searching for relationships with project success, it may be necessary to understand what types of users are involved in the project, how representative they are of other prospective users, how open is the dialogue dur ing system design, and how much influence users have over design and implementation deci sions. Second, research methods (whether qualitative or quantitative) should be sensitive to capturing the nuances of user input to the design and implementation process, reflecting the range of different user opinions and preferences, and not simply try to capture the average, most frequent, or ‘modal’ opinions of target users. Third, paying attention to the comments of the most vocal participants in user design meetings may be misleading, as such highly voca members may not speak for their more reticent peers.

Our process model is similar to other communication models that have been proposed in the literature. Similar to the early conduit or container models of communication (Shannon & Weaver, 1949; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995), our process model suggests that greater bandwidth between message-senders and message-receivers may improve communication effectiveness. In addition, however, our model also incorporates elements corresponding to more contemporary context or systems models of communication (Ciborra et al., 1984; Thralls & Blyler, 1993; Suchan & Dulek, 1998), which consider the broader context in which communication occurs, as well as problems in the sense-making process that may occur between members of different occupational groups (Von Meier, 1999).

Several implications for practitioners should also follow from our analysis. First, to simply encourage just any type of user participation in software projects may not be sufficient. Project managers should try to ensure that users feel empowered to share their ideas freely, and should recognize the greater risks that accompany situations in which users are reluctant to share their opinions. Users who participate on software design teams may experience such constraints either because the firm has a culture where certain issues are ‘undiscussable’, or because their experiences are so ingrained in the status quo that they are unable to imagine their work processes being any different. Project managers may need to create a ‘safe space that encourages both users and developers to be creative in identifying alternatives to the status quo, whether in terms of tools, work processes, job rewards or incentives, and other factors. This may require external facilitators in design meetings, or brain-storming with employees from other departments or users with prior experience in other companies or industries. In the absence of such a ‘safe space’, users may be reluctant to express their criticisms of the status quo, or may be incapable of envisioning a workplace much different from the one they inhabit.

Another implication for practitioners is that relying on a select group of users to represent other users’ interests in project design meetings creates the potential that, over time, the ‘participating’ users will differ in critical ways from their peers. Although more cautious selection process for appointing users to the design team may be one solution, other options would be to follow a specialized communication method during design team meetings (e.g. the Precision Communication Model) (Bostrom, 1989) or to supplement project design meetings with techniques such as contextual inquiry, which permit designers to spend time observing users in their native environment so as to better understand the context in which the technology will be used (Beyer & Holtzblatt, 1995; 1997; Leonard & Rayport, 1997). If CompuSys had adopted practices such as contextual inquiry before, or as part of, CONFIG’s redeployment, it may have been possible to detect the system’s lack of fit with the users’ jobs and the lack of incentives for sales reps to use a complex system that was unrelated to their sales performance.

## Strengths and limitations

This study is based on a single case of a failed software project in which it appears that users led developers astray by directing them to focus on the wrong set of issues. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such case study in the IS literature to explicitly draw attention to this problem. Although we are unaware of the extent to which these events may be generalized to other firms, we believe that our communication process model (Figure 3) has value for researchers and practitioners in other firms. The model provides a sequential framework to consider what information may or may not be formulated or transmitted from users to developers (or among other members of a software project team). We hope that our critical case study of CONFIG and our process model will serve as a rallying call for researchers to rec ognize the potential risks involved in advocating for increased user participation when participation alone may not facilitate open and honest communication among project team members.

## CONCLUSION

This study has analysed the longitudinal nature of user participation and its outcomes in a sin gle system development project over a 12-year lifespan. We have concluded that although user participation is a generally desirable condition for successful project outcomes, if user partic ipation is not properly managed to ensure open and honest communication, then the resulting system may not fit users’ needs and may be rejected or under-utilized. We believe that the outcomes in the case of CONFIG are both interesting and unusual, in part because CompuSys followed many principles of proper system design, especially in gaining high levels of user participation, both in terms of a subset of users who attended numerous system design meetings and through several rounds of user opinion surveys. Despite the firm’s best intentions to develop a system that would be useful to the target users and, in particular, by driving the redesign priorities based on the problems most frequently reported by users as the key barriers to use, the redeployment effort produced little improvement in users’ perceptions of CONFIG’s usefulness or their actual usage of the system. We believe that these results are sufficiently intriguing to challenge the received wisdom regarding the presumed benefits of user partici pation. Moreover, we urge IS practitioners and researchers to consider whether similar phenomena may occur in other product design or system design contexts, to explore other possible explanations for these events, and to identify strategies for avoiding these problems in the future.

## Biographies

Michael Gallivan is an Assistant Professor of Computer Information Systems (CIS) in the J Mack Robinson College of Business at Georgia State University. His research focuses on individual and organizational influences on technology adoption and technology-related learning. Dr Gallivan is also interested in how disruptive technological changes alter industry structures and competitive advantage His research has been published in Information Systems Journal, Information & Management, Information Technology & People IEEE Transactions on Professional Communication, and Data Base for Advances in Information Systems. He currently serves on the editorial board o Information Technology & People and Journal of Information Technology. He earned his bachelor’s degree from Harvard College, his MBA from the University of California, Berkeley, and his PhD from MIT’s Sloan School of Management.

Mark Keil is a Professor of Computer Information Systems (CIS) in the J. Mack Robinson College of Business at Georgia State University. His research focuses on software project management, with particular emphasis on understanding and preventing software proiect escalation. His research is also aimed at providing better tools for assessing software project risk and removing barriers to software use. Keil’s research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Sloan Management Review, Communications of the ACM Journal of Management Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Decision Support Systems, and other journals. He currently serves on the editorial board of IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management. He has also served as an Associate Editor for MIS Quarterly, and as Co-Editor of Data Base for Advances in Information Systems. He earned his bachelor’s degree from Princeton University, his master’s degree from MIT’s Sloan School of Management, and his doctorate in management information systems from the Harvard Business School.
