# The reluctance to report bad news on troubled software projects: a theoretical model

H. Jeff Smith\* & Mark Keil<sup>†</sup>

\*Wake Forest University, Babcock Graduate School of Management, Winston-Salem, NC 27109–7659, email: jeff.smith@mba.wfu.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30302–4015, USA, email: mkeil@gsu.edu

Abstract. By one recent account, only 26% of software development projects are completed on schedule, within budget, and with the promised functionality. The remaining 74% are troubled in some way: they are either cancelled before the development cycle is completed (28%) or are delivered late, over budget, and with reduced functionality (46%). In many cases, the most cost-effective solution would be to abort the troubled project early in the cycle, but senior managers are often unaware of the project’s problems. Anecdotal evidence and at least one recent study suggest that losses are sometimes increased by the reluctance of organizational members to transmit negative information concerning a project and its status. Thus, although evidence of a failing course of action may exist in the lower ranks of an organization, this information sometimes fails to be communicated up the hierarchy or is substantially distorted in the process. The result is that decisionmakers with the authority to change the direction of the project are unaware of its true status. By modelling this reluctance to transmit negative project status information and improving our understanding of the phenomenon, we believe that prescriptions can be developed eventually to reduce the losses from troubled development projects. In this theory development paper, we examine the reluctance to transmit negative information and develop a theoretical model that explains this phenomenon within a software project context. The model we develop draws on literature from the fields of organizational behaviour and communications, ethics, economics, information systems and psychology, and points the direction for future research.

Keywords: IS project failure, project management, project status reporting, whistle blowing

## INTRODUCTION

The reported statistics on information systems (IS) projects are bleak: a recent study reported that only 26% of projects are delivered on time, on budget, and with the promised functionality; 28% are cancelled before the development cycle is completed, whereas the remaining 46% are completed behind schedule, over budget, or with less functionality than was originally promised (Standish, 1999).

What explains this state of affairs? There are, of course, many contributing factors. Traditionally, researchers have focused on various implementation or risk factors and have advocated better project management and more user involvement as solutions to the problem. In general, this would appear to be sound advice, but we believe that some failures involve psychological, social, and organizational issues that cannot be addressed with techniques such as the critical path method or joint application development. In fact, there is reason to believe that one factor promoting problems is the reluctance of organizational members to transmit negative information concerning a project and its status. Although evidence of a failing course of action may exist in the lower ranks of an organization, this information sometimes fails to be communicated up the hierarchy (Keil & Robey, 1999) or is substantially distorted in the process (Iacovou & Dexter, 1996). The result is that decision-makers with the authority to change the direction of the proiect are unaware of its true status

The case of CONFIRM provides one potent example. In 1987, Marriott, Hilton, and Budget Rent-a-Car entered into a joint venture with AMRIS to develop a state-of-the-art \$55.7 million computerized reservation system called CONFIRM that would allow consumers to make airline, hotel and car rental reservations through a single system. After several delays, Hilton began beta testing the CONFIRM system in April 1992, and major problems, of which many AMRIS employees had been aware since as early as 1990, became apparent. At the end of April, the AMRIS chairperson wrote to the three partners saying: ‘Unfortunately, things have not gone as planned . . . the individuals whom we gave responsibility for managing CONFIRM . . . have apparently deliberately concealed a number of important technical and performance problems.’ AMRIS fired eight senior executives and replaced another 15 employees. On 1 May 1992, the vice-chairperson of AMRIS circulated a letter internally stating that ‘some people who have been part of CONFIRM RS management did not disclose the true status of the project in a timely manner. This has created more difficult problems, of both business ethics and finance, than would have existed if those people had come forward with accurate information.’ In July 1992, after spending 3.5 years and \$125 million on CONFIRM, the project was cancelled (Oz, 1994a).

Although it is tempting to conclude that the CONFIRM case is an unusual exception, an increasing amount of evidence suggests that it is not. Many software developers and project managers, when saddled with a troubled project, are reluctant to report the actual status (hereafter, referred to as ‘the phenomenon’). After conducting interviews with IS project managers, Glass (2002) concluded that there may be ‘an epidemic of lying in our field.’ As an example, he quoted one IS manager as saying ‘Honesty is one of the first casualties when a project becomes shaky.’ Keil & Robey (2001) have reported that even IS auditors are frequently reluctant to report negative project status information because of personal and organizationa issues. This is particularly interesting, as such reporting might well be considered a roleprescribed activity for IS auditors; if they are reluctant to report, one can only imagine the frequency of the phenomenon throughout the ranks of programmers, systems analysts and project managers. Thus, although statistics regarding the prevalence of this phenomenon are elusive, it is reasonable to believe that reluctance to report bad news about a software project’s status is rather common. Further, it may also be an expensive phenomenon in terms of project costs: Iacovou (1999) provided some theoretical linkage between the reluctance to report negative project status news, an organization’s preparedness for failure, and the ultimate impact of the failure. And, in an empirical context, Keil & Robey (1999) documented some problems associated with the phenomenon.

Of course, the reluctance to report bad news is a general phenomenon that holds in many contexts beyond that of IS (Tesser & Rosen, 1975). However, the phenomenon may be especially likely to occur within IS for two reasons. First, even when an observer believes that an IS project is behind schedule (s)he may not be sure of the extent of the trouble. In particular, soft ware’s intangible nature makes it difficult to estimate the proportion of work that has been completed. Second, the boundaries on IS projects are seldom static (Zmud, 1980; Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991). So, although an observer may suspect that an IS project is in trouble, (s)he may not be able to confirm his or her suspicions, and (s)he may therefore be unwilling to report status information. Thus, although the phenomenon can no doubt be observed in project environs that cut across many domains, IS projects appear to be especially prone to it. In this paper, we develop a theoretical model that explains this phenomenon. Our model draws on lit erature from the fields of organizational behaviour and communications, ethics, economics, information systems and psychology. Each of these bodies of literature offers some usefu insights into the phenomenon, but no single body of literature provides a direct explanation for all aspects of behaviour associated with the phenomenon.

The paper makes three important contributions. First, the model can guide future research aimed at increasing our understanding of why individuals are reluctant to report bad news on IS development projects. This should serve as a useful foundation for expanding our understanding of troubled projects. Second, the model and the integration of various streams of literature should prove useful to researchers who seek, in a more general context, to extend present theories regarding bad news reporting and ‘whistle-blowing.’ Third, by providing a foundation for improving our understanding of the phenomenon, prescriptions to reduce the incidences of project failure can eventually be developed.

The remainder of the paper is organized into five major sections. First, we briefly intro duce our general theoretical framework, which is taken from the ‘whistle-blowing’ literature. We then expand this framework by introducing personal, organizational, and project factors that may impact the individual’s perceptions, evaluations and assessments. In this discussion, we explain the primary bodies of literature that ground the model. We then discuss directions for future research, and we consider implications for practice. Finally, we provide a brief conclusion that summarizes what we believe to be the primary contributions of our work.

## GENERAL THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

A stream of literature on organizational ‘whistle-blowing’ provides our general theoretica framework. Whistle-blowers are organization members who disclose information about dysfunctional organizational activities to either persons or organizations who may be able to address the problems (Dozier & Miceli, 1985, p. 824). (In the immediate context, the organizational dysfunction is grounded in resources being devoted to a project that is not delivering what was intended when the resources were allocated.) It is assumed that the individual ‘lacks the power and authority’ to handle the situation and must therefore ‘appeal to someone of greater power or authority’ (Miceli & Near, 1992, p. 15). The reporting channels, which presumably include those individuals of greater power and authority, could be either internal or external ones (Miceli & Near, 1992).

Researchers who study whistle-blowing contend that individuals undertake a predictable series of assessments in these situations (see Figure 1). Drawing upon the work of Latane & Darley (1968; 1970), Dozier & Miceli (1985) present a model of whistle-blowing, which includes both the assessments that individuals make in deciding whether or not to blow the whistle and the range of alternatives that need to be considered if they decide to take action. Dozier and Miceli suggest that an individual first considers whether the situation is deserving of action. This means deciding whether or not the bad news ought to be reported. Second (s)he considers whether there is a personal responsibility for taking action. The distinction between an assessment that the status ought to be reported and a personal responsibility to report has received strong theoretical support from (Dozier & Miceli, 1985), with grounding in the ‘bystander’ work of (Latane & Darley, 1970). It is conceivable, for example, that a bystander, who is part of a crowd, feels strongly that someone should help a victim but feels a diminished personal responsibility to do so. and the extension of this argument to the project status reporting context appears rather direct: an individual could feel strongly that someone should report the project status, even if he or she feels a low level of personal responsibility to do so. (It should be noted that the literature and anecdotal experience suggest few examples of the contrarian relationship in which one feels a strong personal responsibility to report but no generic need, and we agree that such a situation constitutes something akin to a ‘null set’ for all practical purposes. Indeed, the covert premise of Dozier & Miceli (1985) appears to be that the assessment of personal responsibility is salient only when the generic need is seen as strong.) For example, a systems analyst on the CONFIRM project might well have felt that someone within the ranks of IS managers should ‘blow the whistle’ on the misreported status information; however, the systems analyst might not have felt a personal obligation to do so. The two constructs should have strong discriminant validity in an empirical sense. And, in fact, a recent study Smith et al. (2001) demonstrated the separation of the two constructs in a laboratory experiment that had subjects take the role of an IS project manager.

After this assessment of a personal obligation to report, a subject then considers which (if any) of the reporting alternatives he or she will embrace. Generally, reporting alternatives wil be in two broad categories, with some subcategories in each (these categorizations are based on an unpublished dissertation by Blackburn (1988), an adaptation by Miceli & Near (1992; p. 57), and our own extensions).

1. Internal reporting alternatives. The entity to which the individual reports the project status resides within the organization’s own boundaries. There are two broad subcategories: alternatives within the normal reporting chain (e.g. going to an executive who is the supervisor of one’s own supervisor) and alternatives outside that chain (e.g. reporting the project status to an organization’s ombudsperson or ethics officer).

2. External reporting alternatives. The entity to which the individual reports the project status resides outside the organization’s own boundaries. There are several subcategories: professional information paths (e.g. reporting the project status to a professional organization), legal istic information paths (e.g. informing an entity that might take legal action), publicity-related information paths (e.g. informing a newspaper reporter), and interorganizational information paths (e.g. informing one of the organization’s customers).

The individual can be expected to assess the efficaciousness of each alternative from an instrumental perspective (i.e. will the alternative work?) and from a cost/benefit perspective (i.e. balancing perceived benefits against expected risks). Dozier (1988) and Farrell & Petersen (1982) have concluded that retaliation risk acts as a ‘cost’ in the individual’s assessment (Miceli & Near, 1992, p. 153). [Retaliation is usually viewed as more likely when individuals pursue external channels (Near & Miceli, 1986).] Finally, the individual makes a choice, which could be to reject all the reporting alternatives, thus exhibiting a reluctance to report the project status information.

For example, in contrast with the systems analyst discussed above, a mid-level manager on the CONFIRM project might have concluded that (s)he had a strong personal obligation to report the negative status information. (S)he would then consider the possible channels fo reporting the information. There might be an opportunity to go directly to a senior executive within AMRIS or there might be an internal ombudsperson to whom the information could be communicated. However, the manager might believe that neither of these would be efficacious, in that senior management might not be receptive to the negative message, even if it came through the internal ombudsperson. Thus, the manager might then consider some external reporting options, such as communication to executives at Marriott, Hilton or Budget Rent-a Car. Alternatively (s)he might consider contacting a media representative or the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), a professional organization. (S)he might consider whether any of these options could be effected anonymously, which could reduce the risk of retribution. In the end, (s)he would have to consider both the benefits and costs for each alternative. (S)he might even decide, despite a high level of felt personal responsibility, to remain silent.

This basic model, as seen in Figure 1, describes the fundamental decision-making process that can be expected in project status reporting situations. But the model can be expanded to consider a number of additional factors.

## Perceptions of project situation → status ought to be reported (direct relationship)

When an IS professional (be (s)he a programmer, systems analyst, project manager or a person working in some other context) assesses whether the project status should be reported, (s)he no doubt looks at several factors associated with the project situation itself. Which perceptions are likely to be important? Most likely, the salient factors are those that drive an observer to conclude that a crisis is imminent; in those situations, individuals are most likely to conclude that the status ought to be reported. What leads one to such a ‘crisis point’? The model of Billings et al. (1980) holds that the salient factors in such a perception are perceived risk and perceived time pressure. (The model of Billings et al. (1980) includes the factors ‘perceived value of possible loss’ and ‘perceived probability of loss.’ For the purposes of this discussion, we have subsumed these factors under the rubric of ‘risk’, which is commonly understood to include both ) In addition to these items, we claim that two additional factors from other theoretical bases are also salient: the perceived levels of behavioural immorality and information asymmetry associated with the management of the project and its reporting.

We consider each of these and, for each, provide a note on personal factors that may affect the perceptions of the project situation. We then note a few personal factors that may moderate the path between the perceptions and the assessment that the status ought to be reported (numeral 1 in Figure 2).

## Risk perception

Risk perception can be defined as ‘a decision-maker’s assessment of the risk inherent in a sit uation’ (Sitkin & Pablo, 1992, p. 12). To determine the impact of risk perception on risk-taking behaviour, it is necessary to understand how individuals assess risk. Previous research has indicated that risk perception has two key components: the probability that failure will occur and the magnitude of the possible loss from that failure (Sjoberg, 1980; Boehm, 1991; Haimes, 1991; Collins & Ruefli, 1992; Dunegan et al., 1992; Yates & Stone, 1992; Barki et al., 1993; Mellers & Chang, 1994; Sherer, 1994), which together define the expected value of the loss.

A threat of loss from failure becomes more salient to an observer as the expected value of loss from the project failure, which could be related to schedule, budget, and/or functionality, increases. If the expected loss is perceived to be of trivial size, the observer should perceive little need to report the status. As the size of the expected loss grows larger, the observer should feel more strongly that reporting is required.

Further, although the point has been largely ignored in the risk literature, we hold that the context of the loss is a subtle but important component of the magnitude. Three factors are salient in assessing the context of the possible loss: (1) the type of loss that may be experienced; (2) the scope of the loss in terms of the number of people that would potentially be affected; and (3) the nature of the relationship between the decision-maker and those who wil potentially be affected by the loss.

With respect to the type of loss arising from software project failures, previous research has focused on financial loss to the organization (Barki et al., 1993). Although financial losses are quite common in corporate IS project scenarios, there could also be losses of physical property or in some situations, the threat of bodily harm. Consider, for example, a medical device that operates improperly because of a known software bug that is not corrected. Although research on the type of loss is limited, related research (Braithwaite, 1982; Matthews, 1987; Miceli & Near, 1992, p. 140) suggests that individuals should be more likely to claim that a project’s status should be reported when they expect losses to be associated with bodily harm rather than with financial losses or damages to physical property.

With respect to the scope of the loss, it is reasonable to assume that some losses will involve one or only a few individuals, whereas other losses might impact a large number of people. Hence, the scope of the loss may be perceived as affecting an individual, a group of individuals (e.g. an IS project team), an entire organization (e.g. corporation) or multiple organizations, or even society as a whole. The greater the scope of the loss, the more likely it is that the decisionmaker will conclude that the project status should be reported.

In considering the loss context, we can visualize a variety of different relationships that may exist between the decision-maker and those who will potentially be affected by the loss. For example, there may be some contexts in which a loved one might be harmed by a failure to report bad news whereas in other circumstances the target of harm may be an anonymous person with no relationship to the decision-maker. The closer the nature of the relationship between the decision-maker and those affected by the loss, the more likely it is that the decision-maker will conclude that the project status should be reported.

It should be noted, however, that not all individuals will perform their risk assessments in the same manner, because they will differ with respect to risk propensity (Kogan & Wallach, 1964; Fishburn, 1977; Harnett & Cummings, 1980; MacCrimmon & Wehrung, 1990; Sitkin & Pablo, 1992; Farmer, 1993; Fu, 1993). For example, if an individual has a high-risk-taking propensity, (s)he may tend to underestimate the risks involved in a situation. A risk-seeking decision-maker is more likely to recognize and weigh positive outcomes, thereby overestimating the probability of a gain relative to the probability of a loss (Brockhaus, 1980; Vlek & Stallen, 1980). This overestimation will result in a lowering of risk perceptions. Additionally, a risk-averse decision-maker will weigh negative outcomes more highly, leading to a heightened perception of risk (Schneider & Lopes, 1986). Thus, risk propensity probably affects how the individual perceives the level of risk in the project situation.

Additionally, risk perception may be affected by cognitive biases (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Specifically, in what is known as the recency effect, an individual may give a disproportionate weight to later learned information (Whipple & McManamon, 1992; Haugtvedt & Wegener, 1994; Sekely & Blakney, 1994). This may be especially salient in project reporting situations, as an individual could receive a sequence of cues regarding status, size of expected loss, and so on. Those cues received most recently, be they positive or negative, could receive a disproportionately large weight in the observer’s risk calculus.

## Time pressure

Lacking time pressure, an individual is likely to sense that reporting can be postponed, as no urgency will be associated with the immediate project situation. As Billings et al. (1980, p. 305) have noted: ‘Without time pressure, a problem will be left to the future and systematically mis perceived. The more distant a future negative consequence, the less negative it will seem. The full adverse impact of a negative outcome . . . is not perceived when it is believed to be far away . . . the more time that is seen as being available, the greater the chance of formulating a solution to avoid the negative outcome.’ It is reasonable to expect therefore that IS professionals will be more likely to claim that a project’s status should be reported when they perceive time pressure to be greater.

We should not assume, however, that all individuals will perceive time pressure in a given sit uation in exactly the same way. It is known, for example, that individuals vary in their perception of time urgency. ‘The notion is that some people are more concerned about time and its passage than others, and, as a result, may be more prone to suffer the physical and psychological symptoms associated with strain when time demands are high’ (Landy et al., 1991; p. 644). Three subcomponents of time urgency (among seven that have been identified) appear to us to be especially salient in individuals’ assessments of time pressure: awareness of time, sched uling and deadline control (see Landy et al., 1991). Individuals who are more sensitive to time urgency, particularly on the dimensions of these three subcomponents, should be more likely to perceive high levels of time pressure in a given project situation.

In addition, it is known that individuals vary along a continuum with respect to their use of time: at one end of the continuum is monochronic time use (doing one thing at a time), and at the other end is polychronic use (doing two or more things simultaneously). (See Bluedorn et al., 1992 for a crisp overview of the distinctions.) Kaufman et al. (1991) have developed a scale that measures individuals’ tendencies on this continuum, and they have evidence that one’s position on this scale is associated with one’s perceived time pressures. In particular, those with monochronic tendencies are more likely to have feelings of role overload than those with polychronic tendencies. A similar relationship should hold with an individual’s perceptions of time pressure in a given project situation: those with monochronic tendencies will be more likely to perceive time pressure than will those with polychronic tendencies.

## Level of behavioural immorality

There is also reason to believe that individuals will draw different conclusions regarding whether or not project status should be reported based on whether or not they perceive that immoral activities are contributing to the lack of awareness of the project status. It is conceiv able, of course, that there could be ‘incorrect or undesirable, that is, technically misguided and potentially harmful’ (Miceli & Near, 1992; p. 15) practices on a certain IS project and that these would lead to a good chance of project failure. Such a condition could occur even though all parties were behaving morally. On the other hand, certain individuals associated with a project could engage in practices that are generally considered immoral, such as lying or stealing. For example, a manager might falsify project reports, which would be perceived by most observers as an immoral activity. and it will be recalled that vice-chairman of AMRIS represented the reporting behaviours in the CONFIRM case as being lapses of ‘business ethics’ (Oz, 1994a).

Of course, the salient reporting decision in our model is one associated with the project status rather than the immoral behaviour itself. One might conclude, for example, that a project’s status should be reported but that some immoral behaviour should be overlooked. However, it is likely that an individual who perceives that immoral behaviour is occurring will link that perceived immorality with his or her assessments of a felt need for reporting status. If one concludes that the lack of awareness of project status is associated with overt concealment of relevant facts, one will likely feel that it is even more important for the status to be brought to light.

But what determines whether an individual perceives a certain behaviour as ‘immoral’? Individuals view these behaviours through their own ethical lenses. The field of moral philosophy offers two different lenses through which perceptions can be formed: deontologica (sometimes called formalistic or duty-based) and teleological (sometimes called consequentialist or utilitarian). [For a more extensive review of these perspectives, see Frankena (1973).]

Deontological perceptions revolve around the level of behavioural immorality that an individual believes is occurring in a certain situation. Such immoral behaviour might include lying, concealment of important facts or stealing. The observer will often look to the perceived intent of the actors (Kant, 1981). Teleological perceptions, on the other hand, are grounded in an assessment of the consequences that may result from an action. These consequences may be positive or negative, and they accrue irrespective of the actors’ intent. In general, teleologica assessments hinge on the observer’s perceptions of probable outcomes and the weightings they assign to the different parties (Mill, 1979).

Prior research (see, especially, Brady & Wheeler, 1996) suggests that some individuals are more oriented towards deontological assessments, whereas others are more oriented toward teleological ones. It is reasonable to assume that individuals will draw conclusions about the level of perceived immorality by utilizing their own combination of deontological and teleologica reasoning.

## Level of information asymmetry

From the discipline of economics come some useful observations about the relationship between principals (usually, stockholders of a company) and agents (usually, the company’s managers). ‘Agency theory assumes that individuals are motivated to reach decisions [that] maximize their own economic interests, rather than the economic interests of their firm’ (Harrell & Harrison, 1994, p. 570). A particular phenomenon, possession of privately held information, can contribute to dysfunctional agent behaviour:

‘When the manager’s own economic interests diverge from those of the firm, the manager is said to have an incentive to shirk. When the manager possesses relevant information that is not available to senior management, he or she is said to possess privately held information. Agency theory posits that a manager who . . . [in addition to other factors] possesses relevant privately held information will reach decisions [that] reflect the manager’s own interests, which may conflict with the objectives of his or her firm.’ (Harrell & Harrison, 1994, p. 570)

A manager might well conclude that if it were possible to hide negative project information and sustain that hiding, the need for reporting would be lessened. A small stream of empirical research has shown that there is a relationship between the existence of dysfunctional phenomena (among them, possession of privately held information) and a manager’s willing ness to continue a failing project in conflict with the objectives of his or her firm (Harrell & Harrison, 1994; Tuttle et al., 1997). Although this stream of work has not yet been extended into issues of reporting, the same agency arguments should hold: managers should assess less of a need to report the bad news when they perceive high levels of sustainable information asymmetry.

## Ethical climate

A growing body of work has addressed the ‘ethical climate’ of organizations, and a validated instrument for measuring such a climate has been developed (Victor & Cullen, 1988). Wimbush & Shepard (1994) have argued that ‘ethical behaviour’ will be a more likely outcome when work groups are characterized by the dimensions of caring, independence, law and code, and rules; ‘unethical behaviour’ will be a more likely outcome when work groups are characterized by the instrumental dimension. If one ignores this confusion created by the nomenclature of ‘ethicality’, it seems reasonable to conclude, strictly from a behavioural standpoint, that there will be associations between the five dimensions and an individual’s felt responsibility for reporting, based on the same arguments as espoused by Wimbush & Shepard (1994). One would expect, then, that IS professionals in organizations characterized by the ‘caring’, ‘rules’, ‘law and code’, and/or ‘independence’ dimensions of ethical climate will feel more personal responsibility for reporting negative project status information than will those in organizations characterized by the ‘instrumental’ dimension.

## Reporting norms

In addition to the established dimensions of the ‘ethical climate’, some organizations exhibit norms that suggest individuals are expected to report negative information; in others, norms against such behaviour are prevalent. In some cases, particularly those in the former category, the organization provides a formal statement that defines the expectations, but informal organizational norms are quite salient in both the former and latter categories. Of particular note is the organizational history with respect to those who do report bad news: are they treated with respect, or are they punished? Unfortunately, the work on ‘ethical climate’ does not address this concept directly, so we note it as a separate organizational factor that is related to, though not isomorphic with, the dimensions of ‘ethical climate’.

It is reasonable to assume that, in an organization in which it is known that ‘bad news gets you killed’ (i.e. that there is a history of retribution against those who report bad news) individuals will infer a lower level of personal responsibility for reporting. Such a culture has effectively constructed an incentive to ‘shirk’, by providing a disincentive for individuals to report bad news, even though such reporting would be beneficial from the perspective of the stockholders (Harrell & Harrison, 1994). We therefore expect that organizational norms, effected in historica precedents regarding reporting of bad project news, will impact an IS professional’s assessments of his or her personal reporting responsibility.

## Looking across the model

Looking across the model, we see that individuals form perceptions regarding a particular project situation, and these influence their assessment, in a generic sense, of whether the status ought to be reported. This leads to a specific assessment of the level at which the individual feels a personal responsibility to report the status information. There are many personal and organizational factors that are predicted to have an effect on the perceptions, assessments and evaluations.

As noted at the outset, the general phenomenon addressed by this model, reluctance to report negative project status information, is not unique to the IS domain. Nevertheless, many of the relationships may be particularly salient to IS researchers and practitioners, as IS projects may be especially susceptible to the phenomenon. Further, some of the constructs (particularly those related to risk) are known to be quite important ones for IS project management.

It is obvious that the interactions between an individual’s psychological profile, the organi zational domain, and the specific project context are at the same time rich and complex. The model reflects this richness and complexity, and it also reveals the challenges to researchers who wish to investigate this phenomenon.

## DIRECTIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

The model developed in this paper can serve as a guidepost for researchers who are attempt ing to examine various aspects of the phenomenon. However, given the richness of both the phenomenon and its theoretical foundations, it would appear virtually impossible for any one study (or even a set of studies) to consider all the factors. Thus, to improve our understanding, researchers must carve out specific foci from within the model as they develop research agen das. Rarely will researchers encounter naturally occurring situations in which formal, controlled tests of this model’s hypotheses will be possible. This augurs, therefore, for a series of controlled experiments, each of which holds most factors constant and varies a few so tha specific hypotheses can be tested.

At the project level, several factors were identified as having theoretical associations with reporting decisions. From among these, we believe that risk, and, particularly, the expected losses from failure, along with time pressure, might be especially promising in providing understanding within the IS domain. For example, in a recent study, Smith et al. (2001) demonstrated that the impact of loss from failure has significant explanatory power in reporting decisions Researchers might also profitably consider the type of loss that could occur, such as differences between bodily harm (perhaps from a malfunctioning medical device) and financial losses (Braithwaite, 1982; Matthews, 1987; Miceli & Near, 1992, p. 140); the number of people affected by a failure; or the nature of the relationship between the decision-maker and those who might experience the loss. In addition to the risk construct, we believe that the level of time pressure may be especially salient for IS projects. Such perceived time pressure may well impact upon the way in which individuals frame their assessments (Billings et al., 1980).

Of course, in a broader experimental context, one might use path analytical methods to test all the model’s linkages, albeit in a narrow domain. For example, subjects might be presented with scenarios that held risk factors and time pressure constant but that varied the level of behavioural immorality associated with a project’s management. Further, the subjects could confront some different reporting options, with most of the cost/benefits held constant but with some manipulations of reprisal risk. Suppose that the subjects were also asked to complete survey instruments that measured many of their personal factors (e.g. their own type of mora reasoning). The researchers would have in hand enough data to conduct a path analysis that tested a subset of the overall model and some specific hypotheses from each of the paths. The results would provide a great deal of understanding, even if they were far shy of a full test of the complete model.

Further, lurking in the background at the project level is a basic empirical question: how often does the phenomenon occur, and how often does it cause resources to continue to be committed to failing projects? Although we provided a brief consideration of this linkage, little is known about the actual frequency of the phenomenon. Most of the ‘project failures’ documentation (e.g. Flowers, 1996; Glass, 1998; 1999) consists of (often brief) anecdotal stories, the authors of which were unlikely to have been attuned to the phenomenon. A set of broad, fieldbased studies could be quite helpful in ascertaining the frequency of the phenomenon and the linkages to project failure.

At the personal level, a large number of individual factors may impact individuals’ assessments and reporting decisions (see Tables 1–3). It would obviously be impossible for researchers to measure all of these in any single study; thus, researchers must choose those that are most probably salient, given the other relationships that are being tested in that study. For example, because their study tested the relationship between risk and reporting, Smith et al. (2001) measured perceived risk propensity of each subject. Similarly, were researchers to vary time pressure in an experimental context, they might profitably measure each subject’s sensitivity to time urgency (Landy et al., 1991). Throughout this paper, we have attempted to highlight such moderating relationships (noted by the numbered arrows 1, 2 and 3 in Figure 2) and urge researchers to consider these relationships carefully in their designs.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

Although one contribution of this paper is to researchers who extend this domain of inquiry, project managers and executives may also glean some immediate insights about project reporting behaviours. There are three areas in which they may wish to focus attention: managing projects, managing individuals and managing the culture.

## Managing projects

An IS professional’s perceptions of a project situation are expected to flavour the individual’s subsequent assessments regarding status reporting (see leftmost box in Figure 2). Of course, assuming a certain amount of rationality on the part of the individual, these perceptions should be grounded to a great degree in the reality of the project situation. Other publications have offered advice regarding two of the perceptual areas identified in Figure 2, and we do not attempt to duplicate that advice here: risk management (e.g. Applegate et al., 1999, Chapte 11; Schmidt et al., 2001) and moral behaviour on software projects (e.g. Smith, 1993; Oz, 1992; 1994b; Mason et al., 1995). But in two perceptual areas that are highlighted in Figure 2, time pressure and information asymmetry, less has been written from a practical perspective, and we offer the following observations.

## Time pressure

Other things being equal, time pressure will tend to increase employees’ willingness to report bad news. So, on the surface, one might surmise that managers should create a time pressured atmosphere if they want to encourage bad news reporting. However, there may be a cost in doing so. Not every project is under extraordinary time pressure and to avoid false alarms, managers would do well to ensure that deadlines and time pressure are not artificially imposed. To maximize the probability of honest status reporting, while minimizing the potentia for false alarms, managers should ensure that schedules for projects are no more aggressive than they need be and that communication about schedules is straightforward, thus reducing the number of ‘crisis’ situations to which the organization is exposed. This is not to say that short deadlines must always be avoided or that some ‘all-nighters’ might not be necessary at some point in a project’s life. However, adrenaline can only be maintained at a high level in occasional, short bursts, and it is during these periods of high adrenaline flow that reporting ‘crises’ will most often occur. We once observed an organization that set short deadlines fo almost all IS projects as a matter of practice. The organization’s managers had concluded, somewhat cynically, that such short deadlines served as a roadblock against changing require ments during the development process. These deadlines were often met, and the requirements were seldom changed along the way (although we would argue that an effective change management scheme would have probably produced the same outcome from that perspective). However, the perceptions of a ‘crisis’ that led to assessed needs for status reporting were stronger than reality necessitated.

But simply setting realistic deadlines for overall projects does not guarantee that those deadlines will be communicated in a forthright manner down through the hierarchy. On several occasions, we have observed supervisors communicating artificially short deadlines to their programmers as a motivational mechanism. By inflating the importance of these short-term goals, the supervisors are able to create a sense of excitement in the programming ranks and to also provide frequent, positive feedback for goal attainment, an approach that Glass (2002) says is claimed by ‘management theory folks’ to ‘increase motivation and thus performance.

At the same time, however, this managerial technique runs the risk of inducing an artificially created ‘crisis’ in which low-level IS employees conclude that time pressure is greater than it really is. In one case, a programmer wrongly concluded that there were serious exposures to a two-year schedule because of motivational comments made by a supervisor, and this led the programmer to make negative remarks about the schedule to a company executive (thus leading to a reprimand for the supervisor). In our view, the best managerial principle for the long run is to establish and communicate deadlines with the urgency that reality demands, but nothing more than that.

## Information asymmetry

The level of information asymmetry on an IS project is inextricably tied to the level of project status monitoring, and it appears that a pendulum has swung over time in this regard. In the 1950s and 1960s, there was little monitoring of programmers’ work, but the 1970s and 1980s saw much use of the ‘systems life cycle’ framework that demanded many checks and balances with substantial overseeing of the project’s progress. During the 1990s, many alternative development approaches, such as prototyping and end user development, became common, perhaps because there is substantial evidence that some of the new techniques require fewer resources. For example, one study held that prototyped projects required only 25% of the resources of those managed under the systems life cycle (Schultheis & Sumner, 1992, p. 755, table 17-12). In their pristine forms, these alternative approaches tend to include less monitoring, so the level of information asymmetry would be expected to increase. At first glance. then, it appears that the current managerial trend would make the problems of non-reporting worse rather than better.

At the same time, though, there appears to be a growing interest in applying project management tools and techniques to IS projects (Schwalbe, 2000). In recent years, project management certification through the Project Management Institute has become more popular: in 1993, there were roughly 1000 certified project management professionals but, by 1997, that number had grown to more than 6000 (Schwalbe, 2000, p. 15). However, whether traditional project management tools can be successfully applied to new web application development projects that are developed in ‘internet time’ has yet to be demonstrated. Thus, our advice to IS managers is to pay careful attention to the balance between the control structure (which can reduce information asymmetry) and the resource investment (which, in many cases, favours the less control-oriented alternatives). There is no ‘magic bullet’, but an overt assessment of the trade-off is clearly warranted.

## Managing individuals

A perusal of Tables 1–3 and the discussions of individual characteristics in the text lead to one general conclusion: different employees may form different perceptions of the same project situation, and their assessments of whether bad news should be reported may therefore differ.

Thus, managers may be able to assess the characteristics of their own employees and cali brate the news they receive accordingly. For example, employee A, an IS professional with a doctorate in computer science who has worked for an organization for 30 years, may be more likely to conclude that certain status information ought to be reported than would employee B, a newly hired junior programmer with only a high school diploma (see Table 1). Knowing this a manager should be especially attuned to the opinions expressed by employee A. The man ager might view these opinions as an ‘early warning signal’ for problems ahead: if the manage senses that employee A is concerned about some project activities, those activities may merit particular scrutiny.

On the other hand, some employees may be especially prone to misperceptions of project information, and this could also suggest a need for careful management attention to clarify all project-related information. Consider employee C, who is known to be especially risk averse, sensitive to time urgency, and possessive of monochronic tendencies. This employee would be more likely to form perceptions that a project was reaching a ‘crisis’ stage than would employee D, who is a risk seeker, insensitive to time urgency, and largely polychronic in his or her ten dencies. Therefore, employee C’s manager may find it useful to validate any concerns raised by this employee rather than taking them at face value. Furthermore, employee C’s manager may want to be especially careful in communicating with employee C to ensure that (s)he understands the true status of the project and does not harbour incorrect assumptions. In fact, the overall staffing of each project might benefit from an assessment of the salient personal characteristics of prospective team members before they are assigned, to ensure that there is some diversity among team members. Although employees such as C and D might on occa sion exhibit personality clashes, their differing perspectives on a project could sometimes counterbalance one another in a positive sense. Thus, we recommend that IS managers give careful consideration to the different personality characteristics highlighted in this paper, since the contextual management of these individuals can have both positive and negative implications.

## Managing the culture

Executives should also pay attention to the overall culture in which IS projects are conceived and executed. Many of our organizations exhibit cultures in which ‘bad news gets you killed. As discussed earlier, such environments, which establish an unspoken norm against bad news reporting, can have an adverse impact on an individual's assessment of his or her obligation to report negative information concerning troubled software projects. Instead of shooting the messenger, managers would do well to establish an organizational climate that encourages individuals to come forward with accurate project status information, regardless of whether the news is good or bad. For example, some firms give cash rewards to employees who come for ward, and other firms place written commendations in employees’ files when they report improper behaviour (Miceli & Near, 1992, pp. 299–301). Although most of those commendations are associated with whistle-blowing on illegal activities, we believe that the same mechanism could be used to encourage good reporting behaviours on IS projects.

Further, executives should realize that their own behaviours and attitudes can create an environment in which IS professionals feel pressured to hide the truth. Glass (2002) quotes one IS manager as saying ‘. . . you tell lies to live in a culture which, in one way or another, fosters the telling of lies . . .’ To a great degree, employees see their executives as role models and often emulate their behaviours. As an example, consider Greyhound Lines’ TRIPS project. In October 1991, Greyhound emerged from Chapter 11 with a plan to cut costs and to build a new computerized reservation system, known as TRIPS, which would become operational by summer 1993. The original software, developed by an outside vendor, proved to be a disaster when it was tested at the end of 1992. In early 1993, Thomas Thompson, the vice-president in charge of the system’s development, tried to issue a warning to Greyhound’s senior executives, presenting a report that favoured a more gradual implementation of the system. His proposal, however, was rejected by Greyhound’s CFO, who argued that company had already made a commitment to its investors that TRIPS would be launched nation-wide by summer 1993. Copies of Thompson’s report were reportedly destroyed and several managers and employees working on the TRIPS project say they were discouraged from raising issues about the system. In a stock-offering prospectus filed with the SEC in April 1993, Greyhound renewed its pledge to deliver TRIPS by summer 1993.

By May, the company had installed the system in 50 locations only to find that the terminals would freeze up unpredictably. In June, TRIPS technicians were in an office ‘discussing whether there still might be a way to delay the introduction when [the CEO] walked in and said it was not to be discussed.’ Instead, the board was told that TRIPS was ready to go for the summer travel season. When the system was put into service, however, it ‘took as long as 45 s to respond to a single keystroke and 5 min to print a ticket.’ By early September 1993, Greyhound had effectively pulled the plug on the system in an effort to regroup. After the TRIPS disaster, the senior executives departed from the firm, Greyhound’s stock plunged, and its bonds were reduced to junk status (Tomsho, 1994).

Senior managers establish reporting norms through their words and actions. In the short term, the Greyhound executives appeared to embrace a negative reporting norm. However, they found out quickly, through loss of their own employment and their firm’s financial crash, that such a norm is often a counterproductive one.

## CONCLUSIONS

This paper addresses the problems of troubled software projects from a new perspective. We have argued that the losses from troubled projects can be explained, in part, by the unwillingness of individuals to report bad news. This approach represents a contribution in several different respects. In its narrowest sense, the model can guide future research that increases our understanding of why individuals are reluctant to report bad news, and this should serve as a useful foundation for expanding our understanding of troubled projects. However, in a broader sense, the model and the integration of various streams of literature should also prove usefu to researchers who seek to extend present theories of project management and whistleblowing. For those interested in software project management, our model opens a new domain of inquiry, as few previous studies have examined the reluctance to report bad news as a causal factor in project management decisions. For whistle-blowing researchers, our mode provides a contribution as well, in that it integrates previous research from many other disciplines and expands upon the treatment of immorality of behaviour as a contributing factor. Fur ther, the expanded model suggests several practical steps that IS and general managers can take in improving the management of IS projects.

In sum, it is our hope that this model will be useful to researchers and practitioners both within and outside the IS field. Of course, the ultimate test of the model’s usefulness lies not i its specific boxes and arrows, but in its ability to improve practice in this critically important area.

## Biographies

H. Jeff Smith is an Associate Professor of Management at the Babcock Graduate School of Management, Wake Forest University, in Winston-Salem, NC, USA. His research focuses on societal and regulatory issues associated with strategic uses of information technology. His research also examines organizational impediments to successful implementation of information technology applications. His research has appeared in California Management Review, Communications of the ACM, Harvard Business Review,

MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, Sloan Management Review, and in other journals. His book Managing Privacy: Information Technology and Corporate America was published by the University of North Carolina Press and received the 1994 Donald McGannon Book Award for Social and Ethical Relevance in Communication Policy Research (administered by Fordham University). He holds BS degrees in computer science and mathematics from North Carolina State University; an MBA degree from the University of North Carolina in Chapel Hill; and a DBA degree from Harvard University. He worked for the International Business Machines (IBM) Corporation for several vears in the area of software development

Mark Keil is a Professor of Computer Information Systems (CIS) in the J. Mack Robinson College of Business at Georgia State University. His research focuses on software project management, with particular emphasis on understanding and preventing software project escalation. His research is also aimed at providing better tools for assess: ing software project risk and removing barriers to software use. Keil’s research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Sloan Management Review, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems, IEEE Trans actions on Engineering Management, Decision Support Systems, and other journals. He currently serves on the editorial board of IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management. He has also served as an Associate Editor for MIS Quarterly, and as Co-Editor of Data Base for Advances in Information Systems, He earned his bachelor’s degree from Princeton University, his master’s degree from MIT's Sloan School of Management and his doctorate in management information systems from the Harvard Business School.
