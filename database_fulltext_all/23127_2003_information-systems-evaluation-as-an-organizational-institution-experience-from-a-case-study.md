# Information systems evaluation as an organizational institution – experience from a case study

Vassilis Serafeimidis\*<sup>,1</sup> & Steve Smithson<sup>†</sup>

\*Atos KPMG Consulting, 10th Floor Canary Wharf, One Canada Square, London E14 5AG, UK, and Department of Computing, University of Surrey, Guildford GU2 5XH, UK, email: vasileios.serafeimidis@atoskpmgconsulting.co.uk, and <sup>†</sup>Information Systems Department, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK, email: s.smithson@lse.ac.uk

Abstract. Information systems evaluation has strong social and organizational dimensions while existing research focuses primarily on the formal and positivistic characteristics. We investigate the organizational roles of the key stakeholders within the particular evaluation context (orientation). We focus on four evaluation orientations: control, sense-making, learning and exploratory. Experience from a case study is used to analyse these concepts and relations. The case organization attempted to move from a state of an ad-hoc evaluation practice to a more business-driven one. New tools, processes, roles and responsibilities were developed during the attempt. We examine the behavioural and organizational integration aspects of evaluation during that journey.

Keywords: IS evaluation, organizational roles, organizational change, evaluation roles, evaluation orientations

## INTRODUCTION

Information systems (IS) evaluation is embedded in many social and organizational processes, and thus is a particularly complex decision-making process. Evaluation happens in many ways (e.g. formally, informally), uses diverse criteria (e.g. financial, technical, social), follows rigorous methodologies or ‘gut feelings’, often becomes a political instrument that influences the bal ance of organizational power and stimulates organizational changes.

The existing literature (e.g. Willcocks & Lester, 1994; Ballantine et al., 1995; Ward et al., 1996; Farbey et al., 1999; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2000) identifies noticeable gaps between academic theories, commercially available methodologies and actual evaluation practice within organizations. In other words there are formal evaluation practices promoted by organizational rules and structures, informal practices implemented by stakeholders involved, and finally academic recommendations which in many cases recognize the delicate nature of evaluation but are not ‘used’ in practice.

The (better) theories tend to emphasize the complexity and richness of the evaluation problem situation (or context) while the available methodologies tend to over-simplify the process through ‘cook-books’ that focus on the more measurable aspects of the outcome of IS investment. Meanwhile, the actual use of such methodologies in practice is often largely determined by the subjective views of individual stakeholders facing a combination of business, organizational and technological pressures.

The reasons for the apparent gaps, we believe, are related to the institutional dimensions of IS evaluation as an organizational process. The limited consideration of the organizational/ institutional context where evaluation is integrated (e.g. the system’s development life cycle, the IS management practices and processes) and, furthermore, the limited understanding of stakeholders’ behaviour (the socialization) leads to mismatches between theory and practice. The lack of appropriate cultural and structural foundations (e.g. organizational learning and maturity, training, change management) could also explain the ‘crisis of utilisation’ (Legge, 1984) of the evaluation approaches.

In this paper, we offer an alternative, interpretive approach to understanding IS evaluation based on the notion of stakeholders, their evaluation schemas and their roles in the evaluation process. This is discussed against a background that considers four particular ‘orientations’ for IS evaluation, based on the organizational context and impact. This is illustrated through a case study of an insurance company in the UK. This company attempted to break away from the traditional approaches to evaluation and to adopt tools, techniques and concepts that have a more business and organizational flavour. The following section presents our institutional suggestions for understanding IS evaluation. The case study is described in the section ‘The case study of a UK insurance organization’, and the findings analysed in the section ‘Discussing the case study’ using our institutional framework and treating the introduction of the new evaluation procedures as an example of institutional change. This provides additional insight and lessons that could be useful to other organizations that are considering shedding the straitjacket of traditional evaluation. These are summarized in the conclusion.

## WAYS OF APPROACHING INFORMATION SYSTEMS EVALUATION

## The traditional ways

Like much IS research, the study of IS evaluation has been dominated by a positivistic and scientific paradigm (e.g. Walsham, 1995a; Lee et al., 1997). The traditional (formal/rational or functionalist) conception sees evaluation as an external judgement of an IS that is treated as if it existed in isolation from its human and organizational components and effects. It also places excessive emphasis on the technological and accounting/financial aspects (e.g. software engineering methods, discounted cash flow techniques) at the expense of the organiza tional and social aspects. In so doing, it neglects the organizational context and process of IS development and its content, elements that are critical to the successful application of information technology (IT) in support of the business. In general, more attention has been focused over the years on prescribing how to carry out evaluations (with project-driven and costfocused accounting frameworks) rather than analysing and understanding their role, interactions, effects and organizational impacts (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1988; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998).

Formal/rational approaches have been challenged for their internal validity and their external generalizability in other areas of social research (e.g. Legge, 1984). They do not take into account the fact that evaluation is a socially embedded process in which formal procedures entwine with the informal assessments by which actors make sense of their situation. Therefore, they contribute to one piece of the picture but are not rich enough to describe the complex impacts within organizations. Many authors (e.g. Hirschheim & Smithson, 1988; Iivari, 1988; Walsham, 1993; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998) argue that IS evaluation would be improved by using an ‘interpretive’ epistemology.

Interpretive methodologies of evaluation actively analyse the experience of organizational reality, focusing on stakeholder interests and perspectives. . . . They increase the effectiveness of organizational activity by ensuring a match between elements of organizational change and the dynamics of organizational culture and politics. (Symons, 1993, p. 74)

## Interpretive and cognitive perspectives

In this section we adopt the interpretive paradigm and introduce a number of concepts that we use to analyse the case study, A more detailed description of those concepts can also be founo in Serafeimidis (2001).

## Evaluation stakeholder analysis

The organizational and subjective nature of IS evaluation brings into the foreground the human actor, a stakeholder, of an evaluation exercise. Many authors argue that an organization-wide participatory stakeholder analysis is the first essential step to the formulation of the ‘evaluation party’ and then the evaluation itself (Guba & Lincoln, 1989; Gregory & Jackson, 1992; Pouloudi & Whitley, 1997). However, the identification of the stakeholders is not trivial. The existing literature lacks systematic guidelines to assist in their identification and classification. What is usually available refers to generic lists (e.g. Gilbert et al., 1988) with questionable genera applicability. A useful starting point, with primary focus on IS evaluation stakeholders, is the works of Ginzberg & Zmud (1988) and Farbey et al. (1993; 1999).

## ‘Evaluation schema’

Cognitive psychology attempts to provide the necessary structures to reveal and understand the individual’s knowledge, views, emotions and perceptions. The idea of a ‘schema’ (Bartlett,

1932) has been used as an individual’s interpretation of their reality. It is a network of general knowledge based on previous experience which provides predictive and explanatory power for understanding (Norman, 1983). A more flexible and evolving notion is that of ‘mental models (Craik, 1943) which are dynamically constructed, as creations of the moment, by activating stored schemas. Giddens (1984) uses the similar term ‘interpretative schemes’ as a ‘modality of shared cognitive structures for understanding and as an initiative for actions. Orlikowski & Gash (1994) develop the concept of ‘technological frames’ in order to investigate cognitions and values of stakeholders (in particular those of technologists and users) of an IS and to explain and anticipate actions and meanings.

Such cognitive structures are clearly highly relevant to understanding the individual stakeholder’s views of the evaluation and/or the IS under evaluation. As such, in principle they could form the basis for developing individuals’ ‘evaluation schemas’, which could form an extremely useful mechanism for interpreting and eliciting the interests of both individuals and groups within the ‘evaluation party’. However, in practical terms, as with all similar cognitive models (e.g. images, mental models), this approach is highly likely to face severe problems (e.g. Norman, 1983; Johnson-Laird, 1989). Cognitive structures are very difficult to elicit and operationalize because of their abstract nature and anyway they are highly dynamic and subject to constant change.

## Evaluation roles

In order to overcome the practical difficulties in eliciting ‘evaluation schemas’, an alternative approach to understanding the actions of these individuals is to examine their organizationa roles. A social and/or organizational role is associated with a set of behaviours appropriate and expected for a given position (or status) (Brown, 1970; Levinson, 1971; Merton, 1971; Biddle, 1979; Fincham & Rhodes, 1988). For Parsons (1951) roles belong to the social system and can be explained through role expectations held by participants and supported by sanctions. Mintzberg (1973, p. 54), in discussing the roles of a manager, defines the term as:

an organized set of behaviours belonging to an identifiable office or position. Individual personality may affect how a role is performed, but not that it is performed. Thus, actors, managers, and others play roles that are predetermined, although individuals may interpret them in different ways.

In the centre of this argument are the structurally given demands (e.g. norms, expectations, responsibilities) and the behaviour of an individual acting out a role (e.g. individual roleconceptions and individual role-performance). Roles and schemas are at the heart of people’s behaviour and are critical in the interpretation of others’ actions and the meanings given to particular situations.

There are many evaluation roles. Focusing in particular on the principle that distinguishes the relationship of the stakeholder to the evaluation, a number of individual roles can be determined. These could include the ‘strategist for evaluation’, the ‘evaluator’, the ‘champion’ and the ‘sponsor’. Furthermore, at a broader level, relevant stakeholders would include senior executives, departmental user managers and the users themselves (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995), as well as customers, trading partners and industry regulators, depending on the sys tem’s context. In considering the implementation of intranet technology, Scheepers (1999) identified five key roles: technology champion, sponsor, co-ordinator, developer and content provider. In this paper we focus on the roles of the strategist for evaluation and the evaluator, in order to understand the interplay between evaluation concepts and actions. Furthermore, the organizational integration of evaluation approaches primarily involves those who perceive, implement and apply such a change, these strategists and evaluators.

The ‘strategist for evaluation’ is involved in the creation, implementation and institutionalization of evaluation norms, principles, structures and methods. The tasks of a strategist, in general terms (Mintzberg, 1994), include the analysis of the situation, the production and reproduction of normative values (Walsham, 1993), and the maintenance and change of power relations. In a more unconventional way, a strategist can encourage and facilitate others to question conventional wisdom and increase awareness. The organizational status and power of the strategist should provide the authority to question traditions and to make commitments for change.

‘Evaluators’, on the other hand, receive the ‘evaluation strategy’, including the evaluation methods, tools and techniques, from the strategists and enact the strategy, according to thei interpretations. Every stakeholder from the evaluation party can act as evaluator and evalua tors cannot be considered as isolated from the evaluation action. They are recipients of the changes they initiate and, therefore, either beneficiaries or victims themselves (Guba & Lincoln, 1989). The evaluator can be viewed and interpreted by his audience in various ways. In the case of a formal/rational evaluation exercise based on technical and economic criteria, the role of a formal evaluator includes not only the quantitative assessments, but also a ritual element of demonstrating management competence (Walsham, 1993).

## The organizational orientations of information systems evaluation

The organizational context (both internal and external) strongly influences the role that evaluation is expected to play (Willcocks & Lester, 1994; Walsham, 1995b; Serafeimidis, 1997; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 1999) and consequently the performance of the evaluation roles. The context includes the organizational characteristics (e.g. culture, norms), structural limitations, the organizational expectations from evaluation and external requirements (e.g. academic lit erature). All these factors restrict and enable its impacts and contributions. Considering this relationship, four different orientations of IS evaluation can be identified.

Previous literature in the area of strategic decision making (Thompson, 1967; Earl & Hop wood, 1987; Harrison, 1995) identifies four generic types of decisions which occur by compu tation, compromise, judgement and inspiration. Similar analyses have been used in the area of evaluation by Farbey et al. (1992; 1994) and Hellstern (1986). The underlying principles of these classifications refer to the beliefs about causation (means and processes) and the clar ity/predictability of the objectives of the decision-making or evaluation process (and IS in ou case). Decision making and evaluation are very closely linked (Jain et al., 1991; Remenyi &

Sherwood-Smith, 1999) without being synonymous. Evaluation can be seen as informing decision making, which itself becomes a logical conclusion to the evaluation process. The closeness of this relationship can be seen by trying to consider one without the other. Decision making without any (even informal) evaluation of the options and likely impact is akin to tossing dice while evaluation without decision making would suggest a (possibly implicit) decision either not to decide or to ignore the evaluation.

Incorporating these dimensions, we propose four possible orientations for IS evaluation (see Figure 1). This recognizes the importance of the context in the perception and performance of evaluation. It further considers the need for contingency approaches to evaluation because of the diversity of contexts and organizational changes attempted by IS, the clarity and uncertainty of the expected outcomes (which are related to the type of IS) and the different needs along the time horizon.

The classification defines four evaluation subcontexts based on two dimensions. The horizontal axis refers to the impact on the organization of the proposed investment, ranging from tactical to strategic. This takes two of the three levels of Anthony’s (1965) classic taxonomy that has also been applied by Irani & Love (2001) to IS evaluation. The operational level has been omitted in this paper in order to simplify the analysis and because its scope in terms of organizational impact is inherently limited.

The vertical axis refers to the clarity and attainability of the objectives of the IS and their evaluation. It ranges from consensus to no consensus addressing the issue of multiple interests and the plurality of impacts. This axis further depends on the nature of IS and its perceived contributions. The identification of objectives leads to the determination of relevant evaluation criteria. All these are subject to contextual factors. Each orientation does not exclude any other but complements it.

Control evaluation refers to cases where the expected outcomes of the investment, usually quantitative, are fairly certain and there is an organizational consensus around them (e.g. stock control systems). Therefore, evaluation has clear objectives to achieve. The previous experi ence and the narrow scope (tactical) make evaluation contributions predictable and more certain.

This orientation is probably the one most quoted in the literature and is still the most popular in practice (e.g. Blackler & Brown, 1988; Angell & Smithson, 1991; Farbey et al., 1993; Baker, 1995). Evaluation is deliberate and plays an instrumental and formal role to control organiza tional and IS resources and human behaviour. Control evaluation is closely related to the efficiency and effectiveness of IS investments, including user satisfaction (Chen et al., 2000) and productivity (Prattipati & Mensah, 1997; Menon et al., 2000). It is usually supported by rationalistic decision models (Banker & Kemerer, 1992) that attempt to model organizational reality and hence control IS (as a capital investment) and measure their achievement in terms of goals and purposes. It considers the quantitative financial and technical issues and their associated risks. Social and soft issues are usually ignored. Control evaluation promotes order and mechanisms for the organizational success of IS investments (see also Currie, 1989; Symons, 1993; Willcocks & Lester, 1994; Currie, 1995; Hoogeweegen et al., 1998; Ballantine & Stray, 1999; Balasubramanian et al., 2000).

In cases where the objectives of the investment are not clear or predictable (e.g. a decision support system, a groupware system), sense-making evaluation would form the basis of attempts to reach consensus. Like control evaluation, sense-making operates where the rela tionship between actions and their impacts is relatively predictable (tactical). Its contribution is significant where there is a disagreement or ambiguity concerning the objectives. In this situ ation, sense-making evaluation assists in reaching consensus. Viewing organizations as social and political systems (Cyert & March, 1963) or circuits of power (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992), the sense-making orientation can be seen as a dialectic process between formal and informal behaviour (Boland & Pondy, 1983; Symons, 1993; Walsham, 1993). Therefore, the content of a sense-making evaluation includes both informal and hidden elements as well as formal information. Hence, it becomes easier to deal with qualitative values and their quantification. It should include the ability to reveal and disseminate multiple views and interests, and thus a platform for negotiation and compromise between the various individuals and groups involved in the evaluation exercise. Sense-making evaluation could form a medium for making explicit stakeholders’ schemas, sharing informal views and objectives, and disseminating formal evaluation information before negotiation (Avgerou, 1995). Channels of communication are necessary; where such channels are not formally defined, informal communication is used instead. Sense-making evaluation thus needs to take into account the social infrastructure and history (e.g. values, beliefs, myths, rituals).

Social learning evaluation contributes to decreasing uncertainty of strategic changes. The expected objectives are usually clear (e.g. increase customer base through telephone banking), but there is uncertainty of their achievement. The need for flexibility and emergent practices leads to the necessary increase of experience. Learning evaluation can facilitate a singleloop learning within the life cycle and/or a double-loop learning as a learning culture. Socia learning attempts to increase the knowledge capital of the organizations in areas such as the management of IT investments and the application of evaluation approaches.

The contribution of evaluation as a social learning mechanism is likely to be significant when objectives are relatively clear but the results of (strategic) actions are difficult to predict. Evaluation acts as a feedback mechanism applying a critical spirit to actions taken and results achieved (e.g. Hirschheim & Smithson, 1987; Earl, 1989; Galliers, 1991; Farbey et al., 1993; Symons, 1993; Walsham, 1993; Farbey et al., 1995). The actions judged could have been simply experimental (e.g. prototypes) to provide a further source of knowledge. Furthermore, the learning orientation potentially contributes to organizational learning and ‘understanding (Farbey et al., 1999).

The extreme uncertainty and risks which characterize the potential impacts of IS strategies enhance the relationship of evaluation and learning. Various authors (e.g. Farbey et al., 1993; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Willcocks & Lester, 1996) argue that some reasons for IT failing to deliver benefits are related to learning. These include the inability of senior managers to align IS with business and IS evaluation with strategic thinking.

Exploratory evaluation is needed when the social learning faces a lack of consensus in terms of the objectives and/or the sense-making cannot deal with the strategic nature of the change and its uncertainty (e.g. major outsourcing decisions, just-in-time manufacturing systems). This may take place against a backdrop where IS clearly has, or will have, strategic value but where the IS function has only a low status in the organization (Avison et al., 1999). The exploratory orientation may initiate a paradigm change when necessary.

Exploratory evaluation is appropriate when an existing paradigm is no longer relevant. This makes communication and learning problematic. By definition, exploratory evaluation aims to elicit participants’ experience, and their aims and intentions to explain new situations through developing a new paradigm. It is based on communication that, because of disagreements, eventually becomes argumentation. Therefore, the validity, clarity, truthfulness and correctness of the claims and arguments are important. A hermeneutic approach (Sarker & Lee, 1999) may be effective in this situation. By challenging the validity of claims, individuals re-establish mutual understanding and consensus. New organizational forms can then occur in addition to existing bureaucracies and new norms and learning can flourish. Exploratory evaluation might require a number of iterations to reach a stable state. Relevant techniques could include scenario generation (see Brooke, 2000) based on Clegg et al. (1996).

While this situation is normally conceptualized as a business/IS strategy problem, evaluation still serves an important role; for example, the use of risk analysis. The objectives, as well as the tools and techniques used, are likely to be very different from those of the other organizational orientations of IS evaluation. Thus, exploratory evaluation stands at the opposite end of the spectrum from control evaluation. Uncertainty characterizes both the objectives and the strategies used to achieve them. These issues are usually not explicit and limited knowledge and experience are available. The evaluation then attempts through exploration to achieve understanding, justify claims, develop knowledge and introduce necessary changes. Exploratory evaluation changes the schemas of the stakeholders and the assumptions that influence them. It can be seen as an alternative to the ‘act of faith’ decisions that are common at times of high uncertainty.

## THE CASE STUDY OF A UK INSURANCE ORGANIZATION

This section describes the organizational initiatives related to IS evaluation enacted by the case study organization. These developments took place throughout the 1990s. Coleman & Jamieson (1994) discussed the early methodological developments in the same case study. We have covered those developments in our analysis and studied the organization for a longer period of time.

## Research method

The research philosophy used in this study has been broadly interpretive following appropriate principles identified by Galliers (1992), Walsham (1993) and Yin (1994). For example, emphasis was placed on issues concerning the context, the social processes of evaluation and the meanings attributed to these processes by the interviewees (stakeholders). In the centre of this research were the evaluation stakeholders. Semistructured, open-ended interviews were mainly used in order to allow participants to give their personal experience and interpretation of the events. The interviews were based on a broad list of relevant issues as well as topics of interest gathered from previous interviews and other sources. In addition, interviewees were encouraged to ‘tell the story’ of what happened from their own perspective and their own knowledge. A questionnaire-like list was usually sent to the interviewee a week in advance; each interview was recorded and transcribed within a week. Each transcript was reviewed and sent to the interviewees for approval. The results were sorted into chronological order, with every effort made to preserve the original wording used by the interviewees.

The interviews were conducted in the late 1990s while the earlier initiatives (since 1993) were reconstructed from documentation (written material ranging from guidelines to internal newsletters, press announcements, and archival records such as organizational charts, budgets) and the memories of the interviewees. We conducted interviews with six managers (inter viewed one to three times each) from various parts of the company. The managers interviewed ranged from upper to lower level middle management and included policy makers as well as operational managers. A small number of members of the original development team (who later became redundant) were also interviewed. In addition, the researchers attended a num ber of key internal meetings. Having multiple data sources enabled triangulation and cross checking of the data to be achieved, a crucial factor to ‘interpret’ a phenomenon and reach more robust conclusions.

## Background history

The organization in question was founded in 1848 and is one of the 10 largest life insurance companies in the world managing funds of over £150 billion. During the period of study it was the largest British provider of individual life and general insurance as well as pensions. We studied the largest division (Home Service), which deals with customers in their homes (offering every type of insurance) and provides services to all the internal groups of the corporation. This division consists of five business areas, accounts for approximately 75% of the UK turnover and employs approximately 20 000 staff including around 160 IS developers in a separate systems department and an annual IT budget between £70 and £95 million.

## Improving the information technology investment evaluation practices

During the 1980s, the main concern was the planning and control of IT resources, using a capacity planning tool and later a budget estimation system. In general, the management of IT investments was carried out in an ad-hoc way. By the early 1990s, this approach was no longer sustainable as the managers started to realize that the evaluation of IT benefits should be based on business results rather than technical performance. There was a clear need to establish a causal link between IT expenditure and business performance in bottom line terms. The then Systems Strategy Manager said:

My job is to ensure that in spending our limited [IT] resources we deliver the best service possible for the business. . . . It is always substantial to get down to bottom line results. For instance, if the business case says: ‘The new sales management system will improve productivity by 20%’, it is important first to identify the link between the technology and the business benefit. Before constructing a business case, it is essential to establish the logical credentials and really understand where that 20% saving is going to come from.

## The development of the Project Appraisal Method

A corporate IT investment appraisal methodology was seen as the best way to select the right portfolio of IT projects and to ensure, through effective risk analysis and benefits management, the delivery of high-quality systems and the achievement of both hard and soft benefits desirable by the business. It had become clear that traditional cost–benefit analysis could not cope any more with the assessment of the complex returns and the strategic nature of the IT investments, and few business or systems people had a formal grounding in project accounting. After the Finance Director had become personally interested in information economics (Parker et al., 1988), and a new Systems Strategy Manager was appointed, the division initiated the development of the Project Appraisal Method (PAM).

The development of PAM, which started in the early 1990s, was mainly bottom-up, from a departmental and divisional level to a corporate one. The development team was purely internal (without external consultants), led by the manager of the Business Systems Coordination Group and championed by the manager of the IT Systems Steering Group.

A rigorous appraisal mechanism like this implied a clear description of the corporate busi ness objectives as the sources for the requirements of the IT investment and the appraisal process should measure the success of achieving them. Working top-down, the project team analysed the division’s business goals, measures and key performance indicators and identified a subset of seven (out of the initial 200) which were key for IS project appraisal. These were: customer base, customer service, sales effectiveness, unit cost, exploitation of business opportunities, company’s brand and product design. Two extra objectives were added later on: management information and staff attitude to IT. These were prioritized by the key stakeholders, using a Delphi approach. Even though the development was initiated by the Systems Department of one division, the rest of the corporation were aware of the project through seminars, reports and leaflets. Training sessions were carried out within various user groups and user managers, who had traditionally avoided active involvement in IT decisions and seemed keen on being involved in the new attempt.

The theoretical foundations of PAM lay in information economics (Parker et al., 1988) but PAM adopted various concepts and techniques. It consisted of three main streams, appraising: the hard financial costs and benefits, the risks, and the strategic and intangible benefits. The main deliverables were: a financial model of the costs and benefits, a risk management plan, and a benefits profile. All these were incorporated in a well-defined IS development life cycle. The financial appraisal used spreadsheets to model cash flows, automatically calculate mea sures such as the project payback period, the internal rate of return and the net present value, and to encourage sensitivity analysis. The risk appraisal used standard computer-based ques tionnaires to identify the main risks in a project, to estimate the probability of them happening and to calculate their potential impact. The most innovative stream, the intangible IT benefits, evaluated project benefits against the nine key organizational objectives to produce a total score and a graphical benefits profile. As well as assessing the initial business case, this would form the basis for a benefits delivery plan.

Towards the end of the development of PAM, issues of cost reduction became uppermost in the corporation rather than intangible and strategic aspects. The ‘balanced’ focus of PAM became less attractive. At the same time (1993), a major reorganization resulted in most of PAM’s development team (including the leader) being moved elsewhere or made redundant. Furthermore, the team had failed to obtain the full support of the Finance Director for PAM and the Marketing Division quickly followed his lead in rejecting PAM.

## Using Project Appraisal Method

The ultimate user of PAM was a small, new and centrally positioned Impact Assessment Group (IAG) which was responsible for allocating system development resources, controlling IT related changes and their impact on the business. Their main role was to validate project pro posals and choose the ‘best’ portfolio. However, the bulk of business stakeholders (who knew little about project appraisal) felt ‘forced’ to use PAM as the only way to communicate with the IAG and the systems people, and the only way to get their proposed systems implemented.

PAM was used for a period of 12–18 months successfully according to the Systems Strategy Manager who claimed:

We have saved at least £1.8 million over the last six months. The risks could now be clearly identified and managed by explicitly setting out accountabilities, metrics and milestones and benefits delivery has improved in general.

In spite of the original claims, PAM was unsuccessful in optimizing the project portfolio and was only used to justify individual projects. This resulted in projects being approved that exceeded the resources available and an additional prioritization process was introduced. Having taken the trouble to learn PAM, business managers became dissatisfied with having to resort to additional (more political) means to ‘support’ their proposals. Gradually, the situation deteriorated because of a shortage of developers; many project proposals had to be rejected, although they had proved their potential value through PAM. The problems that arose were not due to faults within PAM itself but with the overall decision-making process.

## The post-Project Appraisal Method age

A driving force for change was a directive in the mid-1990s that the business and systems groups should collaborate in project selection and prioritization. PAM was now expected to reveal the views of the various stakeholders as the first step towards communication and consensus. The changes were implemented through a new organizational group, the Development Directorate (DD), made up of senior business and systems managers, headed by the Finance Director. Its objective was to move the responsibility for project approval away from the Systems Department and the IAG to the overall business. In addition, another new role was that of Account Managers (AMs), usually middle managers with a systems background, to act as bridges (or interfaces) between each business area and the IAG.

Account managers are senior to the IAG, respected people with grey hair and a nice suit so that business people would trust and respect them. (A member of the IAG)

In addition to the initial evaluation using PAM, an extra cross-assessment of the proposals was performed according to various business and strategic criteria. While the results of PAM could be used to compare similar projects, they could not take into account the different size of projects.

As a result of all these changes and the prevailing emphasis on costs, the use of PAM declined considerably. The financial appraisal component was still used but the intangible benefits profile was very rarely used and the risk analysis component had largely fallen into disuse altogether. The fortunes of the IAG were closely tied to PAM and by mid-1995 IAG was absorbed into the Systems Resource Management unit. There, new delivery managers carried out the impact assessment exercise and were accountable for the project delivery. They worked with the AMs in forwarding proposals to the DD for approval.

The concept of AMs proved to be successful in co-ordinating business and systems plans and their role and number were expanded. The role and status of the DD increased significantly. It became responsible for the approval of the IT budget for business units, for the maintenance of the IT project portfolio for the division, and more generally it shifted from IT planning to become more of an overall business planning and advisory board. More recent IS evaluations have worked well with the significant involvement of the Finance Department. The financial elements were evaluated by NPV (Net Present Value), IRR (Internal Rate of Return) and payback techniques. Nonfinancial costs and benefits were identified and attempts were made to quantify them in high-level terms. The risk analysis elements have been incorporated into the project management plans and cover mainly the development risks. These are presented in a text format; no questionnaires or forms are used any more. A high-level benefits delivery plan identifies the activities needed to realize the benefits and allocates responsibilities to accountable individuals to deliver the benefits

## DISCUSSING THE CASE STUDY

This section first identifies the evaluation stakeholders in the case study. It then uses the four orientations of IS evaluation to re-examine the evidence from the case study. It analyses each orientation in terms of activities, roles and organizational integration.

## Evaluation stakeholders

This study involved stakeholders who acted out the roles of ‘strategists for evaluation’ and ‘evaluators’. The strategists included PAM’s development team, the DD and the AMs. These groups comprised a mixture of systems and business experts. Evaluators included the people from the business units who dealt with the IS project proposals and the IAG. In many cases these included the project managers and the business sponsors and in some cases even the AMs. During the development and use of PAM the ‘strategists’ played their central role and drove the appraisal decisions. Gradually they moved closer to the ‘evaluators’ themselves. Now, evaluation has become an integral part of the planning process and the contribution of AMs has shifted more to executing evaluation. The Finance Department’s role has changed significantly from the appraisal of individual IT projects to the approval of the overall budgets as well as the tools and techniques used for financial evaluation. Another critical group, the senior managers, supported and sponsored the various evaluation initiatives but their initial enthusiasm was overtaken by the need for cost reduction and the importance of monetary returns.

The increasing role of IT, and its various innovative uses, was responsible for the involvement of a growing number of actors in the decision processes. Although PAM introduced guidelines to identify the basic stakeholders (i.e. business sponsor, champion, project manager), this was not enough. In today's complicated IS the search for the evaluation party' is vital. The need to incorporate multiple values and views is essential, something that is reflected in the various changes in the decision-making procedures. The need to involve stakeholder groups from business areas in the formulation of evaluation schemas was beneficial but maintaining communication between them proved difficult. For example, the lack of formal feedback from the evaluators to the strategists contributed to the limited success of PAM.

## Evaluation as an organizational control mechanism

In the case study, control evaluation was very popular among senior managers, especially while cost pressures dominated IT investment decisions. Thus, tighter control of IT budgets was preferred to the traditional ad-hoc ways of IS management. Rigorous mechanisms were introduced to assess the results of proposed business/IT changes. They enhanced technica efficiency with the search for the effective use of resources and increase of business/organizational value.

In terms of organizational roles, control evaluation fits the type of role associated with bureaucratic environments – those with clear responsibilities and authorities. Their tasks derive from the promotion of rationality in decision making and their authority depends mostly on their organizational seniority. This set-up would tend to promote formal channels of communication and this was seen in the case study.

For the strategists, the promotion of rationalistic control models as parts of PAM practice was a major task. Evaluators applied control evaluation for a different purpose to achieve improved utilization of IT resources. However, strategists acted politically when they promoted control evaluation approaches to achieve personal and/or departmental objectives. For example, system managers initiated PAM to control and utilize resources spent by their own department in order to improve its organizational credibility. On occasion, evaluators used PAM to ‘abuse’ formal evaluation mechanisms in order to promote political and personal interests. Evaluation is usually highly political and subjective and evaluators can thus manipulate data for their own benefit (see also Currie, 1989; Farbey et al., 1992; 1995; Ward et al., 1996).

At an operational level, life cycle models provided necessary milestones to establish control. Well-defined process models and methods were built upon the life cycle to introduce control in operational terms as a sequence of actions, their deliverables and their interconnections. In terms of organizational integration, control evaluation fits mechanistic and bureaucratic organizations such that it becomes the ‘official’ way of managing IS projects (Symons, 1993). Within this large, well-established insurance company, control evaluation fitted the rationalistic culture of the organization at the time. PAM also fitted the centralized bureaucratic way of raising and approving IS project proposals. However, it resulted in tension and dissatisfaction between the business areas and IS departments because of the limited satisfaction of the former’s requests. For example, IAG was taking decisions on behalf of business and systems.

Although fairly bureaucratic organizations, such as the case study one, have considerable experience of such formal control mechanisms, this does not mean that their introduction and operation are necessarily straightforward. The ‘abuses’ noted above suggest that strategists need to police the application of these mechanisms.

## Evaluation as sense-making

There was evidence of sense-making evaluation in the case study. While perhaps the culture was not totally helpful, there was evidence that strategists (e.g. PAM’s development team) facil itated communication between different groups to achieve consensus, primarily between evaluators and authorization committees. Sense-making was also demonstrated during the filtering of business objectives. Evaluators supported sense-making in the evaluation party. For exam ple, AMs facilitated communication between business managers and the IAG. However, there was also evidence that strategists (e.g. the Systems Department) promoted that particular culture when it suited their own interests. Informal channels were used to seek agreements with senior authorization committees and usually resulted in the success of the proposals. Because of the nature of evaluation, informal activities and influences were expected. Business sponsors and evaluators acted politically to ‘support’ the results of PAM in order to convince the IAG of their viability.

Evidence from the case study suggests that formal evaluation methodologies can become the starting point for discussion and dissemination of the necessary information in order to reach consensus. PAM provided a shared but restricted vocabulary and discourse structure that was learnable by all parts of the organization. It thus became a legitimated common lan guage for interaction between the systems and business managers as each group translated its requirements to a common set of values. Moreover, the process of establishing (or managing) an evaluation approach was an opportunity for communication in itself. Managers from business and systems had to communicate and attempt to understand each other’s views and requirements.

To achieve successful sense-making evaluation roles, it follows that evaluation strategists should promote and develop (usually formal) communication channels and a common lan guage for interaction. Evaluators are likely to have more difficult tasks to perform in sensemaking evaluation. They should help in revealing informal and personal interests and act as facilitators among the members of the evaluation party. To integrate sense-making evaluation with the organization’s regular management processes, organizations need to provide the necessary support. This might require a new culture and structures to be developed. Such a culture should allow disputes and encourage interaction and communication. Sense-making can appear in every stage of the IS life cycle and it can probably be facilitated by dialectic, responsive evaluation approaches (e.g. Walsham, 1993; Avgerou, 1995) and subjective methods such as multiobjective multicriteria analysis (Farbey et al., 1993). The contribution of a standard evaluation platform/method in negotiation, conflict resolution and consensus achievement was clear in the case.

## Evaluation as social learning

Evidence from the case revealed that an attempt to develop and institutionalize an evaluation approach promoted learning in terms of business values, the alignment of IS with these values and the business education of systems managers. The development of PAM contributed significantly to organizational learning. During its development the need for understanding, the organization in terms of clear business goals and objectives and their prioritization were fundamental issues in understanding the business itself. This was the starting point for measuring the contribution of IS to the success of the organization. Business objectives existed at a corporate level but evaluators (and in some cases the project sponsors) had not visualized them in relation to IS. Systems people were helped to understand the organization and its objectives better and prompted business representatives from different business areas to define the divisional objectives and performance indicators. Also, all the participants managed to learn the fundamental principles of investment appraisal and a common language for communication. According to the head of PAM’s development team:

Nevertheless, what was more useful was the exercise because in some cases the scores and tools themselves were not very important but bringing together the sponsoring team and other parties, was useful, in order to gain from a discussion and clarifications.

During its use, PAM assisted business managers to understand their needs and to formulate them in a ‘standard’ way. As an IAG member claimed, ‘PAM made us smarter’. It is significant to mention the innovative nature of PAM and the particular consideration of the intangible and strategic benefits deriving from IS investments. Although these tools gradually fell into disuse, managers learned to treat business/systems strategy as an integration of IS considerations into the business planning and strategy formulation process. Most of the users found the techniques and tools easy to apply and the very act of applying them was extremely useful in clarifying the project’s objectives. There was a significant improvement, especially in the financial awareness of the IS development teams. Good quality cost benefit and sensitivity analyses became the rule with a clear focus on delivering the maximum total return to the business from the first day of a project’s life.

The ability of an organization to learn (e.g. Nevis et al., 1995; Argyris & Schon, 1996) is likely to be a necessary precondition of an emergent and contingent approach to evaluation against a constantly changing context. Knowledge is the content element that enhances stakeholders schemas and organizational memory. Therefore, stakeholders become skilled in understanding assumptions and norms, and able to challenge and change them.

The contribution of individuals to learning is two-fold. Initially they need to be receptive to lessons and experience provided by others to update their schemas. Secondly, they should be in a position to diffuse and share their own experience. Strategists are likely to be expected to establish the vehicles for knowledge dissemination and a general culture that promotes organizational learning (e.g. encouraging experimentation and feedback). Evaluators need to take into consideration the knowledge available and further diffuse it. Learning is strongly facilitated by communication actions. Evaluators are thus the actors who should develop knowledge through interaction. Their communication with strategists requires previous experience and knowledge of a ‘language’ for interaction. Learning can flourish in most organizational forms. In mechanistic organizations, learning mechanisms can be integrated with the formal processes as official feedback channels. Flexible and organic organizations require higher degrees of learning abilities (e.g. the openness and willingness to re-examine well-established assumptions associated with double-loop learning – see Argyris & Schon, 1996).

## Exploratory evaluation

In the case study, the questioned credibility and the tarnished organizational image of the IS department initiated the development of PAM and financial capital investment appraisal was challenged against a broader IS investment conceptualization. The case shows how evaluation practices can shift the balance of power and become a political instrument. PAM was initially perceived as a vehicle for power (and organizational survival) Later it became responsible for shifting the power from the IAG to AMs and the business areas. Advanced evaluation mech anisms (interpretive in nature) potentially threaten the dominance of finance departments in IT management. However, the organization’s Finance Department did not accept the new balance of power and hence, they rejected PAM. Those who questioned the existing paradigms lacked the necessary authority to act decisively in broad organizational terms (e.g. PAM’s team was formed by system managers who accepted business objectives decided by senior managers).

Exploratory evaluation is likely to be particularly vulnerable to the balance of organizational power. Thus, control is attempted through political arrangements and bargaining. Such eval uation probably not only opens horizons and elicits ideas, it can also be used to legitimize decisions and justify actions. It is potentially a vehicle for improving intraorganizational relationships (e.g. between IS function and finance) or an institutional arrangement that fosters structural and normative changes. Such evaluation becomes important in dealing with unfa miliar situations; for example, the exploration of intangible IS impacts (e.g. PAM) and radically new strategic projects.

The role of strategist would seem to be very important in exploratory evaluation in order to maintain a framework of assumptions, values and norms for communication and understand ing. The strategist would need to recognize the time for initiating explorations (e.g. when the current paradigm is not relevant any more) or even act as a catalyst. Sometimes, strategists invite outsiders (e.g. academics, external consultants) to question conventional wisdom and increase awareness by using provocative tactics. However, all this assumes that the strategists have the organizational power to question existing paradigms.

Evaluators are empowered with the authority of accepting or rejecting business claims. They act as catalysts of political influences in the evaluation party. Evaluators often use evaluation in post hoc terms to legitimize their decisions. Strategists can become advisors and sources of expertise. This could happen when they do not formulate an evaluation paradigm themselves (after consensus achievement), but they encourage and facilitate others to think about it in a creative way.

Exploratory IS evaluation is likely to be the most difficult to integrate into the organization as it is not always clear how it ‘looks’ and what is needed to facilitate it. Exploratory evaluation is largely an emergent phenomenon (as seen in the case study) which can be stimulated by creating appropriate institutions, social and cultural arrangements. These arrangements would aim to improve the validity and distribution of knowledge concerning the organization’s possible futures. Exploratory evaluation can potentially foster innovative organizational forms (Mintzberg, 1994).

## The organizational integration of information systems evaluation

The organizational integration of IS evaluation with other organizational entities is likely to be specific to its orientation. A number of critical success factors, which can find practical use, for the integration of each orientation are considered here.

Control evaluation needs to be incorporated in harmony with other management processes such as financial investment appraisal, IS planning, IS project management and IS development. It is also necessary to support formal communication channels. Control evaluation assumes a rational organizational behaviour where the ritual imposed by a formal evaluation methodology can operate as a countermeasure for strategic actions. However, political influences and power games often interfere as individuals and/or groups try to promote their own interests. The allocation of the legitimate power (authority) and how the evaluation approach affects social influences are also important. Control evaluation is the most commonly held idea and it has proved a useful contribution to IS management. For all their faults, formal/control evaluation approaches remain a common part of organizational culture and are unlikely to disappear as they represent organizational stability. However, control evaluation is likely to be limited if used alone today because of its restricted focus.

Sense-making seeks consensus and commitment among stakeholders. It utilizes communication to expose informal interests and assist in agreement of a common set of objectives. In order for evaluation to play its sense-making role successfully, a common language should be established and maintained. This can be facilitated by an accepted (formal) method. In addition, it might be necessary for special facilitators (e.g. AMs) to be introduced to assist in the dissemination of the multiple views. Conflicts and broad interests should be exposed and then negotiated and those affected by the situation should be actively involved. However, a broad coverage does not necessarily guarantee equal representation as power and political games can shadow them.

Social learning evaluation requires a trigger to operate and knowledge to be stored and become available to relevant stakeholders at the right time. It should be facilitated by forma processes as well as informal reasoning. Learning can be achieved by many ways including sense-making and experimentation. The knowledge stored in organizational memory needs to be accessible. Furthermore, learning evaluation requires a conceptual foundation (e.g. skills, other experience/knowledge) which could be acquired by prior training and/or education (e.g. in the case of a new evaluation approach introduced).

Exploratory evaluation questions the validity of existing paradigms and leads to innovation and organizational renewal. It shapes its context by radically challenging its assumptions, principles and norms. It can develop knowledge that assists in dealing with new requirements and uncertainty. Exploratory evaluation requires more than traditional structures and methods;

it needs the power to promote institutional and culture arrangements. Special attention should be paid to actions that involve political and power underpinnings. Individuals should be empowered by skills, learning capabilities and knowledge to operate and perform their roles.

In practice, organizations and organizational situations are too rich and complex to slot neatly into one of the four quadrants of our matrix. Nevertheless, we feel that this approach, supported by the lessons of the case study, does potentially have practical value. It demonstrates clearly that the four orientations of evaluation can be found in practice, even in a single case. As such, it points out relevant alternative approaches to evaluation and the type of circumstances where these approaches may be valuable. Without necessarily having an exact match with any of our quadrants or with specific situations within the case study, ‘strat egists’ for evaluation could still recognize facets of their own situation and thus consider a different approach. In particular, it clearly identifies the limitations of the traditional control approaches.

## CONCLUSION

In this paper we argued that IS evaluation is a complex, human-centred activity that cannot be divorced from its organizational context. Thus, the roles of the actors concerned are of considerable importance. The diffusion of computer-based systems throughout the operation and management of organizations means that the contexts of IS evaluation are highly variable and that these contexts give certain orientations to the evaluation in question. We identified four specific orientations: control, sense-making, social learning and exploratory, and illustrated them through a case study.

Just as IS frequently involves significant organizational change (with all its concomitant prob lems), its evaluation may also often be linked to organizational change. The development of the organizational roles associated with evaluation (e.g. ‘evaluator’, ‘strategist for evaluation’) is inherently related to the ‘political’ power and organizational values and norms that characteriz questions of organizational change.

This argument leads to a number of principles for managing the linkage between IS evalu ation and organizational change:

• Control evaluation elements are normally needed to ensure continuity between IS and other management functions.

• Communication and sense-making are necessary between the key stakeholders. A ‘common’ evaluation language, usually facilitated by the control elements, is likely to be a key enabler.

• Exploration and experimentation can enhance an organization’s knowledge and experience of IS evaluation.

• IS evaluation can be in all four orientations at different points in time. This reinforces the need for clarity in defining the roles of those involved in the evaluation.

• A proper change management approach should be considered for the organizational integration of IS evaluation and its evolving roles.

• The main principle remains that different evaluation orientations require different approaches.

In the case study we observed attempts to increase the awareness regarding the business contribution of IT, and to promote a richer evaluation culture including the consideration of qualitative benefits and the associated risks. Evaluation then contributed considerably to organizational learning (e.g. organizational objectives) and facilitated communication, and brought business and systems managers closer together.

The proposed methodological approaches were supported by tools and techniques (e.g. risk analysis questionnaires, benefits profile) which unfortunately had limited relevance to the cognitive needs of the users (the other members of the evaluation party). They also had only a limited compatibility with techniques used in other management processes (e.g. project management).

The various attempts to integrate IS evaluation approaches with the organization (during introduction or evolution) became a stimulus for organizational changes (e.g. new structures, enhanced user manager knowledge of IT potential). However, not all of those changes were expected or desirable. Many evaluation-related changes were not considered in advance by the development teams and therefore were not managed adequately. This restricted their organizational success.

We argue that academics and practitioners should focus on organizational roles. Two types of roles were recognized as key in relationship to IS evaluation – the strategist and the evaluator. Their specifics are strongly influenced by the interaction between the organizational context and the evaluation orientation. The evaluation roles assigned to the stakeholders often shifted because of reorganizations.

Our research suffers from the inherent limitations of interpretive case studies, in terms of generalization, as well as the difficulty of dealing with abstract notions, such as roles, within specific organizational contexts. Nevertheless, we feel that sufficient insight has been provided by this study, in terms of the development of concepts, that further research in this direction would be encouraged.

IS evaluation will always be an important topic in the decision making concerning IT investments. The increasing interrelationships between business and IT in the ‘digital economy require the integrated evaluation of business solutions, their enabling IS and other organizational structures and processes.
