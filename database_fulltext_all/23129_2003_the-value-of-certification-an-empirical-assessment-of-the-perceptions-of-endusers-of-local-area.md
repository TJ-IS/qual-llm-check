# The value of certification: an empirical assessment of the perceptions of end-users of local area networks

Casey G. Cegielski\*, Carl M. Rebman<sup>†</sup> & Brian J. Reithel<sup>‡</sup>

\*Department of Management, College of Business, Auburn University, AL, email: casey@business.auburn.edu., <sup>†</sup>University of San Diego, School of Business Administration, CA, and <sup>‡</sup>University of Mississippi, School of Business Administration, MS, USA

Abstract. Certification is one criterion that employers seek when hiring network professionals. Many employers assume a certified network professional is better able to manage networked resources than is a non-certified counterpart. Therefore, employers often offer a monetary premium to attract certified network professionals. By measuring end-user perceptions, the current study empirically assesses the popular industry assumption that certified network professionals are more adept managers of network resources than those individuals who are not certified network professionals. Analyses of a data derived from 299 end-users of 11 separate local area networks indicate no statistically significant difference exists between the capabilities of certified network professionals and non-certified network professionals.

Keywords: Professional certification, technology acceptance model, information systems network

## INTRODUCTION

In the modern global economy, information systems networks (ISN) are crucial business com munication tools (Passmore, 2000). Global area, wide area and local area networks are vital components of the interconnected centrepiece of the worldwide digital business communications infrastructure. To leverage the advantages of information systems network connectivity, businesses continue to network corporate IT resources at an unprecedented rate (O’Keefe & Masud, 1999).

Despite the fact that utilization of ISN as a business tool continues to grow, there remains a serious shortage of skilled individuals to manage ISN resources (Watson. 2000). The US Department of Labor predicts that the position of network architect will continue to increase a a rate of 100 000 new positions annually for the several years. Because of the shortage of ISN professionals, firms offer considerable compensation packages to individual with the skills to manage ISN resources (Prencipe, 2000).

Firms are actively recruiting ISN professionals but are firms getting the value they anticipate from those individuals whom they hire? Professional certification (for this study limited to Microsoft and Novell) is one of the most common tools used by employers to measure the capability of a potential hire for a position as an information systems network professional (Williamson, 1997). Typically, an ISN professional that holds a certification can garner significantly more compensation than a non-certified counterpart (Williamson, 1997). The underlying assumption of employers regarding ISN certification is this: a certified individual is better able to facilitate the management of organizational ISN resources (Williamson, 1997). Generally, the current study measures, and subsequently compares, the perceptions of end-users of local area networks. Specifically, the study assesses the perceptual differences between end-users of local area networks managed by certified ISN professionals to the perceptions of end-users of local area networks managed by non-certified ISN professionals. The motivation for this investigation is to determine whether ISN professional certifications significantly affect the perceptions of end-users of local area networks. Practically, the results of the analysis contained herein provide a beginning towards quantifying the value of ISN professional certification.

## PROFESSIONALISM AND CERTIFICATION

An examination of the framework of the elements that constitute a profession is necessary to secure a perspective of the scope and nature of the categorization. The elements that comprise a profession vary greatly with regard to the consulted source. For the purpose of the current study, the least common denominators connected with the various notions of professions and the designation of a professional are adequate as a framework for this discussion. The denominators used herein are expertise, certification and autonomy (Haskell, 1984). The relationship of the constructs is illustrated in Figure 1.

‘As a person learns more and becomes skilful within a particular domain of knowledge, the structure and processes of memory are altered and refined’ (Kellogg, 1995). Such is the defining characteristic of expertise among professionals. Typically, one gains expertise in a knowledge domain through formal education and the application of learned knowledge (Roy & MacNeill, 1967).

In the conventional sense, it is easy to determine one’s level of education. Although one may have completed the formal education requirements of a given profession, he or she may not be able to apply the learnt knowledge with a high degree of skill. Thus, there must be some way to identify those who posses expertise without having to rely on word-of-mouth testimonials, prior personal contact, or time-consuming and risky trial-and-error employment (Haskell, 1984). Certification is the process by which claimants to the title of expert are identified (Sagar, 1995). Certification is a method of estimation of an individual’s expertise via a standardized measurement instrument (Perks, 1993). Before the estimation of one’s level of expertise, a predetermined minimum level of achievement on said instrument creates a threshold that will delineate an expert level of knowledge from a non-expert level of knowledge. Typically, an indi vidual or group of individual practitioners (who are deemed experts in the requisite knowledge domain) determines the expert knowledge threshold (Sagar, 1995). Thus, professional certification is a device for narrowing the range of choice and therefore simplifying the intrinsically problematic process of selecting an expert.

Because certification relies on acceptable ranges of knowledge application, it is inherent that a professional has the independence to apply his knowledge within the full spectrum of an acceptably defined range. That said, autonomy is as the element of discretion given to a pro fessional in accomplishing his tasks (Perks, 1993). Independence gives the professional a certain amount of power and prestige because he or she retains control over his or her own work Thus, with professionalism there is autonomy, and with autonomy there is an independence of decision-making and the imposition of responsibility for those decisions (Sagar, 1995).

## LOCAL AREA NETWORKS AND CERTIFICATION

Figure 2 presents an operational model of the impact of the information systems network professional on the perception of end-users of local area networks. The foundation of the mode is Stallings’ view (Stallings, 1997) of the driving force behind the use of local area networks. It consists of two essential components: (1) data sharing and (2) resource sharing (Biagion et al., 1993). Data sharing is a critical function of a local area network because individual users of computers do not work in isolation (Abeysundara & Kamal, 1991). Hence, there is a need for users to access data from several sources to accomplish job requirements. Concurrently, resource sharing allows users to access limited physical resources (printers, storage devices, and Internet connections) that are not proprietary components of a single workstation. The data sharing and resource sharing functions are factors that affect end-user performance (Hammond & O’Reilly, 1986). Thus, users of a local area network will perceive each of the functions to have an impact on their work. Consequently, end-users will formulate measurable attitudes regarding the local area network and its subsequent administration.

## PERCEIVED EASE OF USE AND PERCEIVED USEFULNESS

The attitudes of the end-users of a local area network are operationalized as ‘perceived usefulness’ and ‘perceived ease of use.’ Davis (1989) theorized that one’s acceptance of an information system is, in part, a function of the user’s attitude toward a system. Thus, an investigator could measure the attitude of a user toward a system to gain insight regarding a user’s acceptance of a system. Davis (1989) proposed that researchers measure a user’s attitude towards a system by assessing the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use of a system as a means to quantifying a user’s acceptance of a system. Perceived usefulness is the extent to which people use or do not use a system based on the perception that they believe the system will help them perform their job better (Davis, 1989). In contrast, perceived ease of use is the degree to which a person believes using a particular system would be free of effort (Davis, 1989). Subsequent testing of the Davis instrument proved the tool both reliable and valid for the intended purpose. Analysis of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use revealed reliabilities of 0.98 and 0.94. Additionally, the scales proved convergent. The developer found perceived usefulness significantly correlated with reported usage $\left( r = 0 . 6 3 \right)$ and perceived ease significantly correlated with current usage $\left( r = 0 . 4 5 \right)$ (Davis, 1989).

Several researchers have continued to develop the Davis (Davis, 1989) technology accep tance model (Mathieson, 1991; Adams et al., 1992; Szajna, 1994; Hu & Chau, 1999). Each of the previous studies replicated Davis’ work to test the reliability and validity of the instrument. In all cases, the authors reported that the Davis instrument demonstrated high predictive valid ity for assessing innovation acceptance. Fenech (1998) tested the behavioural constructs of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as predictors of user acceptance of the World Wide Web. In his study, Fenech concluded that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use were meaningful predictors of technology acceptance so long as the user had a positive level of computer self-efficacy. Chau (1996) extended the TAM model by incorporating two unique dimensions of perceived usefulness, near-term and long-term. Recently, the dimension of gender has been incorporated into the TAM (Venkatesh & Morris, 2000). Each of the afore mentioned studies contributes to the generalizability of the instrument as an appropriate measurement tool to predict end-user acceptance of information technology. The aforementioned findings are particularly important given the current study expands the framework of the general definition of an information technology to include the technology management function of the human resource component inherent in any information system.

## THE CURRENT STUDY

Data sharing and resource sharing are essential functions of a local area network. Both are measurable functions via end-user perceived usefulness and end-user perceived ease of use of a local area network. The current study proposes that an information systems network professional responsible for the administration of a local area network affects both of the essential network function. Therefore, the current study posits the following proposition.

Therefore, certified network professionals are no better able to facilitate the data sharing and resource sharing functions of a local area network than their non-certified counterparts.

## Subjects

In total, 299 subjects from 11 different firms participated in the current study (Table 1). All 11 firms operate in the financial services industry in the Southeastern USA. Site visits and interviews with information systems executives at each of the 11 firms provided the schema for homogenous classification of end-users. The basis for the classification of end-users is formal job descriptions provide by management. Three classifications of end-users exist in the study: (1) administrative support personnel; (2) customer service associates; and (3) transactionprocessing personnel. Information compiled during the initial site visits and subsequent interviews supported the supposition that, for each of the aforementioned end-user groups, the participating firms are homogenous with respect to hardware and software. In total, six out of the 11 firms in the study used certified network professional; out of the 299 subjects in the study, 173 used local area networks managed by certified network professionals.

## A priori analysis

The study design utilizes a multiple analysis of variance (MANOVA). A review of the literature of end-user technology acceptance failed to produce a common effect size. Thus, calculation of effect size for the study effect size is:

$$
d = (M _ {a} - M _ {b}) / \sigma
$$

where the measure of effect size is ‘d’ and ‘M’ denotes the mean of a sample (Cohen, 1988). For the computation of ‘d’, s is a pooled variance, as there is no assumption of homogeneity of variance. Additionally, the study assumes an a of 0.05. The power attained in the current study exceeds 0.995 for each test.

## DISCUSSION OF THE RESULTS OF THE CURRENT STUDY

The results of the current study indicate that end-users of local area networks administered by certified network professionals do not have higher perceptions of the network than end-users of LANs administered by non-certified network professionals. At a P < 0.05 level of signifi cance, none of the operationalized survey instrument items for either perceived usefulness or perceived ease of use exhibits a statistical difference. Furthermore, there is no statistically significant difference, where $P < 0 . 0 5 ,$ , for either of the aggregated construct items (perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use). Thus, the analysis of the results presented herein provides a basis for an important conclusion. The findings in the current study fail to support the general industry assumption regarding the superior capabilities of certified information systems network professionals. The results of the current study are particularly important for practitioners. Generally, employers may conclude ISN certifications do not warrant excessive consideration in the hiring process of ISN professionals. Specifically, professional ISN certifications should provide neither the sole basis for hiring decisions of ISN professionals nor justification of elevated compensation packages for those professionals. As a cautionary note, do not infer from the results of the current study that ISN certification is ‘valueless.’ Employers seeking ISN professionals may wish to develop an aggregate means of assessing an individual’s capabilities that includes ISN certification and a host of other appropriately weighted assessment factors such as formal education, experience, and demonstration of knowledge.

## LIMITATIONS OF THE CURRENT STUDY AND IMPLICATIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

There are several limitations inherent in this study. First, the current study utilizes one acceptable means of assessing network administrators. There are other methods, such as quality of services models, to assess the dimension examined herein. An examination of the same subject sample using a different methodology may produce a different conclusion. Additionally, this study considers only two types of commercially available network certifications. Many additional certifications are available to information systems network professionals. Furthermore, the current study involves only firms in the financial services industry. Replication of the current study including a variety of industries may result in different findings. Finally, with respect to the findings of Fenech (1998), the current study assumes a positive level of end-user computer self-efficacy among the participating subjects.

## CONCLUSION

Traditionally, certification in a profession is a useful tool to identify competency among practitioners. Many professions, accounting, medicine and law for example, utilize certification, in part, for this purpose. The general context of certification implies an individual, according to prescribed standards set forth by a certifying constituency, is capable in a given knowledge domain. With respect to ISN professionals, certification may not be the most appropriate designation of competency.

Given the continuing integration of the global economy via information systems networks, reliance on ISNs as a prima facie business tool will drive employer demand for qualified network professionals. Identifying competent ISN professionals in today’s competitive environment is a paramount IT staffing concern. Thus, firms seeking to hire ISN professionals should consider all of the available evaluation criteria rather than rely on a certification to justify a hiring decision. By developing a holistic hiring practice for ISN professionals, firms can more aptly assess the capabilities of an individual and his capacity to manage the mission-critica resource of corporate information systems networks.
