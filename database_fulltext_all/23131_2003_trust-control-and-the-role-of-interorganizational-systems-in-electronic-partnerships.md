# Trust, control and the role of interorganizational systems in electronic partnerships

Michael J. Gallivan & Gordon Depledge

Computer Information Systems Department, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, PO Box 4015, Atlanta, GA 30302, USA, e-mail: mgallivan@gsu.edu

Abstract. To survive and thrive in today’s competitive marketplace, organizations are increasingly migrating to new organizational structures in which partnerships and interorganizational systems (IOSs) are becoming more important. The success of these partnerships depends on both trust and control – complex constructs that act on and shape each other over time. Many organizations assume that high levels of trust are necessary for net-enabled electronic partnerships. We examine this assumption from two perspectives: the initial decision to enter into a partnership or alliance, and its ongoing operation. Our findings suggest that researchers have treated trust simplistically, failing to distinguish the need for trust (which is inversely related to the organization’s ability to control its partners) and the level of trust (which is an actual quantity that may change during the lifetime of the partnership). In many organizations, there is a gap between these two trust dimensions, which management attempts to close by changing the level of control. In this process, the IOS is key. To understand the relationship of IOS use to trust and control, we applied structured content analysis and analytic induction to 16 published case studies and used the results to create a framework for relating trust and control. At the heart of the framework is the recognition that trust and control are not simple substitutes for each other. Rather, they form a dialectic, where it makes sense to consider each construct only in relationship to the other. Using the framework, we identify areas for follow-up research and suggestions for practitioners.

Keywords: trust, control, interorganizational systems, McDonaldization

## INTRODUCTION

The increasingly global competitiveness of markets, shorter product lifecycles and customer demand for higher quality and service require that organizations continually adapt their structures. IT advances have long been perceived as enablers of structural change (Huber, 1990; George & King, 1991; Lucas, 1996). Indeed, internet-based systems, with their high degree of standardization and low cost, have accelerated the incidence of IT-enabled restructuring both within and among organizations. Accompanying the new organizational forms to leverage internet-based systems are new terms to describe them: hyperarchies (Evans & Wurster, 1997), modular organizations (Sanchez & Mahoney, 1996), net-enabled organizations (Straub & Watson, 2001) and virtual integration (Magretta, 1998). Much research has focused on ITenabled intraorganizational changes in structure and processes (Huber, 1990; Orlikowski, 1996; Karsten, 1999; Manzevski & Chudoba, 2000), but interorganizational changes, particularly those enabled by internet-based interorganizational systems (IOSs), have enabled dramatic changes in co-ordinating activities among networks of firms. As Straub and Watson observed:

The entire concept of firm boundaries has come under attack as net-enablement stimulates electronic transactions and electronic information exchange among many and various parties . . . It is inevitable that information sharing between the firm and [its] complementors (suppliers, outsourcers, strategic partners) and supplementors (competitors) will increase with the advancement of net-enablement. Straub & Watson (2001, p. 338).

Underlying this trend towards net-enablement of electronic partnerships is the widely held belief that high levels of trust are required for such partnerships to succeed (Ring, 1996; Jones & Bowie, 1998). There is also much confusion about the roles of trust, control and IOS use, which calls for a clearer definition of these constructs. In this paper, we examine the existing contradictory frameworks conceptualizing the roles of trust and control within interorganizational relationships (Gulati, 1995: Kramer & Tyler, 1996: Jones & Bowie, 1998). Based on this review, we argue for, and provide clearer definitions of, key constructs. For trust, we distinguish between the need for trust and the actual level of trust in a partnership. For control, we demonstrate how constructs from Ritzer’s (1996) social critique, known as ‘McDonaldization’, aid in conceptualizing the forms of control embodied in an IOS. We use our novel conceptualization of trust and control to examine the various roles they play in managers’ decisions to enter into and maintain electronic partnerships.

We apply these insights to the analysis of 16 published studies of electronic partnerships across an array of national, industry and functional contexts. We used two techniques, structured content analysis and analytic induction. Our analysis showed that the use of an IOS may leverage and reinforce trust, as some researchers have argued (Hart & Saunders, 1997; Karahannas & Jones, 1999), but IOS use may also reinforce control. Our content analysis revealed a ratio greater than 6:1 between phrases representing control mechanisms and phrases representing trust in such partnerships. Moreover, we found far greater emphasis on the need for trust in the partnerships we analysed, rather than explicit recognition of the level of trust.

Finally, the results of our analytic induction complement the content analysis, which helped us in generating a framework that explains how national culture, industry context and partnership type influence the appropriate balance between trust and control. Using our framework, we identify directions for future research and implications for practitioners responsible for such partnerships.

## CONFLICTING BELIEFS ABOUT TRUST AND CONTROL

For organizations that rely on partnerships with external stakeholders, some researchers claim that trust and control are opposite ways of achieving a successful partnership (Bradach & Eccles, 1989; Handy, 1995); others insist that trust is essential for all partnerships (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996; Meyerson et al., 1996). Jones & Bowie (1998, p. 276) claim that ‘the efficiency of such [virtual] corporations depends on features – speed and flexibility – that require high levels of mutual trust and cooperation’, and studies of supply chain partnerships in the electronics (Zaheer et al., 1998), automotive (Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Bensaou, 1999; Corbett et al., 1999; Hartley, 2000) and foodservice industries support this claim. [‘Virtual corporation’ or ‘virtual organization’ has many meanings: from a firm that relies on external stakeholders to perform business functions to the notion of employees who telecommute or work remotely. Due to confusion surrounding these terms (Watson-Manheim et al., 2002), we prefer the term electronic partnership (Hart & Saunders, 1998).] However, although both the scholarly literature and the trade press view trust as critical for partnerships to succeed, researchers and consultants often provide managers with contradictory advice. Harrison & St John (1996, p. 59) cautioned managers to ‘avoid formalization and monitoring of contractual agreements, which lead to conflict and distrust’, but they also advised them to ‘avoid excessive trust, which leads to its violation (e.g. fraud)’. Markus (2000) also expressed the contradiction, noting that ‘while it is generally recognized that too little control is bad . . . too much control is also bad’. Among other problems, she noted that excessive control ‘can damage the relationship between controllers and controllees [causing] . . . unintended human and social consequences’ (Markus 2000, p. 175). In short, trust is perceived to be necessary for partnership success, but too much trust leaves one partner vulnerable to opportunistic behaviour, whereas too much control can lead to distrust, cheating and other problems.

This confusion is evident in the frameworks that depict trust and control in partnerships, which view the two constructs as opposite, mutually interdependent or dialectic. Das & Teng (1998, 1999) characterize trust and control as opposite, but interdependent mechanisms to ensure confidence in partnership outcomes and to reduce relational risk. Other researchers perceive trust and control as mutually reinforcing, where higher levels of one enhance the other (Bachmann, 2001; Knights et al., 2001; O’Leary et al., 2002). Indeed, in the extreme, Grey & Garsten (2001, p. 229) even regard trust as ‘a social process through which control is effected in the sense of rendering people and events relatively predictable’. Still other interpretations suggest a more complex relationship between these constructs. Das & Teng (1998) regard trust and control as partial substitutes for each other, but they also recognize that the specific forms of control used will affect the level of trust between partners, and that the level of trust between the partners moderates the efficacy of the control mechanisms.

Confusion is also evident in the inconsistent use of the same terms. Bachmann et al. (2001, p. v) lamented that terms such as ‘trust and control can be made to mean, at least to some extent, what a theorist or practitioner wants them to mean’. For example, many authors discuss the need for trust in electronic partnerships, but they have different types of trust in mind. Often the same word refers to different types of trust, or different terms for trust refer to the same construct. Similarly, researchers use the term control (or its converse, risk) in an ambiguous manner. Although researchers often assume specific phenomena when they use these terms, such assumptions are not always articulated, leading to ambiguity and confusion.

Unfortunately, the desire for simple frameworks depicting the relationships between trust, control and risk often precludes clear specification of which forms of these constructs the authors have in mind. On the other hand, any effort to specify the phenomena precisely can increase a framework’s complexity. In one recent paper that does articulate assumptions and specifies its definitions clearly, Das & Teng (2001) posit a complex web of relationships among two forms of trust (goodwill and competence trust), three forms of control (behaviour, output and social control) and two dimensions of risk – each with two values (relational vs. performance risk; and objective vs. subjective risk). We view the complexity generated by precision as a price worth paying to articulate assumptions and resolve contradictions about trust and control.

## DISTINGUISHING THE NEED FOR AND LEVEL OF TRUST

Not only are researchers confused about how trust and control are related, but they also disagree on the definition of trust itself. Trust has myriad facets. Scholars have identified institutional vs. interpersonal (Zucker, 1986), interpersonal vs. interorganizational (Zaheer et al., 1998), resilient vs. swift (Meyerson et al., 1996; Ring, 1996); calculus-based, knowledge-based and deterrence-based (Sheppard & Tuchinsky, 1996) and characteristic-based trust (Lane & Bachmann, 1996). The object of trust may also be the partner’s competence, benevolence and integrity (McKnight et al., 1998), or the partner’s competence, caring, openness and reliability may be ‘interdependent dimensions of trust’ (Hart & Saunders, 1997).

We define trust as ‘a willingness to make oneself vulnerable to potential harm from another party’. Thus, an essential ingredient is ‘an exposure to potential loss or harm for the firm – a degree of vulnerability – that is directly related to future actions taken by one’s partner in a situation of mutual dependence and environmental uncertainty’ (Arino et al., 2001, p. 110).

Although managers often use the term trust, what they actually mean is their confidence in another party’s behaviour; these terms are not identical. Confidence in a partner is achieved through a combination of trust in and control over the partner’s behaviour (Das & Teng, 1998). Although confidence results from either trust or control, a partner’s trust is present only when the ability to control the other party’s behaviour is absent. Moreover, although some researchers claim that trust is a prerequisite for using an IOS (Hart & Saunders, 1997; Karahannas &

Despite the numerous conceptual models of trust, one critical distinction is still lacking. No model clearly articulates the difference between the related constructs of need for trust ano level of trust in a partner. . . . much intellectual effort has been dedicated, and academic ink shed, in the cause of constructing and refining classifications of . . . trust and of precisely mapping out its role in social relations . . . Social theories and research do indeed tend to deploy the concept of trust in diverse and often contradictory ways. Knights et al. (2001, p. 312).

Both our baseline conceptual model and our framework make this distinction clear. We also recognize the difference between trust–control interactions in the initial process of entering an alliance and in maintaining it.

## Trust and control during alliance formation

In deciding whether they should enter into an alliance with another firm, managers must iden tify the desired outcomes from the alliance and the likelihood of achieving them. Business outcomes are subject to two types of risk: relational risk and business performance risk (Das & Teng, 2001). As business performance risk is not unique to alliances but is present in any scenario, we disregard business risk when evaluating alliance possibilities. Relational risk (the risk that a partner may fail to honour its commitments), however, is unique to partnerships. Figure 2 shows a decision-making process model, in which managers consider suitable combinations of trust and control that satisfy a given threshold of relational risk, incorporating concepts from Das & Teng’s (1998; 1999; 2001) previous work. Managers assess their relational risk threshold, their level of trust in the prospective partner, and ensure that the level of control they can exert over the alliance will meet or exceed the need for control implied by their relational risk profile. Figure 2 assumes that managers have established a known level of trust in the potential partner. The degree of relational risk is an aggregate of two factors: ‘trust’ and ‘control’ (Das & Teng, 2001). For a given partnership to occur, managers must identify the maximum level of relationa risk that can be tolerated. After identifying this threshold level, managers may examine the factors underlying it. In most cases, managers will have a specific partner in mind and thus a known level of trust in that partner. Changes to this trust level may occur as the partners explore different options for structuring the partnership and negotiating any agreements. In doing so, managers explicitly consider the level of trust in the partner and, from this, they deter mine the need for control that will allow them to remain within their relational risk threshold Decisions about the partnership explicitly compare this calculated need for control with the existing or proposed level of control for structuring and governing the alliance. If the existing o proposed level of control meets or exceeds the need for control, the alliance may proceed; oth erwise, the level of control must be increased to satisfy the need for control, or the prospective alliance should not proceed.

## Trust and control during an alliance

A process similar to that in Figure 2 continues after an alliance is formed, except that it is more complex: Not only can the level of control change over time, but also events can change the level of trust (Arino et al., 2001). In turn, a change in the level of control dynamically affects the need for trust, by definition; experience collaborating with the partner may simultaneously mod ify the level of trust. Given the many possibilities for using an IOS to operationalize trust and control with a partner and, given the complex relationships that occur when changes in the level of control affect the level of trust between partners (Das & Teng, 1998), these outcomes are non-deterministic and therefore difficult to anticipate. Thus, although our distinction between level of trust and need for trust is a useful insight, understanding the dynamic inter action between the levels of trust and control that occur in electronic partnerships requires that new theory be developed based on an examination of real partnerships in action.

## Confusion over control

Control, like trust, has no single, clear definition. We agree with Leifer and Mills’ definition of control as ‘a regulatory process by which the elements of a system are made more predictable through the establishment of standards in the pursuit of some desired objective or state’ (Leife & Mills, 1996, p. 117). Researchers have identified numerous forms of control, including forma and informal (Eisenhardt, 1985; Simons, 1996; Das & Teng, 1998). Formal control systems are based on measurement and often lead to rewards or sanctions, depending on conformance with specified procedures. In contrast, informal control is based on socializing individuals to accept the norms, values and culture of an organization as their own, thus ensuring compliant behaviour (Ouchi, 1979; Van Maanen & Schein, 1979).

Control. like trust, is a multifaceted concept that reguires precise definition in the context of a given scenario. The multiple facets of control lead to different interpretations of its nature and existence – whether as process controls or as output controls (Das & Teng, 1998). We have adopted sociologist George Ritzer’s perspective on control, known as ‘McDonaldization’. Ritzer developed his framework to serve as a critique of contemporary society, given its predominant focus on rationalization and standardization. As the name implies, Ritzer developed his theory by examining fast food restaurants, notably McDonald’s and other franchise operations. In articulating his framework, Ritzer freely acknowledges his debt to prior management scholars and practitioners (e.g. Weber, Taylor and Ford). Indeed, he specifically describes his work as an ‘extension of Weber’s theory of rationalization’ (Ritzer, 1996, p. 18). In contrast to Weber, who focused on the rules of formal bureaucracy as the means for achieving certain outcomes, Ritzer has identified four dimensions epitomized by the fast food industry as the set of mechanisms for rationalizing behaviour – efficiency, predictability, calculability and control. These dimensions of McDonaldization either directly operationalize or reflect the existence of control. The presence of any one of them signifies control.

Efficiency refers to ‘choosing a means to reach a specific end rapidly, with the lowest cost or effort’ (Keel, 1998). Although control may not be sufficient to ensure efficiency, control is a necessary condition for efficiency. Without a well-formulated and regulated approach to achieving an objective, an efficient path to the outcome is unlikely. Predictability is ‘the attempt to structure our environment so that surprise and “differentness” do not encroach upon our sensibilities’ (Keel, 1998). Predictability is a reflection of control. Calculability denotes an emphasis on the ‘things that can be calculated, counted, quantified’ (Ritzer, 1996, p. 142) and is reflected in the importance placed on measurable specifications, service level agreements and cost analyses. Calculability is impossible without measurement processes to ensure that they are under control. Control itself is Ritzer’s fourth dimension. Although many types of control exist, he emphasizes the threat of replacing humans with machines as a means of control to eliminate human error and variability. IT is clearly a ‘machine’ that may be substituted for human agency and judgement. Firms use IT to control employees, in both manufacturing environments (Zuboff, 1988) as well as high-skilled (Garson, 1989) and low-skilled service jobs (Cash & Ostrofsky, 1989).

Although Ritzer identifies four dimensions, we perceive the first three to be related to contro as each is either a consequence of control (efficiency, predictability) or a means of achieving control (calculability). Further, although Ritzer uses McDonaldization as a framework to critique contemporary society, we view the framework as value neutral. In support of our viewpoint, we draw, on Ritzer's own statement that he chose the name because McDonald's is the most important manifestation of this process’, yet he ‘bear[s] no particular animus toward McDonald’s’ (Ritzer, 1996, p. xix). Ritzer even enumerates ‘the advantages of McDonaldization’. In summary, we perceive McDonaldization as a theory of control, and we consider its dimensions as value-neutral phenomena that may occur in many domains. Below, we incorporate these dimensions of McDonaldization into our theory-building efforts, noting that just as IOSs may reflect and reinforce trust (Hart & Saunders, 1997, 1998), they may also be used to reinforce efficiency, predictability, calculability and control in the electronic partnership.

## DEVELOPING A NEW CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

In order to build new theory exploring the relationship between trust, control and IOS use, we employed secondary analysis of published case studies of electronic partnerships (Jauch et al., 1980; Jarvenpaa, 1991) from IS journals and collections published by leading business schools. We examined the case collections of Harvard Business School and Stanford Business School in the USA and the Ivey School of Business at the University of Western Ontario in Canada. We used ABI/Inform to search for articles that included case studies in academic or practitioner journals (e.g. Harvard Business Review), employing three selection criteria: the publication had to (1) include sufficient details about the individual firms and events in the partnership; (2) be published in 1996 or later; and (3) describe partnerships that were using or developing IOSs. Although we found dozens of studies related to trust and e-commerce, many focused on trust in business-to-consumer websites, with far fewer examining the development or use of IOSs for business-to-business partnerships. We omitted several studies of B2B partnerships that lacked specific details about the partners involved (Riggins & Mukhopadhyay, 1994; Narayanan & Raman, 2000).

We identified 16 studies that met our criteria, including case studies from academic journals, practitioner journals, conference proceedings and teaching cases. In seeking to build theory, we used two complementary research methods: structured content analysis (Lacity & Janson, 1994) and analytic induction (Orlikowski et al., 1991). Using content analysis, we searched for passages that corresponded to constructs related to trust (need for trust, level of trust, exam ples of trusting behaviour or behaviour reflecting distrust), constructs related to control (the dimensions of McDonaldization) and IOS usage (including specific details of IOS functionality). We counted the number of passages denoting each construct, using the agreed-on definitions of constructs in Table 1. Both authors read each case and independently coded the passages that invoked these constructs. We then met to resolve any discrepancies (Jauch et al., 1980; Lacity & Janson, 1994), and finally calculated numeric sums and ratios to represent the relative number of passages pertaining to each construct.

Our second method, analytic induction, seeks to identify patterns within the qualitative data, for example by helping to identify new constructs, temporal or causal relationships among constructs and possible contradictions among different scenarios. This inductive approach is often characterized as qualitative pattern matching for theory-building (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Analytic induction differs from grounded theory by allowing both predefined and emergent con structs (Eisenhardt, 1989; Orlikowski et al., 1991).

## RESULTS OF CONTENT ANALYSIS

Details of our content analysis appear in Appendix A (see Supplementary material), and Table 1 summarizes the relevant counts and ratios for each case study. To conserve space here, we provide a brief summary of each case study in Appendix B (see Supplementary material) and offer three general observations from our analysis. First, there were few references to trust. Eleven case studies lacked any references to the existence or level of trust, or references to any synonyms for trust (goodwill, faith, honesty, dependability). Of the five cases that explic itly referred to the existence of trust, only three of them contained multiple references to trust (or its synonyms). Together, the passages referring to trust represented only 4.3% of the tota passages coded. Secondly, most references to trust referred to the need for trust in the partnership or descriptions of behaviour that indirectly reflected high or low levels of trust, rather than explicit references to the level of or existence of trust. We decided to specify further whether each passage dealt with (a) the need for trust; (b) the actual level of trust; (c) behaviours that reflect trust between firms; and (d) behaviours that reflect mistrust. Using this more detailed analysis of subcategories of trust, we found that, of all the passages that concerned trust, only 17% explicitly mentioned the existence or actual level of trust among firms, whereas most passages referred to the need for trust (31%) or behaviours that reflect trust (41%) or mistrust (11%). Thirdly, many passages that we coded as signifying efficiency, predictability or calculability could also have been coded as examples of control. Several passages described control measures used to achieve efficiency of operations or predictability in the quality or timing of outputs. Although Ritzer treated the four dimensions of McDonaldization as distinct constructs, we regard efficiency and predictability as objectives of control, and calculability as a mechanism for achieving control, consistent with Leifer & Mills’s (1996) definition of contro cited earlier.

## RESULTS OF ANALYTIC INDUCTION

In analysing each case study, we examined the context in which the focal firm and its partners operated and noted several contextual attributes. listed in Table 2. For each study, we noted the national and regional locations where the partners were based, the specific industry context, partnership type and whether the partnering firms developed their own IOS or leveraged existing systems.

We performed pattern matching (Miles & Huberman, 1994) to help us analytically to induce new constructs and relationships. By examining how such attributes covaried across the case studies, we were able to identify factors that shaped the initial features of these partnerships, as well as their evolution over time (Arino et al., 2001). The numeric sums and ratios (see Table 1) were helpful in identifying general patterns showing the case studies’ relative emphasis on trust or control. We compared and contrasted these ratios as one step in our analytic induction process.

Below, we identify four contextual attributes that emerged from our inductive analysis as relevant to choices that firms made about trust and control: (1) national culture; (2) industry context; (3) partnership type; and (4) the type of task performed or expertise provided.

## National culture

The focal firm was North American in 13 studies In six of the 13 the focal firm was based in Silicon Valley, California. Nearly all cases, however, featured foreign partner firms, most often smaller firms in south-east Asia. Although the focal firm was outside North America in only three cases, these cases drew our attention to the importance of societal culture in influencing the appropriate balance of trust and control. Management scholars have argued that the national or societal culture in which firms operate will determine the norms for how much trust and control are appropriate (Fukuyama, 1995; Lane & Bachmann, 1996). One set of case study authors, when examining implementation of an industry-wide IOS in Italy (named Sprintel), specifically argued that some societal cultures have strong norms for trust (Kuma et al., 1998), and that, where control is required, it is manifested informally through socia embeddedness resulting from common family ownership of business and a ‘diffuse view o relationships’. Given such a societal context for trust among textile firms in northern Italy, Kumar et al. (1998) argued that formal controls, such as those embodied within the Sprintel IOS, offered little value to its adopters. Another case study of cross-border European partnerships formed by Pellton Industries a Dutch firm, (Corbett et al., 1999) also illustrated the importance of national culture in shaping norms for trust among partners.

Although we would have preferred greater diversity of focal firm location, the differences we observed provide sufficient evidence to acknowledge national or societal culture as an impor tant factor that shapes the appropriate levels of trust and control in business partnerships. Researchers have argued that differences among national cultures for norms of trust are them selves influenced by institutional structures that evolve over time, such as the legal infrastructure and professional standards bodies (Lane & Bachmann, 1996; Martinsons & Westwood, 1997). Such differences correspond to Zucker’s (1986) concept of institutional trust, which reduces the need for high levels of interpersonal trust or formal controls. It is widely believed that norms for trust vary among different societies, because of variations in the institutional structures present, although this has also been a subject of debate within the international management community (Doney et al., 1998; Cannon et al., 1999).

## Industry context

Eight studies were based in either the computer hardware (five) or the automotive industry (three). Other industry settings included financial services, defence R & D, consumer products and food distribution. Automotive industry studies consistently emphasized the historically low levels of trust that pervaded US firms (Dyer, 1996; Mudambi & Helper, 1998; Bensaou, 1999; Hartley, 2000). Even in the one study referring to the high levels of trust at Chrysler, Dyer (1996) noted that it was only their near-failure in the 1980s that forced them to adopt trust based, long-term relationships with suppliers. Previously, ‘the typical relationship between Chrysler and its suppliers was characterized by mutual distrust and suspicion’ (Dyer, 1996). This is consistent with Bensaou’s (1999) observation that it is far less common for suppliers to the US auto industry to make asset-specific investments in one of the ‘Big 3’ automakers (Ford, Chrysler, GM), reflecting the low levels of trust and commitment present, compared with suppliers to the Japanese auto industry. In studies of the semiconductor industry (Adaptec and NetLogic), control over suppliers was a much more dominant factor in overseeing their partnerships, compared with trust, which was never explicitly mentioned in these cases. Industry context is thus a second construct that shapes the norms for trust and control. There may even be an interaction effect between national culture and industry context, given Bensaou’s (1999) empirical finding that different practices of trust and control occur in the same industry but across nations (US vs. Japan).

## Partnership type and nature of the task

Although supply chain relationships dominated, several cases featured other types of partnerships in addition to supply chains, for example where the focal firm engaged in multiple types of partnerships with differ ent partners.

For example, executives at NetLogic successfully juggled several partnerships, ranging from high-control, low-trust relationships for highly predictable tasks (microchip fabrication) to low-control, high-trust relationships for less predictable, open-ended tasks, such as sales and marketing or joint R & D. ## Discerning trust when it is implicit

Content analysis can identify only themes explicitly stated in the text, whereas analytic induction can suggest new constructs and identify commonalities across case studies, even when a specific word or phrase is not explicitly mentioned. These complementary techniques were particularly important in assessing whether trust was present in cases where the word ‘trust itself was never explicitly mentioned. In contrast to our content analysis, which might erroneously conclude that trust between partners was lacking when the word ‘trust’ did not appear, the process of analytic induction helped to identify both scenarios where trust was truly lacking (Adaptec, Ford, Procter & Gamble) as well as scenarios where high levels of trust existed, but were implicit in the relationship (Dell, Apple Computer). The latter cases neglected to mention ‘trust’ explicitly, yet they described activities that clearly reflected high levels of trust. Such behaviours included high levels of information sharing among partners (e.g. Dell), co-location to encourage ongoing interaction among staff (Chrysler, Telecom New Zealand) and creation of asset-specific investments, such as customized IOSs, distribution centres and manufacturing plants (Apple Computer, Chrysler). (Chrysler encouraged each design engineer at its supplier firms to purchase a \$40 000 CAD/CAM system to be compatible with its systems, representing a huge asset-specific investment for these supplier firms.) In summary, the absence of any explicit mention of trust does not signify low levels of trust per se; instead, the level of trust may be reflected in the broader context of behaviours that indicate high levels of trust (information sharing, co-location of personnel asset-specific investments) or low levels ot trust (frequent monitoring, rigid contracts and performance penalties). Although we found many examples where the conventional wisdom about trust and control as opposites was illustrated (e.g. supply chain partnerships between Ford, Adaptec, Instill and their respective suppliers), we also found examples that aptly revealed the possibility for trust and control to mutually reinforce each other (Dell, Sun Microsystems, Chrysler). These virtuous cycles of trust and control were also invoked in other recent studies (Knights et al., 2001; O’Leary et al., 2002).

## Operationalizing partnership objectives through IOS functionality

In terms of the types of IOS functionalities reflected in the case studies, some partnerships deployed IOSs to support two-way information sharing (e.g. shared forecasts, production planning), but we also found cases where the sole purpose of the IOS was to monitor a partner’s performance (e.g. Adaptec’s Alliance system). There is a crucial distinction between an IOS that monitors another party’s performance vs. one that facilitates information flow in both directions. Systems that allow one partner to monitor and control another without providing any direct benefit to the controllee signal distrust and further undermine whatever trust does exist – a classic vicious cycle. Alternatively, an IOS that enables mutual information sharing fosters an environment in which both members perceive themselves as working to achieve shared goals. Such open sharing both reflects trust and will very likely reinforce trust – a classic virtual cycle. Thus, the types of IOS functionality used often intensify whatever level of trust already exists between partners.

We also found some counterintuitive examples where IOS control features served to enhance the level of trust. Both Chrysler and Sun implemented systems to measure and eval uate suppliers’ ongoing performance (initiatives named SCORE and Scorecard respectively). Although the measurement processes clearly illustrated Ritzer’s notion of calculability, and clearly sought to achieve efficiency and control, nevertheless the control processes were enthusiastically welcomed by both firms’ suppliers. At each firm, the introduction of supplier performance evaluation was accompanied by statements from the focal firms (Chrysler and Sun) regarding their long-term commitment to key suppliers. Thus, rather than these control processes necessarily ‘chasing out trust’ as some scholars have argued, these measurement and evaluation features strengthened trust. Dyer (1996, p. 49) observed that Chrysler’s SCORE system for measuring and evaluating suppliers’ performance ‘became, almost by accident, the company’s most important method for building trust, lowering costs, and improving communication’ with suppliers.

Leveraging control features of an IOS to reinforce trust, however, requires open communication regarding the focal film’s objectives. At both Chrysler and Sun, the new controls were accompanied by open declarations of trust, where both focal firms agreed to transparency of the measurement processes. They also announced their intentions to share any financial gains resulting from future productivity improvements with suppliers. (For example, Chrysler agreed to share 50% of any financial gains resulting from efficiency improvements resulting from suggestions made by suppliers. Likewise, Sun increased its commitment to buy commodity components from suppliers, in direct proportion to suppliers’ achieving high performance scores in its Scorecard evaluation process.) Thus, increasing the level of control within a partnership need not undermine trust, particularly when the control objectives are discussed openly, and such controls serve the needs of both parties, rather than providing one-sided benefits to the firm initiating them, which is, unfortunately, very common (Riggins & Mukhopadhyay, 1994).

The SCORE and Scorecard supplier performance evaluation systems could be perceived, on the one hand, as examples of IOS functionality that reinforce control over suppliers and, hence, one might erroneously conclude that these IOS initiatives would reflect and reinforce low levels of trust among partners. On the other hand, as the measurement processes and the results generated by the new processes were shared openly with suppliers, these controls also facilitated knowledge sharing and served to clarify the goals and values underlying the partnership. One of Chrysler’s supplier executives claimed that ‘SCORE is a success because it is a communications program, not just a cost-cutting program. By learning how to communicate, we’ve learned how to help each other’ (Dyer, 1996, p. 51). Hence, IOS control features should not be regarded as always undermining trust or reflecting distrust among partners. On the contrary, where strong trust exists, the use of certain control processes can increase each partner’s ability to work effectively with its counterpart; however, when the same control processes are imposed by one partner on another within a context of distrust, these techniques can have the opposite effect – triggering resentment and further weakening trust. This recognition that shared control systems may enhance trust, particularly when implemented within a trusting milieu, is consistent with Simons’ pioneering work on control, where he argues that certain ‘levers of control . . . seek to inspire and promote commitment to an organization’s core values (Simons, 1996, p. 84).

## A NEW CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

Summarizing the constructs that we identified above, we found four factors that influence managers’ choices of trust and control in partnerships: national culture, industry context, partnership type and the nature of the task to be performed or expertise provided. We also found that different types of IOS functionality can be used to achieve various objectives. For example, control mechanisms may be used either to enhance trust or to undermine it. We acknowledge that control mechanisms may be substitutes that reduce the need for trust among partners, but control systems do not necessarily undermine the level of trust that exists among partners (as shown in our examples of the SCORE and Scorecard initiatives at Chrysler and Sun respectively). We believe this provides an explanation for prior, conflicting researcher views regarding whether trust and control are opposites that substitute for each other or complements that reinforce each other. To capture the dynamic relationship between trust and control fully, we distinguish between the partnership as originally designed vs. the partnership in action. In our Figure 2, we presented a decision process model portraying several factors that managers contemplating a business partnership must consider. Here, we present two additional conceptual models, Figure 3 (an elaboration of Figure 2) and Figure 4, a new model that represents ‘partnership in action’. There are separate forces that influence a firm’s initial decision of whether to enter a partnership and how to structure it vs. the factors that influence its evolution over time. This distinction between initial design and ongoing operation is consistent with recent work by Arino et al. (2001), who argued that partnership quality is shaped by four sets of forces: (1) the initial conditions surrounding the partnership; (2) the negotiation processes that yield a decision to proceed; (3) the partners’ interactions and experience with each other; (4) exogenous events beyond the alliance’s scope affecting business performance. Whereas the first two forces influence the partnership as designed (Figure 3), the last two forces are important to the partnership in action (Figure 4).

Figure 3 extends Figure 2 with insights from our inductive analysis of the case studies. As described previously, when facing the choice of whether to form a partnership and how to structure it, managers must consider both business performance risk and relational risk (Das & Teng, 2001). Essentially, managers must ensure that the aggregate risk that they face does not exceed their total risk threshold and, as relational risk is unique to the partnership, managers must consider the forces that shape it. Based on our inductive analysis of the 16 case studies, we have shown that national culture and industry context are forces that shape norms for the appropriate level of trust in a business partnership. In addition, we showed that partnership type, as well as the nature of the functions to be performed by the partner, influence the relational risk incurred in the partnership. In considering their initial level of trust in a prospective partner, managers take into consideration the norms for trust within their culture and within the specific industry. In high-trust cultures such as Germany (Lane & Bachmann, 1996) and Japan (Bensaou, 1999), managers wil probably assume a high level of trust in the partner (until proven otherwise) but, in low-trust cultures, any decision to proceed with a partnership will probably require knowledge-based trust resulting from direct experience with the partner or the assurance of adequate controls. Such controls help to ensure that, when societal or industry norms for trust are low, the need for trust does not exceed the actual level of trust in the partner. Lacking such knowledge-based trust, one firm may make itself vulnerable by relying on fragile trust in a partner (Ring, 1996; Meyerson et al., 1996). Subsequently, if its faith in the partner is rewarded, a more resilient (Ring, 1996) form of trust may develop over time.

In considering their relational risk profile, managers contemplating a partnership must also consider the specific type of partnership and the nature of the business functions or expertise for which they plan to rely on the partner. Certain partnership types (e.g. joint ventures) and tasks with low predictability or low task programmability (Eisenhardt, 1985) present greater risk for the partner to act opportunistically or otherwise fail to meet its commitments. These contextual factors should be taken into consideration as factors that influence the level of relational risk and, hence, the need for trust in the prospective partner. Managers will judge the degree of relational risk as acceptable if the level of trust in the partner exceeds the need for trust or, conversely, if the level of control meets or exceeds the need for control. Where these conditions are lacking, managers should forgo collaboration with the partner or increase the level of control to ensure that their level of trust in the partner exceeds their need for trust.

## THE PARTNERSHIP IN ACTION

When we examine the partnership as it evolves in practice, the picture that emerges is one of a complex, self-regulating system (Ashby, 1956; Bertalanffy, 1973). Figure 4 shows that an IOS may be used in one of two fundamentally different ways: either as a control mechanism to monitor the partner (ensuring greater predictability, efficiency or calculability of performance) or as a collaborative platform to share knowledge, disseminate firm values and reinforce a sense of a shared mission between firms. As argued above, relational risk is determined by the aggregate level of trust and control; increasing either of these forces has a direct effect in reducing the relational risk. In addition, however, the bidirectional arrows between trust and control indicate that, when trust increases, control may decrease and, similarly, when control increases, trust may decrease; these relationships are not deterministic, however. Thus, when the level of trust or control changes, there is some likelihood that the corresponding parameter will change, but this is a probabilistic relationship, rather than a consistent one. What compli cates this bidirectional relationship are moderating effects, such that an increase in trust may leave unchanged the specific forms of control that are used, but increase the efficacy of these controls, as a result of the partner’s greater compliance with existing control mechanisms (Das & Teng, 1998). Alternatively, reducing trust between partners does not necessarily alter the specific forms of control, but lower trust may impede the efficacy of existing controls because of the partner’s resistance. We reiterate that these effects are non-deterministic, however, and the downstream effect of an increase in control may be reduced trust among partners (particularly where trust was initially low) or enhanced trust (particular where trust was already high).

Figure 4 portrays the partnership in action as, over time, both partners will have an opportunity to observe the performance outcomes to date, comparing these results with prior expec tations. As time passes and outcomes are judged as being either better or worse relative to anticipated target levels, each partner engages in a sense-making process regarding the cause of any variance from target levels. Deviations attributable to exogenous events not resulting from the partner’s actions – and hence perceived as performance risk – should have no effect on existing levels of trust and control among partners; however, any deviations attributed to the partner’s behaviour (e.g. acting opportunistically or, conversely, ‘going the extra mile’) will probably affect the levels of trust and control among partners. The feedback loops running from alliance outcomes' back to trust and control serve to maintain homeostasis in the overall partnership ‘system’ (Ashby, 1956).

We use a brief example to illustrate: if the interim outcomes of a supply chain partnership between firms A and B are below target levels and if Partner A attributes this deficiency to Partner B’s failure to fulfil its obligations, then Partner A will reduce its level of trust for Partner B. Partner A may also choose to increase the level of control exerted over Partner B, possibly using an IOS to monitor Partner B’s activities. These adjustments seek to maintain the aggre gate level of relational risk at a level acceptable to Partner A; however, unpredictable, second order effects may also occur. If Partner A increases its level of control over Partner B, the latter may perceive this change as an affront to the relationship, and Partner B may resist Partner A’s efforts to control them and/or reduce their own level of trust in Partner A. Alternatively, a positive outcome variance (i.e. better than expected results), which is attributed to Partner B’s outstanding performance, may increase trust levels between the partners, possibly reducing Partner A's level of control over Partner B These direct adiustments in the levels of trust ano control may, in turn, alter the ways in which an IOS is used over time, reducing direct monitoring while enhancing collaborative uses (e.g. knowledge sharing) and bringing the partners closer together. In the latter scenario, it is also possible that, when trust levels increase, the specific control mechanisms used may remain unchanged, yet these same controls will be more effective, yielding better outcomes (Das & Teng, 1998). Over time, the partnership will continue to evolve in response to interim performance outcomes, each partner’s attributions about any variance from target levels and in response to exogenous ‘jolts’ that occur in the business environment.

Although we have sought to maintain our ‘partnership in action’ framework at a workable level of complexity, it should be obvious that this is a complex system, the results of which are not always predictable. This lack of determinism results, in part, from the unpredictability of human nature – how each partner will make sense of performance results and make attributions about the other partner – as well as from second-order, moderator effects described above. Although the complexity of this process may exceed that of other models of trust and control, we believe that it accurately reflects the phenomenon at hand. Consider the following description of partnership in action.

A critical determinant of future levels of [trust] . . . between the partners is their experience with each other’s behavior as the venture develops. These direct experiences will influence the parties’ views of each other’s capabilities and trustworthiness in the face of external challenges and internal difficulties. The interpretation of these experiences is a complex, multidimensional problem that is a function of the number, frequency, and gravity of their interactions; the difference between actual and expected outcomes; the nature of any transgressions; the intentions or motivations attributed to a partner’s behavior (Arino et al., 2001, p. 112).

Our overall characterization of the relationship between trust and control is that they form a dialectic, with each construct having meaning only in relation to the other. Thus, trust and control cannot exist in a vacuum, as it makes sense to discuss trust only within a given control context and vice versa. If the level of trust or control in a partnership falls to ‘absolute zero’, then the corresponding construct has no meaning. A minimal level of trust must exist for control mechanisms to have any value and vice versa. We believe that this representation of trust and control as a dialectic is more realistic than earlier representations that viewed them either as simple substitutes or complements to each other. It is also consistent with our view of the partnership in action as an open system that evolves in response to observed performance outcomes and attributions about the other party, while seeking to maintain equilibrium over time.

In aggregate, we believe that the frameworks we have presented show the subtle nuances among the need for trust, level of trust, need for control, level of control and IOS usage when business partnerships are contemplated, created and structured (Figure 3) and then throughout the evolution of the relationship (Figure 4). These representations help to account for the many contradictory findings that we observed in the literature by showing that trust and control have some characteristics of substitutes, but they also demonstrate more complex, non-deterministic interactions with each other. Based on our analysis of 16 case studies of partnerships, we recognize that high levels of control do not necessarily reflect the absence of trust, nor does the lack of any explicit reference to trust prove that trust is absent. In many of the case studies that we analysed, the implicit nature of trust was reflected in specific behav iours, such as information sharing, co-location of employees and the formation of tangible or intangible asset-specific investments to sustain the partnership in the future. This insight that trust may exist, but not be explicitly articulated, is consistent with Bensaou’s findings from the US and Japanese auto industries, where he also identified several ways in which trust is indi rectly manifested: ‘information-sharing practices, the social climate of the relationship, the . . . flexibility or rigidity of the boundary spanners’ [job], . . . and high or low levels of interaction with the partner’s employees’ (Bensaou, 1999, p. 39). Where these trusting behaviours occur, high levels of trust are present, even if trust is not explicitly articulated. Where these behaviours are lacking and where one firm seeks to enforce high levels of control over the other (e.g. through rigid procedures or performance monitoring), then the presence of such controlling behaviour, combined with the absence of any explicit reference to trust, will indeed reflect that trust is low or absent. In such cases, it may be that no true partnership exists, but merely what Ring & Van de Ven (1992) labelled ‘recurrent transactional contracting’, which has only the superficial manifestations of a partnership, but lacks true partnership qualities of shared risks and rewards.

We have also argued that the types of IOS functionality used often intensify the level of trust that already exists (whether high or low trust). This insight is consistent with earlier findings that demonstrated the paradox that firms’ use of an IOS both reflected and enhanced trust in the partner most of the time; yet sometimes, the IOS served as a substitute for trust, allowing one partner to monitor closely the other’s behaviour (Karahannas & Jones, 1999). Our analysis suggests that it is not the mere fact that an IOS was developed or used by the partners that proves trust is present, but rather the objectives sought by the firm who designed and implemented the IOS and the types of functionalities used (whether monitoring or collaborative fea tures). Although many different types of partnership may use an IOS to support their operations, we argue that two-way, open information sharing reflects and reinforces high levels of trust, whereas one-sided information flow (i.e. monitoring) reflects and reinforces low levels of trust. This is consistent with prior work by Zuboff (1988), who extended philosopher Jeremy Bentham’s notion of the panopticon to show that it is not the innate capabilities of a technology that serve to enslave or liberate employees, but rather managers’ intentions for deploying a given technology – whether to empower employees (by informating their jobs) or to subjugate them (by automating their jobs). In either case, managers’ intentions for choosing specific IT functionality both reflect and reinforce the trust they hold for employees (Garson, 1989) or, in our case, for external business partners.

## LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

In contrast to the conventional wisdom, we believe that trust and control have a relationship o neither simple substitutes nor complements. Rather, we perceive them as forming a dialectic, where it makes sense to consider each construct only in relationship to the other. Trust and control exist within a complex open system that evolves over time and depends, in part, on attributions that each partner makes about the other. One implication of our framework is that some managers may choose to limit their degree of relational risk by structuring alliances to ensure high levels of control – by leveraging the dimensions of McDonaldization (efficiency, predictability, calculability and control). Yet we advise managers to be cautious in structuring high-control relationships, as the second-order effects of such high levels of control may serve to undermine trust and, in turn, reduce the efficacy of any control mechanisms in use.

We acknowledge several limitations of this work. First of all, our empirical data rest upon secondary analysis of materials that were developed and published for other purposes. More than two-thirds of the case studies we reviewed were teaching cases that were developed by faculty for purposes other than examining trust and control issues per se. Authors of teaching and research cases may have included or omitted specific information from their work in order to achieve a particular paedagogical or research objective respectively. Despite these limitations, our research approach had the advantage of capturing data on a relatively large number of firms and allowed for longitudinal study of their partnerships as they evolved over time. Although secondary data analysis has some disadvantages, our access to such rich longitudinal data about many partnerships offers many advantages over cross-sectional, primary source data. The case studies that we examined help to shed light on how trust and contro evolve over time, rather than merely offering a static view of partnerships. Moreover, our access to rich secondary data offers other benefits over primary source data, including access to senior executives and reduced requirements for data collection, travel expenses, transcription efforts, etc. (Jarvenpaa, 1991).

Secondly, although there was some diversity in terms of the societal context and partnership types for the cases we reviewed, the focal firms were largely North American firms forming supply chain partnerships. Thus, there was less diversity in our sample of case studies than would be ideal for theorizing about how national culture and partnership type shape the initial level and evolution of trust and control over time. As described above, only a few cases examined partnerships where the focal firm was outside North America, although many cases examined partnerships that crossed national borders. Although we believe that the constructs generated from our inductive analysis hold promise for explaining the interplay between trust and control, we recognize that our frameworks may be limited by the context of the case studies that we examined. We therefore acknowledge and underscore the advice offered by Kumar et al. (1998):

. . . caution is needed when applying economic theories and their predictions to societies different from those in which the theories were developed [since they] . . . may rest upon premises that are valid mainly in their originating cultural context. Kumar et al. (1998, p. 220).

The frameworks we offer here should be tested and extended by examining more partnerships that span a broad range of contexts. There may be additional contextual attributes that matter in shaping trust and control, which we have not surfaced here. Replicating or extending our work by collecting more data from other types of partnerships in other settings may help to identify other meaningful attributes and suggest potential interactions among those attributes we described above.

We believe that our work helps to shed light on the prior debate regarding the relationship between trust and control, first by distinguishing between the constructs need for trust and level of trust and, secondly, by our recognition that a given IOS may be used in a manner that reflects and reinforces either trust or control. These insights are important to other researchers who seek to understand the evolution of trust, control and use of IOS in business partnerships. In the future, researchers may wish to extend our model by seeking evidence that trust and con trol can form virtual cycles and vicious cycles (Ring, 1996) through what Marcolin (2002) labels ‘spiralling effects’. We believe that the frameworks we describe here can provide researchers with the opportunity to examine such self-reinforcing patterns or other complex interaction effects between trust, control and IOS use as they manifest themselves over time.
