# Disruptive information system innovation: the case of internet computing

Kalle Lyytinen\* & Gregory M. Rose<sup>†,1</sup>

\*Department of Information Systems, Case Western Reserve University, 10900 Euclid Avenue, Cleveland, OH 44106, USA, email: kalle@po.cwru.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Department of Accounting, Management Information Systems and Business Law, Washington State University, 14204 NE Salmon Creek Avenue, Vancouver, WA 98686, USA, email: grose@wsu.edu

Abstract. Information system (IS) innovation can be defined as a novel organizational application of digital computer and information communication technologies (ICT). This paper discusses how modalities of applying ICT technologies in their form and scope exhibit radical breaks, which are introduced herein as ‘disruptive IS innovations’. This notion of disruptive IS innovation is developed by drawing upon and extending Swanson’s (1994) theory of IS innovation as well as the concept of radical innovation. Disruptive innovations strongly influence the future trajectory of the adoption and use of ICT in organizational contexts and make the trajectory deviate from its expected course. In doing so, these disruptive innovations distinctly define what an IS is and how it is deployed in order to address current and future organizational and managerial prerogatives. Such changes are triggered breakthroughs in the capability of ICT that lead to the revision and expansion of associated cognitive models (frames) of computing. Disruptive IS innovations are those that lead to changes in the application of ICT that are both pervasive and radical. The pervasive nature implies that innovative activity spans all innovation subsets of the quad-core model of IS innovation introduced herein. Innovation types include: IS use and development processes; application architecture and capability; and base technologies. Radical in nature, disruptive is innovations depart in significant ways from existing alternatives and lead to deviation from expected use and diffusion trajectory. This paper demonstrates the importance of a concept of disruptive IS innovation by investigating how changes triggered by internet computing (Lyytinen et al., 1998) meet the conditions of a disruptive IS innovation defined herein. The analysis also affirms both the pervasive and radical nature of internet computing and explains how internet computing has fundamentally transformed the application portfolio, development practices and IS services over time. The analysis demonstrates that, with the concept of disruptive IS innovation, we can fruitfully analyse ‘long’ waves of ICT evolution – an issue that has largely been overlooked in the IS community. On a theoretical plane, the paper advocates the view that we need to look beyond linear, unidirectional, and atomistic concepts of the diffusion of IS innovations where innovative activity takes places in a linear fashion by oscillating between small technological innovations and small organizational innovations. In contrast, IS innovation can exhibit fundamental discontinuity; we need to theoretically grasp such disruptive moments. The recent influx of innovation, spurred by internet-based technology, offers one such moment.

Keywords: innovation theory and models, internet, IS applications, IS innovation, quad-core model, software management, system development

## INTRODUCTION

Over the years, academics have argued that problem-solving and design spaces related to information systems (IS) are ‘framed’ through lenses of technological capability and their appropriateness in solving business problems (Galliers & Somogyi, 1987; Friedman & Cornford, 1989; Tsichritzis, 1997; Sauer, 1999). Moreover, such lenses are changed periodically. The development of information systems (ISD) has thus been both enabled and constrained by developers’ current conceptions of technological capability. These conceptions include the following features: computing power/cost relationships; the functionality of the computing environment (e.g. technical and semantic interoperability); data access mechanisms and data transfer functionality; environmental requirements of devices (e.g. size or climate conditions); non-functional features like reliability, or scalability; and the ease at which the software solution can be organized, developed and maintained. Recently, organizational computing has entered an era of internet computing, which has invigorated the dawn of new tools, techniques and practices that collectively represent a distinct way of conceiving and developing IS.

By adopting Bijker’s (1987) concept of technology frames, Lyytinen et al. (1998) called this new wave of developing IS an internet-based technological frame named internet computing. The frame concept captures an inherent relationship between changed design practices, their outcomes and emerging new cognitive models of computing capability. These researchers position internet computing as a radical break from earlier frames built on mainframes, personal computers and client-server computing (Lyytinen et al., 1998; Alter et al., 2001; Baskerville et al., 2001). They also anticipate that technical elements of such frames will cascade over time into a series of innovations that feature novel designs, unseen applications and transformed development practices.

The weakness of this approach for studying changes in ISD development and applications is that it identifies discontinuities only in the cognitive realm of models and practices, but ignores their organic relationships to their technical antecedents and organizational outcomes.

Likewise, such analyses do not relate the frame concept to analyses of innovation as observed in technological and organizational progress.

Unfortunately, the concept of radically different forms of IS innovation, i.e. developing new applications and utilizing very different concepts of IS, has not yet entered into discourses related to the organizational and technical advances associated with information technology (i.e. studies of IT innovation and adoption). Research of this sort is widely known as ‘IS innovation research’ (Fichman, 1992; Swanson, 1994; Prescott & Conger, 1995). In these studies, the pace, form and scope of IS innovation (i.e. novel changes in IS processes and applications) is seen as a piecemeal process of technological and organizational perfection, which over time leads to higher levels of information communication technology (ICT) exploitation.

To date, the study of IS innovation deals primarily with factors that explain the volume and extent of improvement in ICT deployment. However, these studies leave unquestioned what occurs in the environment of the adopting organizations and communities. As such, they do not provide an understanding of whether there are changes in the kinds of improvement. Nor do they give insight into which factors and contingencies, if any, lead to such changes in kind. As a result, research has so far paid scant attention to how IS innovation activity is influenced by qualitative changes in computing capability and functionality and associated changes in the guiding technological frames. Similarly, there is a dearth of research that seeks to explain why there are differences in the pace and scope of IS innovation during different periods. What work there is has been limited to either the notion of fashion (Newell et al., 2000) or the imitation argument called the ‘Kodak effect’ (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992).

In this paper, we attempt to extend the existing focus of IS innovation research by probing changes in IS development and use as triggered by the recent and fast expansion of internet computing and associated shifts in technological frames. By doing so, we hope to advance IS innovation theory, which recognizes inherent relationships between changes in computing functionality and associated cognitive models and the pace and form of IS innovation. The study also seeks to improve conceptual means that help capture and understand dynamic changes in IS innovation and to clearly define ‘disruptive’ moments in its evolution. Such findings can be used by both (1) researchers, to develop longitudinal accounts of changes in IS innovation behaviours and (2) managers, to understand radical breaks in how ICT is applied and managed in organizational contexts.

The idea put forward here of disruptive IS innovation addresses the observed need to develop contextual models of IS innovation (Bayer & Malone, 1989; Fichman, 1992; Swanson, 1994). It does so by trying to explain why IS innovation content and speed vary over time. Likewise, the concept of disruptive IS innovation lends much needed insight into which antecedents can explain such variations. To accomplish these objectives, this paper first develops a concept of disruptive IS innovation. Second, it analyses and illustrates the value of this concept by using internet computing (Lyytinen et al., 1998) as an example of a disruptive IS innovation. The concept of ‘disruption’ thus highlights the different qualities of the IS innovation before and after the qualitative shift in the technological frames. The concept also delineates how discontinuities in the technological trajectory of ICT can pave the way for drastic changes in IS development and use, in kind and in scope.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section ‘Internet computing’ discusses the concept of internet computing and outlines its main features. The next section develops a model of disruptive IS innovation by extending Swanson’s (1994) model of IS innovation cores and drawing upon Zaltman’s concept of the radical nature of innovations (Zaltman et al., 1977). Section ‘Internet as disruptive IS innovation’ illustrates the use of the disruptive IS innovation model by analysing to what extent internet computing (Lyytinen et al., 1998) embraces a disruptive IS innovation. The paper concludes with a critical discussion of the implications of our findings for IS innovation research.

## INTERNET COMPUTING

Recently, Lyytinen et al. (1998) proposed 20 conjectures<sup>2</sup> that capture the main features of internet computing (Table 1). The concept of internet computing involves a relatively broad and evolving set of distributed computing models and solutions that rely on open, ubiquitous networks and associated sets of protocols and services. It draws upon models of computing that operate within open, heterogeneous and distributed computing environments (March et al., 2000). Internet computing therefore consists of an aggregate of concepts, techniques and solution patterns used by a community of IS developers in its problem-solving practice (Lyytinen et al., 1998). Like Kuhn’s (1970) concept of ‘paradigms’, the concept has a sociological, community-oriented foundation. Thus, internet computing focuses on specific puzzle-solving activities during systems development. These activities are both enabled and constrained by cognitive schemata embedded in communities of practice with their shared resources and tools.

The concept of internet computing in Lyytinen et al. (1998) was organized into the following four sets of conjectures: (1) the root conjecture, which argues that internet computing forms a new technology frame in Bijker’s (1987) sense; (2) a set of technology conjectures, which describe the content of the new functionality and capacity related to internet computing; (3) a set of service conjectures that characterize new services and applications rendered by internet computing; and (4) a set of development conjectures that specify the individual and organizational competencies needed to effectively operate in conjunction with internet computing.

These conjectures signal a fundamental change in systems development and its outcomes. The sum of these conjectures cover the architecture and organization of future computing services, the way they are designed and evaluated and the manner in which these services are embedded in organizational contexts. Consequently, a cascading stream of IS innovation would change the goals, content and organization of IS activities owing to internet computing.

## A THEORETICAL MODEL OF DISRUPTIVE IS INNOVATION

In order to understand how changes in technological frames influence the evolution of organizational computing, we need to develop a theoretical model that helps identify and explain how qualitative changes in the computing activity precede such cognitive shifts. This section outlines such a model called the Disruptive IS Innovation Model. Firstly, this section explains the concept of IS innovation and investigates the existing IS innovation literature and its limited focus on understanding separate innovations on the demand side. Then, the concept of disruptive innovation is discussed by drawing upon studies of industrial innovation. Finally, the concept of disruptive IS innovation is introduced and defined.

## IS innovation and studies of IS innovation

The concept of innovation is not unique to the IS field. A general and widely accepted definition of innovation is that it involves an ‘adoption of an idea or behavior that is new to the organization adopting it’ (Daft, 1978, p. 197). Similarly, IS innovation can be specialized as ‘innovation in the application of digital and communications technologies’ (Swanson, 1994, p. 1072). Conceptually, IS innovations involve both a technological component (hardware and software) and an organizational dimension captured by such features as new forms of work, business processes or organization methods. Different IS innovations may involve these elements in different proportions. IS innovation activity covers a broad range of activities that involve new IS products or services, new types or forms of information and communication technologies used, or new types of organizational arrangements to manage and deliver IS services. Each IS innovation type can reshape the content, scope and organization of IS tasks (Swanson, 1994). The impact of IS on the adopting organizations takes place through new IS services and products while other types of IS innovation are inherent to the tasks of delivering and managing those services.

As pointed out by Swanson (1994), past IS innovation research has been fragmented and limited in that concepts are drawn primarily from the general diffusion of innovation theory (Rogers, 1995) without a significant elaboration. This stream assumes a ‘pull’ driven demand and focuses primarily on explaining and predicting adopters’ and adopter populations’ behaviours. Therefore, past research on IS innovation has primarily sought to discern the patterns of adoption and diffusion of IS innovations across adoption populations (Fichman, 1992; Prescott & Conger, 1995; for a criticism see Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001). Such research has thus far largely neglected how new IS innovation spaces emerge and how they are influenced by technological advances in ICT. Consequently, pull-side IS innovation models have been inadequate in explaining why organizations start to adopt innovations that are different in kind instead of continuing to follow an established and incremental innovations pattern. For example, these models have not made clear how and why qualitative changes in IS innovation take place and how new types of innovations like internet computing emerge in the IS arena. One reason for this is that a more careful delineation of both push and pull forces is needed in order to explain the emergence of radically new types of IS innovations.

The necessity for developing a more holistic perspective in understanding IS innovation is demonstrated by Hughes’ (1983; 1987) seminal investigation of the discovery and adoption of electricity and electrically powered machines in the early part of the 20th century. Analysis of pull forces alone could not explain this phenomenon. Demand for electric machines did not exist without the preceding push of innovations that enabled the building and management of the technological infrastructure for electricity. Only when this infrastructure was available could adopters of ‘electricity’ even conceive of wanting such innovations. In conjunction with building an infrastructure, there developed a need on the push side to invent appliances that would require the widespread use of electricity, e.g. electric light. In the early stages of the diffusion of electricity, electric lighting was developed and seen as the push-side appliance that created what we now would call a ‘killer-application’ that stimulated the demand for the infrastructure to be built. Hence, the creation of the push-side infrastructure innovations evoked a pull-side reaction over time for more infrastructure and demanded continued changes in that infrastructure. Similarly, the growth on the pull-side created a subsequent demand for continued electric infrastructure innovation. The infrastructural innovation thus acted over time as a push-side innovation that led to the increased demand for more appliances (pull) and the inventions of new technologies (push).

This example of an interplay between push and pull forces in the diffusion of innovations demonstrates the importance of incorporating push forces in IS research. While theories that lean solely on push-side explanations are simplified versions of technological determinism, IS research to date has been flawed in terms of being dominantly engaged with the pull-side analysis. This has lead IS researchers to largely neglect the importance of push-side forces in continued IS innovation and in understanding waves of IS innovation.

In consequence, studies of ‘push-side’ IS innovation are nearly non-existent to date. Analysis of integrated, holistic innovation processes would identify environmental technologies, processes and structures that enable or further constrain IS innovation. Consequently, the concepts of IS innovation (in contrast to non-IS innovation), the typologies of IS innovation or the distinguishing features of or factors leading to different classes of IS innovation need more of the community’s attention (Swanson, 1994). There is also a paucity of systematic research that examines the technical, business, organizational and cultural antecedents of different IS innovations (Swanson, 1994). This deficiency is still more pronounced in the efforts made to understand grander waves of IS innovation that are triggered by revolutionary leaps in computing capability.<sup>3</sup> All in all, there is a significant absence of study of the innovation waves in IS despite their recognized large-scale impact on IS organizations and adopters of ICT.

Another consequence of the pull-side focus is the atomistic nature of innovation studies. Within pull-side innovation studies, instances of IS innovation (like the adoption of EDI or CASE tools) are examined as isolated phenomena or events. The researcher’s primary interest is in identifying factors that can explain individuals’ adoption and infusion events or processes (Prescott & Conger, 1995; Kwon & Zmud, 1987). An investigator thus primarily analyses instances of innovation events and behaviours or their aggregations of a ‘given’ innovation. As a result, pull-side studies cannot analyse more holistic interdependencies between systems or different types of innovations where these innovation events unfold. In summary, IS innovation research to date has left us ill-equipped to understand innovations that are interrelated across industry or institutional boundaries or that are caused by primary technological changes external to the organizations adopting those innovations.

## The concept of disruptive innovation in industrial innovation

Because of its atomistic and demand-driven focus, past IS innovation research has not produced evolutionary or dynamic studies of IS innovation behaviour similar to those advanced in the theory of industrial innovation (Dosi, 1982; Foster, 1986; Teece, 1986; Christensen, 1992a,b; Christensen & Bower, 1996; Utterback, 1996). Such studies have put forward and largely corroborated the importance of disruptive technological product innovations and technology platforms in industrial evolution (Teece, 1986; Christensen & Bower, 1996; Utterback, 1996). These theories have been developed to explain sharp and deep transformations in the industrial organization and market structure in high-technology industries, including mainframe computers, disk drives or operating systems. These outcomes have resulted from changes in kinds of technology or architecture (Henderson & Clark, 1990; Christensen, 1992a,b). Only by identifying such moments of disruption in industrial innovation have researchers been able to understand how both push- and pull-side dynamics advance technological markets.

Architectural innovations are innovations of a different kind that radically deviate from an established trajectory of performance improvement, or redefine what performance means (Christensen & Bower, 1996). They normally modify the concept of technological advancement in a given industry or field over time and install different cognitive frames that make sense of the technology and allow for its effective use. Such architectural innovations are normally generic and clustered, hence requiring a continuous provision of complementary assets to make the technology useful and commercially viable. An example of such generic architectural innovation is the dominant ‘Wintel’ platform for desktop computing that replaced command line computing. Typically, evolution of these platforms requires the development of co-specialized assets (e.g. application software, tools and development approaches, cognitive skills). The adoption of such technologies and the garnering of related specialized assets become mutually reinforcing (through a positive feedback cycle) and therefore create steeply increasing rates of adoption. This cycle results in a bell-shaped (or cumulative S-curve) technology adoption curve (Figure 1).

## The concept of disruptive innovation in IS

The prognosis of the radical nature of internet computing by Lyytinen et al. (1998) suggests that profound changes have taken and will continue to take place within the IS field and they will do so as a collective change to the entire technology frame that brings about disruptive innovations in the IS field. Explaining such changes within existing IS innovation theory would reduce these innovations to a set of isolated events and would offer an incomplete analysis of the dynamics of innovation that are triggered by internet computing. If the larger systemic process could be explained, then individual events in IS innovation could thereafter be understood as they truly are in concert with each other.

To fill this intellectual void, we will next formulate the concept of an IS disruptive innovation that serves as a useful theoretical construct in understanding the dynamics of adopting IT in organizational contexts. It seeks to incorporate drastic cognitive shifts in the problem-solving frames and the advancement of the uses of IS in organizations with qualitative changes in computing platforms and capability. Although IS innovations are, as Swanson (1994) rightly points out, different from the sole technological innovations discussed in the previous section, IS innovation is ultimately driven by ingrained cognitive models of computing capability. These, in turn, are largely driven by advances in the ICT product markets that are subject to disruptive architectural innovation.

In IS, a disruptive systemic event occurs in this market when a constellation of linear growths in computing capability (which are also linear growths in underlying complexity) eventually overwhelm current computing metaphors. These are mental models that are used as complexity reduction techniques in solving organizational computing problems. When this happens, something qualitatively new emerges in the computing space. Typically, these models first emerge as computing artefacts, which embed the new idea (Berners-Lee & Fischetti, 1999). These are then refined over time in models and principles of architecture, solution patterns, application scope and so forth.

Evolution of the HTML and WWW and its embedding in Andresen’s first browser represents such an event-artefact pair in internet computing (Berners-Lee & Fischetti, 1999; Tuomi, 2002). The concept of a thin browser executing HTML over a request-response protocol in an open network simplified (masked) the underlying complexity of all that lay beneath it. This simple artefact (and associated principles) spiralled into a new wave of new computing models, tools and new development methods known as internet computing. Accordingly, we will assume that major shifts triggered in computing functionality and capability introduce ‘waves’ of disruptive IS innovation. These innovations come in waves that result from architectural discoveries in computing capability. They knit together interrelated advances – because of the nature of cospecialized assets (Teece, 1986) – in organizational computing that eventually span over a whole array of IS activities. Likewise, as they diffuse, they diminish and replace existing forms of IT use and they do so systematically and holistically.

## Diffusion of IS innovations and disruptive innovation

The concept of disruptive IS innovation diffusion can be illustrated by examining co-located bell-shaped diffusion curves<sup>4</sup> that represent adoption rates of different types of computing capabilities that embed different architectural concepts at a given point in time (see Figure 1). Typical examples of these could be the adoption of batch-oriented systems during the 1960s, the adoption of transaction-oriented systems in the 1970s, or the adoption of client-server computing in the late 1980s. The figure thus aggregates all IS investment activities (or costs) across all organizations over a given technology platform at a given point in time. To phrase it differently, each point in the each bell-shaped curve describes the proportion of IS innovation activity (i.e. the amount of co-specialized assets at that point for that specific technological architecture) dedicated to utilizing and exploiting each architectural solution. Each diffusion curve thereby codifies the evolution, adoption and perfection of a specific architectural platform and associated technological frame. Over time, this leads to the creation and perfection of different solution ‘puzzles’ for each platform in that each is associated with specific design principles or solution concepts that do not exist in other platforms (Tsichritzis, 1997). From this follows that the longer the distance in time (or the larger the cumulative learning as reflected in the integral of the bell curve between two time points) from the emergence of this technology platform the more mature are the standardized and refined co-specialized assets and asso ciated technological frames.

From the viewpoint of an IS innovator, a disruptive IS innovation takes place when his or her locus of IS activity shifts from one technology adoption curve to another, successive bell-curve; this shift demands the unlearning and learning of new skills. As expected, this shift carries a higher risk (but offers also more benefits) the longer the ‘distance’ in height between the two consecutive adoption curves. In situations like these, there is more uncertainty in how to deploy a specific technology platform, but at the same time the company may produce unique and inimitable competitive capabilities for using it. For these shifts to take place successfully, each subsequent platform must be able to partly substitute the earlier functional compatibility insofar as that the subsequent platform permits the design, implementation, and running of applications that was possible during the earlier platform (or to utilize assets that were garnered during that earlier technology platform), only more cheaply and quickly. Each subsequent platform by necessity must be complementary (otherwise it would not be architectural innovation) in that, by adopting them, organizations can expand their innovation space into new IS solution spaces, processes or capabilities that did not previously exist. Hence, the rationale to shift from one platform to another by adopting organizations is justified in terms of efficiency (cost/functionality) and effectiveness (new services, new capabilities) reasons.

As long as organizations remain in the same curve, there is no need to perform the cognitive adjustments demanded by the complementary nature of different architectures. Yet, if and when the subsequent technological platform becomes successful over time, a large majority of innovative IS activity must and will shift to this architecture and make it a ‘dominant design owing to its better economies of scale and scope. At any given point of time, depending on the situation, organizations can thus adopt new technological architectures or refrain from doing so. Both decisions carry specific benefits, risks and opportunity costs with them. Consequently, different organizations identify, perceive and value such items in different ways. This behaviour leads to the specific shape of each adoption curve.

When a new architecture carries a large amount of complementary elements, the shift becomes increasingly disruptive for any adopting organization. The greater disruption is as a result of moving to another world populated by different cognitive elements, the greater the degree of different technological capabilities, constraints and ideas of what one can or cannot do with the computing capability. Hence, the more the new platform offers complementary capabilities, the more disruptive it is likely to be across the whole adoption population. Its deployment will demand more imagination, more experimentation, and more unlearning in order to truly reap the benefits from investing in it. In situations like this, we can speak of truly disruptive IS innovations.

As shown in Figure 1, different organizations (or even the same organization) in a given point in time may invest in several architectural platforms. However, over time there is a tendency to shift investments into specific consecutive architectures because of economies of scope and scale. Owing to these properties, organizations (covering both organizations developing and using IS if these are separate) normally operate and manage technologies and capabilities that span several architectural innovations (the ‘legacy system’ challenge). Consequently, organizations must maintain or must obtain the skills necessary to manage and design systems with different architectural features.

Therefore, there must be some specific characteristics that distinguish a normal IS innovation and a disruptive IS innovation. These conditions are discussed next.

## Dimensions of disruptive IS innovation

In the history of computing, recognized shifts in technological frames have taken place from mainframes, to PCs, to client/server, etc. (Tsichritzis, 1997; Lyytinen et al., 1998). This paper proposes two necessary and sufficient conditions that help distinguish such disruptive IS innovations from ‘normal IS innovations’. In particular, it is proposed that for an IS innovation enabled by an architectural shift to be disruptive, it must be both radical and pervasive.

In order to be disruptive, a new architectural innovation must imply radical changes in how to operate in the IS world. The innovations must depart significantly (Zaltman et al., 1977; Iivari, 1986) from the ‘existing alternatives: the more an innovation differs from the existing alternatives, the higher the degree of radicalness’ (Zaltman et al., 1977, p. 21). This implies that IS developers must unlearn earlier habits and drop their prevailing cognitive schemata. It also means that IS innovators, particularly those in the early stages of the diffusion curve, must experiment, engage in ‘bricolage’ (Ciborra & Lanzara, 1994), and continuously negotiate what the technology signifies and how it can be exploited for different problem situations.

Simply being radical is not enough to make a specific technological change disruptive. A change that is localized and radical would not be disruptive if its impacts were not felt beyond the narrow boundaries of the innovation site or community. In addition to being radical, changes in the innovation activity must also be pervasive in the sense that a very broad range of IS activities will be affected, a large number of different communities must be enrolled and these changes are inherently related. In other words, changes must incorporate new IS products or services, new types or forms of information and communication technology used, and new types of organizational arrangements to manage and deliver IS services at the same time. This concept of pervasive IS innovation is described below by drawing upon Swanson’s theory of IS innovation.

## Pervasive nature of disruptive IS innovations: a quad-core model of IS innovation

If IS innovation is truly pervasive, it should widely impact the spectrum of IS development and use. Swanson’s tri-core model provides one typology of the spectrum with which pervasiveness can be observed. The choice of Swanson’s model is justified by its IT specificity (i.e. it seeks to classify different types of IS innovation), and available empirical support (Grover & Teng, 1997).

Swanson’s (1994) tri-core model distinguishes three core areas of IS innovation within an organization (see Figure 2). The innermost core involves changes in ISD processes (type I). It covers changes in technologies, tools, techniques, methodologies, or administrative principles that are deployed to specify, design and implement IS. The second core represents innovations that involve the uses of ICT that support the administrative core of the organization (type II). These IS support administrative organizational functions, including payroll, inventory control, executive support systems or group support systems. The third core deals with innovations where uses of IT impact the primary business functions of the organization (type III). Typical applications in this area could be ERP, electronic procurement systems, order entry systems or EDI applications that support interactions with the suppliers. Type II and III innovations thus involve IS products in the service of basic business processes and products.

While broad, this typology is limited for our purposes of demonstrating true pervasiveness in that it does not recognize major changes in the base technologies used by IS service providers (internal IS departments, or external software houses, or system integrators) as important antecedents. Yet, as we argued above, unprecedented changes in computational capability clearly form an integrated element in IS innovation in the sense that the existence of novel technologies (like the ones suggested in conjecture TC4 noted in Table 1) is, by definition, a necessary but insufficient antecedent for other types of innovations like changes in IS processes (e.g. ISC4). Hence, while Swanson’s model helps categorize innovation in terms of new IS applications or new ways to develop applications, it is insufficient in illustrating how some sets of IS innovations are disruptive while others are not. These differences depend on how much they draw upon and embed radical breaks in the use of ICT and associated cognitive models of IT capacity.<sup>5</sup> Inventions in base technology capability can be used to establish necessary but insufficient conditions that are antecedent to the other three cores of IS innovation. Thus the base technology core must form an integral part of a model that identifies disruptive IS innovation (see also, e.g. Galliers & Somogyi, 1987; Friedman & Cornford, 1989; Tsichritzis, 1997; Sauer, 1999). The recognition of such advances is thus necessary if we want to provide an account of how the diffusion of different kinds of IS innovations takes place over time. ## INTERNET AS DISRUPTIVE IS INNOVATION

The following section illustrates how internet computing as defined by Table 1 can be seen as a disruptive IS innovation. This requires that internet computing meets the conditions outlined in above section that define a disruptive IS innovation. The affirmative result of this analysis helps clarify why and how internet-induced innovations have changed the trajectory of using ICT in organizational contexts by defining in a drastically new way what an IS is and/or how it can be deployed. At the same time, it demonstrates the usefulness of the disruptive IS innovation model in identifying, describing and clarifying systemic ‘shock’ events that enable unprecedented advances in organizational computing.

The mapping of the internet computing conjectures into the disruptive IS innovation model also serves the following analytical goal: if internet computing were to become truly disruptive, it would have to encompass a whole range of cascading innovations that relate to managing, developing and offering IS services in organizations as identified by IS innovation cores I–III. Thus, the mapping must result in an outcome where internet computing affects all cores and, moreover, we can see strong interactions between cores. Second, for each mapping we must demonstrate that the resulting change is radical. This can be shown by analysing to what extent the behaviours inscribed by the conjectures have led or will lead to significant departures from existing alternatives. If these conditions are met, we can then argue that internet computing has fundamentally changed the use of IT in organizations, their performance and how they are deployed and developed.

## Pervasive nature of internet-induced innovation

In order to demonstrate the pervasiveness of internet computing, the sum of characteristics of this technology frame (as defined in Tables 1 and 2) should be seen concurrently across the entire spectrum of innovations within the quad-core model of IS innovation. Indeed, the characteristics from Tables 1 and 2 span across all innovation cores and their subdomains (as shown in Table 4). Likewise, Table 4 shows that each of the characteristics of internet computing can be exhaustively classified into the quad-core model. The following sections will provide an analytical rationale for the obtained classification.

## Classification of conjectures into type I IS innovations

The type I innovations portrayed in Table 3 deal with improvements or changes in both the administrative (type I-a) and technical (type I-b) processes. Most of them, however, are organizational in nature and thus do not involve changes in the technical execution of work, although they may be influenced by such changes as increased technical complexity. These advances are similar to those type I innovations recognized by Swanson (1994) for internal IS departments, including the departmentalization of maintenance teams or the addition of a CIO as an administrative process innovation. The difference here is that these forms of type I innovation reflect the repercussions in all the ‘outer’ cores and illustrate how they will shape the ISD processes in the internet era.

## Classification of conjectures into type II and type III IS innovations

Type II and III innovations include advances in organizational IS that result in specific IT application types that support or enable new business processes and business models. The emergence of internet computing should trigger changes in the IS applications produced within cores II and III. ## Classification of conjectures as type 0 IS innovations

The remaining 14 conjectures in Table 1 fall into innovation core 0 and its subcategories. As we noted above, type 0-a innovations produce new core technologies that are made available in the technological environment either through a market mechanism or by simply sharing the code among collaborators (Tuomi, 2002). Examples of pre-internet type 0-a innovations would be the DBMS concept (Swanson, 1994) that was created externally from the firms who would adopt it. Within these firms, IS departments then found the need to create a new division of labour to utilize this technology. Specifically, firms found they had to modify their processes (type I-b innovation) to include the position of a DBA.

The frame characteristics (TC1 through TC9) define innovations within the internet era that together have fundamentally changed core IT capability.<sup>8</sup> These conjectures thereby identify the type 0-a innovation core during the internet era. The core of internet computing features include the following: uniform and ubiquitous clients (i.e. browsers connected to the internet) from whom the presentation and functional layer are separated in the development of applications; published service and interoperability standards (e.g. XML, WAP, HTML) that enable the loosely coupled integration of devices and services into the ‘applications’; distributed and heterogeneous data (Web pages, PDF files, relational tables, and so forth located across the Web) that enable distributed and new types of service configurations; and highly functional and layered software components and middleware (such as Java Beans and CORBA) that enable flexibility, robustness and development speed. The computing capability enabled by these features is distributed, multifunctional, flexible and pervasive, elements offered by none of the earlier computing platforms.

Beyond innovations in core technology, other innovations in the technology environment have emerged that facilitate innovation in cores I–III. These include changes in technology enablers for ISD (type 0-b) and IS services (type 0-c). A change in the ways software ‘pieces will be technically organized and managed (proposition ISC1), e.g. establishes a technology factor that influences ISD. This conjecture is evidenced in the marketplace through the availability of huge component marketplaces (e.g. Sun has a portal for an enormous number of such retailers at http://java.sun.com/products/javabeans/marketing.html) and by such technology standards as Java Beans for easy reuse of Java code (http://java.sun.com/products/ javabeans/).

When this type of change occurs, it represents an innovation in ISD modality, and we therefore classify it as a type 0-b innovation. A similar ISD modality innovation that was independent of the internet-induced frame included software libraries or source code management systems. Likewise, the creation and availability of robust access and runtime mechanisms for finding and executing software components (ISC2) forms a change in the technological modality influencing ISD that thus falls into category type 0-b. The existence of a large and mature internetfocused middleware server market demonstrates this conjecture ISC2 (both BEA WebLogic and IBM WebSphere are in mature versions 7.0 and 5.0, respectively (http://www.bea.com/ press/realitycheck\_ibm.shtml).

Changes in the technological environment can also transform the nature of the envisioned IS services and subsequently define new ways in which software can be applied in order to solve business problems. An example of this type of innovation before the internet era was the concept of a point and click graphical user interface invented by Xerox, or the concept of a uniform user interface through the Common User Access framework as suggested by IBM in the late 1980s. Within internet technology, Lyytinen et al. (1998) propose that the concept of an ‘appropriate’ IS service to be used in solving business problems would change. Specifically, ISbased services would now ensure the following: (1) 24-hour a day availability across a multitude of often ‘unknown’ client types (ISC3); (2) changes in functionality, form or capability must take place at a record pace (ISC4); and (3) the services are media-oriented (ISC5). These conjectures can be seen in the concepts of public web applications (ISC3) available at all times of day, the concept of ‘internet time’ for development (ISC4) (e.g. Baskerville et al., 2001), and the increasing complexity of video and graphics in modern web applications (ISC5). Each of these conjectures thus describe advances in environmental IS service modalities that will have an effect on the types of applications built and the way they are built. Therefore, ISC3, ISC4 and ISC5 can be classified as type 0-c.

With the classification of all the features in Tables 1 and 2 in the quad-core model, all the innovation cores (Table 3) are exhausted and thus show pervasiveness. Successful mapping lends analytical support for the claim forwarded in Lyytinen et al. (1998) that internet computing wil trigger a broad change in IS innovation that allows for the discussion of a new technological frame in the Bijkerian (1987) sense (RC1 in Table 4). Likewise, exhausting the characteristics of the internet frame in the new quad-core model demonstrates its usefulness as a means of describing the breadth of change in disruptive innovation.<sup>9</sup>

## Radical nature of internet-induced IS innovation

To demonstrate that internet computing is not only pervasive but also disruptive, it must be shown that the changes in each core are radical. To be radical, they must depart significantly from ‘existing alternatives’ (Zaltman et al., 1977, p. 21). The following section assesses the level of radicalness by evaluating to what extent changes represent a significant departure from the existing IT base functionality, IS services and processes (Hage, 1980) in each of the cores. We will seek such evidence mainly from the published literature.

## Radical impact within core 0

The shift in the technological features and capabilities recognized by features TC1 through TC9 has been observed by some to establish a radical break in the cognitive frames and concepts that are mobilized when developing and assembling IS (Lyytinen et al., 1998; Alter et al., 2001; Carstensen & Vogelsang, 2001).<sup>10</sup> The new computing paradigm demands to operate – in contrast to the old monolithic application system concept – with a computational service concept that, in turn, is built from a set of diverse and decomposable information and communication services. These services enable high levels of interactivity, flexibility and mobility across locations, devices and organizational contexts (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). Turoff & Hiltz (1998) describe this new computing capability as ‘super-connectivity’ as it enables ‘perfect communication and information transmission’. As a result, Alter et al. (2001) discuss the need to reconsider the fundamental concepts of IS and ISD by arguing that ‘with the trend toward ebusiness, academics need new fundamental concepts for describing and evaluating information systems’ (Alter et al., 2001, p. 11). This suggests that internet computing departs in significant ways from earlier technological cores (core 0-a).

Similarly, for ISD modality innovations (core 0-b), some authors argue that fundamentally different software development principles are emerging and are driven by such concepts as FAD (Frantic Application Development) (Glass, 2001), unprecedented tradeoffs between quality and speed, or quality and design complexity (Baskerville et al., 2001). Within core 0-c, the main target of innovation relates to the availability and configurability of services and the need to provide services at any time at any place (IS service modality innovations). These new features will change the traditional IS service concept from a fixed desktop metaphor to a fluid service concept, which moves around computing devices. Therefore, new modalities dealing with ubiquitous service management, dynamic service introduction or managing multi-channel services are emerging (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002).

## Radical impact within core I

As already argued in Lyytinen et al. (1998), the impact of the internet computing in core I is radical in the sense that the organizational modus operandi and the technological basis of software development has changed. Alter et al. (2001), Baskerville et al. (2001), Carstensen & Vogelsang (2001) and Braa et al. (2000) have recently made similar observations that internetbased ISD departs significantly from existing established routines.<sup>11</sup> Although many of the concepts and ideas underlying the new development imperatives have evolved over the last decade, beginning with the advent of client-server computing (Alter et al., 2001), the fundamental qualitative change resulting from internet computing in the development processes is largely acknowledged. This new modus operandi includes: high levels of distribution in the development processes and development outcomes (Carstensen & Vogelsang, 2001); a new technological environment that is richer and contains a vast number of components and artefacts that are glued together (Braa et al., 2000; Carstensen & Vogelsang, 2001); increased diversity in the development outcomes (Braa et al., 2000); organic and fast development processes (Baskerville et al., 2001); and tinkering, bricolage and flexible organizational and coordination strategies for system development (Braa et al., 2000). It is important to observe that an influx of debates around the new development forms has taken place during the turn of the millennium, thus indicating the ambiguity and uncertainty surrounding how new imperatives from cores 0 and II/III should be responded to via changes in the IS process (Balasumbramarian & Bashian. 1998: Isakowitz et al.. 1998: Braa et al., 2000: Baskerville et al., 2001). There is adequate evidence that lends support that internet computing has created radical changes in core I.

## Radical impact within cores II and III

Recently, several authors have observed that internet computing is transforming enterprisewide systems into infrastructural backbones for any IS service (Norris et al., 2000). It involves the virtualization of organizations in that the governance structures and the spatial and functional arrangements of the organizational forms and processes will depart radically from current forms (Mowshowitz, 1997). At the same time, the concept of the IS is evolving into a business web (see, e.g. Scott’s and Markus’ arguments in Alter et al., 2001). In this configuration, computing takes place with internet and Web standards and their associated public infrastructure. Accordingly, Swanson’s type II applications, such as employee payroll and benefits services, will continue to take advantage of HTML interfaces and internet delivery. Type III applications, such as manufacturing support or supply chain management, will do likewise. Further, the focus on innovation in IS services has now shifted to internet computing based on flexible services and will likely continue to fundamentally transform organizational structures and processes.

Owing to this virtuality and flexibility, managers now envision radical and original business solutions where ‘the information system is considered an integral part of the business web and inseparable from the organization itself and the organization’s business partners, and the hardware, software, communications and users extend beyond the enterprise and maybe are not easily identified’ (Tapscott et al., 2000). Hence, within cores II and III, the internet has been testified by many to enable and necessitate radically different IS services (Norris et al., 2000; Alter et al., 2001). This is consistent with claims made in Lyytinen et al. (1998) that suggest that internet computing will result in new IS services (cores II and III) that were not possible in the previous regime.

Thus, radicalness is demonstrated in each core and subcore of the quad-core model. Characteristics of internet computing can both be exhausted by classification within the quad-core model and exhaust the categories and subcategories of the quad-core model. Likewise, these innovations can be described as radical within each category. Collectively, the usefulness of the disruptive IS innovation model in understanding the impact of internet computing can be shown as a means to describe a radical break in IS innovation.

## CONCLUSIONS

## Research contributions and discussion

This paper makes three primary contributions. First, on a theoretical plane, we formulate the concept of a disruptive IS innovation and illustrate its applicability by analysing internet computing and its impact on the IS field. Second, we recommend expanding IS innovation studies from studying instances of IS innovation to the study of innovation types and their interactions. Third, we expand Swanson’s (1994) innovation model in order that the resulting model is more suitable to analysing long-term changes in the IS field.

Our analysis demonstrates that the developed model can identify interrelated changes in the scope and pace of multiple IS innovations that occur during a major shift in base technology capability. Such disruptive innovations are defined by their unique combination of both scope and depth of innovation impact. Metaphorically, disruptive IS innovations behave like earthquakes: they come unexpectedly, but when they do hit, they do not go unnoticed. Moreover, those affected cannot continue to live like before (radical change), and their impact is widely felt in the sustaining communities (pervasive change).

The concept of disruptive IS innovation has several important implications for the study of discontinuities in IS innovations. This concept helps us deal with the following limitations in the existing IS research. First, we have only a rudimentary knowledge of the location of disruption origins. Second, we have not historically been able to predict the emergence of such changes. Third, we have difficulty recognizing and measuring the pervasive and radical nature of disruptions. Finally, our field has failed to acknowledge the importance of incorporating a holistic view (and therefore adopting push-side analyses) into IS innovation analysis and understanding the nature of discontinuity.

As with earthquakes, our ability to predict the timing and scope of disruptive events is limited. They grow dynamically to become both radical and pervasive innovations only after specific initial conditions have been met – conditions that are difficult to detect and foresee. For example, very few, if any, could accurately forecast the impact and scope of internet-induced IS innovation or its exact timing in the early 1990s before the invention of the HTTP://WWW. This specific, base technology innovation, however, was the ‘tipping’ event that pushed internet computing (and computing in general), which had been evolving over the past 20 years, into a new trajectory. This small step created in a very short time a complex interlocking web, which could feed and sustain innovation in all IS innovation cores and the base technology core (through a feedback loop), hence resulting in a few years of pervasive and radical change in IS activities. Carefully studying the systemic connections across all innovation cores during such situations can, in the future, help to better understand how disruptive IS innovations emerge.

The disruptive model of IS innovation also offers a new theoretical device for understanding the ‘push’ side dynamics of IS innovation, a topic that has been neglected in IS research since the early work of Nolan (1973; 1979). This line of research fell into disrepute as a result of its unfounded theoretical foundation and shallow empirical evidence. We believe, however, that the baby should not be thrown out with the bath water. The need for this genre of investigation – trying to understand how changes in computing capability and artefacts affect subsequent IS innovation – is now more acute than ever as discontinuity has become prominent in the evolution of IS. Another additional benefit of this type of investigation is that it enables us to determine how ‘long waves of IS innovation’ are driven by changes in the IT computing capability (Friedman & Cornford, 1989). In the future, IS researchers should be keener to probe theoretically and empirically emergent and complex innovation phenomena, a topic that has recently generated an increased interest in organization research (Axelrod & Cohen, 2000).

The developed model underlines the importance of understanding discontinuities in IS innovation, i.e. the belief that types of IS innovation can and will change and the pace at which innovative activity unfolds will also change over time. The model does not replace, but rather supplements linear demand side conceptions of IS innovation (Fichman, 1992, Swanson, 1994; Prescott & Conger, 1995). By being dynamic, it also suggests that it is vital for IS researchers to understand changes in the speed of IS innovation and how they are caused by positive feedback loops while more time and investments are being allocated to perfect cospecialized assets related to the emerging computing platform. In the future, such analyses need to incorporate the investigation of pull-side factors including the imitation (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992) or creation of fashions (Newell et al., 2000).

The disruptive IS innovation model operates on a different level of granularity when compared with the more ‘traditional’ studies that focus on specific innovation instances and events. In contrast, the disruptive IS innovation model analyses types of innovations and their interactions. As a consequence, in traditional studies, specific features of IS innovation have been abstracted from the research model (Fichman, 1992; Prescott & Conger, 1995). Such studies help to explain users’ innovation adoption behaviours, but leave unattended how different types of IT innovations interact with institutional and organizational domains over time and how such processes unfold (see however, Tuomi, 2002). They also fail to answer to what extent the pace of innovation varies over time and why. The granularity adopted here seeks instead to make visible interdependencies between seemingly disparate ‘atomic’ IS innovations (e.g. adopting a browser, using components, speeding up development times, and/or new customerfocused applications) and thus help address questions of pace and dependency. IS researchers can better explore both theoretically and empirically how a variety of IS innovations become complementary over time.

The inclusion of the base innovation core into the Swanson’s (1994) IS innovation model also has important theoretical and methodological implications. It extends investigations in terms of their scope and time frame both theoretically and empirically. In the past, most IS innovation studies have focused separately on the pull type of explanations, specifically type I, II or III IS innovations with limited time spans that range from months to at most a few years (Swanson, 1994; Grover et al., 1997).<sup>12</sup> In contrast, the disruptive IS innovation model invites us to study dynamic interactions and concurrent changes in a larger technological system over longer time periods (like years or a decade). With this view, it is possible to analyse where each specific IS innovation type is seen to be shaped by and shape a rich set of interactions among a set of innovators that operate within separate innovation cores (Dosi, 1982; Hughes, 1983; Tuomi, 2002). Investigations incorporating the base technology core help recognize that disruptive IS innovations emerge only when specific technological, market and institutional contingencies are in place (both on the push and pull sides).

The analysis of internet computing as a disruptive IS innovation offers an occasion to systematically organize and interpret changes that have taken place during recent years in IS innovation. Predictions made by Lyytinen et al. (1998) – that internet computing will influence nearly all spheres of IS activity – now have strong face validity. Moreover, we have illustrated their strong interrelatedness, which reflects Swanson’s (1994) concept of order effects between innovation cores (i.e. that innovations in the base core will continue to strongly shape all IS innovation activity in a push–pull fashion). The scope, pace and means of such influence remains an important empirical research topic. There are healthy signs of research activity that is focused on understanding such phenomena (Baskerville et al., 2001; Carstensen & Vogelsang, 2001).

Finally, this paper demonstrates the usefulness of ‘technological frames’ (Lyytinen et al., 1998) as an intellectual means of capturing substantial shifts in cognitive schemata that underlie IS innovation – a topic not addressed by Swanson’s original model. It can be used to overcome some limitations of earlier research, which has ignored why changes in the cognitive realm both enable and constrain IS innovation. We feel, however, that both the concept and the detailed elements of the technological frame need more refinement in future.

## Implications

Our research results have implications for both academia and practitioners. Managers and ISD specialists can use the disruptive IS innovation model to evaluate to what extent they should organize and plan for the inevitable change (‘the wave’) that is going to occur next in light of risks, benefits and opportunity costs. This includes asking questions such as: when should organizations start learning and when should they jump to the next innovation curve? How should they manage skills and competencies in terms of hiring or contracting the talent needed in different innovation cores and how should they develop flexible, yet systematic strategies that move toward the new technology platform while at the same time managing the diminishing investment in the earlier technology? Although it is not within the scope of this paper to answer these questions, we hope that our concept of disruptive IS innovation provides a useful conceptual sensitizing device to cope with the inevitable complexities of socio-technical change caused by the future waves of IS innovation.

The concept of disruptive IS innovation has multiple implications for future IS innovation research including: instrumentation and measurement, refinement of the concept, empirical research focus, changing nature of internet computing (what are its limits?), and which research strategies should be followed. We will address each one of them briefly.

With regards to instrumentation, more research is needed to identify how to measure the pervasive and radical constructs. If they can be measured, it may be possible to identify and analyse potentially disruptive innovations in the future. In this article, we have addressed the issue of measuring these dimensions only through specific examples from the literature of how internet computing transformed each IS innovation core. Valid instruments for measuring these constructs are not currently available and developing such instruments poses a major challenge. While these constructs do not have validated measures associated with them, the traits that capture the pervasive nature of disruptive IS innovation are relatively well understood. In order to define these, we must ask the following: did development processes change; did applications change; did any base technologies change. Our current definition of pervasive suggests that all (four) innovation cores must be affected. Grover et al. (1997) provides one candidate set of measures for those cores from the traditional tri-core model (Swanson, 1994). However, measures for the base core must be included and the existing Grover et al. (1997) instrument needs to be scrutinized further. Likewise, a means to capture if these changes were indeed radical would be needed. Grover et al. (1997) provide no mechanism for capturing such information.

Beyond measuring the constructs of radicalness and pervasiveness, another major challenge in measuring disruptiveness is the idea that a radical innovation varies from one core to another. Moreover, the radical nature of a technological innovation is often only understood ex post when its innovation potential has been exhausted and its impact lays bare before us. The current definition of the concept implies the use of perceptual measures, which easily become contextual and reflect the viewers’ understanding of the studied technology rather than its novel traits. This weakness could be overcome by combining systematic qualitative analyses of the systemic technology features within the innovation (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1993) with a careful polling of the opinions of technology experts within the innovation cores using feedback (i.e. Delphi techniques).

If causality, pervasiveness and radicalness can be measured, it would also be important to validate the notion that disruptive IS innovation is always seen in the complete set of four cores. For example, it remains to be seen if it is possible to have radical changes in the development processes, which would in turn have a radical impact on applications. Although in theory such a situation is possible, we could not conceive any situations based on the analysis of the existent IS innovation literature where such causality alone would hold. The impact of IS method, tool and process research on other IS innovation cores is nearly nonexistent.<sup>13</sup> In fact, the outcomes of core I research have been a disappointment for both the research community and the industry for two decades. There is no empirical evidence that any process technology improvement alone has had a significant impact on IS applications and subsequent IS innovation. We therefore would not change our definition until more convincing evidence of the rich and powerful effects between these innovation cores emerges. However, these issues need to be looked at empirically.

Besides validating the aforementioned models, more empirical research is needed to understand how internet computing has affected IS innovation. Within core I, the concept of methodology and the principles of ‘good’ development practice need to be empirically investigated in light of the demands that emerge from the whole innovating system (both push- and pull-side factors). Their main rationale and evolution is dependent on ‘cascading’ imperatives from base core and application cores (II and III). Hence, if the preceding core and the succeeding outer cores continue to experience major qualitative shifts, core I will continue to be deeply affected. There is a need for detailed empirical studies beyond those already published (Braa et al., 2000; Baskerville et al., 2001; Carstensen & Vogelsang, 2001) to explore how order effects work ‘their way’ through the innovative system. In a similar vein, we need more research to explain the interactions between base core innovations and core II and III innovations. It is clearly neither the push nor the pull alone that enables continued innovation in the applications and IS services. One poorly understood area here is the role of mediating mechanisms that create and sustain strong order effects. Another area is how various organizations manage boundary-spanning activities that are needed to identify and absorb technological, business and organizational skills to cope with radical change.

More research is also needed to discover to what extent the 20 conjectures discussed above capture the essential features of internet computing and to what extent it is undergoing an internal maturing process that can either slow down or speed up the level of future IS innovation. For example, which base technology characteristics will be driving technology investments in the next 5 years (like increased mobility)? Or what additional features will be integrated into this regime over time (like peer-to-peer computing models, or service examination and configuration mechanisms)?

Finally, the concept of disruptive IS innovation lends support of how current research into IS innovation should be organized (Lyytinen et al., 1998) as summarized in Table 5. Proposition R1 suggests that short-term, tenure focused, solitary research is not preferred in studying disruptive technological innovations. The scale and scope of developments in the technological regime are large in that they will require more interdisciplinary and longitudinal research. We need more interdisciplinary collaboration among researchers working in different IS innovation cores. This is recommended by order effects and the pervasive nature of disruptive innovations.

Proposition R2 indicates that some streams of IS research will lose their relevance during periods of disruptive innovation. As witnessed by the death of formal methodologies reported in several studies (see e.g. Braa et al., 2001), old rules and goals of normative research will not necessarily apply anymore. IS researchers may have to question some deeply held beliefs and conceptual frameworks. Just as firms developing IS based on internet computing need to reevaluate how they develop application services, IS researchers need to re-evaluate the relevance and conceptual adequacy of their theories and research techniques.

Proposition R3 suggests that the scale of system development research needs to change. We have experienced a period of rapid and radical IS innovation, much of which has taken place outside traditional innovation sites. Future systems are increasingly built to run partly over public infrastructures defined by publicly defined standards. The resulting need to focus on environments that lie outside the control of a single organization suggests that we should redirect ISD research to take into account this broader technological and institutional environment. IS researchers should seek to understand how and why changes in these environments occur and how they shape subsequent development and use of IS.

The fourth proposition R4 suggests that, in addition to simply rethinking existing research topics, radically new topics may emerge. For example, more research that helps IS developers craft appropriate techniques to build new types of systems that utilize internet computing is needed. These include, for example, how to specify and design component-based systems with distributed multimedia data that is built by multi-skilled, hyper-learning teams.

The aforementioned propositions should guide future IS research, which has for the most part too narrowly focused on the pull side of IS innovation. It is difficult to believe that IS innovation is something that simply appears in organizations, as if drawn over a wall randomly, without any impact from the technological and environmental context. Ignoring the surrounding technological environment also leads to narrowly focused decision-making and reactive management practices. We hope that the concept of disruptive IS innovation will expand thinking to a holistic view representative of the world in which we work and live.
