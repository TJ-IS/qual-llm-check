# National culture and group support systems: filtering communication to dampen power differentials

BCY TAN $^{1}$ , RT WATSON $^{2}$ and KWOK-KEE WEI $^{1}$

$^{1}$ Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 0511, Republic of Singapore and $^{2}$ Department of Management, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602-6256, USA

The body of group support systems (GSS) knowledge, which has been accumulated mainly in North America, may not be useful in other countries because theories grounded on one culture need not necessarily apply in other cultures. To understand better how GSS technology can be successfully applied in a variety of cultures, researchers need to compare GSS effects systematically across different cultures. This paper outlines seven dimensions of national culture and focuses on one dimension: power distance. Power distance effects can be detrimental in some decision situations. This paper examines how a GSS can reduce power distance effects by restricting communication modality in groups. It discusses the potential impact of GSS intervention in both high and low power distance countries, and suggests how this impact can be moderated by task type and group size. It arrives at several propositions for experimental investigation.

## Introduction

Culture exists at the national (Hofstede, 1991), regional (Weiss, 1988) and organizational levels (Deal & Kennedy, 1982). At the national level, several dimensions of culture have been identified (Hofstede, 1991). Within a country, cultural variations can also be distinct. For instance, nine ‘nations’ have been identified in North America (Garreau, 1981). At the organizational level, Deal and Kennedy (1982) have proposed four generic corporate cultures while Hofstede et al. (1990) have suggested six dimensions of culture. To date, there have been no known attempts to integrate these various levels of culture. Thus, this paper focuses on national culture in the belief that it represents the highest level of cultural differentiation between groups.

Information systems (IS) theories and research, like those of management and social psychology, are heavily influenced by national culture. Considerable IS research has been conducted by North Americans based on observations in North American organizations or using North American subjects. However, these theories need not necessarily apply in other cultures (Triandis, 1987; Boyacigiller & Adler, 1991). For example, the attempt to export management-by-objectives, a popular management technique originated in the USA, to Germany and France in the 1960s failed mainly because of cultural differences. Similarly, although Theory Y is regarded as a more effective management strategy in the USA, Theory X is often the preferred and practised management strategy in many Asian countries (Hofstede, 1980). Other instances of failure in applying North American management philosophy to other cultures are illustrated in Daft (1991). These examples suggest that each culture may have a unique way of doing business and a unique set of values to guide human behaviour. As IS departments expand beyond the confines of national boundaries to support the global operations of their parent organizations, it is beneficial for IS researchers to expand their horizons by incorporating a global dimension in their research. Thus, existing IS theories and research findings can be examined to assess their cultural robustness and IS researchers can address a fundamental issue: can knowledge grounded in North American culture be applied in other cultures?

While this paper raises the general issue of cultural relativism in IS research, it focuses specifically on one aspect of IS research: group support systems (GSS). The objective of a GSS is to alter group behaviour so as to enhance a group's decision outcome. A GSS accomplishes its goal by equipping groups with a repertoire of capabilities (e.g. electronic communication and anonymity) to intervene with group behaviour. There is a need to examine the relationship between culture and GSS effects because the very intent of a GSS is to change group behaviour, the very essence of culture. The manner in which group behaviour can be changed by a GSS is likely to depend on the cultural environment. For example, a GSS can often promote more equal participation in North American groups because such behaviour is in accordance with the egalitarian philosophy of the culture (Benbasat & Lim, 1993). However, it is not clear whether a GSS can achieve the same effect if used in a culture where inequality of participation has been accepted as a norm for centuries.

Though culture is a potential determinant of GSS effects, it has not featured prominently in GSS research. Important variables listed in GSS literature (DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987; Dennis et al., 1988) include group size, member proximity, task type and time dispersion, but not culture. The theory of adaptive structuration (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994), which explains how a group adopts a GSS over time through an appropriation process, does not explicitly address the role of culture in the appropriation process. With one exception (Watson et al., 1994), no empirical studies have examined cultural variables and explicitly stated the cultures in which they have been conducted. These observations are not surprising given North America's lead in GSS research. However, as GSS technology diffuses globally, it is important that culture becomes a key research variable. GSS research needs to address the issue of supporting decision-making groups in different cultures.

In order to understand the relationship between culture and GSS effects, researchers from three universities (University of Georgia in the USA, National University of Singapore in Singapore, and University of Oulu in Finland) have established a joint research project. The goal of this project is to understand how GSS technology can be applied successfully to improve the quality of group meetings in a variety of cultures. The key features of this project are the research sites and plan. The three research sites represent different cultures (as defined by Hofstede, 1991). Besides their cultural diversity, these sites also represent major business regions (North America, East Asia and Europe) in different parts of the world. The research plan comprises a series of experiments. Each experiment will be designed, piloted and conducted at one site, and then replicated at the other two sites. Data from each set of three matching experiments will be pooled and analysed using national culture as an independent variable to add a cultural perspective to existing GSS theories.

This paper is the first of a series that will lay the foundation for this research project. It outlines seven dimensions of national culture (Hall, 1976; Hall & Hall, 1990; Hofstede, 1991) and focuses on one dimension: power distance. It discusses the potential impact of GSS intervention on power distance effects.

In particular, the electronic communication and anonymity features of a GSS are examined and discussed. The electronic communication feature permits groups to augment or replace their verbal and visual interaction with electronic communication. The anonymity feature allows groups to disassociate views from contributors. Several propositions for experimental investigation have been proposed in this paper. Subsequent papers from this research project will focus on other dimensions of national culture.

## National culture

Culture is the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another (Hofstede, 1991). Cultural differences between people of different nations and societies have existed for many centuries and are stable over the long term. For example, Chinese culture is based on the teachings of Confucius while Western thinking owes a debt to Greek philosophers such as Plato (Hofstede, 1991). Both of these philosophers lived more than 2000 years ago. Seven dimensions of national culture have been identified. These are power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism, masculinity, time orientation (Hofstede, 1991), monochrony (Hall & Hall, 1990) and context (Hall, 1976). Each dimension and its possible effects on group decision-making are now discussed.

Power distance (PDI) is the extent to which the less powerful members of organizations within a country expect and accept that power is distributed unequally (Hofstede, 1991). In low power distance countries, status differences among individuals are less significant and people believe in the legitimate use of power and equal rights. Groups are more likely to follow an egalitarian philosophy when making decisions. In high power distance countries, status differences among individuals are very pronounced. Might prevails over right and the powerful have more privileges than others. During group decision-making, higher status group members are likely to exude considerably more influence than others. Countries with high scores in this dimension have high power distance. Singapore has high power distance while the USA and Finland have low power distance (see Table 1).

Uncertainty avoidance (UAI) is the extent to which the members of a culture feel threatened by uncertain or unknown situations (Hofstede, 1991). Weak uncertainty avoidance countries are characterised by people comfortable in ambiguous situations and with unfamiliar risks. When making group decisions, deviant and innovative ideas and behaviour are more likely to be tolerated. In strong uncertainty avoidance countries, people accept only familiar risks and fear ambiguous situations. Groups are more likely to suppress deviant ideas and behaviour and resist innovation when making decisions. Countries with high scores in this dimension have strong uncertainty avoidance. The USA and Finland have moderate uncertainty avoidance while Singapore has very weak uncertainty avoidance (see Table 1).

Individualism (IDV) and collectivism are conceived as two extremes of a bipolar continuum. Individualism describes countries in which ties between individuals are loose and people are expected to look after themselves and their immediate families (Hofstede, 1991). When making group decisions, task prevails over relationship and group members are expected to be frank and candid. Collectivism describes countries in which people from birth onwards are integrated into strong, cohesive groups, which throughout their lifetime continue to protect them in exchange for unquestioning loyalty (Hofstede, 1991). When making group decisions, relationship prevails over task. Group members often strive to maintain harmony and avoid confrontation. Individualistic countries have high scores and collectivistic countries have low scores on this dimension. The USA is strongly individualistic, Finland is moderately individualistic, and Singapore is strongly collectivistic (see Table 1).

Masculinity (MAS) pertains to countries in which social gender roles are clearly distinct, as opposed to femininity which pertains to countries in which social gender roles overlap (Hofstede, 1991). In masculine countries, men tend to be assertive and ambitious while women tend to be tender. During group decision-making, men are often more dominant than women. In some instances, women are not involved in important group decisions. In feminine countries, men and women tend to be equally modest and tender. Both men and women exercise more equal influence when making group decisions. Masculine countries have high scores and feminine countries have low scores on this dimension. The USA is moderately masculine and Finland is feminine. Singapore is moderate on this dimension (see Table 1).

Time orientation (LTO) can be described by a continuum with long-term and short-term orientation at its two poles (Hofstede, 1991). Countries with a long-term orientation are characterised by people who persevere towards slow results and save for the future. Groups are more likely to examine problems in the light of related issues and more willing to accept difficult solutions when making decisions. Countries with a short-term orientation are characterised by people who expect quick results. During group decision-making, problems are often considered independently and quick solutions are sought. Countries with high scores on this dimension have a long-term orientation. The USA has a short-term orientation while Singapore is moderate on this dimension (see Table 1). The score for Finland is not available and will be collected.

Monochrony (MON) and polychrony are two ends of a bipolar continuum describing the attitudes of societies toward their use of time (Hall & Hall, 1990). In monochronic countries, people focus on issues one at a time and emphasise schedules and procedures for task completion. Groups are likely to adhere rigidly to pre-defined schedules and acceptable procedures when making decisions. Issues on the agenda are likely to be dealt with serially by decision-making groups. In polychronic countries, people perform their activities in parallel and focus on task completion rather than adherence to procedures. During group decision-making, issues on the agenda are likely to be addressed concurrently. Groups are likely to demonstrate greater flexibility with schedules and procedures during task performance. The scores for the three research sites on monochrony are not available and will be collected.

Context (CON) refers to the amount of information that surrounds events and delineates the extent to which information must be explicitly coded by members of a culture during communication (Hall, 1976). High context countries are characterised by events which carry a significant amount of implicit information understood by people. When making group decisions, little information needs to be explicitly coded and communicated. In low context countries, events typically do not carry a significant amount of shared and implicit information. Decision-making groups need to exchange much information explicitly. The scores for the three research sites on context are not available and will be collected.

The seven dimensions of national culture outline the behavioural norms of each nation. Because these dimensions affect the way people relate to each other in their respective cultures, they influence both the task performance and social aspects of decision-making groups. Hence, these dimensions are relevant variables for investigation in GSS research. Power distance has been selected for investigation for various reasons: (1) power plays an important role in organizations (Pfeffer, 1992); (2) authority significantly influences group and individual behaviour (Milgram, 1974); (3) there is an existing body of literature on leadership and power (Hofstede, 1980). Singapore differs from the USA and Finland on power distance. Although the USA and Finland are close on this dimension, they differ on dimensions to be investigated in subsequent research.

Table 1 summarises the scores of the three research sites. These scores were computed from the results of instruments (Hofstede, 1980; Hofstede & Bond, 1988) administered to about 120,000 respondents (Hofstede, 1991). Caution should be exercised when using these scores because flaws with Hofstede's (1991) work have been noted by researchers (Erez & Earley, 1993). First, all respondents were employees of a single large organization (IBM) with unique characteristics. This may limit the applicability of Hofstede's (1991) findings. However, other studies have lent support to the stability of these findings (e.g. Shackleton & Ali, 1990). Second, items in the instrument were not face valid. These items may not be applicable in many situations. However, additional items created using the general constructs put forward by Hofstede (1980) can overcome this limitation (Erez & Earley, 1987).

## Power distance effects

Many East Asian and South-East Asian countries are characterised by high power distance (Hofstede, 1991). In these countries, there are considerable differences between higher and lower status individuals. Lower status individuals are usually dependent on higher status individuals and tend to treat them with respect and obedience. Moreover, lower status individuals are often reluctant to criticise higher status individuals. When groups make decisions, the views of higher status group members usually carry greater credibility regardless of merit. Lower status group members are likely to accept the views of higher status group members, though the extent of acceptance may vary from situation to situation. Consequently, group decisions usually reflect closely the views of higher status group members.

Many countries in Europe and North America are characterised by low power distance (Hofstede, 1991). In these countries, higher and lower status individuals are often interdependent and there is a greater tendency for them to treat each other as equals. In many situations, lower status individuals are free to criticise higher status individuals so long as the criticisms are valid and logical. When groups make decisions, the views of group members are more likely to be judged according to merit rather than the status of contributors. In many instances, lower status group members may not accept the views of higher status group members unless these views are supported by an acceptable rationale. Consequently, group members are expected to contribute relatively equally to group decisions regardless of status.

These two scenarios illustrate how power distance can affect group decision-making. Power distance effects are sometimes beneficial and at other times detrimental. In some situations, they promote efficiency by regulating the process of task performance (Allison, 1971); in other situations, they mask critical information from key decision makers and lead to major decision fiascos (Linde, 1988). Therefore, it is important to examine how a GSS can alter power distance effects and enhance the quality of group decisions in some situations.

Power distance effects can be understood better by examining their nature. Studies in social psychology have shown that there are two dominant types of influence during group decision-making: informational and normative influence (Kaplan & Miller, 1987). Informational influence is based on the acceptance of information from others as evidence about reality. Its effects result from the sharing of relevant factual information about the issues under consideration. Normative influence is based on the desire to conform to the expectations of others. Its effects result from exposure to others' preferences and subsequent conformity to norms that are implicit or explicit in these preferences.

Clapper et al. (1991) cite four sources of normative influence: majority power, persuasiveness, dominance and hierarchy status. The effects of majority power are widely documented in the social psychology literature (Levine & Russo, 1987). Majority power is a strong force driving a group towards conformity. When making group decisions, group members have a strong tendency to follow the views of the majority, regardless of their validity. At its extreme, this phenomenon is known as 'groupthink' (Janis, 1971). The persuasiveness of group members, aside from the informational content of their communication, is a source of normative influence. Group members who consistently project their views are able to exert a strong influence on others, even when they are in a minority position (Moscovici & Faucheux, 1972). Group members with dominant personalities constitute a source of normative influence. These group members usually exercise their influence through their visibility and by making their positions identifiable (Wolf, 1979). Factors such as social status, economic status and age (Latane & Wolf, 1981) contribute to hierarchy status, another source of normative influence. When making group decisions, group members with greater hierarchy status are often more influential, irrespective of the merits of their views. Based on the above description, it is clear that power distance effects are a normative influence arising from hierarchy status.

## GSS intervention

Existing empirical studies have examined the impact of a GSS on majorities (e.g. Watson et al., 1988; Zigurs et al., 1988; Clapper et al., 1991) and on minorities comprising elected group leaders who are not higher status individuals (e.g. George et al., 1990). However, GSS researchers have paid little attention to the impact of GSS on power distance effects or influence exuded by higher status individuals (an exception is Tan et al., 1993), a prime concern in high power distance countries. Given that power distance effects are occasionally dysfunctional, it is important to see how a GSS can dampen power distance effects and whether this impact of a GSS differs across cultures.

A GSS can potentially dampen power distance effects by manipulating the communication network, modality or strategy during group decision-making (Clapper et al., 1991). A communication network represents a fixed pattern of communication links among group members (Shaw, 1978). It determines the other group members with which each group member can communicate. Examples include the 'wheel', 'circle', 'chain', 'kite' and 'star' patterns. A communication mode is the amalgam of methods and devices that can be used to exchange information in a communication link (Weeks & Chapanis, 1976). Communication modalities are characterised by their cues such as verbal, visual and textual, and their features such as anonymity and immediacy of feedback. They can be placed on a rich-lean continuum (Daft & Lengel, 1986). A communication strategy is a set of steps taken, based on implicit and explicit guidelines, by groups to resolve their problems optimally (Eils & John, 1980). Examples are the Delphi and nominal group techniques.

This paper examines the potential impact of GSS intervention through the manipulation of communication modality, holding communication network and strategy constant. [Although a GSS may alter power distance effects by changing the communication network and strategy, the impact due to these changes is likely to be small compared with that resulting from changes in communication modality.] Based on the assumption of immediacy of feedback, it focuses on two aspects of communication modality: cues (verbal, visual and textual) and anonymity (identified and anonymous). It assesses their potential ability to reduce power distance effects in high and low power distance countries. In this paper, unsupported groups refer to groups that communicate using verbal, visual and textual cues. They meet at the same time and place without a GSS. Face-to-face GSS groups refer to groups that communicate using visual and textual cues. They are similar to unsupported groups except that they have a GSS to support them. [When a GSS is used, electronic communication may augment or substitute for verbal communication. In this paper, face-to-face GSS groups refer to groups that have completely replaced verbal with electronic communication.] Dispersed GSS groups refer to groups that communicate using only textual cues. [With existing technologies, dispersed GSS groups may decide whether or not to augment textual with video and audio communication (Rice, 1984). In this paper, dispersed GSS groups refer to groups that use only textual communication.] They meet at the same time but at different places. They make decisions through a GSS which permits electronic communication. The identified condition refers to a situation where views are tagged with the identities of their contributors. The anonymous condition refers to a situation where views are not associated with their contributors. [The ability of a GSS to enforce anonymity depends on situational factors such as group size and group history (Valacich et al., 1992). In this paper, anonymous groups refer to groups that have complete anonymity during group decision-making.]

## GSS intervention through communication modality

Verbal communication, especially its paralinguistic aspects (McGrath, 1984) such as the tone and loudness of voice and rate of speech, is a rich medium and has the potential to carry a significant amount of normative cues. Face-to-face GSS groups can reduce normative influence by replacing verbal with electronic communication. When this happens, higher status group members are not able to intimidate others through their tone and loudness of voice and rate of speech. However, the use of electronic communication does not remove visual cues. Higher status group members can still use visual cues (Rutter et al., 1977) such as visual orientation and facial expression to express their approval or disapproval of certain views. A certain amount of power distance effects may still be present in a face-to-face GSS setting.

When dispersed GSS groups meet, communication is carried out only through textual cues. The removal of verbal and visual cues eliminates a significant amount of normative influence. Higher status group members are unable to influence others through their tone and loudness of voice and rate of speech. Neither are they able to exercise influence through their visual orientation and facial expression. Power distance effects should be minimal in a dispersed GSS setting.

The preceding discussion suggests that power distance effects can be reduced by restricting communication cues. However, this reduction may also be dependent on anonymity. In the identified condition, group members can associate views with their contributors. Higher status group members can still promote their views, though with increasing difficulty as communication cues are removed. In the anonymous condition, it is less likely that group members are able to associate views with their contributors. Higher status group members are likely to find it very difficult to promote their views. Hence, the reduction of power distance effects, through the restriction on communication cues, is likely to be more pronounced in the anonymous than the identified condition (see Figure 1). No differences between the identified and anonymous conditions have been predicted for unsupported groups because it is very difficult, if not impossible, to impose anonymity effectively in this setting.

These observations are supported by the first principle of social impact theory (Latane & Wolf, 1981), which suggests that the degree of social impact, or pressure to change, on an individual is a multiplicative function of the strength, immediacy and number of others who are potential sources of influence in the situation. An individual is likely to experience more social impact, or more pressure to change, the stronger, the more immediate and the greater the number of potential sources of influence (Latane & Wolf, 1981).

Strength refers to the salience, power, importance or intensity of the sources of influence (Latane & Wolf, 1981). It is determined by factors such as the social status, economic status and age of the sources of influence, the prior relationship between the sources and targets of influence, and the future power of the sources over targets of influence. Immediacy refers to the proximity in space or time between the sources and targets of influence, and the absence of intervening barriers (Latane & Wolf, 1981). It includes the richness of the communication modality between the sources and targets of influence. Number refers to how many sources of influence are present (Latane & Wolf, 1981).

Proposition 1: The reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention will be greater in the anonymous than the identified condition.

The findings from several empirical studies support this proposition. Dubrovsky et al. (1991) and Tan et al. (1993) reported that the influence and dominance of higher status group members tend to decline when communication cues are reduced. Connolly et al. (1990) and Jessup et al. (1990) found that group members contributed more comments in anonymous than in identified settings. When members of anonymous groups are more willing to contribute, the probability of dominance by any individual group member is reduced.

## GSS intervention moderated by power distance

Proposition 1 is likely to be valid for both high and low power distance countries. However, the strength of its predicted effects is likely to be contingent upon the power distance of a country. In the identified condition, higher status group members can make their views known even with a reduction of communication cues. Criticising the views of higher status group members is culturally unacceptable in high power distance countries. Hence, the impact of GSS intervention is likely to be minimal in high power distance countries compared with low power distance countries. In the anonymous condition, it is very difficult for higher status group members to make their views known. Their ability to exercise influence is likely to be greatly reduced. Since power distance effects are more pronounced in high power distance countries, the impact of GSS intervention is also likely to be more significant in high power distance countries compared with low power distance countries (see Figure 2). [In interpreting Figure 2, the focus should be on the relative slopes rather than the specific points on the graphs. For example, anonymous dispersed GSS groups in high power distance countries need not necessarily experience lower power distance effects than identified dispersed GSS groups in low power distance countries.]

Proposition 2: The reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention will depend on the interaction between anonymity and power distance.

## GSS intervention moderated by task type

Two important task types are intellective and preference tasks (DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987). An intellective task is a task for which there is a correct answer, regardless of whether the answer is intuitive and demonstrable (McGrath, 1984). Examples are the student admission task in Zigurs et al. (1988) and the compensatory damages task in Kaplan and Miller (1987). A preference task is a task for which the 'correct' answer is that negotiated and agreed upon by decision makers based on shared cultural values and norms, social comparison and influences, or shared information (McGrath, 1984). Examples are the foundation task in Watson et al. (1988) and the exemplary damages task in Kaplan and Miller (1987). Groups, rather than individuals, are often used to perform intellective and preference tasks. Hence, these two task types are good candidates for GSS research (Tan et al., 1994).

Intellective and preference tasks consist mainly of issues located on different sides of an informational-normative continuum of influence (Laughlin & Earley, 1982). Kaplan and Miller (1987) show that informational influence predominates when groups are solving intellective tasks while normative influence predominates when groups are performing preference tasks. Higher status group members are likely to have more opportunities to exercise normative influence when the group is performing preference tasks than when it is solving intellective tasks. Hence, power distance effects are likely to be stronger with preference than intellective tasks. Consequently, the predicted interaction effect in Proposition 2 and the differences between high and low power distance countries in Propositions 2a and 2b are likely to be more pronounced with preference than intellective tasks.

Proposition 3: The reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention will be more pronounced with preference than intellective tasks.

## Corollaries to Proposition 3 are:

Proposition 3a: In the identified condition, the greater reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention in low over high power distance countries will be more pronounced with preference than intellective tasks.

Proposition 3b: In the anonymous condition, the greater reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention in high over low power distance countries will be more pronounced with preference than intellective tasks.

Proposition 3a predicts that the widening of the gap between the discontinuous graphs in Figure 2 will be more pronounced with preference than intellective tasks. Proposition 3b posits that the narrowing of the gap between the continuous graphs in Figure 2 will be more pronounced with preference than intellective tasks. Proposition 3 has some empirical support. Tan et al. (1993) found that the influence of higher status group members, and the reduction of this influence with a GSS, are more pronounced for a preference than an intellective task.

## GSS intervention moderated by group size

The classification of groups according to size is arbitrary (DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987). However, when considered from a relative point of view, smaller groups behave differently from larger groups (Thomas & Fink, 1963). As group size increases, the total quantity of information exchange tends to increase while the frequency, duration and intimacy of information exchange between group members tend to decline (Hare, 1976). Smaller groups are more likely to resolve opinion differences actively (Hare et al., 1967). Consensus is harder to achieve in larger groups, which are more likely to experience communication difficulties (Hare, 1976). Since smaller groups permit group members to influence each other to a greater extent, they should allow higher status group members to exercise normative influence more effectively. Hence, power distance effects are likely to be stronger with smaller than larger groups. Consequently, the predicted interaction effect in Proposition 2 and the differences between high and low power distance countries in Propositions 2a and 2b are likely to be more pronounced with smaller than larger groups.

These observations are supported by the third principle of social impact theory (Latane & Wolf, 1981). This principle posits that the degree of social impact, or pressure to change, on an individual is a reciprocal multiplicative function of the strength, immediacy and number of others who are potential targets of influence in the situation. An individual is likely to experience less social impact, or less pressure to change, the stronger, the more immediate and the greater the number of potential targets of influence (Latane & Wolf, 1981). Strength, immediacy and number are defined as in the first principle of social impact theory except that the sources and targets of influence are interchanged (Latane & Wolf, 1981). By formulating the degree of social impact as a reciprocal function of the number of targets of influence, the third principle of social impact theory suggests that the influence of higher status group members is distributed among others present. It predicts that power distance effects are stronger in smaller than larger groups.

Proposition 4: The reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention will be more pronounced with smaller than larger groups.

## Corollaries to Proposition 4 are:

Proposition 4a: In the identified condition, the greater reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention in low over high power distance countries will be more pronounced with smaller than larger groups.

Proposition 4b: In the anonymous condition, the greater reduction of power distance effects through GSS intervention in high over low power distance countries will be more pronounced with smaller than larger groups.

Proposition 4a predicts that the widening of the gap between the discontinuous graphs in Figure 2 will be more pronounced with smaller than larger groups. Proposition 4b suggests that the narrowing of the gap between the continuous graphs in Figure 2 will be more pronounced with smaller than larger groups. Propositions 4 has some empirical support. In a GSS environment, Dennis et al. (1990) and Valacich et al. (1992) found that the average group member generated more comments in larger than smaller groups. When members of larger groups are more willing to contribute, the probability of dominance by any individual group member is reduced.

The discussion of task type and group size assumes that other contextual variables, not discussed in this paper, are held constant. However, it should be noted that, in practice, contextual variables do not exert their effects singly and independently of each other. Rather, contextual variables exert their effects in combination to shape the overall context for group decision-making. This overall context, in turn, determines the potential strength of power distance effects and the potential impact of GSS intervention.

## Conclusion

Table 2 summarises the research propositions. It highlights the combination of variables that must be explored to test these propositions. Theoretically, the impact of a GSS can differ in high and low power distance countries. However, empirical GSS studies have largely ignored such cultural factors (Watson et al., 1994). Therefore, the extent to which the findings of these studies are generalisable across different countries is unknown. Researchers can shed light on this issue by replicating existing studies in a range of cultures to isolate universal from culturally-specific GSS impact. Future GSS studies can also administer instruments measuring cultural dimensions (Hofstede, 1980; Erez & Earley, 1987) to subjects to ascertain the cultural environment within which these studies are conducted. This will permit the results of these studies to be interpreted with cultural factors in mind.
