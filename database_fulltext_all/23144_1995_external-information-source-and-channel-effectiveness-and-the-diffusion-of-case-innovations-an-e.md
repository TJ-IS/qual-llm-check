# External information source and channel effectiveness and the diffusion of CASE innovations: an empirical study

A RAI

Department of Management and Pontikes Center for the Management of Information, College of Business and Administration, Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA

This paper examines the relationship between perceived ‘knowledge transfer’ effectiveness of different information channels for computer-aided software engineering (CASE) technology and CASE innovation diffusion. Specifically, the knowledge transfer effectiveness of seven information sources and channels is assessed. CASE innovation behaviour is modelled using three stages: initiation, adoption and implementation. Three types of CASE innovations are examined: two technical process innovations and one administrative innovation. Data for the empirical study were collected through a large-scale national survey of senior IS managers. A total of 405 usable responses were received. The perceived knowledge transfer effectiveness of the information sources and channels is associated differently with the initiation, adoption and implementation phases of the innovation process. Further, these associations are not uniform across the three categories of CASE innovations. The study has implications for how knowledge barriers can be alleviated during the CASE diffusion process.

## Introduction

CASE technology is receiving increasing attention since it is recognised as a possible means to enhance the productivity and performance of the software development process. It has been suggested that CASE can substantially affect the quality and productivity of systems work (Feuche, 1989; Martin, 1989; Forte & Norman, 1992). In a recent empirical investigation, Norman and Nunamaker (1988) found that software developers perceive that CASE tools improve productivity. Despite the touted advantages of CASE, the rate of diffusion of the technology in the market-place has been slow. This has been attributed to the substantial knowledge barriers associated with its adoption (Rai & Howard, 1994). If the touted benefits of CASE are to be realised, it is clearly important to provide mechanisms to lower such knowledge barriers and create a conducive environment to support the uptake of CASE innovations.

An information source has been defined as a medium in which knowledge/information is stored (Chakrabarti et al., 1993). The use of external information sources has been recognised as one means to lower knowledge barriers, thereby facilitating the diffusion of innovations (Utterback, 1971). However, little empirical research has been directed toward examining the impact of different information sources on the diffusion of software process innovations. Two notable exceptions were identified. First, Zmud (1983) examined the relationship between information sources and the diffusion of software practices. More recently, Nilakanta and Scamell (1990) studied the impact of information sources and communication channels on the diffusion of database design tools and techniques.

Given the lack of empirical research in the area, the present study addresses the relationship between IS management's perception of the knowledge transfer effectiveness of different external information sources and channels and CASE innovation behaviour. This question is important as considerable resources are typically expended to lower knowledge barriers to CASE technology in information system departments (ISD). Furthermore, there are several stories of failed CASE implementation efforts, perhaps because these knowledge barriers are not dealt with effectively during the CASE diffusion process.

In this study CASE technology is not treated in a monolithic sense and a distinction is made based on whether CASE tools are being applied to support logical (front-end) systems work, automate physical (back-end) systems work, or support the management of information system projects. The innovation process is modelled using a three-stage approach (initiation, adoption and implementation) as proposed by Thompson (1965). The seven information sources considered represent mass media, mediating institutions and external contacts. Our objective is to gain a better understanding of the relationship between the perceived effectiveness of these seven sources in lowering knowledge barriers about CASE technology and the initiation, adoption and implementation of the three classes of CASE innovations considered.

The primary research question addressed here then is:

What is the relationship between the perceived effectiveness of different external information sources and channels in lowering knowledge barriers to CASE technology and the initiation, adoption and implementation of logical support, physical automation and project management CASE tool innovations?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: (1) the rationale for adopting a process innovation approach to study the organizational diffusion of CASE technology is developed; (2) the research hypotheses are enumerated; (3) the data collection procedures, sample characteristics and measures used for each of the study variables are discussed; (4) an interpretation of the statistical results is presented; and (5) finally some concluding remarks are offered.

## CASE technology as a process innovation

While several definitions of CASE have been suggested, we adopt the definition of CASE put forward at the International Workshop on Computer-Aided Software Engineering as ‘tools and methods to support an engineering approach to software development at all stages of the process’ (Forte & Norman, 1992). Forte and Norman emphasise that CASE tools should support such a software engineering discipline irrespective of the specific task or notation. It is important to make a distinction between CASE as product and process innovations. From the perspective of the vendor community, CASE technology is a product innovation. However, software development groups in corporations use software engineering technology for the development of application systems. CASE tools are used as a means to create an application system – the product – and thus represent the process technology deployed for this purpose (Fichman & Kemerer, 1993).

In addition, past studies have emphasised the importance of carefully defining the focal target of analysis when studying complex organizational phenomena (Evan, 1966; Daft, 1978; Zmud, 1982). In our present context, it is important to make a distinction between technical and administrative process innovations. The former are targeted at the actual production work processes and the latter at the management of the work process. Zmud (1983) made a similar distinction while examining the impact of organizational factors on the diffusion of technical and administrative software innovations. In their study of the relationship between information sources and diffusion of database design tools, Nilakanta and Scamell (1990) identified two primary classes of tools: (1) requirements analysis and (2) logical database design. They argued that the tasks which these tools are applied to are rather different and possible associations with antecedent variables under investigation should accordingly be assessed separately.

The different categories of CASE innovations considered in the present study are shown in Table 1. Most modern software engineering innovations are targeted toward the technical processes of systems development (Zmud, 1982). However, recent CASE tools contain features to support project management (Minium, 1989). A distinction is thus made between CASE tools designed to support technical and administrative systems development processes. Further, two classes of technical process innovations are considered: (a) CASE tools that support the logical (front-end) development functions and (b) those that automate functions related to the physical construction (back-end) of system development. We briefly elaborate on this distinction.

Front-end CASE tools assist in logical activities (analysis and design) such as strategic systems planning, drawing data flow and entity–relationship diagrams, maintaining data dictionaries/repositories, designing reports and screens, prototyping and normalisation of data designs. These tools are designed to support the programmer/analyst in the semi-structured activities of planning, analysis and design. Back-end CASE tools can be used to restructure existing code, analyse programs and database structures, and generate test data and code/database schema. Such tools are specifically designed to automate structured activities during the construction phase of systems development. In addition to the two categories of technical process innovations, CASE innovation for a key administrative process, project management, is also considered.

## Focus of the study and hypotheses

Organizational innovation is often modelled as a stage-based process. The popular three-stage model (initiation, adoption and implementation) postulated by Thompson (1965) has been applied by researchers in a variety of disciplines including information systems. Initiation implies an effort to evaluate a possible solution for an existing problem; adoption of an innovation implies an organizational mandate for change; implementation implies that the innovation becomes part of routine behaviour.

The Thompson model is both conceptually simple and elegant. Further, it differentiates between adoption and post-adoption behaviour. This distinction is important as recent research indicates that post-adoption behaviour is explained better by political and social models while adoption behaviour may be explained better by rational models (Markus, 1983; Laudon, 1985; Orlikowski, 1993). The studies by Zmud (1982), Nilakanta and Scamell (1990) and Grover and Goslar (1993) employ a similar conceptual framework to model the innovation process of information technology. This three-phase approach is also used in the present study to model the diffusion of CASE innovations.

Classical diffusion theory emphasises the importance of the communication process in the innovation diffusion process (Rogers, 1983). In accordance with this theory, the individual adoption process unfolds as a series of stages which are influenced differentially by various information sources/channels. Fichman (1992) provides a good discussion of the problems in using classical diffusion theory directly to understand diffusion of information technology within organizations. Classical diffusion theory assumes voluntary adoption decisions by individuals whereas organizations may mandate adoption by their members. Furthermore, classical diffusion theory was framed and tested with innovations that imposed low knowledge burdens and adopter interdependencies. Innovations such as CASE may impose significant knowledge burdens and, given the collaborative nature of systems work, can require adopter interdependencies.

Some prior research has been directed to examine the relationship between external information sources and channels and the diffusion of software process innovations in organizations (Zmud, 1983; Hauptman, 1986; Nilakanta & Scamell, 1990). However, only Nilakanta and Scamell (1990) modelled the diffusion process as a series of stages. They specifically focused on the influence of different information sources/channels on the diffusion of radical and incremental database design technologies. Zmud (1983) limited his analysis to a higher level and investigated the association between different sources/channels and the overall usage of technical and administrative software practices.

Zmud (1983) found a positive association between mass media availability (e.g. journal subscriptions) and the usage of modern technical software practices. Availability of external contacts for individuals (workshops, professional meetings and tuition payments) were found to be ‘ineffective, at best’. In the Nilakanta and Scamell (1990) study, different sources were found to influence the diffusion of radical and incremental innovations, and ‘more sources’ were found to influence the diffusion of radical innovations. This is not surprising as radical innovations call for increased knowledge transfer into the organization.

Based on the above discussion, it is apparent that limited understanding exists of the relationships between information sources/channels and the diffusion of software development innovations. Managers should be able to invest in appropriate sources and channels which provide access to requisite knowledge for a given phase of the organizational innovation process for a given type of software process innovation. The issue becomes more complicated as managers may believe that adequate learning is taking place but such perceived learning may not be associated with desired innovation behaviour. It is this latter issue that is examined here.

Three categories of information sources and channels are considered: (1) mediating institutions (consultants and vendor support); (2) mass media (trade publications, video/audio tapes, text and reference books); and (3) channels designed to facilitate external contacts and scanning (seminars, product shows and contacts in other organizations). Attewell (1992) points out that mediating institutions have played an important role in diffusing innovations, especially when these innovations have reportedly low macro-adoption levels, as is the case with CASE technology today. Mass media can be a powerful vehicle to disseminate reports on experiences, product announcements, potentials and pitfalls, and can serve as a vehicle to trigger excitement and quell unfounded concerns about innovations. Access to mass media information is cheaper than other channels/sources considered here and information presented in such sources is typically not tailored to the specific context of individual organizations. Seminars and product shows can serve as signalling devices for new introductions and provide an opportunity for the vendor community to flaunt the relative advantage of their products. Informal information exchange with other organizations can provide a useful forum to share experiences/solutions.

This study focuses on each of the seven sources (as opposed to an aggregate category-level treatment) as each of these sources is quite distinct. A similar detailed approach of focusing on specific sources/channels as opposed to broader categories has been followed by the related studies conducted by Zmud (1982) and Nilakanta and Scamell (1990). Our theoretical framework should then shed more light on the relationship between the perceived knowledge transfer effectiveness of specific information channels/sources and the initiation, adoption and implementation of the three CASE innovations considered.

The hypotheses tested are stated in null form as:

H1: There is no significant relationship between the perceived effectiveness of information sources and channels in lowering knowledge barriers to CASE technology and the organizational initiation, adoption and implementation of logical support CASE innovations (for front-end system development activities).

H2: There is no significant relationship between the perceived effectiveness of information sources and channels in lowering knowledge barriers to CASE technology and the initiation, adoption and implementation of physical automation CASE innovations (for back-end system development activities).

H3 There is no significant relationship between the effectiveness of information sources and channels in lowering knowledge barriers to CASE technology and the initiation, adoption and implementation of project management CASE innovations.

## Research method

## Pilot study and pretest of measurement instrument

Thirteen senior IS managers from different organizations located in the north-eastern Ohio region were interviewed as part of a larger study on CASE innovation diffusion. A detailed questionnaire was developed using existing literature and interviews with the IS executives. A pretest of the survey instrument was conducted with a convenience sample of 21 senior IS managers (thirteen of whom were interviewed, plus eight others) of whom 14 completed the questionnaire and commented on the clarity of instructions and understandability of items. Based on feedback received, minor revisions were made to the instrument.

## Data collection

The mailing list used for the national survey was selected after an extensive search. Organizations included in the mailing list met at least one of the following three criteria: (1) were members of the Fortune 500 and the six non-industrial sectors; (2) had an MIS budget exceeding \$250,000; and (3) reported annual sales greater than \$50 million. This mailing list is updated by the vendor twice a year, thereby ensuring its currency. The mailing list includes addresses and titles of 34,581 IS executives across the USA. In the case of multiple individuals per site, the records of the most senior executive were retained. A random selection of 2,700 questionnaires was mailed out, with one questionnaire sent to each IS department.

A total of 405 usable responses were received, representing a response rate of 15%. The response rate was clearly lower than desired, probably for the following reasons. First, the survey instrument was fairly long, with over a hundred questions. Second, some IS managers who were not presently exploring or using CASE may have chosen not to respond to the survey. Third, despite assurances of anonymity for the respondents, some IS managers may have chosen not to share their experiences as they could perceive CASE innovation initiatives to be strategic. This remark was made by one of the individuals who returned the questionnaire unanswered.

Table 2 shows the industry composition of the mailing list and the responses received. A chi-square test indicated that there was no significant difference in the make-up of the original mailing list, sample selected and responses received by industry type ( $\alpha = 0.05$ ). As can be observed, the industry distribution shows a substantial representation from manufacturing (46.17%).

## Measures of CASE innovation

Data were collected regarding the six front-end logical support functions, six back-end physical automation functions and one administration function (project management) indicated in Table 1. Respondents were asked to indicate the extent to which each was being applied. The six possible responses employed (0 = CASE tool not used, 1 = a few people/projects experiment with the tool, 2 = a few people/projects use tool regularly, 3 = many people/projects use tool regularly, 4 = most people/projects use tool regularly, and 5 = CASE tool used on a routine basis) were identical to those used by Zmud (1982) and Nilakanta and Scamell (1990).

Bayer and Melone (1989) suggest that acquisition of an innovation is a reflection of an organizational innovation initiative. We applied a similar theme and used the degree of experimentation undertaken with the innovation to determine a measure of its initiation. The number of key development functions (in each category) for which CASE tools are being experimented with (corresponding to a numerical value of 1 on the scale) is then used as a measure of CASE initiation. Adoption was measured as the number of system functions for which CASE tools were being used by a few people/projects regularly (corresponding to a numerical value of 2 on the scale). The aggregate usage level of CASE tools beyond the adoption level (corresponding to a numerical value of 3, 4 or 5 on the scale) was employed as a measure of implementation.

Measures for initiation and adoption can range from 0 to 6 for both front- and back-end CASE innovations as six system functions are used for each of these innovation categories (in accordance with Table 1). A score of 0 indicates that the ISD has not initiated CASE innovation activity while a score of 6 reflects a high degree of innovation-initiation. A similar interpretation can be accorded to variations in the adoption scores. The measures for initiation and adoption of project management CASE innovations are dichotomous as one item is used.

The measures for implementation can range from 0 (no function beyond the adoption level) to 18 (CASE tool usage for all functions at routine level) for both front- and back-end CASE innovations. For example, an ISD with routine CASE usage for all six front-end functions is accorded a score of $6 \times (5 - 2) = 18$ while an ISD with a usage level of 3 on the scale for each of the six functions is accorded a score of $6 \times (3 - 2) = 6$ . The implementation score for project management CASE tools can range from 0 (usage at the adoption level but not greater) to 3 (routine usage).

Measures for information sources and control variables A possible shortcoming of the Nilakanta and Scamell (1990) study is that ‘information sources and channels were assessed as to their general usefulness, rather than their particular usefulness in communicating about innovations ...’ (Swanson et al., 1991). The items (questions/statements) in the present study were worded to focus attention specifically on CASE technology. Three categories of information sources and channels are considered: (1) mediating institutions (consultants and vendor support); (2) mass media (trade publications, video/audio tapes, text and reference books); and (3) channels designed to facilitate external contacts and scanning (seminars, product shows and contacts in other organizations). The items gauge the perceived role of these information sources and channels in lowering knowledge barriers related to CASE (Table 3). Methodology expertise (a control variable) was gauged using the two items shown in Table 4. Zmud (1982) emphasises the importance of controlling for key variables that influence compatibility of the innovation with the organization. In his study of the diffusion of software practices, Zmud controlled for professionalism as a means to account for the segment for whom the innovation was compatible. A similar approach is adopted here by controlling for methodology expertise as CASE tools can be viewed as methodology companions. Respondents were asked to answer each item pertaining to methodology expertise and information sources/channels on a 7-point scale. This scale ranged from (1) ‘strongly disagree’ to (4) ‘neutral’ to (7) ‘strongly agree’. The two items used to assess methodology expertise were factor analysed and loaded on the same factor. The scale reliability, as determined by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.821. The mean value of the two items was used as a measure for methodology expertise.

In addition to methodology expertise, two other control variables are considered in the present study: (1) time elapsed since CASE experimentation was initiated, and (2) ISD size. The above discussion highlights the importance of employing appropriate subsamples that minimise (or ideally eliminate) the natural correlation between the 'need-pull' for information assimilation, because of greater exposure to CASE tools, and the degree of innovation-initiation. Accordingly, the subsamples used to study the initiation process do not include adopters and implementors. Non-initiators are included and accorded a value of 0 for level of initiation. Their inclusion provides for a rich variation in values ranging from 'no initiation' to 'complete initiation' in the CASE initiation process. Using a similar rationale, the subsamples used to study the adoption process do not include implementors. Initiators are included and accorded a value of 0 for level of adoption. The values range then from 'no adoption' to 'full adoption'. Similarly, adopters are included in the subsamples used to study the implementation process and the values range from ‘no implementation’ to ‘complete implementation’. The subsamples then reflect logical groupings designed to capture appropriate variances in the initiation, adoption and implementation processes. ## Initiation phase

It is apparent from Table 9 that the perceived knowledge transfer effectiveness of four sources was positively associated with initiation of front-end CASE innovations. Seminars and product shows (Com 1), consultants (Com 2), vendor representatives (Com 4) and video/audio tapes (Com 5) appear to promote the initiation of such CASE tools. Learning attributed to video/audio tapes and contacts with programmer/analysts in other organizations (Com 6) was significantly associated with the initiation of CASE tools for back-end functions. Seminars and product shows and vendor representatives were significantly associated with the initiation of project management CASE tools.

These results suggest that mass media has a minimal 'signalling' effect on the relative advantage of the technology vis-à-vis other development methods. In this category, only video/audio tapes and the initiation of front-end and back-end CASE innovations emerged as significant. No significant associations were observed between two important mass media outlets, trade publications (Com 3) and text and reference books (Com 7), and CASE innovation initiation. It is possible that the content delivered by the mass media may not be signalling effectively the relative advantage of the technology over traditional and other emergent development technologies. It is further conceivable that IS management may be less likely to read 'bulky' books and other publications to identify possible enhancements to current development practices. It appears that mediating institutions and informal contacts are effective in stimulating innovation-initiation behaviour by promoting the acquisition of and experimentation with CASE tools. Such sources are ‘richer’ than mass media and may be more effective in reducing uncertainty and ambiguity among stakeholders and managers making investment decisions for technology exploration initiatives.

## Adoption phase

Significant positive relationships were observed between the adoption of front-end CASE tools and learning attributed to (1) consultants and (2) trade publications. Interestingly, trade publications were not found to be associated with the initiation of these tools. Programmers/analysts may have attitudinal barriers about the role of CASE technology in supporting logical tasks and mass media may be creating such necessary awareness.

Significant relationships were observed between adoption of project management CASE tools and both trade publications and video/audio tapes. In addition to these mass media outlets, learning attributed to vendor representatives was significantly associated with the adoption of project management tools. No significant relationships were observed between the seven information sources and the adoption of CASE innovations for back-end systems development tasks.

The significant positive relationships observed between innovation–adoption and learning attributed to consultants fits in well with the enabling role of mediating institutions as put forth by Attewell (1992). Overall, learning attributed to most information sources/channels was observed to have a limited association with adoption behaviour. This suggests that the nature of knowledge transferred from these sources may not be adequately meeting the actual challenges of this phase.

The positive associations between implementation of front-end CASE innovations and both off-site seminars and product shows is contrary to Zmud's (1983) finding that channels directed at increasing external contacts are ineffective in promoting innovation use. External contacts with programmers/analysts in other organizations also appear to be important in promoting the routinisation of back-end and project management CASE tools. No significant associations are observed between any of the mass media sources and implementation of the three CASE innovations.

Formal arrangements, especially consultant and vendor support, appear to play some role in enabling the adoption and implementation of CASE technology. Consultants seem effective in propagating the usage of front-end CASE tools. It could be argued that the deployment of these tools requires a business problem-solving orientation in addition to technical competence. The ‘rare event learning’ (due to multiple clients in a variety of contexts facing a variety of problems) that characterises the consulting profession may position them to resolve previously encountered problems more readily than if the user community was contending with such problems in isolation. Vendors, on the other hand, appear to be effective in promoting the diffusion of back-end CASE tools. Arguably, these products embody significant technical knowledge that needs to be shared/disseminated by the vendor community. Both consultants and vendors seem to promote usage levels of project management CASE tools.

## Concluding remarks

The results of this study should be generalised with caution. The low response rate raises issues about non-response bias. Replication of the study in different contexts should provide for a comparison of results and add to the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, variations within each of the three broad categories of CASE innovations are not considered. It is also conceivable that there was a ‘cycling’ effect in the responses of some of the IS managers. In other words, some respondents may ex poste attribute successful usage to learning achieved via the use of certain information sources/channels. The temporal nature of the innovation process was managed by carefully defining subsamples and applying appropriate statistical controls.

Learning attributed to mass media, with the exception of video/audio tapes, is not associated with CASE innovation-initiation. CASE initiation, in general, seems to be facilitated by mediating institutions and channels directed at increasing external contacts. Such sources seem to be playing a significant role in informing potential adopters about the relative advantage of using these innovations. Given the novelty of CASE, signalling information conveyed by these sources appears to be more effective than print-based media. Traditional print-based mass media may become effective as the adopter base of CASE increases and the technology matures. Learning due to both mediating institutions (consultants, vendors) and mass media (trade journals, video/audio tapes) is found to be positively associated with CASE adoption behaviour.

Institutionalising CASE requires that the technology be applied to projects undertaken earlier (from an enhancement or maintenance standpoint). Mediating institutions and external contacts may be appropriate mechanisms to alleviate knowledge barriers and other implementation hurdles such as interfacing existing mission-critical system environments and CASE-based development environments. These results are consistent with the issues raised by Attewell (1992) regarding knowledge transfer for the diffusion of complex technologies. Mediating institutions such as consultants can play an important role in propagating usage of CASE innovations by sharing their ‘rare event learning’ with user organizations. Organizations may need to supplement such knowledge with external contacts to enable a fruitful interchange of ideas, problems and solutions. Some organizations are taking bold initiatives in this regard. A utility company that the author is familiar with has organized a consortium of seventeen other similar firms to share experiences on CASE innovation efforts.

However, the associations between the perceived knowledge transfer effectiveness of the information sources and channels considered and adoption and implementation of CASE are both few and weak. The 'learning by using' concept put forward by Rosenberg (1982) and Attewell (1992) could be important for the diffusion of complex technologies such as CASE. All the knowledge needed for successful implementation is unlikely to be directly transferable. Rather, some of the knowledge may need to be created de novo by in situ experimentation in the specific context of the user organization. Orlikowski (1989, 1993) emphasizes the redefinition of social networks and relationships that CASE can bring about in organizations. This may be especially true in the later stages of the innovation process such as adoption and implementation (Laudon, 1985). It may therefore be useful to adopt a combined rational and political/social perspective to study the CASE innovation–diffusion process.
