# Operational, environmental and managerial factors in non-alignment of business strategies and IS strategies for the Police Service in England and Wales

JAA SILLINCE and CEB FROST

Management School, University of Sheffield, 9 Mappin Street, Sheffield S1 4DT, UK

The paper examines the operational, environmental (including political and European) and managerial imperatives and forces in understanding information systems (IS) strategy formulation within the Police Service. It shows the extent to which national bodies and national databases have proliferated without any framework, because of public dislike of a national police force. It also examines the political issues that have not yet been addressed by the IS strategy. It then places the police case within a wider context and suggests that factors present in the police case may exist in commercial organizations and that these factors would help to explain failed or delayed alignment between business strategies and IS strategies.

## Introduction

The alignment between business strategies and information systems (IS) strategies has been a critical issue in recent IS research (Kling, 1991; Kraemer, 1991). There are, however, very few empirical studies that provide evidence to support the need for alignment, nor does much evidence exist about how the two elements interact (Burn, 1994). Thus two important questions are whether alignment is necessary, and the nature of the relationship between business and IS strategies. This paper considers the case of strategy development within the Police Service of England and Wales, and attempts to find answers to both these questions. It is based on interviews carried out during 1993–94 at the Home Office, in the Police Department Science and Technology Group, in the Home Office/Treasury Study (HOTS) Information Management (IM) Strategy Group, in the Police Advisory Group (IT), in the Technical Policy Division (IT), in the Research and Planning Unit, and in South Yorkshire and Derbyshire Police Forces.

The paper first establishes the strategic significance of IS within the Police Service, using Earl's (1989) position framework in terms of purpose, scope and use. It next considers the problems that have motivated a police IS strategy, and the political considerations that constrain solutions to these problems. A comparison is then made between business strategy–IS strategy links for the police (non-aligned) and for UK primary care (aligned). It is suggested that the lack of conflict resolution which characterises non-alignment in this case may also be relevant to commercial organizations.

## The main organizations involved in the study

The Police Service of England and Wales comprises 43 independent police forces, controlled by elected local councillors who oversee operational, strategic and budgetary matters. Central government influence is exerted through the Home Office, a government ministry whose functional remit extends over police, courts, probation and prison services. Police investigations that culminate in criminal prosecutions are placed in the hands of the Crown Prosecution Service, which is responsible for processing each case and taking it to court. Much evaluative work on police effectiveness and efficiency has been done by the Home Office (which includes the Police Department Science and Technology Group, the Police Advisory Group (IT), the Technical Policy Division (IT) and the Research and Planning Unit). However, this has largely been qualitative, and a more focused and single-minded approach to evaluation has in recent years been taken by the Audit Commission, a quasi-governmental body responsible for undertaking and publishing managerial and financial audits of public and other organizations.

## Purpose of IS

The Police Service of England and Wales is fragmented into 43 independent police forces, each controlled by elected local councillors. Much of the pressure for a police IS strategy (arguments of cost effectiveness, rationalisation, coordination, benefits of data sharing) tend in the same direction as a national approach to police management. Because of the unpopularity of a national police force both with the public and with the police themselves, any operational need to develop rational and planned responses to external police bodies, such as Interpol, Europol and others, is affected by a marked ambivalence. Despite this, there are operational and career pressures on individual officers to cooperate nationally and internationally, and moreover specific successful cases of international police cooperation publicise the benefits of a national approach to police investigations.

A national IS strategy, however, has complex political implications, which are explored next. In England and Wales any public discussion of the possibility of a national police force has raised considerable public opposition (Oliver, 1987; Phillips, 1991). Even after the disturbances in major cities in 1981, when Home Office Circular 39/1982 was issued emphasising the need for police forces to monitor and anticipate such disturbances, there was no recommendation that police forces should pass on information to local or to central government.

Beyond the 43 independent police forces in England and Wales, there are units with non-local jurisdiction which could be regarded as the beginnings of a national police presence, for example Regional Crime Squads.

These have been set up to deal with situations where crime overlaps the borders of particular police authorities. Since 1992 there has also been established the National Criminal Intelligence Service (NCIS), which is responsible for the task of collecting, developing and analysing criminal intelligence from all forces in England and Wales, and for liaising with Customs' authorities and Interpol. NCIS possesses the National Intelligence Computer System, the most comprehensive computer network on serious crime in Britain. Although NCIS has no powers to direct either individual police forces or regional crime squads on operational matters, critics point to its duplication of regional crime squad activities, and this might suggest that further rationalisation may occur in the future. As it is, although non-routine command and control systems have existed for some time, many emergencies are reacted to on an ad hoc basis (for example, the CRISIS computer system developed after the Bradford fire disaster). The large number of national police bodies might be seen as evidence of a lack of determination to grasp the nettle of a national police force. Instead of one national body, a large number of institutions have been created with very specific functions and circumscribed powers. Seldom is the question asked as to the logic of all forces having research establishments, personnel departments, recruiting and training departments, etc., or of the potential savings of bringing these support arms together (Phillips, 1991). ## Scope of IS

IS applications that provide support for operational officers include command and control systems and databases of criminal records (see Figure 4). Of these systems, command and control systems are the ones most visible to the general public. These transactionbased systems enable police headquarters to maintain contact with, supervise and record the activities of police officers on patrol. Although this technology has existed since 1978 (see Figure 5) it is still subject to problems. A typical police force's command and control system may break down up to 15 times a day, and during those periods important events are not being logged. This has important and costly implications for reporting, as well as for loss of coordination.

## Use of IS

Several aspects of IS strategy have demonstrated the evolutionary nature of change in the use of police IT. In operations, areas for research and development have included decision support systems (DSS) and expert systems for processing and structuring criminal investigations. Typically, such systems make use of extensive databases of past cases to find common patterns and/or output a handful of cases for detectives to concentrate on (Fazlollahi & Gordon, 1993).

Change in IS usage is sometimes discontinuous, as when crises lead to shifts in spending priorities and changed policies. Riots, accidents and strikes have all left their lasting effects on police priorities and policies. Moreover, major crises in confidence regarding police procedures have created landmark legislation, which has had important implications for the collection and use of information. An example is provided by the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 which severely circumscribes the methods available to the police for detention and questioning, and for other types of data collection and recording. This Act has given rise to a decision support system in the shape of the Metropolitan Police's electronic reference book (PACE) which codifies such matters as whether fingerprints can lawfully be taken from a suspect while he is in prison, and procedures for stop and search. Development costs have been justified in terms of the avoidance of civil actions brought against the police over incorrect interpretation of the 1984 Act. The Act has forced considerable changes in police operating procedures, not least in opening up the backstage areas of policing by laying down recording requirements (Irving & McKenzie, 1988; Reiner, 1992) and in making forensic evidence and other information more necessary before embarking upon a criminal prosecution (Clarke, 1993).

Evolutionary change has been influenced by international, particularly European, institutional developments. One area where international cooperation is important is technology transfer and common requirements analysis for procurement. An example is provided by recent developments in computerised fingerprint systems. Such systems are most effective when all records that were previously paper-based have been computerised. Despite the installation of automatic fingerprint recognition (AFR) at the Scottish Criminal Records Office, a national ARF systems procurement policy for England and Wales was abandoned in 1992 by the Home Office, when it was discovered that commercially available systems were unable to handle large enough databases or to link them to criminal records systems. The US Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) also had this problem, and so the two organizations made a collaborative agreement to find a solution. This is expected to become available by 1997 (Anon., 1991a, 1991b).

## Problem 1: Too little control of too much data

Over time, the Home Office has come to demand more and more information from the 43 police forces of England and Wales. There has been little planning to this, such that there is considerable scope for improved efficiency in collecting, distributing and analysing management data within the Police Service. Currently, police forces are required to provide 97 different categories of statistical information for central government departments. These have increased incrementally and without any overall plan. The most costly of these relate to notifiable offences, road accidents, court proceedings, motoring offences, quality of service, prescription of controlled drugs, breath tests and training. A recent report recommended that these data should be standardised and controlled by a core set of specifications which would be regularly reviewed, and that eventually the Police Department Management Board should have responsibility for the overall selection and control of this large information flow and the criteria used for this selection should be published (Home Office, 1992a). One of the chief motivations for this report was an attempt to reduce the amount of information collected and thus to reduce costs. This move is analogous to the development in the NHS of data requirements models (such as the Minimum Data Sets Model) for each information user suggested in the Körner Report (Körner Committee, 1987).

As far as police forces are concerned, some statistics (crime, traffic, manpower, crime prevention and quality of service) are seen as more useful than others (vehicles, immigration, public order and police custody) (Home Office, 1992a). But also some element of standardisation to enable cross-force comparisons is also involved; for example, at present forces' financial accounts are based on different twelve-month periods. Also, it has been acknowledged that the use of electronic data interchange (EDI) throughout the policing system is necessary for the efficient distribution of data. Such a system might also include the criminal justice system, which accounts for a third of all police communications (Home Office, 1992b). Pilot studies are now being carried out in some police forces with the CREST system used for Crown Court proceedings, the MASS system used by magistrates courts and the SCOPE system used by the Crown Prosecution Service.

## Problem 2: Effectiveness of data

A particular criticism of the management of police information has been that the information collected has been inappropriate and should be directed more at current concerns such as performance measurement (Home Office, 1989). The Home Office (1992a) has been critical of the way in which such a vast amount of data is so little used, and has recommended that in some cases data collection be severely curtailed or less frequently undertaken, or that sample surveys replace comprehensive data collection. The Home Office has also decided that the database used by H.M. Inspectorate of Constabulary (HMIC), which includes a so-called Matrix of Indicators (measures of forces' efficiency and effectiveness as assessed by the HMIC's visits to forces), should serve as a model for a future management information system (MIS) for both the Police Department of the Home Office and for the HMIC. The Matrix of Indicators represents a starting point for the development of a common data set of evaluative measures for cross-service comparisons. Currently, the system suffers from a number of problems, and only a limited amount of data is input, with some topics not dealt with in sufficient detail. Some data are out of date and very few among the large number of potential users are allowed access to the system. Eventually, however, the proposed MIS could subsume many existing though unrelated police management databases. Besides the MIS, a new executive information system is also proposed, for bringing important information to the attention of the Police Department Management Board. The data exchange between the Police Department and the HMIC will, it is proposed, be enhanced by a new Consultancy And Review Unit (CARU). This new unit will be charged with responsibility for 'stimulating change within the police service', in particular with increasing the strategic nature of policy.

One of the many studies that has been suggested for CARU to undertake or supervise is the formulation of a Police Department/HMIC information strategy. Work on this strategy began in 1992 and is expected to be published in late 1994. The strategy study has undergone a burst of activity involving a large input from consultants and has been undertaken under the direction of an Assistant Chief Constable. The study has produced ten activities and deliverables, including business objectives, information modelling, applications portfolio, technical infrastructure, management issues and financial issues. One aim of this strategy will be the reduction of present information requirements, which have grown without supervision or control. Another will be the standardisation of equipment and software, to prevent ‘reinventing the wheel’, to cut costs (running at £150 million a year in total) and to enable interoperability between the systems used by different forces. The considerable diversity of current systems is the result of a period of IS expansion which had the advantage of encouraging innovation and of enabling forces to move at their own pace and in a way appropriate to their local area. However, eventually greater central coordination and control were seen to be necessary (Home Office, 1992b). Thus the IS strategy is one sign of the Police Service having reached the ‘control’ stage of organizational learning about IS (Gibson & Nolan, 1974).

## Problem 3: Difficult transfer of systems to other police forces

Another problem has been the difficulty of transferring new technology and institutional learning (such as how to combat a particular crime or criminal) from one force to another. In major police investigations, a command and control system known as HOLMES (Home Office Large Major Enquiry System) is used, but it has proved to be very difficult to reuse such that one force benefits from what has been learned by another (Bond, 1989). At the level of IS, there is the need for software standardisation, and the development of standards for ensuring portability and interoperability between different forces' systems.

## Problem 4: Need for standardisation and centralisation of specifications

Staff at the Police Requirements Support Branch of the Home Office are now turning to the task of developing core software specifications. An important role is played by the Home Office Scientific Research and Development Branch which carries out work on major new technological products for Police Service use. For example, it carries out feasibility and software development work for police units, and research and development, such as the application of knowledge-based and other AI technologies.

## Problem 5: Need for large national databases

The international dimension to crime and terrorism has given greater impetus to the need for large national facilities for information storage, retrieval and transfer. For example, the increasing international traffic in drugs, and the fact that since 1992 the most effective checks have been carried out at external European Union borders (Britain's rejection of Schengen – the agreement to use only external European Union borders for the purposes of border controls – notwithstanding) have led to the setting up of the National Drugs Intelligence Unit, with its own database, and also the Customs and Excise Departmental Reference Information Computer to deal with drugs traffic at an organizational level, rather than at the level of individuals.

## Issues restricting choice of IS strategy

Four issues have continued to dominate the political background to strategy formulation (see Figure 7). These are (1) whether policy should be top down or bottom up, (2) its correlated co-issue of the extent to which information should be qualitative or quantitative, (3) the extent to which the Police Service should be under political or professional control, and (4) the international dimension of police information strategy. On the first three issues, the government has taken a position but has been opposed by the Police Service.

Management by Objectives (MBO) was introduced into the Police Service as a way of identifying clear objectives, measuring performance and obtaining value for money (HMSO, 1982). It came after extensive evidence from the United States, which showed the value of such an approach as long as it was modified to the particular circumstances of police work (Lubans & Edgar, 1979). The aim of MBO was presumed to be to identify problems, set objectives, deploy resources and evaluate results. However, this required a large amount of information, which was mostly absent or in an inappropriate form in the Police Service at that time. This lack of information, together with the lack of a clear definition of what police work tries to achieve, has severely limited the effectiveness of MBO (Vecchio, 1991; Butler, 1992). Such difficulties prove a boon for suppliers of computerised management information systems; Kraemer and Kling (1988) found that government services in which efficiency was emphasised more than equity or effectiveness were more likely to be well-resourced for computing. Despite the fact that many organizations regard it as old-fashioned, MBO ideas continue to be used in Audit Commission advice on police resourcing. This lays down, in a top-down fashion, guidelines for effectiveness and efficiency, and encourages police authorities to use them to compete for extra resources (Audit Commission, 1990a, 1990b, 1991a, 1991b; Love, 1991).

An alternative view of MBO comes from a reading of the original definition (Drucker, 1974). Drucker emphasised that competition was undesirable in institutions like the Police Service. Competition would force police authorities to follow policies that would avoid controversy, avoid prioritisation and would not promote the abandoning of old, unnecessary activities. Many argue that this is what has happened. Moreover, a series of new initiatives that do not directly improve scores on performance indicators has diluted the police effort. These initiatives, mostly aimed at reassurance and thus at securing greater community support for the police, include increased cautioning of adult offenders (with implications for resources for recording), detoxification centres (to reduce the number of deaths by drunken people in police custody), the Scarman Principle of 1981 (putting good public relations above prosecution of minor offences), victim support schemes, community involvement and consultative committees (Hilton, 1987).

According to Drucker, the setting of objectives should be done in a bottom-up fashion by the lowest managers. To prevent their objectives from being completely over-ruled by their superiors, he suggested that there should be more managers than their superior could supervise in detail, forcing a significant element of managerial autonomy and delegation. A first stage might therefore involve each police manager setting just one objective for one key area for a period of six months; a tentative step enabling learning to occur without the cost of a massive investment in information systems (Panzarella, 1984). Involving lower level police managers should mean increased motivation because these managers were attempting to achieve goals set by themselves. Important motivational factors include a policeman's need for responsibility, challenging assignments and recognition of work well done. However, the current interpretation of MBO within the Police Service is rather different: superiors monitoring the performance of subordinates against rational criteria derived from goals. Such monitoring has a very different motivational effect from the one originally proposed by Drucker. The crucial point, however, from the standpoint of information systems, is that current top-down operationalisation of MBO has very large information system requirements, whereas Drucker's bottom-up concept of MBO requires much less information. Since most evaluation is done from one hierarchical level to the one above, much information can be 'soft'. The large information system requirements of the current implementation of MBO mean that, besides the direct cost of the new systems needed, a significant proportion of officers' time is spent on recording and documenting events (Roche, 1987; Fazlollahi & Gordon, 1993).

## Issue 2: Quantitative versus qualitative data

The difficulties of operationalising MBO partly centre around the debate about the sufficiency of quantitative performance indicators. The Home Office has taken a sceptical stance toward quantitative measures, whereas the National Audit Office has supported them. Not surprisingly, therefore, there has been criticism of National Audit Office (NAO) attempts to measure performance indicators. The NAO has suggested that despite increases in civilianisation and establishment, the number of operational patrol officers has not increased. Home Office representations to the 1991 Commons Public Accounts Committee admitted the validity of these criticisms, but stated that there was uncertainty as to whether deterrent levels and crimes averted could ever be measured (Anon., 1991c). The NAO would like the Home Office to be more systematic in its collection of quantitative management information from individual forces, whereas the Home Office has traditionally valued qualitative data more highly. Also tied to the NAO view is one that sees a strategy formulation role related to a more critical and rigorous quality control role for HM Inspectorate.

Nevertheless, the Home Office has been deeply involved in exhorting police forces to engage in managerial and administrative modernisation (Sinclair & Miller, 1984) and a circular on the subject appeared as early as 1983 (Home Office, 1983). This process parallels, but at a slower rate, efforts made elsewhere since the 1980s to modernise the public service and public utilities (Stewart & Clarke, 1987; Taylor & Williams, 1989). This modernisation process is deeply integrated with the introduction and use of information systems and computer networks. Some have argued that this has given rise to a new form of public administration called ‘information polity’ (Taylor & Williams, 1990), one based not so much on administration (for example, allocation of scarce resources based upon rules of fairness and need) than upon collection of performance indicators which are then used in budgetary processes to reward good performance.

The 1983 Home Office circular indicated a reluctance to increase any force's establishment unless the HM Inspectorate could give that force a clean bill of health on effectiveness and efficiency grounds. These grounds included civilianisation. It was followed by the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984, which stressed similar constraints upon police procedures. The combined effect of these measures was to encourage police forces to improve their strategic planning arrangements (Popham, 1989).

As a response to the difficulties of defining and measuring performance, several other developments have occurred. A Home Office Financial Information Systems working group was set up to develop ways HM Inspectors could evaluate the quantity of resources used for particular areas and particular outputs, at an organizational level (Sinclair & Miller, 1984). Another development has been the investigation by twelve forces over ten years of Planning, Programming and Budgeting, a management method for using output measures for assessing police efficiency (Southgate, 1984). The investigation found it impossible to establish appropriate measures and so was abandoned, with the recommendation that surveys should have greater prominence in future for assessing effectiveness and efficiency of police work.

The Home Office's view has traditionally been a pragmatic one of not relying upon quantitative input-output ratios (Sinclair & Miller, 1984). Yet, the speed with which police forces have committed themselves to some form of MBO ran against all Home Office advice and pressure. The irony is that these measures had been developed as a response to the Home Office's own Circular 114/1983.

The debate between the NAO and the Home Office was brought to an end by the 1991 House of Commons Public Accounts Committee, which supported the National Audit Office (HMSO, 1992), in favour of some quantitative measures of police service performance. This conformed with prevailing government policy (HMSO, 1991). For example, all police forces are now expected to set and publish target times for answering telephone calls and arriving at the scene of serious accidents. Achievement of targets is monitored by annual inspections. Other targets are expected but have so far not been specified.

## Issue 3: Political versus professional control

However the quality of service is monitored, an important question is whether police service control should be in the hands of politicians (for example, the Home Office and local authorities as now) or in the hands of local chief constables, with public control some way off in Westminster and Whitehall. The 1964 Police Act provides an ambiguous relationship between police authorities and chief constables, which in practice varies considerably from one authority to another.

Some have argued for more professional control (Guy, 1992). One argument in favour of professional control maintains that public opinion favours more general police patrolling; a more visible police presence, despite evidence that general patrol is ineffective (Eck, 1981). This refers to a management trade-off between patrol and investigation. The argument runs that public control leads to an emphasis on public reassurance, with little effect on detecting or deterring crime.

The question of public versus professional control is important because it determines the kinds of information relevant to monitoring Police Service quality. Public opinion is shaped disproportionately by crime figures, which say little about demands made on police resources. In fact, only about a quarter of police resources is devoted to ‘notifiable’ crimes. A larger proportion is devoted indirectly to crime: crime prevention, traffic accidents, reassurance patrols and much work that has a social services flavour. Thus performance measures are likely to be more useful the more specific they are; for example, the uptake of police advice to prevent burglary and the speed of response to emergency calls (Keogh, 1990). These indicators, and others such as percentage of time beat-force vehicles spend on the road, and classroom occupancy rates in training schools, vary hugely between forces (Guy, 1992). The usefulness of such detailed measures tends to be overlooked when the public’s emotions of fear or vengeance are excited by ‘law and order’ issues. Nevertheless, it may be that certain quantitative indicators are chosen because they are easy to measure rather than because they measure something important. However, it is not true that quantitative, IT-supported measures enjoy a natural supremacy over non-quantitative ones as has been suggested (Pfeffer, 1978); the decision of whether to use quantitative measures will always be a political one.

Financial control of police forces has until now been a matter for both central and local government. The Revenue Support Grant is distributed according to police officer establishment with no allowance for civilianisation, despite the savings that civilianisation represents; and a force cannot restructure, for example, by substituting two constables for one chief superintendent. The rigid controls on capital costs make it difficult to deal with buildings and vehicles on anything other than an arrangement favouring leasing and other revenue-intensive solutions. Financial control has also been exerted by local police authorities. A criticism made by some is that this control is too great, with the result, it is argued, that officers care less about effectiveness of spending (outputs) than about keeping to budget (inputs). Devolving financial accountability to police commanders, so one argument goes, together with detailed performance indicators, will enable costs and value-added to be attributed more accurately to specific cost centres. At present, however, much of this Audit Commission inspired pressure has emphasised costs and has under-emphasised value-added and quality of service. This enabled the police service to take the initiative by publishing its own commitment to setting standards (ACPO, 1990).

Whether the public allows devolved control to police forces depends upon how politicians handle crime issues, and upon the public presentation of these new, detailed performance measures. For example, persuading the public that general patrol, despite being reassuring, is not cost effective, will never be easy. One method may be to divide up the public overview role; for example, two different authorities for prevention and detection (Loveday, 1992). Another example is provided by the growth of public surveys during the 1980s which ask specific questions of a factual nature unlikely to arouse fears, which measure whether police services meet community expectations, and which have shown both under and over provision of police services (Butler, 1992; Taylor & Williams, 1992). Such surveys are a nice way of squaring the circle of using measurement but avoiding an over-reliance on quantifiable figures. But, according to Drucker (1974), such surveys inevitably lead to overspending on less effective areas (patrols) and underspending on more effective areas (investigations).

## Issue 4: The international interface

It has been argued (Imbert, 1989) that at the very least some form of national strategy is needed for deciding how to cope with international agreements and structures, and for preventing fragmentation of police effort. Areas of police work where such international agreements would prove useful include standardisation of computing, telecommunications, and fingerprint and DNA recognition methods. For example, the lack of any national policy in the past has meant that, in the Scotland Yard Special Operations Department, the main conduit of direct enquiries from overseas police forces, there has been no single individual with a national brief, and no national strategy or goals, so that evaluation of results of any international cooperation has been difficult.

Although Britain has excluded itself from Schengen, Britain is part of a projected European Information System (EIS), and will therefore be able to share criminal intelligence on drugs, money laundering, fraud and stolen property with her European neighbours. It is also part of the TREVI and Council of Europe

Pompidou Groups for international police coordination. TREVI involves exchange of liaison officers, the EIS, and technical and scientific support (Anderson, 1992).

Europol, of which Britain is a member, is a European Union institution which came into being at the 1991 Maastricht European Summit. It aims to act as an information exchange for the purposes of preventing and fighting terrorism, drug trafficking and other forms of serious international crime. It does not, however, have its own operational policing capacity – it merely liaises with and informs national police forces (paralleling NCIS's lack of operational capacity at the national level of England and Wales). Thus Europol is a hybrid institution, deriving its authority from individual member states rather than from any operational, self-directed task. Moreover, because Europe does not function as one political entity, and because police forces are bound up with the concept of nation states, Europol will act as a barometer of federalist pressure within Europe and within Britain (Walker, 1992; Boer & Walker, 1993).

## Analytical findings

The above case suggests the following:

Finding 1: Operational, political, European and managerial considerations are important in considering business and IS strategy within a public service organization

To what extent are these patterns and pressures present in IS strategies in other organizations? To answer this we will compare police and UK primary care.

In April 1991 the first wave of GP practices became fundholders, running their own budgets and becoming ‘purchasers’ of medical services available from ‘providers’ such as hospitals. Purchasers were intended to have information on costs, price, outcomes and quality of services, so that they could buy from providers offering the best value for money. Information and IS were to be crucial to the whole strategy of turning the NHS into a market system. There is clear evidence for at least the first three years of fundholding that this crucially important IS element was poorly developed and poorly supported (Frost & Sillince, 1993; Sillince & Frost, 1994a, 1994b). The reforms in primary care were pushed through regardless of the rather primitive IS, IT and data infrastructure that then existed. Reforms were undertaken at breakneck speed for political reasons (to head off political opposition by the medical profession and to show that something could be done within a short period of time), without waiting for IS and IT to catch up. Nevertheless, the reforms were successful in the government’s terms. GPs learned by doing things hands-on in terms of auditing and budgetary control of their spending (bringing prescribing costs down was one of the political motives for the reform) and the principle of Family Health Service Authorities' overview of GP spending (by persuasion rather than by sanction) was thereby reinforced.

However, this was not the case with police information strategies. In this case, the business strategies of change have been less clear-cut in political terms; government has been ambivalent about them. While the model of police forces competing for scarce resources on the basis of quantitative performance indicators was an attractive one for the government, qualitative measures have at least the advantage of flexibility. Again, for the government unequivocally to choose political control of the police (it cannot choose local political control, which led to underfinancing by hostile local politicians in some Labour local authorities) meant that such control would have to be from Westminster and Whitehall. This would mean that mishandling just one problem such as a major strike would lead not only to accusations of incompetence but also to charges of over-control and political interference. And finally, the issue of a national police force has been one which the government has understandably avoided, bearing in mind public opinion. So police goals are not at all clear.

Within the Police Service, IS strategy has concentrated on limited and technological issues (see Figure 6). The business strategies have safely been decoupled from any ultimate political motive. Thus the strategy aims to increase efficiency in the use and distribution of data, and to coordinate sub-strategies. Awkward questions of what kind of data should be used and how it should be controlled have been avoided. Technology has been substituted for ethical choice and responsibility (Ellul, 1980; Dunlop & Kling, 1991). This means that many questions (see Figure 9, for example) remain unanswered.

Finding 2: One of the important differences between the primary care and police cases in that, in the case of primary care, business strategies determined IS strategy, while in the case of the police, they did not

Hypothesis 1: Lack of clarity of goals may lead to non-alignment of business and IS strategies

Figure 9 shows that many of the elements of IS strategy pre-dated the policy statements. Only the core specifications and requirements element was new, and this was derived from technological motives of efficiency rather than business strategies emphasising effectiveness. The Matrix of Indicators had previously been developed by HM Inspector of Constabulary, PNC had been around since 1974 and the European Information System was a product of TREVI, a European collaboration. The police IS strategy fits

Mintzberg's (1978) characterisation of much strategy as a ‘pattern in a stream of decisions’, that is, as a rationalisation and composition of a series of unrelated decisions into an intentionally meaningful whole after the event. So, IS strategy is not always determined by business strategies. Police IS strategy conformed to Mintzberg and Waters’ (1985) typification as ‘process strategy’, whereby it was the process of making the strategy (coordination, information passing, protocols for communication) rather than its content which was important, in contrast to primary care, where there was an ‘imposed strategy’ from outside the primary care system. In the case of primary care, IS was one important element in a much broader change – a move to the ‘internal market’ in the NHS – so that this broader context of change could be used as a source of legitimacy and justification for IS change (Pettigrew, 1987).

Figure 8 shows a model of the integration of business strategies and information strategies within commercial organizations. Comparison with Figure 9 shows that the business strategies of the Police Service are far less tangible or single dimensional than in the commercial model. Police objectives are more politically coloured and consequently less easily evaluated. Whereas the link in Figure 8 is clear, the business strategy–IS strategy link in Figure 9 is less obvious, apart from reference to necessary MIS and database development. In fact, the linkage between business strategy and IS strategy is a mixed one. This can be seen by considering Earl's (1989) three strategy drivers. The police business strategy of more efficient data and IS processes (with an IS strategy expression in core requirements) is clearly technology-led, and the strategy of top-down performance evaluation and more central political control (with their IS strategy expression in a Matrix of Indicators) is clearly business-led. The other police goals are opportunity-led. PNC and EIS additions and upgrades increase the control by the centre in an opportunistic way, without unnecessary public statements of government commitment to a national police force. These opportunistic elements of strategy conform to Quinn's (1980) 'logical incrementalism' where specific commitments are made as late as possible. Murray (1989) similarly characterises IS strategy in two UK financial services organizations not as the 'unfolding of a grand rational plan' but as 'barely disguised incrementalism'.

Hypothesis 2: Goals that are less tangible, multi-dimensional, politically coloured, or difficult to evaluate, may lead to non-alignment of business and IS strategies

Hypothesis 3: Opportunity-led elements of business strategy may lead to non-alignment of business and IS strategies

Rational accounts in the literature of strategy formulation emphasise top-down processes, and the relevance of opportunities, threats, strengths and weaknesses for strategy (Porter, 1980). On the other hand, behavioural accounts give more emphasis to bottom-up processes, whether from specific business functions such as marketing or accounting, or from lower organizational levels (Hill, 1985). In the context of the Police Service, top-down processes originate from the government, whereas bottom-up processes originate from police professionals. The political origins of each of the five business strategies in Figure 9 are different, and there is no simple division between top-down or bottom-up, as Figure 10 makes clear. Again, therefore, police IS strategy formulation was not a simple consensual process and so the impact of business strategies was muted by conflict.

Hypothesis 4: Bottom-up elements of business strategy, together with conflict, may lead to non-alignment of business strategies and IS strategies

Evaluation of alignment has become a subject of increasing interest (Henderson, 1990; Luftman et al., 1993; Sambamurthy et al., 1993). Recent empirical work has focused on the role that IS plays within organizations and the interaction between IS and organizational change (Zmud et al., 1987; Galliers & Sutherland, 1991; Burn, 1993; Sambamurthy et al., 1993; Thurlby, 1993; Premkumar & King, 1992). Burn (1994) used a five-year longitudinal study to relate strategic types of organization (Miles & Snow, 1978) to dependency types of organization (Earl, 1989). She showed that there was a lag in IS and business strategy alignment.

In the case of public service organizations, it is understandable that particular dysfunctions may be allowed to occur for political reasons. We have shown above that a police IS strategy was not aligned with the business goals or business strategies of the Police Service. Politicians do not want to be saddled with a reputation for having created a national police force, and many do not want to be remembered as having reinforced European federalism. So a plausible case can be made for explaining and even justifying non-alignment of IS and business strategies under certain circumstances in such organizations.

## Hypothesis 5: Non-alignment of business and IS strategies may be deliberately engineered

What about commercial organizations? We would argue that even in commercial organizations such influences on alignment are important. In boards where no consensus exists, in pre-crisis periods (Pettigrew, 1985), in organizations where considerable interdepartmental conflict exists and/or where goal conflicts have not been clearly resolved by senior management, such non-alignment or delayed alignment would be more likely.

In a study of two UK financial services organizations, Murray (1989) suggested that:

a large systems development project requires meticulous and flexible planning and co-ordination. Temporal and financial budget overruns can have major knock-on effects into other areas of strategy. Project teams and committees become an arena for organisational politics where divisional antagonisms and competition can surface with surprising vigour. Senior and middle managers may also spend vast quantities of energy covering up for divergencies from the espoused strategy and shifting blame for shortcomings onto other divisions.

Moreover, significant reinterpretation of strategic goals and plans can take place between the various parts of a systems development project, so that a goal takes on a new meaning depending on whether it is being considered by the requirements team or by the design team. The continuous process of estimating resources needed for the project is subjected to unconscious or deliberate manipulation. There are tensions between ‘business needs’ staff and IS staff as each group tries to gain supremacy. This culture gap between business and IS staff was identified as the main problem of IS in business by a recent Price Waterhouse survey of 5,000 IT executives (Grindley, 1991).

## Summary

This paper has shown that national bodies and national databases have proliferated without any national framework, because of public dislike of a national police force. The main business issues of police IS receive only ambivalent support from government, and some are the centre of controversy (political control of the police, a national force, competition between forces for extra resources). Police IS strategy has been decoupled from any political concerns (thus business and IS strategies are not aligned), rendering the strategy ‘safe’ but leaving many questions unanswered. Factors present in the police case may exist in commercial organizations and would help to explain failed or delayed alignment between business and IS strategies.
