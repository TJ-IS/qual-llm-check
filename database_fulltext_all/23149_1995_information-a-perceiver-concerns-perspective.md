# Information: a perceiver-concerns perspective

M. METCALFE $^{1}$ and P. POWELL $^{2}$

$^{1}$ Department of Commerce, Adelaide University, Adelaide, South Australia 5005 and $^{2}$ Information Systems Research Unit, Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

The transmission perspective on information which has dominated information systems (IS) development is only one way of perceiving information. Many of the issues of information systems development of recent years may be better understood if other, alternative, perspectives are encouraged. This paper attempts to synthesise the arguments for an alternative perspective which may be particularly useful for setting information system design attitudes. The paper concludes that a perceiver-concerns perspective can be beneficial in defining information since a concerned perceiver has to be present for information to exist, and the paper considers the implications of this for IS.

## Introduction

Although information systems (IS) development and research continues apace, the investigation of the nature of information has attracted much less interest. This is curious, since the major goal of information systems is information provision and it would seem pertinent to clarify the nature of information before attempting to design systems to provide it. One part of this paradox is explained by the fact that there are commonly held views as to the nature of information but, it is argued here, this is a restrictive rather than an emancipatory situation. A second element might be that there is a view that the debate on information is somehow closed, that a definition or solution exists and hence it is not on the list of current concerns. The aim of this paper is to open up the issue of the nature of information and hopefully stimulate a debate which can only be of benefit to IS provision.

Current information systems literature contains little on the nature of information. Despite Stamper's (1973) enlightening work and the occasional contribution (for example, Liebenau & Backhouse, 1990) current IS issues do not include information and its origins. Though the lack of an analytic rather than an operational definition prevents the risk of premature closure of any debate on information, the debate is not really continuous. In 1987 Galliers pointed out that 'our understanding of information technology has continued to outstrip our understanding of information' (p. vii). Again Galliers (1993), reviewing the UK IS Doctoral Colloquium, does not highlight information as an explicit research area, identifying most IS research to be in decision support systems, artificial intelligence, database management systems, management support systems development, end-user computing and human factors (though this list does present a denotation-of-sorts for information). The lack of clarity on information exists despite the proliferation of texts on information systems. Though research on the nature of information has been undertaken in other fields, this has not permeated most IS work. Concurrently, Avison and Myers (1994) lament the problems of multi-disciplinary subjects such as IS: they see IS as uncritically borrowing too many concepts from other disciplines. Yet, if there is one area that IS should claim as its own, it is information. However, Fox (1983) comments that definitions of the word information 'have been numerous, old fashioned, rudimentary and unsuccessful. Information science lacks an accepted characterisation of the nature of information'. Wellisch (1972), Belkin (1978) and, more recently, Stonier (1991) concur. In part, this stems from a lack of recognition that the nature of information is not fully established, consequently the information systems literature has been dominated by a single metaphor, that of transmission as developed by Shannon and Weaver (1949). The rapid development of technology coupled with the affinity of system analysts for the hard sciences may have weakened the pro-active search for a more meaningful perspective of the nature of information.

The task of understanding the nature of information clearly is not trivial. Ravault (1987) argues that the scientism ideology of the, so-called, information age is totally dependent on the dominant perspective of information. Scientism implies a belief in the so-called scientific method of research and a concomitant belief that all issues of research can only be solved by the use of experimentation. Ravault argues (p. 183) against this ideology which ‘is presented as a period spawned by the technological society when it attempted to apply technologies developed in the hard sciences to the human fields of information and intelligence’. In other words, the information age consists of the application of computing and other telecommunications technologies to human affairs in order to make faster, better, and more efficient decisions, which, thus far, have had a strong tendency to escape the grasp of science and technology. This new scientism or neopositivism is presented by Kuhn (1970) and by most promoters of the information age as ‘the ultimate solution’, which will extricate us and, eventually, the entire world from ‘the crisis into which we are sinking’. The limitations to this ‘solution’ can be understood better if people and social constructs are re-introduced to the perspective of information. Ravault goes on to argue that: ‘The conflation of concepts and terminology necessary to argue for this invasion of human phenomena by technologies and theories belonging to the natural sciences obscures crucial differences. Paradoxically, it is, again, the lack of scrupulous understanding of key concepts such as communications and information that condemns the information age panacea to certain failure’ (p. 183). Though human and organizational issues are at the forefront of current IS research, the focus is more on the system rather than on the information.

This paper re-emphasises the need to include people in any perspective on information (Boland, 1986). It thus provides an alternative language on which to base a different social construct of ‘information’; that is, it argues for another metaphor to stand beside the dominant transmission one. It is not argued here that the communications view of the world is incorrect, but rather that the world and therefore its social constructs are many things at the same time. This paper attempts to develop understanding of information by emphasising a perceiver-concerns perspective. It could be argued that the transmission metaphor is more applicable to closed systems than to information as a facet of human wisdom. In addition to providing an alternative paradigm, the perceiver-concerns perspective presents a different view of the information age, perhaps resulting in more effective information management. Such management includes the provision of a fresh understanding of issues including the relationship between power and information, the value of information and privacy.

This paper distinguishes between information and meaning. Many of the scientism definitions of information (for instance, Stonier, 1991) suggest that attempts to bring humans into the definition of information result in a confusion between meaning and information. The terms are different, but both are dependent on the presence of a person, as discussed later. There is no actual confusion. Rather, it is the scientism approach which is confusing the terms data and information. The usual definition of information in terms of structured data or knowledge achieves little (Fox, 1983); the discussion merely moves to defining data or knowledge. While ‘for the information engineer a “bit” of information is a “bit” regardless of the time of day or the number of times it is used’ (Thayer, 1972, p. 19), for the information manager the context, including timing, may be crucial. Thus, information is a function of the recipient.

The word ‘message’ similarly requires explanation. Message is used here as the possible carrier of information. Information may determine the pattern or form (Young, 1987) of the carrying message but it is something different from the pattern or form. Messages are assumed to include all signs used to communicate, such as language, chemical emissions, gestures, dress, metaphors, mathematics and the written word. Therefore, both non-verbal and verbal signs are possible forms of a message. Communication carries both message and tone, and both affect meaning. Thus communication is the transfer of meanings, feelings and tone from one person to another where the communicator seeks to transfer such. In a computer-based information system, the message only contains data. In a human system a message may, or may not, contain information.

A popular scientism approach to explaining information is to invoke the communications theory image (Shannon & Weaver, 1949) of a transmission. Shannon and Weaver define information as 'only a measure of the difficulty in transmitting the sequences produced from some information source'. However, they were not as interested in the nature of information as in overcoming the technical problems in transmitting messages. Their approach results in language such as 'switches', 'transmissions' and 'emitters' being used. For an electronic communications system this may well be a very useful perspective. Checkland (1981) provides a critique of the transmission metaphor of information which focuses on the neglect of message content. People are not 'emitters' or 'receivers', so attempts to understand human wisdom and the informational content people give to messages may be better served by an alternative perspective.

Hence, it may be more useful to discuss language and human concerns (or interests) when studying issues relevant to people. Shannon and Weaver's machine-type metaphor is used to describe a human activity. The general problem of using machine metaphors to understand social systems is emerging as an issue in organizational theory (Burrell & Morgan, 1979), in accounting (Chua, 1986; Morgan, 1988), in biology (Maturana & Varela, 1987), philosophy (Fox, 1983; Rorty, 1987), semiotics (Sless, 1986) and in the library science literature (Wilson, 1977). All these disciplines stress the need to consider language and concerns when trying to understand social constructs. The work done in these areas might fruitfully be combined and generalised to provide an alternative perspective on the nature of information which gives insight to IS developers.

The principal argument against using machine metaphors in understanding human behaviour is simply that humans are not machine-like. People have intrinsic and extrinsic motivations or concerns (interests). They may not perform in exactly the same way twice. They are adaptive with flawed rationality, capable of attempting the same task in different ways. They respond differently to repeated experiences. They learn and create their reality from images bound in language (Ricoeur, 1978). These attributes are not the images created when talking about machines.

## The nature of information?

A metaphoric approach to understanding the meaning of words (MacCormac, 1976) is to consider the use of a word when it was still recognised as a metaphor. The word information is derived from the Latin verb informare, to give shape (or form) to. The Oxford English Dictionary reports examples of the word 'information' used in the 14th century in the same way as today. Originally someone may have used the verb 'give shape to' as a metaphor when trying to explain the informational content of a message. This suggests that people give shape (or form) to a message; that is, people 'in-form' using the message. A single person and a message are present. The transmission metaphor, however, implies that information is passing between two people. The different emphasis is important. It is argued later that it is perhaps better not to think of information as something that can be transferred (Pratt, 1977).

Fox (1983) considers the attributes of information using a technique of conceptual analysis from linguistic philosophy. The technique examines words through sentence construction, employing a lay-person's use of the term information within some language game (Wittgenstein, 1958). It adopts the premise that the use of words in a particular manner is not mere semantics but also a reflection of the image of the word held by the user. Fox extensively reviews the definitions of information and provides an exhaustive decomposition of these; a brief discussion of his conclusions is presented here. Note that although Fox quotes the Oxford Dictionary as having declared that the meaning of information 'to give shape to' is obsolete, his work is, largely, aimed at addressing this very question.

First, Fox concludes that the information carried in a sentence is largely independent of the carrying sentence, that is, it depends on the language game being played. This is best demonstrated by observing that cynical comments are not taken literally (or, at least, should not be). Conversely, it is possible for the same information to be present in different messages. Generally, this does not appear to be a contentious issue, although Belkin and Robertson (1976) use the phrase ‘information is the structure of any text’. This suggests that if the word order in a sentence were rearranged, the information in it must change. However, Belkin and Robertson are more concerned with defining information science than information.

Second, Fox argues that information is not a process (a spatio-temporal entity); rather, the process is data generation and diffusion. For example, Fox disagrees with Wersig and Neveling (1975) when they state 'information . . . is a process that . . .'. As part of his argument, Fox comments that if information were a process, the sentence 'She was interrupted in the middle of the information' would not be considered poor English. He feels that a great deal of confusion has been caused by not clearly distinguishing the process of informing from the information contained in a message. The transmission metaphor does not help with this distinction. There is nothing wrong with the message 'She was interrupted in the middle of receiving the information'. 'Receiving the information' is part of the communication process. Few IS researchers have taken up this distinction; for example, Blumenthal (1969) defines information using terms like 'recorded, classified, organised, related and interpreted'. This is a better definition of the process of communication than of information. Pratt (1977) makes the distinction and provides a definition, 'information is the name of a class of events, like the word explosion . . . explosions cannot be stored or retrieved'. However, Fox takes issue with Pratt's use of the word 'event'. He believes Pratt is confusing information with 'an informative event'.

Fox points out that use of the transmission metaphor can, paradoxically, lead to information being defined as both a product or commodity and a process. Both definitions are misleading, the latter more so. That information is a product suggests that information can be independent of the sender and perceiver – that people do not have to be present. Much of the traditional economics literature supports this perspective of information as a product, though it might be argued that all products are society-defined and, therefore, require perceivers, or that anything for which there is a market is, by definition, a product. Again this, though, suggests that ‘data’ is being confused with ‘information’. However, accepting this use of the term ‘information’, it is a very strange product: it can be sold, yet retained; it can be used and re-sold in perfect condition; it might depreciate instantly. A second piece of information can make the first priceless or worthless. The vendor often does not know which, as it is very hard to know if the buyer wants some information or what use will be made of it. It is time-dependent: if delivered too early or too late its value changes significantly. Though some of these apply to other products, there is little doubt that if information is a product, it is a very odd one. If one insists on the need for a person or buyer to be present for information to exist, then the perception of information as a product becomes less tolerable.

Wersig and Neveling's (1975) definition of information includes: '[it] is a process that occurs within the human mind when a problem and data useful for its solution are brought into productive union'. They therefore feel a person or, at least, a mind must be present for information to exist. It may be useful to make a distinction here between potential information and actual information; for the former, no one need necessarily be present but, for the latter, someone must. Pratt (1977), Belkin & Robertson (1976) and Boulding (1956) concur with Wersig and Neveling. For example, Belkin and Robertson suggest that information 'transforms the structure of the mind'. The use of the term 'structure' may be too strong, unless structure includes weightings for relative importance and changes in confidence levels. Fox, however, argues that minds do not have to be present. He uses the example of a robot spacecraft collecting samples on a distant planet. He points out that in everyday speech 'the craft' could be said to contain information about the chemical content of the distant planet, even if the craft were destroyed before communicating with earth. Did it hold information, even though that information did not pass through anyone's mind? Though, of course, if machines have minds, in that they can perceive events, then it could be argued that information was present.

It is argued later that the message must be received by someone for actual information to be realised. That is, the sentence ‘the information was lost when it crashed’ carries the implicit assumption that there would have been information if someone had seen the contents. However, Fox’s arguments are really directed at the distinction between communication and information rather than the presence of a mind. His assumption is of the presence of two minds for communication. So, for instance, he uses the example of writing in a private diary, arguing that information exists without communication. While there may not be communication when there is information present, there is at least one mind present. It might be that communication acts could be deemed to involve communication with oneself or with an imaginary person. This paper, therefore, takes the view that at least one perceiver’s mind does need to be present for information to be perceived. This is, however, only a necessary, not sufficient position since the mind must be on the right wavelength; that is, it must have the existing schemata to which it fits. However, this is contrary to the premise of speech acts (Searle, 1969). Speech acts are the basic unit of language for expressing meaning and which express an intention. Littlejohn (1987) notes that an ‘important characteristic of a speech act is that the recipient understands the speaker’s intentions’; and later, ‘in speech acts one’s intention is largely understood by another person by virtue of constitutive rules, because these rules tell others what to count as a particular kind of act’.

One of the common definitions of information involves its use in uncertainty reduction. Fox argues against this definition, suggesting a message may contain information yet increase uncertainty. This challenges the assumption of information theory that more information will reduce uncertainty. However, if information is viewed as altering confidence in decision making, it might be argued that it can reduce uncertainty about certainty! In the example above, the robot spacecraft could have returned home and provided information at odds with a previous mission. The information would then have increased uncertainty. Fox also argues that information does not have to be believed by the sender or perceiver to be still information; for example, a person can extract information from a message even though the message is a lie. The information extracted may be that intended by the sender or it may be that the sender is incorrect or misleading. This assumes that information is simply a message that will only influence if it is believed or doubted; yet we can act on doubts as if we believe. Indeed, if the information is viewed as untrue, then it provides information about the sender. Finally, Fox concludes that information is a proposition, as defined by the philosophy literature. So, information is a message-independent representation of the way the world is. Consideration of ‘meaning’ may provide further insight here.

## Meaning

Many researchers, including Fox, emphasise that information is not meaning; the two are not interchangeable. The word ‘meaning’ cannot be meaningfully substituted for ‘information’ in the sentence ‘that information is misleading’. However, Fox acknowledges the importance of meaning in understanding information: ‘the information carried by a sentence, or set of sentences, is determined by meaning, and, hence, is relative to meaning’. Later, ‘the problem of meaning is thus one of the central obstacles to progress in information science’. Using Fox’s approach, consider the following: ‘the meaning of that sentence is not clear’. The popular use of the transmission metaphor imposes on this sentence an image of two people, the sender and the receiver, and the transfer of meaning. The metaphor is inadequate to distinguish between the elements of information, meaning, message and the presence of different people with different contexts. To say that information has sense meaning is the same as saying the recipient believes it has significance for his or her purposes. O'Shaughnessy (1992) defines the sense meaning of a word as the sense (connotation) that the word evokes, as opposed to the referent meaning which is just the thing to which a word refers. Hence, information is not meaning but information must possess meaning.

Reviewing the lack of progress in semiotics, Sless (1986) comments on meaning. He objects to a perspective that implicitly assumes that meaning is something that can be transmitted faithfully from one person to another. 'This way of treating meaning – as if it were the property of objects – is common to most languages. It points to a process of projection which enables us to externalise our knowledge and expectations and impose them on the objects around us. In other words we objectify meanings. What we ourselves produce seems, even to us, to be independent of us'. This, however, fails to distinguish between the sense and referent meaning above. The sense of a message is given to it by the perceiver who externalises the informational content given to it by the sender. So sense meaning, like information, requires a perceiver, but now the perception is what the message means to the sender.

Sless (1986), Pratt (1977) and Belkin & Robertson (1976) agree that accurate communication requires that the sense meaning given to a message by the perceiver is the same as that given to it by the sender. If the perceiver knows the sender then the perceiver may give the message extra meaning, such as why the message is being sent. However, so long as the perceiver can perceive the core meaning given to the message, communication can occur. It is, of course, expected that not all the informational content given by the sender and the perceiver will match, owing to the different background and situational context of the perceiver relative to the sender (Boulding, 1956). However, interpretation will always involve some conjecture so it is always inherently multiple, hence it is perhaps apposite here to think in terms of adequate interpretation rather than interpretation per se.

Sless criticises the transmission metaphor on the grounds that it assumes participant independence. It, therefore, assumes no recursive relationship between the sender, the perceiver and their environment. Sless argues that a landscape metaphor better explains the problems of the perceiver interpreting the meaning that the sender gave to a message. This metaphor is of a hilly landscape covered in trees and buildings with the sender and perceiver standing at separate positions in the landscape. The sender is trying to point out something in the distance to the perceiver. Owing to their common background there are some things both parties can see, but, because of their differences, each has a unique perspective on the landscape. Sless adds to this image the idea that the ground is elastic. As people walk across the landscape they distort it. The distortion is dependent on the relative position of other people in the landscape. People moving about alter the view of the perceiver, as does the perceiver's own movement. The relative information and perception of the other person's information is distorted and constantly changing. The perceiver has to guess the sender's meaning from his or her own position. Though this metaphor adequately describes that fact that different windows give different perspectives, this is in some ways inadequate since it excludes notions of predispositions which are defined in the schemata of the recipient.

Sless's work emphasises that at least two forms need be given to a message. Significantly, communication requires the perceiver to determine his or her own form (information) and the form given (meaning) by someone else. The sentence ‘the meaning of the sentence is unclear’ means that the perceiver cannot understand what sense meaning the sender gave to a message. To reiterate, meaning is the informational content that the perceiver assumes someone else gave to a message. It is only relevant to communication. Information is something the perceiver gives to a message.

Thus, meaning and information are not the same thing, but the pursuit of a definition of information requires more. What determines the information given to a message? Notice, for example, that the informational content that a person gives to a message can vary with successive re-readings of the same message, implying a message may contain multiple meanings.

## Concerns or interests

Wilson (1977) points out that the key to understanding (or, perhaps, more properly paying attention to) information is ‘concern’. He defines concerns as ‘the range of happenings in the world over which we think we can have control’ (p. 42). ‘Concern is a readiness to exert influence: readiness to act’. It is possible to take issue with this since people may be even more concerned about things over which they feel they have no control. Perceivers’ concerns (interests or self-interests) determine the information content that they give to messages, though interpretation will depend on other things such as the currently held viewpoint. A message contains information for a perceiver if it is about a real or potential change to something that is of importance to the perceiver, although, again, knowing that things will not change might be considered information. For example, what information is carried by the message ‘the price of bread in X is £38?’. Possible responses include: where is X?; bread will not sell in X; one would not buy bread at that price. A health worker concerned about malnutrition may extract the information that one basic food source will be denied to low-income groups. To a supplier of bread it would contain the information that a better margin is available by selling in X. Indeed, several pieces of information may be extracted from the single message, depending on interests. Despite the wide range of information that can be extracted from a message, communication takes place. Usefully, most of one person’s concerns are also the concerns of others. This means that many people will seek to get the same information from a message, thus making the collective interpretation of meaning possible. Alternatively, it is often possible to predict the concerns of others and so be able to guess what information another person will extract from a message.

This must lead to a consideration of the determinants of interests or concerns. Maturana and Varela (1987) contend that these are our experiences over millions of years. Hall (1951) uses the cultural approach to classify concerns, while Maslow (1954) uses a motivation context. However, they overlap noticeably. Hall's classifications (in no particular order) are: subsistence, sex and sexuality, defence, territorial claims, temporality, learning, social interaction, rules of association, play and exploitation of land and people. The list is incomplete since it does not include what the Maori call mana (the ego) and what Maslow calls self-actualisation needs. People are concerned with their self and also with issues such as peer respect. The task of ranking these concerns is complex and contingent, and will not be attempted here. Suffice to say that concerns are the result of a complex interaction of 'nature and nurture', the latter including influence from peers and organizations. Interests, too, are also related to values. Clearly, in addition, cognitive acts are important in determining concerns.

Fortunately, people have many similar concerns, so a sender may be able to predict a perceiver's concerns. This provides an expectation of what information will be extracted or sought from a message. The sender needs to project himself or herself into the perceiver situation and predict the perceiver's concerns. For example, a concern about subsistence leads to an assumption that commercial competitors would find useful information in product research results. This could lead to a consideration of cultural norms and organizational culture, though here one example may suffice. Littlejohn (1987) suggests ‘commercials are an excellent example. Although audience interpretations will vary, the advertiser certainly aims to elicit a predominant meaning and a particular response’. People can influence other people's concerns; it is possible to affect people's value systems via persuasion. Wilson (1983) uses the term ‘cognitive authority’ for those, including specialists, from whom advice is taken. He points out that an essential part of being a manager is to take the advice of specialists. Specialists, however, not only provide messages, they also advise on which areas are worthy of concern. Given this power to determine concerns, specialists can determine how messages will be interpreted. Employers and professionals can in this way construct their clients' reality (Hines, 1989). By standardising concerns (which may involve manipulation of values), specialists may argue that they assist communication; however, it does give them considerable power.

Support for the perceiver-concerns approach can be found in the science literature. Biologists have attempted to explain information in terms of chemical actions. Maturana and Varela (1987) concentrate on this. As they explain everything in terms of chemical reactions, they object to a demarcation between a person and the environment. They point out that people are made up of the chemicals that surround them. Autonomy is merely the concentration of certain chemicals in an outer membrane. Within this 'skin', chemicals are joined together either directly or indirectly by a massive and complicated network. Maturana and Varela, therefore, also object to the use of the transmission metaphor for incoming messages. According to the analysis here, the metaphor is basically false (when applied to humans). It presupposes a unity that is not determined structurally, where interactions are instructive, as though what happens to a system in an interaction is determined by the perturbing agent and not by its structural dynamics. It is evident, however, that such is not the case with communication: each person says what he or she says or hears what he or she hears according to their own structural determination; saying does not ensure listening or acceptance. From the perspective of an observer, there is always ambiguity or vagueness in a communicative interaction – ambiguity being a confusion of concepts, while vagueness is lack of clarity in definition. The phenomenon of communication depends not on what is transmitted, but on what happens to the person who receives it. This is a very different matter from 'transmitting information' (Maturana & Varela, 1987).

Typically, the transmission metaphor suggests that, say, the eye nerves are stimulated by signs which result in a message being sent to the brain. The brain then sends out impulses to the appropriate muscles. Maturana and Varela point out that any stimulus to the eye nerves is transmitted through the entire body, as stimuli to any part of the body are transmitted to the eye. At the same time the eye nerve sends the messages, it is receiving thousands of messages from other parts of the body. The same is true of the brain; it receives stimuli from all over the body. Eye stimuli that reach the brain will be accompanied by many other messages. The brain is operating in an environment that can best be described as 'a room full of people talking at once'. The message from the eye is only one 'voice' among others. The other voices include demands from all parts of the body for action. The brain will interpret the message from the eye in relation to pressures from these other 'voices'. The other 'voices' are the result of the concerns of the person and, therefore, influence how the brain perceives and reacts to, say, an internal memo being read by the eye. More importantly, Maturana and Varela give no perceptive ability to the actions of the brain or any other part of the human nervous system. They use the metaphor (reminiscent of the Chinese box metaphor in artificial intelligence) of a child born in a submarine. The child spends its entire life in this submarine, eventually being given a task of assisting with the running of the submarine. The task is to 'press this knob if that dial gets above that point and pull this lever if that reading gets less than that point'. The dials and readings represent incoming chemical messages and the knobs and levers the outgoing chemical messages. To an outside observer of the 'submarine', it is being carefully steered past obstacles. Yet the person inside is merely responding to the dials and readings. Given no perceptive ability, the brain cannot rank messages as important by any means other than the strength of the chemical messages being received. It certainly cannot glance over the brain of the message sender to see how it interprets the message. Information must, therefore, be unique to the perceiver.

## Information issues

It is now necessary to examine the perceiver-concerns perspective's potential in information systems issues. The perspective considerably assists with the understanding of a number of issues related to information; for example, it helps provide a more meaningful definition of the value of information. The perceiver's concerns approach to valuing information is similar to that of the 'surprise' value approach, that is, the change in expectations invoked by the information. The surprise value is determined by the amount of response a piece of information evokes. A cognitive approach might be useful here, equating information to the degree of cognitive change caused by the message. This incorporates both concerns and accounts for information seeking (or the lack of it). The problem with the surprise approach is response measurement. People may not respond in any noticeable way to disastrous events. Put another way, the surprise value approach rather assumes that people will seek out information that affects their concerns. However, because a person has concerns it does not follow that she or he will actively seek information. It only means that the concerns will influence the information content he or she will extract from a message. When people will actively seek information is not clear. The biology literature (Maturana & Varela, 1987) states this to be dependent on a person's conditioned response to concern, though it is now accepted that conditioning is but one of many response mechanisms. Even if the level of concern is high, the pro-active seeking of information is not ensured. For example, the fear of information may lead cancer sufferers not to seek medical advice. There is, therefore, no guarantee that people will respond by physical action to a message that directly addresses their major concerns. A potential tautology lurks here, since the foregoing depends on the definition of action. Inaction could be defined as acting in a way so as to ignore certain stimuli. The perceiver-concerns perspective suggests that rather than consider response to value information, its value is determined by the degree of the perceiver's concern and the amount and type of change the message suggests.

Boland (1986) comments: ‘our ability to interpret and gain meaning in the world is always specific to a concrete moment in time. Meaning and understanding must always be struggled for and won anew. The universal hermeneutic problem of interpreting the world we confront as well as create is a continuous achievement – a perpetual challenge’. Using the perception of shifting concerns influencing the perception of the messages around us, this comment is more clearly understood. The idea that informational content varies as the perceiver’s concerns change over time is fundamental to the perceiver-concerns approach. This approach might shed light on current topics of interest such as media-richness theory (Lee, 1993) which assumes that “‘information richness’ is an inherent physical property of media’. Lee’s hermeneutic perspective rejects information-richness theory as inadequate for studying e-mail, since e-mail can be rich or lean in communication. Such a view would be supported by a perceiver-concerns perspective on the media.

Similarly, for instance, the phrase ‘information is power’ can be seen differently. While the phrase always required further qualification, it is better understood with a perceiver-concerns perspective. Traditionally, one meaning of the phrase was ‘the opportunity to filter information can influence decision makers’. The perceiver-concerns perspective reinforces the notion that influencing others by restricting the messages they receive can only be expected to work if the person acting as a filter can predict the decision maker’s real concerns. For example, if the decision maker’s main concern is appearing supportive of the information systems manager, then all he or she will seek is information about the manager's preference. Another meaning given to the phrase 'information is power' is that the first person with a piece of information may have a financial advantage. The example often used here is Rothschild's investments after the Battle of Waterloo. The message that the battle had been won by the allies was available to other people before Rothschild. Other people's concerns might not have included stock prices. Rothschild's concerns did include stock prices, so the message had a particular informational content for him. Having the message first is only useful if it serves a concern. The aspect of having the knowledge of how to exploit a piece of information by, say, investing in the share market is covered by the definition of concerns. It includes 'the range of happening in the world over which we think we have control'. Boland (1986) criticises the phrase 'information is power', using the argument that computer systems designers are deluding themselves that they are building a power system when building an 'information' system. The designers believe that the more messages they formalise, capture and process, the more power is possessed. The perceiver's approach emphasises that it is not the capture of messages that is important but the ability to influence concerns. This gives a far greater control over others. It also explains the concerns people have over the influence of the media when selecting topics for inclusion in the news or on the front page of a newspaper; though there is evidence that the media has more influence over what people talk about than over what they actually will think.

## Conclusions

This paper has argued that Wilson's emphasis on ‘concerns’ or interests is an essential alternative perspective necessary to assist in developing useful information systems. If nothing else, it suggests that report generation development cannot assume that a database can become a ‘radio transmitter’, sending relevant information to managers. The database cannot be seen as an isolated signal box, but it is one component within the information system. Transmitter-type language is not sensible under the perceiver-concerns perspective. Reports must assume that only the user can ever know what he or she is currently concerned about. Only the perceiver can give information to a message. In a practical sense this may mean reporting systems needs to commence with eliciting concerns. The need to concentrate on the user is not new but the user is not a 'receiver', he or she is a perceiver with concerns. This emphasis on the perceiver also gives a fresh perspective on the problems of storing 'relevant' data. Information is given birth by, and lives its entire life within, the perceiver's concerns.

As with any perspective on a social construct, the perceiver's concerns perception provides a greater (or, at least, a different) insight into the nature of information; for example, it alters the threats of the so-called ‘information age’. Now it can be seen that those who control concerns need to be controlled. The perspective also explains why ‘communication’ is more than pushing ‘bits’ down a wire. Rather, communication is a complex and fortunate matching of information given and information taken from a message. The perspective further helps the information manager understand issues such as how to value information, how it gives power and, perhaps, the meaning of privacy.

This paper, then, has argued that information, as with many concepts used in organizational settings, needs to be considered in a sociological perspective as well as one based on a machine metaphor. Perceivers' concerns give birth to information, nothing else can. Change to an emphasis on perceivers' concerns will enable better understanding of information, inter-human communications and information systems to grow. It provides a timely re-focus on the user when designing systems. The paper has laid out an alternative view of information but has sign-posted a number of points of contention which may lead to alternative routes and conclusions. There is a clear need for more debate on this topic, and this paper is an attempt to stimulate such discussion.
