# A role for soft systems methodology in information systems development

M.C. WINTER, D.H. BROWN AND P.B. CHECKLAND

Department of Management Science, The Management School, Lancaster University, Lancaster, LA1 4YX, UK

Recent years have seen a growing interest in the use of soft systems methodology (SSM) in work related to computer-based information systems (IS). Against this background, this paper seeks to identify and define, more fully than hitherto, a role for SSM in information systems development which stems from its fundamental principles. Since SSM sees computer-based information systems as systems which serve purposeful human action, the notion of ‘information system’ in SSM is one that necessarily involves two systems, a ‘serving’ system (the information system) and a ‘served’ system of purposeful action. This notion of ‘information system’ leads to certain principles for IS development. These are expressed in the form of a model, referred to as the ‘information system/information system development model’ (IS/ISDM). This model is used to examine several conventional approaches to information systems development including the widely used UK government method SSADM, in order to identify where, in IS work, soft systems ideas might best make a contribution. A possible role for SSM is identified and a brief description of this role is given at the end of the paper.

## Introduction

In spite of the plethora of information system development methods and the apparent clarity of their procedures, the realisation of computer-based information systems in real-world situations remains problematic. Stories of failures abound, a recent example being provided by the Taurus project of the London Stock Exchange (Northedge, 1993). By the time the project was aborted in the spring of 1993 it had already cost the Stock Exchange £75 million, with probably three times this amount additionally spent by the user community. The planning time had extended over ten years and, by the Stock Exchange's own estimate, Taurus remained three years away from implementation. Once again, the problems of an inadequate match between the needs of the user communities and the proposed systems solutions, combined with project resource estimates orders of magnitude adrift, made a too-familiar story.

It is not that the problem of developing adequate systems has lacked serious attention. There has been significant commercial interest and growth both in methods for systems development and in software tools to aid that task. Also, at the theoretical level, the voluminous literature dealing with requirements specification, representational form, systems implementation, project control and development methodologies is ample evidence of a world-wide intellectual commitment to addressing problems in this area. Increasingly, the linkage between information systems and an organization's potential effectiveness and competitiveness is being recognised.

However, deciding on which systems should be built out of the many possible systems which could be built remains problematic and is an analytical challenge ill-served by current methodologies. Such theoretical limitations become more pronounced when we recognise that these methodologies, with their overall focus on the development of a computer system, hardly begin to address the potential research issues which arise from their application within organizations, for example the nature of work, privacy, data integrity, and the changing nature of authority and organizational decision processes. In spite of the breadth of these issues, it remains the case that a major stream of research writing has consistently focused on the principles underlying the creation and design of computer-based systems. A significant part of this work, which has sought to inform and be tested by practice, has recognised that in terms of the conventional life cycle model of system development, the early stages in the development process remain the most difficult. The Central Computing and Telecommunications Agency's (CCTA) work on the early stages of SSADM (CCTA, 1993) is an example of this. If we start with a given requirements specification, through functional and design specifications to system testing, the process of verification is, no matter how complex, amenable to logical analysis. The early stages of the development process however (essentially the 'validation' of the users' requirements), are clearly different, and the positivistic, rational view underpinning the 'information requirements' stage of the majority of methodologies is increasingly recognised as problematical.

Part of this strand of thinking has been the development, within the more general development of soft systems methodology (SSM) (Checkland, 1981; Wilson, 1984; Checkland & Scholes 1990), of an approach to the early and vulnerable stage of the development process in which user needs are shaped, refined and tried out. In logic, the sequence of considerations in an SSM-based approach to working information systems is as follows (Checkland & Holwell, 1993), each point below being contingent on the preceding ones:

(1) meanings attributed to their world by the people concerned;

(2) purposeful activity, purposeful in the light of those meanings;

(3) information support which is relevant to the people carrying out the activities; and

(4) data structures and ways in which they could be manipulated to yield appropriate categories of information.

SSM provides a way of identifying and building relevant purposeful activity models (steps (1) and (2) above) from which the information requirements (step (3)) can be richly debated. Fundamentally, an SSM-based approach to information requirements analysis is interpretivist and accepts a clear distinction between data and information, in which the latter is described as data plus the meaning attributed to it in a particular context.

Interest in SSM in the context of computer-based information systems development (ISD) is widespread (see for example, Checkland, 1981; Wilson, 1984; Schafer et al., 1988; Anderson, 1989; Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990; Patching, 1990; Davies & Ledington, 1991; Jayaratna, 1994; Lewis, 1994; Stowell & West, 1994; and Stowell, 1995). Increasingly its application in ISD work has been the subject of various research activities, workshops, seminars and conferences (see, for example, the Systemist, August 1992). Moreover, the industrial and commercial world is displaying a parallel interest in SSM. Perhaps the most significant example of this so far is the recent CCTA publication Applying Soft Systems Methodology to an SSADM Feasibility Study (CCTA, 1993). SSM is also featured in another CCTA product called COMPACT, a methodology for office systems development (CCTA, 1989). Furthermore, a number of SSM consultancies are now operating, often in IS-related contexts. The last five years, especially, have shown that there is a significant interest in the use of SSM in ISD work.

Within the development of SSM the stance adopted towards work on IS leads to the four contingent points listed above. But within the SSM literature the underlying assumptions have been alluded to and stated baldly rather than spelled out in detail (see Checkland, 1981, pp. 221–222, 234–237; Checkland & Scholes, 1990, pp. 53–58; Checkland & Holwell, 1993). The purpose here is to elaborate the relation of SSM to work on IS, to examine the IS development process implied by SSM and hence to outline more clearly a role for that approach in IS work.

The approach is in four parts. After describing what we take an ‘information system’ to be, we describe and illustrate a model of information system development which derives directly from this notion of ‘information system’. In other words, to create an information system of the kind we describe, certain activities need to be carried out. Collectively, these activities constitute the outline of a model of IS development, a model which enables us to examine afresh methodologies for information systems development such as SSADM (CCTA, 1990), Information Engineering (Martin, 1986) and Multiview (Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990). Such an examination forms the third part of the paper, focusing on the role of these approaches in ISD work. Finally, a role for SSM in ISD work is suggested.

## Information systems and the context of their development

## Fundamental IS principles

This discussion does not take as given any concept of what the phrase ‘information system’ refers to. Rather its object is to develop such a concept, building on and extending earlier accounts (Checkland, 1988; Checkland & Scholes, 1990; Checkland & Holwell, 1993).

The word ‘information’ has, in everyday language, the connotation of being meaningful to human beings. Therefore the concept of ‘information system’ needs to be linked to the idea of being meaningful, to the idea of attributing meaning to what we perceive. That is what is done here.

An important feature of any real-world situation will be the meanings by which the people concerned both create ‘the situation’ as an abstract entity and make sense of it with reference to wider contexts. Now, attributing meaning is a uniquely human act, though meanings may be shared, partially or completely, by groups of people. Thus people create or attribute meanings which enable them to make sense of their world. That is the fundamental characteristic of social life.

These meanings will lead to action being taken by the human players. In particular they will result in intentional or purposeful action being taken, rather than in instinctive action (or simply to random thrashing about!). This purposeful action will be helped by the provision of knowledge; the action will be more likely to help the pursuit of a purpose if it is based on sound knowledge. We can expect the role of something called 'an information system' to have something to do with this supplying of knowledge. Thus an entrepreneur may perceive the world as providing opportunities which can be commercially exploited. That is part of the meaning attribution of entrepreneurs. Suppose such a person sees an opportunity for making a small business out of importing specialist jazz records on the numerous small labels in the USA and selling them by mail to jazz enthusiasts in the UK. That particular meaning attribution will lead to the purposeful action of establishing such a business with minimum overheads. Doing that is more likely to be successful if it is based on knowledge – in this case knowledge of the jazz field, the suppliers, the potential customers and their tastes, and the mechanisms of selling by mail.

In general, meaning attribution leads to purposeful action which will be dependent on and be supported by knowledge. We can expect ‘information systems’ to contribute to providing this support. Thus the nature of an information system is that it is a system which supports, or serves, purposeful action. If we conceptualise the purposeful action through the device of models of human activity (as is done in SSM), then we have a situation in which we have a system served (the activity system) and a system which does the serving (the information system) by providing support of a particular kind. This notion of information systems as serving purposeful human action (expressed via human activity system models) is crucial. The system served/system serving concept is the core of this paper. The clear implication is that the development of a ‘good’ information system can derive only from a prior declared account of the human action served. It is essential to conceptualise the system served before it is possible adequately to conceptualise the system which does the serving. Finally, the knowledge support of purposeful action can be thought of in terms of categories of information (such as information concerning potential customers and their tastes in the example above); and these information items will themselves be structured from various data elements.

Of course, once an information system is in use, the interactions between the action served and the system which supports it will lead to learning which may change both. We are here concerned with the initial thinking needed to create an information system.

Summarising these thoughts, the fundamental principles underpinning the creation of relevant ‘information systems’ would, in logic, follow the path shown in Figure 1. The crucial distinction in the logic is that between the activities concerned with the serving system, typically a computer-based system dealing with (iii) and (iv), and the served purposeful human action ((i) and (ii)). In many real situations, too much work in information systems tries to start with the data, taking as given unexamined or casually inspected implicit answers to questions concerning the higher levels in the hierarchy in Figure 1.

IS as serving systems – an implied development model
In this section, fundamental ideas about the thinking needed to create ‘information systems’ are expressed in the form of a model, referred to as the ‘information system/information system development model’ (IS/ISDM). It provides a framework with which to examine some of the conventional approaches to information systems development in order to identify where in IS work soft systems ideas might usefully make a contribution.

## Core concept

Since computer-based information systems are seen as systems that serve purposeful human action, the notion of ‘information system’ in the model being developed here is one that contains a ‘serving’ system (the information system) and a ‘served’ system which represents purposeful human action in organizations. The model’s core concept then consists of two systems in a served–serving relationship, as illustrated in Figure 2. In the conventional life cycle-based view of information systems, the emphasis is mainly on the serving system. Indeed, in terms of Figure 2, the phrase ‘the system’ in the much-used phrase ‘the system development life cycle’ (SDLC) is a reference to the serving system; the served system is only implicit in the SDLC. In other words, while elements of the served system can be recognised in many SDLC definitions, the balance of activity is heavily centred on the serving system. Here, both systems in Figure 2, served and serving, are treated with equal importance.

Since our focus is on computer-based information systems in organizational contexts, the human activity system on the left in Figure 2 is viewed as a system of organizational activity. In any application of this concept, the served system needs to have a demonstrable relation to actual or intended purposeful human action in real-world organizations (Checkland & Scholes, 1990). Moreover, this system, the context of the IS, can express any particular organizational image which seems appropriate to people in the organization concerned with its informational support. For example, it might be appropriate to choose to view the served system as primarily concerned with making a business out of providing a service to a set of customers. This image of the organization, or some part of it, as a service provider, might then be modelled using various service modelling concepts (Shostack, 1984; Grönroos, 1990). Given the multiple possibilities for conceptualising the served system, however, various implications arise, including implications for information support. Consider for example a prison; the role of a prison might be conceptualised as ‘a punishment system’, a view which implies certain activities and various information requirements. If however the role (i.e. of ‘the served system’) was seen as ‘a rehabilitation system’, then what would follow would be a different set of organizational activities and, very importantly for the IS, a different set of information requirements. Thus, the way in which the organizational activity is conceptualised will dictate what information support is appropriate. Importantly, since the served system is expressed as a human activity system, the organizational activity can be conceptualised in the form of SSM’s human activity system models. These models can then be used to help derive information requirements, i.e. the requirements to be provided by the serving system. Such models have been shown to be useful for thinking about the informational implications of purposeful organizational activity based on declared worldviews (see for example, Wilson, 1984; Checkland & Holwell, 1993; Galliers, 1993). A good example is the current IS/IT situation in the National Health Service; as Checkland & Holwell (1993) state:

... the current information-provision problems in the NHS can be stated in the following way. Government has imposed a new way of conceptualizing the provision of health care; the ‘meanings’ have been changed by the imposition of the purchaser–provider relationship. Hence what is needed is examination of the purposeful activity necessarily implied by that relationship, and its embodiment in a particular organization structure, enabling needed information to be defined and IT to be selected coherently. (p. 9)

Turning now to the ‘information system’ itself, the serving system in Figure 2, this is a computer-based data manipulation system together with people who operate, maintain and modify it. The data manipulation system contains a data processing element and a data storage element realised through various hardware platforms. Such a technical arrangement used to be termed, for obvious reasons, a data processing system. Over the last decade or so, however, the language used to describe such arrangements has changed. Nowadays, such arrangements of information technology are referred to, in operation, as ‘information systems’. But:

What are usually referred to as ‘information systems’ are in fact not that: they are data manipulation systems. A true information system would consist of data manipulation (which machines can be set up to do) together with meaning attribution, which is a uniquely human act. (Checkland & Holwell, 1993, p. 6)

This paper puts the human being, as an attributor of meaning, in a central position in IS work; but two points about this need to be made at this stage of the argument. Firstly, in designing the ‘information system’ in Figure 2, the designers will aim for outputs which have meaning according to the rules and norms of the organization in question. The processed data provided as outputs will in general be seen as meaningful information within the organization. But the designers cannot force users to see the system outputs as meaningful in the way they intend. Would-be-neutral corporate information concerning resource use and costs, for example, may well be interpreted and used politically in a dispute between department heads competing for corporate resources. This kind of thing will emerge in the real-world interaction between the serving system and the action it serves once the computer outputs are available. Secondly, the IS being described here is a ‘formal information system’, as opposed to the informal or ‘undesigned’ information system which Land (1985), for example, includes in his general model of an information system. While recognising the existence and importance of such informal systems in organizations, we are here concerned with the thinking relevant to creating a ‘formal’ (computer-based) information system. ## Assumptions, theoretical and metatheoretical

Two sets of assumptions, theoretical and metatheoretical, underlie the core concept just described. The core concept is based on the so-called ‘law of conceptualisation’ (Checkland, 1981, p. 237), a methodological law which states that conceptualisation of a system which serves another must be preceded by conceptualisation of the system served. Thus, in the case of ‘information systems’:

Systems analysis aiming at information system design, if it is to make much impact, must first concentrate on the activity system which the information system is to serve. (Checkland, 1981, p. 208)

It is difficult to overestimate the importance of this principle, a principle which reflects a so-called ‘infological perspective’ on information systems (Methlie, 1980). The core concept is also based on the assumption that ‘organizations are complex and paradoxical phenomena that can be understood in many different ways’ (Morgan, 1986, p. 13). As Morgan (1986) writes:

... many of our taken-for-granted ideas about organizations are metaphorical, even though we may not recognize them as such. For example, we frequently talk about organizations as if they were machines designed to achieve predetermined goals and objectives, and which should operate smoothly and efficiently. And as a result of this kind of thinking we often attempt to organize and manage them in a mechanistic way, forcing their human qualities into a background role. (p. 13)

Another important element of the core concept is the uniquely human act of meaning attribution, reflecting another theoretical assumption, namely that ‘information’ is taken to be data plus the meaning attributed to it in some context (Holwell, 1989). Different sets of ideas elsewhere convey a similar concept of information (see, for example, Vickers, 1965, 1970; Morgan, 1986; Eden, 1987; Liebenau & Backhouse, 1990; Walsham, 1993). In short, the overriding message from all this work is that ‘information’ should be regarded as a socially constructed phenomenon rather than a concrete phenomenon.

At a metatheoretical level, since the core concept is essentially an assembly of systems concepts, the model's assumptions are essentially those of systems thinking. Checkland (1983) provides the following exposition of the metatheoretical assumptions of systems thinking:

... within the systems movement there are two basic intellectual stances, here called paradigms I and II ... Consider the human observer who, although himself part of reality, can by a familiar mental act think about his own intellectual engagement with the rest of reality, R, in which he uses particular mental processes or methodology, M. One common stance, paradigm I, makes the following assumptions:

(i) $R$ is systematic: the world contains systems;

(ii) $M$ can be systematic.

In this paradigm the world is assumed to contain systems, and understanding or engineering them is assumed to be possible via systematic processes .... The alternative paradigm, paradigm II, has emerged in systems work in real-world problems in the 70s. In this stance the corresponding assumptions to those of paradigm I are as follows:

(i) $R$ is problematical: we cannot know it ontologically; (ii) $M$ can be systemic.

In this paradigm the world is taken to be very puzzling: there is no reason why we should have evolved brains beyond those needed to survive together on the planet's surface; there is no reason to suppose our senses and brains can tell us what reality is really like. But the process of engaging with it, of trying to understand and/or engineer it, can be systemic. (pp. 671–672)

The served system, which represents purposeful human action in real-world organizations, is rooted in the theory of paradigm II, better known as 'soft' systems thinking, while the serving information system, with its link to computer technology, is rooted in the theory of paradigm I, better known as 'hard' systems thinking. More generally, the left-hand side of the core concept (Figure 2) is rooted in the theory of the so-called interpretive paradigm while the right-hand side is rooted in the theory of the so-called functionalist paradigm (Burrell & Morgan, 1979).

## Implied methodology

Having outlined the model's core concept and its underlying assumptions, we now turn to the third and final element of the model, the implied methodology for system development. The term ‘methodology’ is interpreted as ‘principles of method’ (Checkland, 1981) rather than equated with method or technique; in this sense, the model's implied methodology is the set of development principles implied by the core concept rather than a detailed prescription for information systems development. The main principle of the methodology is that, in order to create a formal information system of the kind described and illustrated in the core concept, it is necessary to start not with the information system but with the system served by it, that is to say with the primary activities of the organization, such as making and selling products, providing services etc, rather than with the secondary activities such as keeping records, producing reports etc.

Given the nature of the core concept, the methodology contains principles relating to the served system, labelled ‘organizational analysis’, and principles relating to the serving system, labelled ‘information system engineering’.

Since the essence of the approach is to make sense of purposeful human action in organizations (prior to IS design etc.) through the use of the human activity system concept, the following fundamental principles arise. Firstly, it is necessary to establish the views of the organization and its purposes which are relevant and meaningful to the people in the organization. It is then necessary to conceptualise the activities necessary to pursue these purposes and to debate these (served system) activities with the people in the organization, comparing them with current action. Of course, in any real-world organization, such a debate will take place in a ‘political’ arena in which many different interests are being pursued. It is unlikely that the debate will be characterised by consensus. The normal outcome is an accommodation between conflicting interests rather than a consensus – an accommodation within which action can occur. The required outcome of this debate is a set of meaningful conceptualisations of action relevant to the organization, to which information analysis can be applied. More specifically, these conceptualisations can take the form of human activity system models whose information support people would like to see exist in the organization. The activities in the models are examined to help derive the information requirements of anyone carrying out the activities. Operational information requirements will be requirements for information needed by people to do the activities while performance information requirements will be requirements for information needed by people to control the doing of the activities. The difference between these two types of information is illustrated by Wilson (1984) with reference to an activity ‘obtain stock’:

The information required to do the activity would be in terms of the specification of what is to be stocked, the quantities required, and the time scale allowed for obtaining the stock items. The control information, however, would be dependent upon the definition that has been made of the measure of performance relative to this activity. Such a measure might include the availability of stock items and the value of stock held. Given this measure the information for control would be in terms of the number of nil-stock situations over a certain time period and the value of stock above (or below) a defined level. (pp. 229–230)

These principles can be summarised as follows (where ‘organization’ refers either to the whole of an organization’s activity or to part of it):

\- understand what the organization is taken to be;

\- understand what the organization will therefore do;

\- identify and define organizational performance criteria;

\- identify and define operational information requirements;

\- identify and define performance information requirements.

It should be pointed out that the organizational analysis being described here is seen not as a once-and-for-all examination, carried out at the beginning of an IT system development project. Rather it should be a continuing process, yielding changing information requirements as the organization changes. The link between the served and the serving system is seen as a relationship which should be managed as a continuing process. Concepts and tools which might be useful in managing this relationship, apart from soft systems modelling (Checkland & Scholes, 1990; Wilson, 1984), include service modelling concepts (Grönroos, 1990), organizational learning concepts (Senge, 1990; Lessem, 1993) and interpretative data analysis (Lewis, 1994). Concepts and tools from the ‘socio-technical school’ (Mumford & Henshall, 1979; Mumford & Weir, 1979; Mumford, 1983a, 1983b) are also relevant here, and indeed might be used in conjunction with the purposeful activity modelling from soft systems thinking.

Having identified and defined information requirements, it is necessary now to consider the data which could in principle provide the required information. Consideration needs to be given both to its structure and to its manipulation; thus, since the core of the serving system is a computer-based data manipulation system, the following methodological principles apply:

\- analyse the data manipulation system;

\- design the data manipulation system;

\- construct the data manipulation system;

\- test the data manipulation system;

\- install the data manipulation system.

These activities identify what needs to be done in engineering the serving system rather than how. With regard to the 'how', prototyping is seen as an important part of the engineering process, and clearly implies iteration both between the various activities listed above and between the exploration of the action served and the design of the system to support it. Other obvious tools and techniques which can be used in carrying out the system design activities include data flow modelling (Gane & Sarson, 1977; DeMarco, 1978; Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988), entity relationship modelling/normalisation (Codd, 1970; Chen, 1976; Benyon, 1990) and database design (McFadden & Hoffer, 1994).

Figure 4 shows the complete IS/ISD model. It includes a set of assumptions underlying a core concept (the served–serving concept) which implies a set of development principles; and it is based on a particular perspective or worldview that regards ‘information systems’ as serving systems. In the next section we use this perspective to examine some conventional approaches to IS development.

## Some conventional approaches to IS development examined

In this section the IS/ISD model (Figure 4) will be used to examine several conventional approaches to computer-based information systems development, in order to identify where, in IS work, soft systems ideas might usefully make a contribution. Since the IS/ISDM's implied methodology and the conventional approaches just mentioned are both expressed in terms of activities (activities in conventional approaches being typically referred to using such terms as 'stage', 'step' and 'task'), it is possible to examine these conventional approaches by relating their prescribed development activities to the activities in the IS/ISDM's implied methodology. Such a comparison (Winter, 1993) is summarised here. Three well-known methodologies, namely SSADM (CCTA, 1990), Information Engineering (Martin, 1986) and Multiview (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990) are examined by using the IS/ISD model to examine them as they are described on paper. Of course, a practitioner may in a real instance depart from the requirements of the written account but such idiosyncracies are not susceptible to a general analysis. Here we have to take a methodology to be as its authors describe it. In essence, we find that these well-known approaches to ISD focus most of their attention on the serving system and pay scant attention to the system served, focusing only on data handling activities rather than on primary organizational activities. This probably derives from the fact that the intellectual bed-rock of these approaches is the system development life cycle, a concept which explicitly refers to only one system, the serving system in the IS/ISDM. This indicates a possible role for soft systems ideas in IS work, as will be described at the end of this paper.

The comparison operates as follows. Activities from the IS/ISD model's implied methodology are used as the source of questions such as ‘does the activity exist in the methodology?’ and ‘if so, how is it done?’. In asking these questions it soon becomes apparent that all three approaches contain very similar sets of information system engineering activities, and so this discussion focuses mainly on the declared accounts of organizational analysis, i.e. their treatment of the system served. It is here that major differences are revealed, and these are summarised in Table 1.

## SSADM

Perhaps the most significant finding in relation to the structured systems analysis and design method (SSADM) (CCTA, 1990) is that SSADM has no explicit organizational activity model but operates with accounts of data processing activity whose value is taken to be self-evident. This difference between the IS/ISD model and SSADM can be illustrated as follows. Consider the activity, 'deliver goods to customer'. Figure 5 illustrates how the activity would be treated in the IS/ISD model, Figure 6 how the activity would be modelled in SSADM (on some data flow diagrams (DFDs), the external entity in Figure 6 annotated with the phrase 'goods deliverer' might instead be shown as 'customer'). The model in Figure 5 is a broader model than the model in Figure 6; in terms of the IS/ISD model, Figure 5 is a model of the served system (i.e. a part of primary organizational activity) while Figure 6 is a model of the serving system (i.e. a part of secondary organizational activity, in this case data manipulation activity). In summary, and very significantly, since SSADM only produces models of the kind shown in Figure 6, the methodology would appear to take as given the images, activities and performance aspects of the served system.

Another significant finding is that SSADM's concept of ‘information requirements’ is a reference to the information needed by the serving (data manipulation) system to function. This contrasts with the IS/ISD model's concept of the information requirements in which the emphasis is on information needed by the people tasked with carrying out the activities of the served system. Moreover, SSADM seems to derive information requirements from information which is assumed to be ‘out there’ in real-world organizations, whereas the IS/ISDM derives information requirements from consciously formulated views of real-world organizations. In other words, the IS/ISD model views ‘information’ as a socially constructed phenomenon whereas SSADM seems to view ‘information’ as a concrete phenomenon.

## Multiview

Compared with SSADM and IE, Multiview (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990) is less widely used. However, it does attempt to 'blend' a number of different approaches into a methodological framework and for this reason it is selected as a third approach for the examination. As with SSADM and IE, a summary of the main findings is given here. As Figure 7 illustrates, initially it seems that Multiview is based on a similar concept of 'information system' to the IS concept at the core of the IS/ISD model. Closer examination however reveals the following:

... the first stage looks at the organization – its main purpose, problem themes, and the creation of a statement about what the information system will be ... (p. xv) (italics added)

This contrasts with the IS/ISD model's first activity which results in a statement about what the organization (or part of it) is taken to be. This difference between the IS/ISD model and Multiview surfaces again in another comment concerning Multiview's first stage:

... the first stage in the systems design process is the analysis of the human activity system. It is necessary to understand the purposes of the organization before designing the information system .... The analyst must produce a definition of the purpose of the system before attempting system design, even if the definition has to be refined at a later stage. (p. 59)

Now, the IS/ISD model's stream of organizational analysis is regarded not as a ‘systems design process’, but as a process of organizational analysis, the results of which are expressed in the form of human activity system models which can be analysed for information requirements. More importantly, the last sentence in the extract seems to convey a very different use of ‘system’ to the use of ‘system’ in the organizational analysis part of the IS/ISDM; it apparently refers to the computer system rather than to the served human activity system. An equivalent sentence in the IS/ISDM would be something like this: ‘the analyst, using the human activity system concept, should produce definitions of the (assumed) purposes of the organization before attempting computer design’. In summary then, despite having a very similar concept of information system to the IS/ISD model, the served–serving distinction is somewhat ambiguous in Multiview’s organizational analysis activities.

## Conclusion

Having developed an argued concept of what the phrase ‘information system’ means, and having teased out the implied development process for IS, we have used the resulting model (Figure 4) to examine three well-known approaches to IS development. On the basis of this examination, a number of observations can be made about these approaches to computer-based information systems development:

\- These conventional approaches to information systems development are based on the system development life cycle, a concept which explicitly refers to only one system, namely the IS/ISD model's serving system.

\- In one sense, most IS development methodologies start with the served system, but they do so mainly using methods and techniques for understanding the data-related aspects of organizations; this is an understandable focus given that the main output of these methodologies is an IT system. However, the data in an organization and the information that is produced from it is a function of what the organization does, when, where and how etc. In other words, the relevant data-handling activities of an organization, seen here as secondary activities, are a product of how the organization's primary activities are perceived, e.g. making and selling products, providing services etc.; these primary activities are taken as given in many IS methodologies, including SSADM.

\- A conventional approach which does address the served system in a non-data-focused way (IE) uses the unitary goal-seeking image of organization.

In summary, if we define information as data to which meaning has been attributed in a context, and accept that the nature of computer-based information systems is that they serve purposeful human action, then we are led to a concept of what is meant by the conventional phrase ‘an information system’. This concept is shown in Figure 4. Using this model to examine some well-known IS development methodologies, we find that they are very much focused on the system development activity, rather than on the combination of the organization as ‘the system served’ and the IS as the system which serves it. This suggests that conventional approaches require the addition of explicit processes for understanding the organization which the IS is to serve. It is suggested that soft systems methodology can help here, since it provides explicit well-established ways of modelling purposeful organizational activity. SSM can help make a practical reality of the requirements of the IS/ISD model developed here.
