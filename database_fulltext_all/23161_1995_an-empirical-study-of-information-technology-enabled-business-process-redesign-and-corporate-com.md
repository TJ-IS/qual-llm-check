# An empirical study of information technology enabled business process redesign and corporate competitive strategy

K. D. FIEDLER\*, V. GROVER and J. T. C. TENG

College of Business Administration, University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

Business process redesign (BPR) has been widely touted as a means for leveraging the power of information technology to change business processes radically, resulting in substantial improvements in organizational effectiveness and efficiency. This study represents an early attempt at examining the strategic nature of the phenomenon. Because BPR is radical change and has the ability to alter both the competitive and operational nature of the corporation, it is critical that BPR be coordinated with corporate strategy. Through a survey of information system executives, the relationships between corporate competitive strategy and three types of BPR – intrafunctional, interfunctional and interorganizational – were examined. It was found that organizations following a cost strategy tended to do more BPR projects on interfunctional processes. Furthermore, the positive effect of a cost strategy on interfunctional BPR was found to be strengthened by the degree of integration of IS and business planning. It was also found that while interorganizational BPR was not related to a specific strategy, it was strongly related directly with IS–business planning integration.

## Introduction

Downsizing, layoffs and corporate restructuring are phenomena that have become increasingly prevalent in the present economic environment. The current climate of increased competition and customer demand has forced organizations to look for new opportunities to increase organizational efficiency and effectiveness. Corporate planners are under pressure to examine basic assumptions of how work should be carried out. In this search, corporations are increasingly questioning the nature of business processes (Hammer, 1990) and the traditional practice of relying on functional specialisation as a basis for organizing a business (Drucker, 1988; Malone & Rockart, 1991).

This heightened acceptance of change has expressed itself as an openness in applying information technology (IT) to improve organizational processes. Business process redesign (BPR), or the radical transformation of business processes, has opened new possibilities for corporate planners to implement their firms' strategic objectives. For example, it has been reported that at PHH Corporation, a four billion dollar business service firm, an integral part of their strategic plan is to freeze investments in automation of 'business as usual' and apply those resources to altering the business through business process redesign (Betts, 1992).

The use of IT might be as simple as automating payroll, or as radical as enabling the establishment of a just-in-time inventory system as part of a strategic thrust to redesign how work is carried out with customers and suppliers. As IT is used to enable more radical and strategic change to be achieved, it becomes increasingly important that it is planned and coordinated to support the overall organization's business plan and goals.

The objective of this paper is to examine empirically the relationship between corporate strategy and BPR. In addition, the impact of the integration of IS planning and business strategy on this relationship is studied. This paper starts by reviewing and defining BPR, corporate strategy and IS-business planning integration, followed by a presentation of hypothesised relationships. Next, the methodology used and results from the examination of the proposed hypotheses are discussed. Finally, conclusions and implications from the study are presented.

## Business process redesign and information technology

In earlier, if not less turbulent times, the primary vehicle for applying IT was through the automation of existing processes within the boundaries of a traditional functional area, such as production or accounting. While automation of existing processes may increase the speed at which current business practices are carried out, it is based on the questionable assumption that the original process is satisfactory. Automation can result in an increase in productivity, quality and reduced costs, however the gains are destined to be linear (Boudette, 1990). BPR, however, involves radical change, often in the structure of the process.

An increasing number of businesses ‘are under tremendous pressure to reevaluate the basic ways they do business’ in search of more dramatic improvements in performance (Hayley et al., 1993, p73). For instance, in a 1992 survey of progressive chief information officers (CIOs), 92% said their companies were likely to re-engineer a business process (Maglitta & Sullivan-Trainor, 1991). A more recent survey of 534 CIOs conducted by Deloitte & Touche in 1993, found that 85% had been involved in some re-engineering projects and that, in spite of some disappointments, they were planning to increase their re-engineering efforts by 175% over 1992 levels (Hayley et al., 1993). This evidence points to the increasing importance and pervasiveness of information technology enabled re-engineering projects and the need to explore and understand various aspects of this phenomenon.

## Definition of BPR

The terms business process redesign (BPR), business process re-engineering or simply re-engineering have all been used to describe process change. While definitions may vary, it appears that BPR typically has four critical characteristics: (a) it attempts to achieve dramatic improvement in performance (Alter, 1990; Hammer, 1990; Senn, 1991; Venkatraman, 1991); (b) it involves radical departure from existing practices (Alter, 1990; Hammer, 1990; Venkatraman, 1991); (c) it is usually enabled by information technology (Alter, 1990; Hammer, 1990; Venkatraman, 1991); and (d) it is a deliberate and planned endeavour (Alter, 1990; Davenport & Short, 1990; Hammer, 1990; Senn, 1991; Venkatraman, 1991).

The current competitive business climate has forced planners to consider BPR in their search for dramatic improvement in organizational effectiveness. For example, in a study of fifteen companies that implemented BPR, it was reported that, on average, an organization had improvements of 48% in cost, 60% in defects and 80% in time (Wilder, 1991). A second critical element of BPR, radical departure from existing practices, is enthusiastically endorsed by Michael Hammer (1990):

In a time of rapidly changing technologies and even-shorter product life cycles . . . It is time to stop paving the cow paths. Instead of embedding outdated processes in silicon and software, we should obliterate them and start over. We should ‘reengineer’ our businesses: use the power of modern information technology to radically redesign our business processes in order to achieve dramatic improvements in their performance. [p 104]

BPR is frequently attempted because a more effective way can often be found to produce the desired outcome than the business's traditional practice. The opportunity for this process improvement can be provided by the improved coordination, communication and information manipulation capabilities of IT (Keen, 1991; Davenport, 1993; O'Brien, 1993). As Venkatraman (1991) noted;

Currently, the design of business process is based on a set of principles of organizations developed to exploit the capabilities offered by the Industrial Revolution. . . . the IT revolution could significantly alter some of these principles – thus rendering some modes of organization relatively inefficient. . . . It is clear that the basics for business process design should . . . be reassessed in light of the new capabilities offered by IT. [p 138]

One way that IT enables re-engineering is by allowing tasks to function more independently. Imaging technology, for example, lets banks process loan applications more efficiently by allowing multiple employees to access the same documents. Instead of having to process a paper document serially, different loan officers can evaluate the customers' credit history, employment status and loan eligibility simultaneously using electronic documents (McDonell & Somerville, 1991). Also, the ability of IT in facilitating direct communication can reduce the number of intermediate steps in complex processes.

The fourth critical element of BPR is that it is a deliberately planned endeavour. For example, when Charles Schwab Corporation undertook a comprehensive re-engineering program, they started by developing a business model. Their new business model, called Schwab Architecture and Migration Strategy (SAMS), took five months to build. The project planning team identified 24 interrelated business processes representing every activity of the brokerage firm. Eleven senior vice presidents met each month to guide the review and to serve as SAMS advocates. They believed that their BPR efforts would position the \$626 million firm for 20% growth for the next decade (Bartholomew, 1991).

A project at the Ford Motor Corporation can be used to illustrate the four elements of BPR. The deliberate plan to re-engineer the traditional accounts payable process at Ford, which employed 500 people, was brought about when they learned that Mazda Corporation used only five accounts payable employees. The old accounts payable process at Ford started when the purchasing department authorised a purchase, and sent an invoice to the supplier and accounts payable. When the order arrived, the inventory function sent a copy of the receiving document to accounts payable. Finally, when the mail delivered the supplier's invoice, accounts payable matched the invoice with the purchase order and the receiving document before issuing payment (Hammer, 1990).

It was determined that the desired outcome was authorised payment to the supplier at a substantially lower cost. The use of a shared database provided the opportunity for organizations to redesign the process. After implementation of the new process, the purchase order was entered into a database which could be accessed by the inventory department when the order was received. When the order arrived the inventory function could immediately match the merchandise to the order. The accounts payable function would then use the database to evaluate the status of the order and issue payment. The existence of a shared database allowed Ford to re-engineer the process, eliminating the need to match documents, reduce its work force by 75% and reap dramatic cost savings (Hammer, 1990; Teng et al., 1994a). Based on these observations, BPR is defined as a strategic endeavour whereby information technology is used to enable the planned radical change of business processes with the objective of dramatic improvement in performance. While it may be possible to carry out BPR without IT, this paper will focus on IT-enabled BPR.

## Defining BPR by contrast

While there is limited theoretical or empirical work specifically on re-engineering, insight into the nature of the phenomenon can be gained by examining the similarities and differences between business process redesign and related mature research streams (Grover et al., 1993). To understand BPR it is important to recognise what characteristics are new and to note some aspects of the concept that are related to prior research (Earl, 1994). Three research streams that can contribute to both the practice and definition of BPR are: work redesign and organizational development, information systems development, and organizational innovation. Each of these streams is briefly discussed in this section in terms of its potential contributions to BPR and as a backdrop to highlight how BPR is a fundamental departure from these traditional topics.

For years, researchers in organizational behaviour have used work redesign and organizational development to examine ways to improve performance. One approach that focused on improvements at the job level was work redesign. Hackman (1976) defined work redesign as the alteration of specific jobs to increase employee satisfaction and productivity. Another approach, organizational development, has concentrated on the development of effective work teams (French &

Bell, 1973). Though these methodologies do not preclude radical change, they concentrate on employee satisfaction and planned incremental change (Beer & Walton, 1987). While BPR can be enriched by the experience of work redesign and organizational development, re-engineering's main thrust is on dramatic improvement in performance and does not focus on the job and employee. To improve an antiquated process radically, such as the Ford Motor Company's accounts payable process discussed above, it may be necessary to alter a job in a way that may or may not be conducive to employee satisfaction.

The focus of information system development is on the development of a viable information system and not the radical improvement of the underlying process (Fiedler et al., 1994). While it is possible to use structured system development techniques (such as data flow diagrams, entity relationship diagrams and system flow charts) to identify the need for process change (Colten, 1984; Burch 1992), these techniques often build on the documentation of current processes (Davenport, 1993). Further, while systems professionals are aware of the need to make major changes to business processes, they rarely have the required management and user support for radical change. Even when soft systems methods are applied to address managerial complexity, the primary focus is on information technology development and not on process change (Galliers, 1993; Lambert & Peppard, 1993).

It is also interesting to note that systems development efforts tend to be within functional boundaries. Many systems are developed as the result of requests from functional managers in, say, accounting, marketing or production. Seldom is a cross-functional initiative launched (although this might be changing with the proliferation of databases and corporate wide IS architectural planning). For example, if Ford Motor had adopted the traditional systems development methods, they may have implemented three separate information systems automating inventory, purchasing and accounts payable operations (Hammer, 1990). When and if radical change does occur from IT implementation, it is usually the result of a secondary unplanned effect of the technology (Malone & Rockart, 1991; Sproull & Kiesler, 1991a, 1991b).

The organizational innovation literature has concentrated on determining what context or environment will foster innovation. Some researchers in this area have focused on the distinction between incremental and radical innovation. Incremental innovations were defined as consisting of only a small departure from existing procedures, and radical innovations represented a fundamental change from traditional practices (Ettlie, 1983; Damanpour, 1991). Researchers have found that a strategy for technology innovation, management support of change and technological expertise was associated with radical change, and that decentralisation and structural complexity were related to incremental change (Ettlie et al., 1984; Dewar & Dutton, 1986).

Each of these streams of research helps us to define and understand BPR. Organizational development and work redesign deals with process change and can contribute methods for building employee support and team coordination. Information systems development uses IT to improve processes and can supply techniques for using technology and analyzing processes. Finally, innovation research deals with radical change and can help define the desired characteristics of a supportive environment. However, it can be concluded from this review that none of these fields of research has concentrated fully on the radical process change and improvement that are the driving force behind BPR's growing pervasiveness. The next section will propose a framework for classifying BPR.

## Types of business process redesign

Re-engineering can vary from projects aimed at relatively small isolated processes to attempts to alter entire industries. Because the scope of processes and the nature of re-engineering can vary drastically, different types of BPR need to be defined. This study has adopted a framework that divides re-engineering into process change that occurs: (a) within functional areas (intrafunctional); (b) between functional areas (interfunctional); or (c) between organizations (interorganizational) (Hall et al., 1993; Swatman et al., 1994). This is consistent with Wilkinson (1991) who suggested that re-engineering can occur either within a function, across functions, or external to the organization affecting the supply chain of the business. Also, this framework can be related to Venkatramen's (1991) multilevel model of IT-induced reconfiguration. Venkatramen (1991) described his lowest level as 'localized exploitation, concerned with the exploitation of IT within business functions such as manufacturing or marketing' and an upper level as 'business network redesign, concerned with the reconfiguration of the scope and tasks of the business network involved in the creation and delivery of products and services' (pp 127–128).

The re-engineering of claim processing at a casualty insurance company, for instance, could occur intrafunctionally in customer service by redesigning the linear processing of customer claims and requests to take advantage of electronic documents and shared databases, interfunctionally by allowing simultaneous processing of electronic claims by multiple departments, or interorganizationally by eliminating the mailing of paper documents to an automotive repair shop. For example, one insurance company is considering BPR of its entire claims processing at every level.

Through the use of remote tracking and sensors, the company could be made aware of an accident and dispatch a claims adjuster to the accident site. The adjuster could call an ambulance and a tow truck, issue a claims check and even drive the customer to work. At this level, BPR not only reduces the time and number of people necessary to file a claim, but also provides a new distinct level of service to the customer (Alberthal, 1992).

All things being equal, it could be argued that these three types of re-engineering would also tend to vary by their degree of strategic importance (Teng et al., 1994b). Processes that cross organizational boundaries might tend to be more strategic to a corporation than those within the organization that cross functional boundaries (Johnston & Vitale, 1988; Swatman et al., 1994). The least strategic would generally be processes that are contained within a functional area (Wilkinson, 1991; Venkatraman, 1991; Hall et al., 1993).

This paper empirically explores the important phenomenon of re-engineering. Based on the notions that BPR is planned, and involves radical change of processes with major improvement in performance, it represents a strategic undertaking for the enterprise. It would be expected that corporate strategy has a relationship with strategic endeavours such as BPR. In addition, the importance of IT as a major enabler of BPR would suggest that a well-orchestrated re-engineering effort should involve IT-enabled BPR projects that were well-integrated with corporate strategy.

## Strategy and BPR

Organizational strategy can be defined as the establishment of the basic long-term objectives of an enterprise, and the adoption and commitment of resources to a course of action intended to obtain these corporate objectives (Chandler, 1962). Strategy permeates the organization, defining both the structure and performance of the corporation, and it is through strategy that management attempts to determine the nature of the organization (Grant & King, 1982; Tavakolian, 1989; Das et al., 1991). BPR, through significant changes in process structure, has the potential for changing the nature of the organization. This would indicate that it is important for BPR efforts to be closely linked to corporate strategy (Earl, 1994; Hall et al., 1993). The importance of strategy to BPR is illustrated by the interfunctional BPR effort at a division of Xerox. When Rank Xerox, UK, undertook a comprehensive re-engineering programme, it started by having the senior management team reappraise its external environment and corporate strategy, and then identified the key processes required to achieve that strategy. A task force was then established to define the information requirements of each process as a part of re-engineering the organization's interfunctional processes (Davenport & Short, 1990).

If strategy is the guiding force behind an organization, then corporations with certain strategies would be more likely to adopt and commit the resources necessary for carrying out BPR than those with other strategies. As Michael Earl (1994) noted, BPR can 'be seen as grounded in the resource or capacity school of strategy, concerned inter alia with how do we organize resources to achieve sustainable advantage?' (p 10). To achieve the objective of dramatic performance improvement associated with BPR, an organization must commit not only the resources necessary to implement the enabling technology but also the management support required to bring about the radical change associated with changing the nature of the process. To change the nature of a business process, management may need to alter not only the structure and skills associated with the process but also the roles, incentives and possibly even the shared values of the corporation (Hall et al., 1993). Thus, it can be anticipated that organizations with more aggressive or proactive strategies would be more willing to embrace a course of action and absorb the pain that can be associated with the radical and fundamental change of BPR.

While strategy can be conceptualised broadly to include a wide array of characteristics that can be related to the entire operations of the organization, it may be useful to focus on those aspects of corporate strategy that appear to be more closely associated with a corporation's motivation for carrying our BPR. As noted earlier, one of the major factors contributing to the current interest in BPR is the increasingly competitive environment of most organizations (Earl, 1994), as such, it is reasonable to assume that how an organization chooses to compete can influence its approach toward BPR.

Porter's (1980, 1985) typology for corporate strategy concentrates on classifying the competitive component of the overall strategy in terms of how the organization attempts to position its products or services to compete. This typology would appear to be especially relevant in the current environment of increased competition and is utilised in this study.

Porter (1980, 1985) classifies a corporate competitive strategy in terms of its concentration on either product/service differentiation or lowering cost to provide a competitive advantage in the market place. Corporations choose to compete with either of two mutually exclusive competitive thrusts: (1) cost leadership, through an aggressive development of efficient facilities and cost controls; (2) product or service differentiation by the development of a unique product/service design, customer service or dealer network (Cash et al., 1992;

Seev, 1994). Porter (1980, 1985) also considers whether the cost or differentiation strategy is focused on a narrow segment of the market which he describes as a focus strategy. This study will concentrate on the distinctive cost and differentiation strategy groups. It is important to note that while this scheme for classifying corporate competitive strategies has been widely used and shown to be valid (Govindarajan, 1986; Segev, 1989), other typologies might also be equally appropriate (Venkatraman, 1989).

This typology of examining a cost versus a differentiation competitive strategy has been widely adopted in MIS (Porter & Millar, 1985; Das et al., 1991) and has been used in a number of empirical studies (Wiseman, 1985; Bergeron et al., 1991). Research has shown cost and differentiation groups form opposite extremes in strategic choices and, if linked to IS planning, would be expected to show the most pronounced effects on IS project choices and outcomes (Segev, 1989; Tavakolian, 1989; Roberts et al., 1990; Das et al., 1991). This is reflected in the observation that ‘applications supporting a low-cost strategy are substantially different from applications supporting a differentiation strategy’ (Parsons, 1983, p 14). For example, an information system designed to support the elaborate logistical requirements necessary for customised manufacturing might be required for a differentiation strategy but would be unnecessarily costly and complex for a standardised manufacturing facility with a cost strategy.

Organizations attempt BPR to achieve dramatic improvements in performance. The improvement is obtained by altering processes to take advantage of the attributes of information technology that allow the desired outcome to be achieved more effectively. Re-engineering is enabled by the ability of information technology to eliminate or integrate steps in a process, collapse time and space, and support decisions by reducing the costs associated with production, coordination information (Davenport, 1993; Earl, 1994). Through these various forms of process change, an organization may be able to enhance its cost or differentiation advantages. For example, it is possible that cost savings may be the primary benefit of using information technology to eliminate steps in an interfunctional process. Differentiation benefits, on the other hand, can be associated with eliminating steps in an interorganizational process or intrafunctional process. In other words, it is possible that intrafunctional, interfunctional and interorganizational BPR may have different relationships from the corporate competitive strategy of a firm (as illustrated in Figure 1).

## IS-business planning integration

As an unplanned endeavour can be very counterproductive and costly, an organization needs to direct its investment in IS by aligning information systems strategy with overall corporate strategy (Tavakolian, 1989; Premkumar & King, 1992; Galliers, 1993). Studies have consistently shown that the success of IS planning and applications is affected by the relationship between IT and corporate strategy (Tavakolian, 1989; Lederer & Sethi, 1991, 1992; Sabherwal & King, 1992). However, the need for IS and corporate strategy alignment has not always been recognised. Historically, IT was viewed as a support function that had little to contribute to the overall strategy of the firm. This philosophy of indifference to IT has been replaced in many successful firms by the recognition that IT strategy and corporate strategy should be integrated (Neo, 1988). One way of accomplishing this integration is through effective strategic IS planning. Such integration between corporate and IS planning would enable IS projects to be guided by corporate strategy (King, 1978). In the rest of the paper we will refer to this integration as IS-business planning integration.

It is proposed that the base relationship between strategy and BPR that was investigated by Hypothesis Set 1 will be facilitated by IS-business planning integration (Earl, 1994). While it has been recognised that cost and differentiation strategies consist of distinctly different approaches to the market place (Porter, 1980, 1985; Porter & Millar, 1985), it is also important to realise that they may be facilitated by different levels of information systems (Parsons, 1983; Miller, 1989). If there is not enough integration between IS and corporate planning then there is a risk that a firm pursuing a cost strategy might have a prohibitively expensive system, while a firm with a differentiation strategy might have a system that fails to provide an adequate level of information to support product differentiation (Parsons, 1983). It is important that organizations match their corporate strategy to their information system strategy to ensure the availability of appropriate information systems resources to support corporate desires. Therefore, if BPR is related to a corporate competitive strategy, it would be reasonable to expect that the strength of that relationship would be moderated by the linkage between IS and corporate planning as illustrated in Figure 2. It is also possible that the degree of moderation may vary by type of BPR owing to the variation in strategic importance of the underlying process being re-engineered. It would be anticipated that the more strategic the application, the more important IS-business planning integration. For example, if an interorganizational project directly influences suppliers' and customer coordination, it may be critical for strategy alignment that IS and business planning are integrated. However, it may be possible to align corporate and IS strategy for an isolated intrafunctional project through organizational regulations and standards, making IS-business planning integration less important.

Based on the above discussion the following exploratory null hypotheses are proposed:

Hypotheses Set 2: The relationship between corporate competitive strategy and the extent of BPR will not be affected by the strength of the IS-business planning integration.

$H_{o}$ -2a: The relationship between corporate competitive strategy and the extent of intrafunctional BPR projects will not be affected by the strength of the IS–business planning integration.

$H_{o}$ -2b: The relationship between corporate competitive strategy and the extent of interfunctional BPR projects will not be affected by the strength of the IS-business planning integration.

$H_{o}$ -2c: The relationship between corporate competitive strategy and the extent of interorganizational BPR projects will not be affected by the strength of the IS–business planning integration.

## Methodology

To test the proposed hypotheses, a survey instrument was developed. The survey instrument was developed through an iterative process of review and refinement. This process included a series of interviews and pretests with IS executives that focused on issues of instrument clarity, item wording and validity. To ensure that the respondents understood the BPR concept as discussed in this paper, each subject was exposed to the construct in the cover letter and was given a concise definition preceding the individual questions. In addition, to clarify the BPR concept, it was defined and contrasted to IS automation projects. The subjects were told that BPR is the use of information technology to overhaul radically or change business processes, while automation is the computerisation of existing business processes with minor if any change to the original process. IS executives were then asked to allocate the total percentage of IS projects that they had worked on for the last twelve months between those that occur within functional areas, between functional areas and across organizational boundaries. This study was carried out at the organizational unit of analysis and all constructs (corporate strategy, IS-business planning integration, extent of each type of BPR) are at this level, therefore the percentage of IS projects was used to measure the extent of BPR (intrafunctional, interfunctional and interorganizational) used by the whole organization.

To measure corporate competitive strategy, categorical questions were used. The use of categorical scales to measure the elusive and complex strategy constructs is common (Segev, 1987; Tavakolian, 1989). In this study the nominal measure was adopted from Grover (1993). IS-business planning integration was measured via a four-item scale that examined awareness, specificity, participation and strategy formulation between IS and corporate strategy. This scale was adopted from Premkumar and King (1992) and was measured using a seven-point scale. The study variables and their measures are summarised in Table 1.

A random sample of three-hundred vice presidents and directors of IS from Information Week's list of 500 most progressive users of information technology was mailed a questionnaire. These corporations had been identified as progressive users of IT based on their annual IT expenditures. Given the newness of the re-engineering phenomena, progressive users were surveyed in an attempt to obtain respondents that had experienced process redesign. Five surveys were returned because the individual no longer worked there and fifty-nine were received for an effective response rate of $20\%$ . A test for non-response bias was conducted by comparing all of the early and late respondents' answers, and no significant difference was detected.

## Results

Through an iterative process the questionnaire was tested, reviewed and refined to provide clarity and understandability to the respondent. Construct validity of IS-business planning was evaluated through factor analysis. The four-item scale loaded on a single factor with an eigenvalue of 2.83. As noted in Table 1, the alpha coefficient was an acceptable 0.87 for the scale (Nunnally, 1978). The percentages of total IS projects classified provide indication of face validity for the dependent variables. For instance, intrafunctional automation was the most prevalent form of automation. This is consistent with expectations that traditional systems development typically focuses on projects within functional areas.

The respondents represented a cross-section of industries. The largest number of subjects were in the manufacturing industry (44.1%), while 20.6% were involved in the service industry. An equal number of respondents were in the commercial utilities and financial/insurance industries (11.8%). The other respondents were in the banking (8.8%) and the transportation industries (3%). Also, 80% of the respondents were vice presidents or directors of IS, thereby indicating that the sample represented a high level of seniority which is appropriate given the strategic focus of the study.

As shown in Table 2, this study found that interfunctional BPR made up, on average, 21% of all IS projects and was the most prevalent form of BPR carried out by responding organizations. Intrafunctional BPR projects made up, on average, 12% of the IS projects carried out, while interorganizational BPR was carried out only 10%, on average. While BPR has become quite prevalent, it is important to note that it has only been in existence for a few years. Wide variance found in the responses may be a manifestation of the newness of the BPR phenomenon. In other words, some companies are carrying out a great deal of BPR, while others have just started.

There were no significant differences between the extent of interorganizational BPR for either competitive strategy, so the null hypothesis $H_{o}-1c$ was not rejected.

Hypothesis Set 2 ( $H_{o}$ -2a, $H_{o}$ -2b, $H_{o}$ -2c) was tested to determine if IS–business planning integration would moderate the effect that corporate strategy had on BPR. That is, IS–business planning integration would affect the direction or strength of the relationship between corporate strategy and BPR. A moderator effect is supported if these conditions are satisfied:

(1) the independent and moderator variables are not related (corporate strategy and IS-business planning integration were not significantly correlated; r = 0.13, p = 0.18 – this indicates that multicollinearity is not a limiting factor in the regression);

(2) the product of the moderator and independent variables is significant when the dependent variable is regressed on the independent, moderator and moderator-independent product (shown in Table 4) (Cleary & Kessler, 1982; Cohen & Cohen, 1983; Baron & Kenney, 1986).

## Discussion

This study examined the relationship between corporate competitive strategy and each of the three types of BPR. The results of hypotheses testing are summarised in Table 5 and discussed below.

## Intrafunctional BPR

The study results indicate that more companies with differentiation strategies were carrying out intrafunctional BPR than those with cost strategies. The tendency for organizations that follow a differentiation strategy to have decentralised management, IS departments and funding (Segev, 1989; Tavakolian, 1989) might favour intrafunctional BPR. The financial support for BPR may come from the departmental level in organizations with differentiation strategies and the departments might be reticent to spend their funds outside or across functional boundaries.

The results indicated the lack of either a moderating or direct effect of IS-business planning integration on intrafunctional BPR. It is possible that the 12% of all IS projects that make up intrafunctional BPR efforts are initiated and funded from the bottom up by departments and users. Further, the relatively narrow scope of intrafunctional processes may have less strategic significance than the other two types of processes.

## Interfunctional BPR

As shown in Table 5, both the alternative hypotheses were supported for interfunctional BPR. The study supported the contention that a corporation with a cost strategy would tend to do more interfunctional BPR and that this relationship becomes stronger with IS-business planning integration. Organizations with a cost strategy tend to have a centralised corporate and IS structure (Segev, 1989; Tavakolian, 1989). The centralised authority associated with a cost strategy (Segev, 1989) could facilitate the implementation of interfunctional BPR. Cost strategists are continuously searching for opportunities to reduce the cost of products and services. The ability of interfunctional BPR to provide opportunities for cost savings through the reduction of redundancies and inefficiencies across functional interfaces of processes may explain why it was the most prevalent form of BPR which made up 21% of all of the respondents' IS projects.

It is conceivable that interfunctional BPR would be useful for almost any corporate strategy, however to complete the process of radical change across functional boundaries successfully, a project needs clear management support to overcome parochial ownership of subprocesses. The integration of IS planning and business strategy would be especially important to fund and identify the larger, more strategic projects that traversed functional boundaries.

## Interorganizational BPR

The study found that interorganizational BPR made up 10% of all IS projects and was not related to a particular corporate competitive strategy or moderated by IS-business planning integration. It is possible that the lack of relationship between competitive strategy and interorganizational BPR is because this type of BPR can be driven by either a cost or differentiation strategy. For example, many interorganizational systems using EDI technologies enable corporations to differentiate their services. However, certain interorganizational systems might also be motivated by a cost strategy. For example, General Motors Corporation utilises electronic links to its suppliers to shop around for the lowest price.

## Conclusion

Business process redesign has been widely accepted as a means for leveraging the power of information technology to change organizational processes radically, resulting in substantial improvements in corporate effectiveness and efficiency. It is critical that BPR be coordinated with strategy, because BPR is radical change and has the ability to alter drastically both the competitive and operational nature of the corporation. This study examined the relationships between competitive strategy, BPR and business–IS planning integration.

The study surveyed top IS managers in corporations that were recognised as progressive users of IT. It was found that the most common form of BPR, interfunctional BPR, was associated with a cost strategy. Further, the positive relationship between cost strategy and interfunctional BPR was moderated by IS-business planning integration. While interorganizational BPR was not related to a specific strategy, it was correlated directly with IS-business planning integration.

The results of this study have many implications for practising managers. Some of the greatest opportunities for improvement using BPR are associated with the redesign of processes that cross functional boundaries.

This suggests that organizations should identify and target cross-functional processes as primary candidates for re-engineering, as they might have the potential for maximum performance gains. While a cost strategy can be expected to facilitate this important type of BPR, maximising its favourable impact requires close coordination between corporate and IS planning. One way for IS planners to accomplish this is to be integrally involved in corporate steering committees set up to guide BPR efforts. It is possible that corporations with a differentiation strategy will find they are missing opportunities to carry out interfunctional BPR and they should supply the adequate financial and political resources necessary for this type of re-engineering. The study suggests that no matter what strategy a corporation deploys, it must strive to integrate IS and business planning to take advantage of interorganizational BPR.

This study has attempted carefully to define BPR in contrast to traditional automation. Future studies can build upon this characterisation of BPR. However, as researchers we need to be cautious about the loose labelling of 'faddish' terms that can potentially compromise the accuracy of empirical work. In addition, future researchers may wish to validate our findings using different populations and richer instrumentation. For example, the distinction between smaller and larger projects, which is not made in the current study, can be explicitly recognised. Also, the dichotomous competitive strategy used in this study can be improved upon with items that capture the diversity of the construct.

This is an early study of an important new phenomenon whose characteristics may change over time as BPR techniques and methodologies mature. The study was also limited by its relative small sample size which may adversely impact its generalisability. As an exploratory study it was also limited by the lack of previous theoretical and empirical work. Research in the future should attempt to build on this study and the work done in organizational development, innovation, systems development and other fields both to develop theoretical foundations for BPR and to identify prescriptive guidelines. To provide prescriptive suggestions, researchers will need to expand the scope of this study to examine factors both ‘up-stream’ and ‘downstream’ from those identified in this study (see Figure 4). For example, How will external factors (such as economic cycles, industrial structures, nationalism, competitive intensity and environmental uncertainty) influence BPR? or How will factors internal to the organization (such as corporate culture, IT infrastructure and organizational slack) effect BPR?

Future studies also need to examine how organizations not only carry out successful BPR, but also how they deal with BPR failures. With radical change like that associated with BPR, it may be impossible to go back to pre-BPR practices, therefore studies need to examine how to survive a failed BPR project. Ultimately, organizations need to learn how to prepare for perpetual BPR, by establishing a corporate environment that has the ability to recognise new IT and associated opportunities to redesign their business processes on a continuing basis. It is vital that organizations learn how to motivate and manage employees to think in terms of constant change and productive chaos.
