# A taxonomy of information systems applications: the benefits' evaluation ladder

B. FARBEY $^{1}$ , F. F. LAND $^{2}$ and D. TARGETT $^{1}$

$^{1}$ University of Bath, UK and $^{2}$ London School of Economics, London WC2A 2AE, UK

One of the prime reasons for the deployment of information technology in organizations is the expectation of a positive return on the investment. But the returns from different types of application vary both in terms of the ability to provide straightforward measurement of the costs and benefits, and in the degree of certainty with which estimates of costs and benefits will be realised. This paper provides a framework for classifying applications in the form of an eight-rung ladder, each rung of which represents a class of application. The way in which applications need to be handled and the methods by which their impact can be evaluated changes from rung to rung. Moving up the ladder increases the complexity of evaluation and the degree of risk and uncertainty, but at the same time also increases the potential returns on the investments. The ladder is different from conventional ‘strategic IT’ frameworks: it is a framework for action as well as understanding; it specifically relates to evaluation; and it does not imply any predetermined or time-based sequence of progress.

## Introduction

Every organization is subject to a variety of forces which provide the triggers for change. Pressure for change may come from many sources – some originating inside the organization and some from the organization's environment. The reactive organization responds to the forces for change. The proactive organization may generate its own internal dynamic for change. IT is used in many ways as a response to the triggers and to achieve a wide range of objectives.

IT applications, and their objectives, are therefore a disparate group and this suggests that they may have to be handled in different ways. In particular, organizations may need to employ many evaluation methods, each suitable for evaluating certain types of application. Indeed, type of application and type of objective prove to be two of the most influential factors in the choice of evaluation method. Classifying the uses of information systems may therefore be of fundamental importance in selecting suitable evaluation methods.

Classifications of organizations and their IT applications are plentiful. In considering the relevance of existing frameworks to the task of selecting an appropriate evaluation method, three of their most common features suggest that their usefulness may be limited. To illustrate these features, three celebrated frameworks spanning almost two decades of research into the impact of IT will be used as examples.

The first feature is that many of the frameworks relate to time and seem to suggest that organizations should follow some logical, even deterministic, path through the framework. One of the earliest frameworks was Nolan's stages of growth model (Nolan, 1973). He suggested that there was a pattern of IT expenditure which formed an S-curve of IT budgets against time. Each section of the curve had certain characteristics in terms of application type, management style and control of IT expenditure. He envisaged an organization going through all stages from the earliest to maturity in a strict sequence of originally four and later seven steps. With hindsight one can indeed detect an apparent pattern of application types. However, the process is not deterministic: if there are causal factors they may be more to do with the availability of technology and the competitive environment. Nor is the process strictly ordered: advanced organizations today still choose to develop cost-saving applications. A 1988 study found that nearly 70% of applications are still based on cost-savings (Nairn, 1988) and this is hardly likely to have decreased through the ensuing recession.

The second feature is that the frameworks are usually concerned with the strategic implications of IT rather than its management. A decade after the first Nolan model, McFarlan (1984) developed his grid to help organizations decide whether IT had strategic relevance for them. The grid is a $2 \times 2$ matrix which compares current dependence on IT with the future competitive importance of IT and, as a result, positions organizations as ‘strategic’, ‘turnaround’, ‘factory’ or ‘support’. It is anticipated that, over time, organizations will move from the factory/support side to strategic/turnaround. The grid is therefore concerned with strategic positioning. However, recent research suggests that many failed IT applications are failures not because of any lack of strategic understanding, but because organizations’ decision-making processes are inadequate (Farbey et al., 1994). An over-concentration on strategic issues may have left a gap in organizations’ capabilities at the decision-making and evaluation level.

The third feature is that the frameworks are intended to increase understanding and awareness rather than be frameworks for action. Recently the MIT90s Research Programme (Venkatraman, 1991) proposed the five levels graph which relates degree of transformation to a range of benefits. The purpose of positioning an organization at one of the five levels is that the impact of IT-induced transformation can be understood by its management. This is valuable but there is still a need for action-oriented frameworks. In a critique of IT frameworks Doyle (1991) has questioned their real value. Research into the attitudes of senior general and IT managers (Brady et al., 1992) also revealed a demand for frameworks which could take understanding and awareness through to action and practice.

This paper proposes a model which attempts to overcome these obstacles. It is neither time-based nor ordered; it relates specifically to the needs of evaluation; it is an actionable framework. The paper sets out the model and describes each component in turn. It is illustrated, first, for clarification, by well-known IT case studies and, second, to demonstrate current relevance, by examples from the authors' own study of evaluation practices (Farbey et al., 1993).

## The benefits' evaluation ladder

The model postulates a kind of ladder. Each rung represents a type of change and hence a type of application. Moving up the ladder, each rung represents increasing potential benefits, but also increasing uncertainty on outcomes, increasing risk of failure and increasing difficulty of communicating the 'case' for change to relevant stakeholders. The focus of evaluation and the appropriate evaluation techniques are different for each rung of the ladder. Whereas precise quantification of costs and benefits is possible near the bottom of the ladder, the higher rungs rely more on experimental and judgemental processes. Risk assessment becomes an important component of evaluation near the top of the ladder. At the top of the ladder, decisions require not merely the consent of the top management team: the projects can only succeed with their continuous involvement. They represent some of the most complex and difficult activities faced by senior management. Table 1 illustrates the ladder.

The ladder format is not intended to suggest that it is the task of organizations to climb the ladder step by step. It does not represent a start-up situation in which an organization develops its IT applications from scratch. Instead it relates to a continuing state where the organization has already developed systems and now moves to whichever types of application best suit its circumstances. The ladder representation implies that applications at each level are either more complex, more risky or more subject to business judgement than those below. It is not a strict scale, but generally the higher the rung, the greater the difficulty and risk but the greater the potential reward, i.e. it is easier and more painful to fall off when you are near the top.

Each rung of the ladder is now explained in more detail.

## First rung: mandatory changes

The bottom rung represents forced and mandatory changes; forced in the sense that the organization has to change to survive in a competitive market, mandatory in the sense of external agents enforcing change by new laws or regulations. Decisions are confined to choosing from alternative designs, with perhaps some flexibility as to timing and the degree to which the rules are to be implemented. The task of evaluation is to provide technical assessments and costings of the alternative solutions. Three types of forced change affect information systems decisions:

(1) If an enterprise is under competitive pressure, perhaps because competitors have introduced new methods or improved customer service, it may be a strategic necessity to follow the competitor's lead. A classic example is that of retail banks adopting automatic teller machine (ATM) technology, even though it is almost impossible to meet normal investment criteria to justify the investment (Touche Ross, 1985). Similarly, it is strategically necessary for airlines and travel businesses to adopt IT-based reservation systems.

Once the strategic necessity has been recognised, the role of evaluation is to find the ‘best’ system which will provide the required capability. This may result in the choice of a competitor’s system and paying a royalty, as happened when the success of the SABRE system, introduced by American Airlines (AA), drove other airlines to pay a royalty to AA in order to be allowed to use their system (Copeland & McKenney, 1988). Alternatively Intersun, faced by the advantage the TOPS system had given the Thomson Travel organization (Peltu, 1987), decided to develop its own rival system. Unlike the Thomson organization, Intersun made inadequate preparations and the system had to be abandoned.

(2) If an enterprise is forced to make specific technical changes or improvements, adopting IT solutions may become a matter of technological necessity. The pressures may arise outside the organization or within it.

Good examples of external pressure for technical change come from the pressure to stop further deterioration of the environment. Car manufacturers have by law to provide new mechanisms for reducing the output of harmful emissions. These are in turn monitored and controlled by IT products.

Technological obsolescence is probably the most common internal case of technological necessity. It arises when the cost of maintaining existing systems becomes prohibitive or new technology becomes available which clearly out-performs current technology.

The manager of the technical information office in the UK subsidiary of a global manufacturing company was looking for a system to provide technical documentation (Farbey et al., 1993). The existing system for producing documentation was manual. The increasing rate of new product release made the manual system too cumbersome and slow to meet the needs of the business.

The motivation to change came from two sources. First, although it was group policy to provide documentation with every product, the UK subsidiary had not been very assiduous in carrying this through. There was a great deal of pressure from the parent company to bring the UK into line. This meant that there was potentially a lot more ‘cutting and pasting’ work to be done. At the same time the company acquired another very large manufacturer. The two technical information systems departments were to be merged and the need to find a computer-based solution became even more urgent. The manager had no real choice but to computerise.

However, in this instance the manager used what little room he had for manoeuvre to what he saw as his own advantage. He deliberately set up the system so as to be non-compatible with other existing systems, freeing himself from the domination, as he saw it, of the IT people. He was also determined that they would not be responsible for support, since he considered that they had a poor record of serving their customers.

Even in mandatory systems, therefore, there may be an element of choice. In general this might include questions of how? when? and to what extent? In extremes, it is possible to opt out of a mandatory system altogether and face the penalties. Again the role of evaluation is to find the 'best' way of providing the new technology.

(3) Certain changes in information systems are predetermined by regulation and legislation. They are cases of regulatory or legal necessity.

The changes required to meet the conditions of the privacy laws are mandatory and hence the role of evaluation is to assess the best method of implementing the legislation. Because the privacy laws are confined to information held on computer files, the role of evaluation is extended to consider the alternative of returning to a manual filing system. Another example is the rules which regulate a number of aspects of banking practice and procedures laid down by the European Community.

The most widely used methods of evaluation use classic cost-accounting and work-measurement techniques to evaluate the costs of the alternative solutions. However, problems stemming from the uncertainty of identifying the precise costs of innovative technology make the process of choice hazardous and can, as illustrated by the Intersun case, result in choices based on false analysis and therefore inadequate preparation.

## Second rung: automation

The next rung represents applications designed to replace existing methods in order to reduce costs. The process is one of automating existing routines. Historically they were often the first type of applications to use computers. Book-keeping, stock control and personnel records are the standard examples. In the 1950s and 1960s many organizations first used computers as replacements for punched card equipment to carry out exactly the same tasks but at a lower cost. Such applications tend to be concerned with transaction processing and generally handle large volumes of repetitive work loads. They may provide little in the way of new business approaches, add non value to the product or service and hence have a marginal impact on the profitability of the business.

However, automation can have more important effects:

(1) Reducing the cost base of a business may be an important strategic objective, and can provide competitive advantage. The most successful supermarket chain within a market niche may be the one which contrives to squeeze an extra fraction of a percentage of margin from its sales by means of its cost-saving information handling.

(2) Increased labour productivity, in particular the productivity of white collar labour, can dramatically increase the capacity of an enterprise, or indeed an industry, to manage growth. The retail banking sector is an example. The retail banks today handle vastly more transactions than ever before with a static or diminished workforce. At one time it was estimated that the workforce required to process the number of transactions now handled by the banking system would require virtually the whole of the UK's white collar labour.

Many applications which now provide the essential, basic, factory-style transaction-processing of enterprises fall into this category. An interesting modern example concerns an IT vendor specialising in office automation with a salesforce of many thousands. The company found it difficult to keep its salesmen up-to-date with the latest product, price and promotion information which changed on a daily basis. The costs of sending urgent documents by courier and the confusion caused by the wide range of mail deliveries each day led the company to automate this process (Farbey et al., 1993). With the new system, all marketing updates are sent electronically to the laptop computers of individual salesmen, targeting them according to their information needs.

Benefits on this rung are directly attributable to the replacement of the older methods and technology by the newer. The focus of evaluation is on efficiency and the most appropriate evaluation methods rely on classical work measurement and organization & methods techniques, supplemented by some operational research methods (simulation and modelling).

It is worth noting that while for many organizations this kind of application provides the chief justification for the investment in IT-based systems, in many cases the actual out-turn has been disappointing because the estimates of cost-saving have neglected important, but often hidden transaction costs, for example the costs of coordination and troubleshooting.

## Third rung: direct value added systems

The second rung primarily involved the displacement of costs by automating processes. However, information technology provides an opportunity not merely to replace an existing process or operation, but to employ the application to improve the way business is done. The third rung is represented by applications which not only reduce costs but add value directly, often by doing things which were not done before. They are intended to improve some aspects of business performance which have already been identified as 'valuable'. The added value is directly attributable to the IT system. The additional value may be reflected in the increased margin the enterprise achieves on its turnover, or it may result in an increase in market share. Many value added applications automate existing processes and also provide some new features which add value.

An unusual example of value added concerns a payroll processing system which produced a real time printout of earnings in a payment-by-output system. Productivity was enhanced because the workforce could see higher effort reflected in higher earnings.

A very different example is that of a firm of chartered accountants whose main business is carrying out financial audits (Farbey et al., 1993). However management consultancy has recently been added and this is expanding. There are offices in several UK locations. A new system was required to produce the 3,500 annual accounts, reports relating to consultancy and other documents such as marketing material where high-quality output was necessary. The existing system could do the work but the presentational quality was not good and it was difficult to incorporate complicated graphs, figures and tables. The expansion of the consultancy work, as well as increased competition, implied the need to do more than the basic reporting requirements. It was important to add value for clients in terms of more detailed analyses and more sophisticated presentation. The system was approved and installed on this basis, rather than cost saving.

Many operational, transaction processing applications fall into this category. Typical of these applications is order processing, where enhanced facilities – for example, providing small retailers with details of previous purchases – adds value both to the customer by improved customer service, and to the supplier.

It is important to appreciate, however, that the added value does not necessarily accrue to the enterprise. In a highly competitive situation, and where the technology is not protected by regulations or patents, the added value may benefit the consumer rather than the enterprise. Thus ATMs, which clearly add value to the banking system by making bank services available to its clients twenty-four hours a day for seven days a week, have distributed that added value directly to the consumer, and not on the whole to the banks.

Adding value is an example of increasing effectiveness. The focus of evaluation is to assess what value is added and how that value might be distributed. But estimating the impact of the new capability often proves difficult. Thus the banks did not foresee the impact ATMs would have on their clients. The constant availability of the bank's services encouraged clients to go to the bank (the ATM) more frequently and, for example, to withdraw smaller amounts of cash several times per week instead of making one call per week as before. The outcome is that the bank has to process many more transactions without any change in the total value of transactions.

Classical cost-accounting techniques and work-measurement techniques do not provide useful estimates. Probably the best method of assessment is to use experimental techniques: in the case of the banks, field tests to gauge client response to the availability of the ATM service may have provided a better assessment of the actual impact of the innovation. However, competitive pressure may preclude the use of such techniques. Thomson Holidays owe at least some of their success in gaining competitive advantage with their 'TOPS' package holiday booking system because of the careful way they pre-tested the system with selected travel agents before attempting a nation-wide launch of the system (Peltu, 1987).

## Fourth rung: management information and decision support systems

The next rung represents applications which provide information for planning, control and decision-making. These are often directed at the higher levels of management. Although MIS and DSS are different concepts they are placed on the same rung because they present similar problems in terms of evaluation.

In recent years the emphasis has shifted from transaction processing, the automation of existing procedures, to using the technology to enhance the productivity of managers and other professional workers. The systems provide them with ‘better’ information which allows them to improve their control of the business and make more informed decisions. The word ‘better’ may mean more relevant information, more reliable information, more accurate information, more timely information or information presented in a more easily used form. It can mean providing managers with possible scenarios of the future, or of evaluating alternative courses of action put forward by the managers. Such information is expected to improve the way a manager, or group of managers, carries out the tasks of planning, coordinating, controlling and decision-making.

Some systems in this category provide an infrastructure which permits management to coordinate its activities over a wide range. That these systems are more perilous can be illustrated by the case of a large general technical consultancy company which served management and technical functions in many industries of which the major ones were space and defence (Farbey et al., 1993). The company had a Londonbased head quarters division which supported functional divisions in different parts of the world. This division was responsible for installing an international network to improve inter-division communication, for information and documents, nationally and internationally, and promoting increased international team working. The new system allowed an office in one country to have immediate access to reports and papers of projects carried out in any other office. However, although the basic idea was sound, the system was a failure and abandoned within two years of implementation. Investigation revealed that the failure could be traced to poor evaluation and a faulty IT decision-making process.

A very different example is the marketing information which supermarket management obtains through the introduction of electronic point of sale systems. This helps management improve its use of shelf space, ensures fewer stockouts and reduces waste.

The names given to applications in this category include management information systems, decision support systems, expert advisory systems, executive information systems and group decision support systems. The common features of applications representing the fourth rung is that they provide facilities which add value only if the users of the facilities have the capability (or opportunity) to take advantage of them. The fact that a manager has specified ‘better’ information as a requirement is no guarantee that the better information will improve managerial productivity or add value. In many cases the use of such systems is discretionary, and merely discovering that managers choose to use the system is no guarantee of improved managerial outputs, though the fact that the systems are used in preference to alternative systems suggests that the individual users do value the system.

On this rung the focus of evaluation becomes an assessment of the potential added value framed perhaps by some kind of probability measure or best and worst case analysis. An alternative sometimes used is to ask the information users to assess the value of 'better' information and to back their judgement by 'paying' for the better information. Provided that payment is made from real resources, this is a useful device for profit centres, for example. Further, it establishes a benchmark for subsequent evaluation and an incentive for the information user to achieve the potential benefit. However, viewed from a corporate perspective, the willingness to pay for the facility offers no guarantee that any extra value will accrue to the organization. The mechanism has simply transferred the responsibility for making a decision on the implementation of the new system to the payee.

In practice, evidence that fourth rung systems have appreciably enhanced managerial capability is difficult to obtain (Strassmann, 1985, 1990). One problem is finding appropriate data to evaluate outcomes. Experiments of, for example, the impact of decision support systems on the quality of decisions carried out in controlled conditions can never capture the richness and noisiness of the conditions under which managers have to work. A number of studies attempting to associate managerial productivity with investments in IT have found little evidence that managerial capability is enhanced by the use of IT (Loveman, 1994). The point to be emphasised is that the most important aspect of managerial productivity is that managers should have a high competence in carrying out their substantive tasks – the effective banker is not the one who has the best decision support system, but the one who has the highest skill in carrying out banking tasks. Many authors hint at the importance of managerial capability and flair in determining the quality of managerial decisions (Clemons & Row, 1991, Grindley, 1991; Ciborra, 1994). However, positive empirical evidence is hard to find. Smith (1992) points out: 'We only partially understand what differentiates good from bad problem solving, and our prescriptions and interventions are often inoperable or irrelevant'. Nevertheless it is reasonable to suggest from the limited evidence (see, for example, Webby & O'Connor, 1994) that there is an association between managerial capability and getting value from the use of rung four type systems.

## Fifth rung: infrastructure

The fifth rung is represented by investments which provide a general capability but may not be targeted at any specific application. They are intended to provide the foundation upon which subsequent value-adding applications can be built. Producing no direct benefits to the business, they may therefore not figure prominently in the senior management's value system. A good example is the introduction of office automation into an enterprise. The expectation is that facilities such as local area networks, access to external facilities and databases, electronic mail, work processing, graphics, desktop publishing, the availability of spreadsheet capabilities and local database facilities, will enhance the productivity of the office. In practice, many of these facilities are used as part of the informal, undesigned systems which exist alongside more formal, designed systems in all organizations. Evidence comes from the use people make of electronic mail. Many of the messages, in some cases as many as 50%, are personal messages – conversations between fellow workers (Mackay, 1988).

Actual use of the facilities on offer will evolve through time, and actual use, enhanced efficiency and potential effectiveness have to be estimated on the basis of judgemental predictions. The focus of evaluation turns from specific applications to the capability of an infrastructure to support a range of future activities, and perhaps not to inhibit unforeseen future developments. Evaluation needs to demonstrate the link between the infrastructure investment and subsequent projects whose value to the business can be demonstrated. The complexity of such systems, the amount of information required to make objective judgements and the evaluative nature of the decisions are much greater on this rung than on those below.

Such investments are sometimes seen as necessary in order for the company in question to respond rapidly to any moves by competitors. They can be regarded as buying an option on the future profitability of the firm.

This is well illustrated by the case of a leading finance house providing a range of financial and related services to industry, commerce, the motor and retail trades as well as to the private consumer (Farbey et al., 1993). Recognising that they were less flexible and not able to compete strongly on rates, they decided that they would adopt a strategy of competition on quality of service and speed, coupled with a superior knowledge of and more flexible adaptation to the marketplace. The IT strategy was based on the goals of providing stronger links to customers, using systems that their competitors could not match.

The installed systems provided desktop functionality and a communications infrastructure. This basic infrastructure was used for many purposes. It provided full desktop publishing facilities and two-way communication between the centre and the area offices. Large suppliers were also connected to the system and there was some provision for customers to be connected too. From this base the company were developing executive information systems for their senior executives, and foresaw two major benefits from this initiative. First, the senior executives would be able to 'pick up' their desktops from wherever they were in the company. This was important since they spent much of their time away from their desks. Second, there would be a common pool of management information, leading to more consistent decision making.

A feature of some infrastructure investments is that they provide shared facilities whose use develops at different rates and which provide uncertain benefits. There is nothing new in that. The provision of older types of infrastructure – telephones on every desk, many types of administrative services, transport fleets – has very similar characteristics from the point of view of evaluation.

Of course many decisions in this category do have more precise targets more appropriate to the second or third rung of the ladder. Thus the decision to introduce office automation may include a precise estimate of the impact of the system on the operations of the typing pool, or the savings possible from contract printing by the use of desktop publishing.

Nevertheless, infrastructure type investments are characterised by considerable uncertainty in the kind of impacts the technology will have. More and more of future IT investment is likely to fall into this category – the provision of a range of IT capabilities to enable the enterprise to be responsive to the market and other pressures.

## Sixth rung: inter-organizational systems

The sixth rung is represented by systems which cross organizational boundaries – systems which are shared by two or more organizations, chiefly trading partners. Although the systems are shared, performing functions for all the collaborating organizations, the value added is not necessarily shared equally by all participants.

For example, the most widely discussed inter-organizational systems are electronic data interchange (EDI) systems. There are many examples of EDI systems which have been forced on trading partners by dominant manufacturers as a condition of trading. For the dominated trading partner, for example a supplier to a major retail chain such as Marks and Spencer, the use of EDI can be classed as a rung one mandatory system. In practice even the dominated collaborator can obtain economic benefits from EDI, though it is the major partner who expects to get most value from the system.

Inter-organizational systems have far-reaching consequences for the partners in that they have a major impact on their relationships. Activities previously carried out by one of the partners may migrate to other partners with unexpected and important consequences. One of the most widely discussed examples is American Hospital Supplies (AHS) (Harvard Business School, 1985). AHS put terminals into their customers' offices and permitted the hospitals' purchasing officers to interrogate AHS's inventory. In so doing AHS transferred a significant part of the sales function to the purchaser and discovered subsequently that AHS had become the favoured supplier.

Another example where the initial benefits were overtaken by more important strategic benefits occurred in a defence organization specialising in the design and production of military aircraft equipment (Farbey et al., 1993). The organization's contracting group was responsible for producing proposals and bids for the whole company. This involved liaising with engineers doing the development work and accountants doing the costing. It was the contracting group's overall responsibility to bring together formal proposals to be sent to client organizations, usually government departments in the UK and elsewhere. The group also had to liaise with other defence contractors in the UK and elsewhere with regard to joint projects.

A network was installed, and linked to other networks in the company, and to the systems of potential collaborators. It was originally intended that the network would reduce costs but in the longer term the inter-organizational aspects proved more important. This was because the ability to communicate electronically is now a major criterion for taking part in collaborative international projects, and this collaboration has latterly become the norm in the international defence industry.

Inter-organizational systems evaluation must involve all partners, even the dominated partners. By joining such systems, organizations tie themselves into a standard mode of processing dictated by the joint system. To an extent they lose freedom and flexibility. For inter-organizational systems the focus of evaluation is to balance the loss of total independence against the potential benefits from shared systems and the value of the partnerships underpinned by the systems, where many of these impacts are difficult to assess.

## Seventh rung: strategic systems

The seventh rung represents the strategic use of IT. Strategic use has been defined (Earl, 1989) as:

• gaining competitive advantage

\- improving productivity and performance

\- introducing new ways of managing and organizing

• developing new types of business.

In the public sector, where competitive advantage has perhaps been less relevant, strategic use includes the use of IT to combine the strategic goals of providing improved services to the public, with accountability and management control of the service.

The achievement of strategic use of the technology requires a very close alignment between business strategy and information systems planning. This in turn requires that information systems are seen by senior management to be a strategic resource which must play a role in enabling the business plans to be made effective. Hence the use of IT in this way is characterised by innovation and leadership. But the risks can also be very high. A classic example is the joint venture by Chemical Bank and AT&T to introduce their home-banking product PRONTO. It had to be abandoned in December 1988 with a write-off of an estimated \$70 million in assets. Despite aggressive promotion and technological excellence, PRONTO had attracted only 10,000 subscribers in six years (Wall Street Journal, 1988).

A contrasting case from the same industry sector is revealing. First movers may reap very high rewards – witness Merrill-Lynch and its clients' cash management system (Clemons, 1991). The firm pioneered the concept of combining a charge card, checking account and brokerage service into one product. It breached the traditional boundaries of the banking and securities sectors and, in the event, jumped far ahead of its competitors. Implementation was complex and costly – virtually impossible to justify by the use of conventional methods. Yet its sheer complexity made it difficult to copy and gave Merrill-Lynch a competitive edge. By 1983 Merrill-Lynch had secured a 70% market share.

A modern example comes from a British pharmaceutical company which installed a new information system linking its manufacturing plants, laboratories and marketing departments (Farbey et al., 1993). The cost was less than £1 million. The major benefit was the potential to shorten the licensing period and so bring new drugs to market faster. The value of the benefit was enormous. For a major new drug, each day saved would be worth around £5 million. In addition the system allowed the company to set up a ‘drug database’ which doctors could access from their desks. This service encouraged doctors to buy the company’s products in preference to competitors’ and, it was believed, increased market share.

It is nevertheless true that many of the classic case studies of systems which have been of strategic importance – American Hospital Supplies, McKesson Economist (Clemons & Row, 1988), the Thomson Holidays TOPS system, and many others – started as rung two or rung three systems, and subsequently proved that they had more than operational significance. Their success at a strategic level was due to the fact that senior management realised the important advantage the new systems were offering and in a sense realigned the business around them.

More recently companies have proactively sought to align ITplans with business strategy in order to get the kind of advantages the classic case studies have pointed to. In practice there is as yet little evidence that these companies have been more successful in their use of IT to achieve the goals suggested by Earl than companies which have built systems to achieve apparently lesser goals. It may be that the real value of alignment is in avoiding mistaken investments.

Estimating the out-turn of investments in strategic IT systems is difficult, but must be attempted (Clemons, 1991). Without some process of evaluation, important opportunities may be missed or champions may persuade managements to take actions which may later prove to be catastrophic. Merely ranking projects in some order of preference is better than relying only on hunches. Conventional cost/benefit and return on investment (ROI) methods are rarely suitable for reaching decisions on strategic investments. Indeed some of the systems now seen to confer strategic benefits would have failed any test based on cost/benefit and ROI methods. Evaluation based on a broad appreciation of the business situation, and in particular a focus on the competitive situation, coupled with a tight risk analysis, may be the best way to assess whether or not to invest in an application deemed to provide strategic benefit. Perhaps all such projects should show a clear operational benefit, even if such benefits cannot be justified on a purely ROI basis.

## Eighth rung: business transformation

The top rung of the benefits ladder is represented by the applications of IT which enable changes to take place which transform an enterprise. Business transformations are always strategic in intent. They are often forced on an enterprise by lack of economic success, either because of changes in the economic environment to which the enterprise must respond if it is to survive, or because the enterprise has lost its ability to compete successfully, perhaps because of poor management strategies. More positively, business process re-engineering (BPR) is widely regarded as one of the more important focal points for the deployment of information management (Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1993; IS Analyser, 1993).

A good example of forced transformation is the American automobile industry. In the 1980s the Japanese automobile industry threatened the survival of the major US manufacturers through the productivity of their manufacturing plants and the quality of their products. One major company saw the problem as one of technology and went a long way, at a cost of nearly \$1 billion, to transform its technology. The new plant failed to match the Japanese in productivity or quality. Subsequent examination attempted to understand where the company fell short of its global competitors. As a result of the investigation it was appreciated that transformation must combine changes in technology and changes in management practice with a strong emphasis on transforming human resource management (McKersie & Walton, 1991; Osterman, 1991). Another major manufacturer went through a similar diagnostic process and as a result drew up a programme of more than 80 separate projects ranging from programmes targeted at improving the information flow between design and production engineering and manufacturing, in order to reduce the time taken to bring a new model to the market, to new systems which would enhance the company's relations with its dealer network.

A good example of a company failing to compete is an insurance company which had been market leader but had lost its number one position (Land et al., 1983). Its management for some years had relied on the company's previous success and had failed to appreciate market changes and the rise of competitors. Its procedures, for example, the payment of claims, had not changed for years and had left the company with a poor reputation. A change in senior management was called for and the new management instituted an overall review of management style and practices. It determined that major transformations were needed. Most of these used IT to support or facilitate the changes.

In all these cases, benefits stem from the transformation as a whole. IT provides only one component of what is often a complex series of changes. It is not possible to attribute a portion of the benefit gained to any one factor. The transformation is enabled by the synergy stemming from all the activities needed to make the transformation work. For example, the introduction of just in time (JIT) into a manufacturing company involves changes to large parts of the inward logistics of the enterprise. Inventory management, warehousing, purchase ordering, supplier relations, inward distribution and communications, all play a part in the transformation. IT is involved in all of these and the effective use of IT is a necessary component of the transformation. But companies have to recognise that successful transformations are rare, and that an organization with the need to transform faces some of the most difficult problems its management has ever had to cope with.

The emphasis of evaluation is on the total package, rather than on any component. For IT the important factors become the choice of the most appropriate technological solutions to enable the major changes to be carried through.

## Conclusion

Rung one (mandatory change) has the least uncertainty. The system is following a set pattern and probably both the structure of the system and its technology are predetermined. Rung two (automation) is also highly constrained since an existing process is being computerised but such systems give rise to more uncertainty than rung one because they are less precise: there is no exact predetermined pattern. Rung three (value added systems) goes beyond automation and cost-saving efficiencies to improved effectiveness. Rung four (MIS and DSS) represents a further increase in uncertainty since the benefits are second order, being dependent on managers' skills at interpreting information. Rung five (infrastructure) has potential benefits which may be more than second order. The success of the infrastructure depends upon other secondary applications being undertaken and they may themselves have many of the uncertainties associated with rungs one to four. Rung six (inter-organizational systems) also involves the provision of infrastructures but with the added uncertainties of extending beyond one organization. Rung seven (strategic systems) is aimed at the competitive environment. It therefore goes further than rung six in that the systems must relate to a variety of other organizations: rivals, collaborators, suppliers and so on. Rung eight (transformation) is the most uncertain. It could incorporate any of the previous rungs. However, since transformation is taken to mean radical transformation involving innovation and creativity of a different order to that of the bottom rungs, it has been allocated a rung at the top of the ladder.

So, the rungs of the ladder reflect different levels of management complexity, uncertainty and business risk, implying in turn a different evaluation emphasis for each rung. Movement up the ladder changes the emphasis from precise quantification to more judgemental evaluation styles. This does not mean that quantification becomes irrelevant, but that it should be mediated by other techniques, for example simulation, to handle the risk. The involvement of senior management is always important but the higher the rung the more issues there are for the senior management team and the more closely involved it should be.

Associating any particular IT application with a specific rung can create problems. In the real world there are very few pure cases and there may well be arguments for putting an application on more than one rung. Two principles should be followed. First, ceteris paribus, evaluators should choose the higher rung on the grounds that it is better to over-estimate rather than under-estimate the risk. Second, where higher rung applications have components which belong more properly to lower rungs, the evaluation of such a component should be carried out with the emphasis appropriate to its proper rung. For example, a major change programme in an organization may include the establishment of a personnel database. That database will be subject to the privacy laws and the component of the system which has to comply with the privacy laws should be treated as a bottom rung application. The emphasis must be on choosing the best way of implementing the relevant conditions.

The value of the ladder, however, goes beyond the issue of choosing appropriate evaluation methods. Recent research is suggesting that a determinant of success or failure of IT investments is the poor quality of organizations' IT decision-making processes (Farbey et al., 1994). These processes are the means by which organizations develop strategic ideas and turn them into practical projects, deciding how to create, develop and manage them. They form the vital link between strategy and implementation. Evaluation is at the centre of these processes and it is important to carry it out in the most appropriate way. If evaluation is treated simply as a way of moving projects past organizational obstacles such as steering committees and financial

hurdle rates, there are likely to be two consequences. First, the true purpose of evaluation, assessing the worth of a system and its impact on the organization and beyond, will be missed. Second, the whole IT decision-making process will be made less effective because it will lack the information that proper evaluation would bring. Later difficulties, or even complete failure, may be blamed on implementation when in fact the seeds of failure were sown much earlier.
