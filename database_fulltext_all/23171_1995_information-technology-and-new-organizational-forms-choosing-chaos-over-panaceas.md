# Information technology and new organizational forms: choosing chaos over panaceas

R BASKERVILLE $^{1}$ and S SMITHSON $^{2}$

$^{1}$ School of Management, Binghamton University, Binghamton, New York 13902-6015, USA and $^{2}$ London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

This paper examines five aspects of the critical relationship between information technology (IT) and new emergent forms of organizations. First, there appears to be an unhealthy tendency among IT professionals to elevate any single, highly successful practical experience instantly into an overarching paradigm for managerial success. Second, there is a corresponding tendency for IT researchers to focus their efforts on the search for the single universal formula that will transform any type of organization in any situation from mediocrity to excellence. Third, IT researchers assign a pre-eminence to IT in organizational transformation that neglects many other important social and environmental factors. Fourth, management theorists seem unable to cope with the unpredictability, the multivariate nature and the ‘messiness’ of human organizations in cultural contexts. Even studies that make qualitative allowances will still imply a surreal causal analysis that is mostly speculation. Fifth, several critical factors influence the interaction of changes in IT and emergent organizational forms: these include organizational learning, structural premise and power.

## Introduction

World markets have entered a period of continuous transformation as a result of deregulation, the entry of new players, increasing customer demand and radical changes in technology. This increasing global competition and the accompanying transition from an industrial to an information economy have produced powerful forces of change in many countries. There has been a tremendous increase in competitive pressures. Across the globe, once safe and sleepy markets have been transformed into complex, molten business environments where change and uncertainty seem to be the only constants.

In order to survive and prosper in such harsh environments, organizations need to be highly flexible and responsive to the rapid twists and turns of markets and technologies. Innovation, in product and in process, needs to be accompanied by improved quality and customer service as well as deeper cost-reduction. Traditional, hierarchical bureaucracies and Tayloristic management principles seem incapable of inducing this combination of innovation, flexibility and natural efficiency.

We now find previously stable forms of business and government organizations are no longer viable. We have designed our organizations based on a set of fundamental assumptions that no longer hold. The complexity and speed of the technological, social and information revolution have combined to wreck these cherished assumptions and their associated management techniques (Morgan, 1989; Brown & Duguid, 1991; Huber, 1991).

This has led to a major shift in organizational theory. With organizational survival at stake, managers are reaching feverishly for new paradigms that are more practical in this changing world. Management theorists (e.g. Mintzberg, 1983; Handy, 1990) rushed to offer alternative models as organizations began to dismantle rigid hierarchies and shake out unproductive middle managers. In a frenzy of down-sizing, right-sizing, outsourcing and re-engineering, organizations large and small have followed suit by remaking themselves according to the ideals published after the experiments of the practitioners and the observations of the theoreticians.

These myriad new emergent organizational forms have appeared in the literature under a variety of names: ‘adaptive’ (Toffler, 1985), ‘dissipative’ (Leifer, 1989), ‘imaginary’ (Hedberg, 1991), ‘hybrid’ (Powell, 1987), ‘flex-firm’ (Toffler, 1990), ‘virtual’ (Mackenzie, 1986), ‘semi-permanent symbioses’ (Huber, 1991), ‘postmodern’ (Baskerville et al., 1992). Toffler revived Lockheed’s old ‘skunkworks’ hallmark from the SR-71 spyplane team; and others have characterised such organizations as ‘federal’, ‘networked’ and ‘learning’.

They are typically associated with other novel practices such as empowerment and self-management, or the use of cross-functional teams. Furthermore, the radical reorganization of business processes, called forth by business process re-engineering, fits neatly into this climate of change.

Much of the promised success of such new emergent organizational forms seems to rely extensively on the effective use of advanced information technology (IT). In this paper we explore some of the fundamental errors of this proclaimed reliance on IT, the blind alleys in the new models. We also explore some of the new assumptions that underpin the successes of this organizational apocalypse, the promising pathways in the new models.

This paper represents the programme chairs' findings of the IFIP Working Group 8.2 Working Conference on Information Technology and New Emergent Forms of Organizations. This conference took place in Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA, 11–13 August 1994 at the University of Michigan. The Appendix describes the methodology that was used to delineate the findings from the conference sessions, but we should emphasise here that these findings were not reached by the consensus of the conference members, but are the authors' interpretation of the compiled session reviews. The bullets below represent quotes taken directly from the session reviewers who interpreted each session at the conference. Most of these ideas trace through several conference sessions, and it is difficult to attribute these to a single paper or presentation.

We discovered five major findings in the conference sessions. The paper is organized into five sections according to these findings. The first three findings regard essential fallacies that seem to confuse much of the new organizational theory with regard to information systems. These three findings have been grouped into our section on debunking the IT panacea. The final section considers two findings that promise a set of assumptions that may be useful for constructing a solid foundation for the new organizational theory with regard to information systems.

## Information technology and new organizational forms: debunking the IT panacea

There are three major erroneous assumptions that currently confuse the literature on information technology and new organizational forms; these involve the ideology, universality and the cruciality of IT.

## Finding 1

Novel approaches to information systems management may take on ideological textures, almost like religious sects or philosophical schools. Critical thinking is sometimes lost in the enthusiasm and rhetoric over the latest ‘discoveries’.

This complaint is not surprising given the waves of new techniques that crash over and engulf entire societies of managers. Most recently, we have been inundated by ‘total quality management’, ‘continuous quality improvement’ and ‘business process re-engineering’ (BPR). These were preceded by ‘management by objectives’, ‘market-driven product design’ and ‘zero defects’. As one of the most recent offerings, BPR can be singled out with complaints that it is:

\- 'not entirely novel in its approach'

\- ‘not new, and should not be viewed as such’

\- 'fad rather than fact'

\- ‘apocalyptic and quasi-religious’.

Part of the problem with BPR, as with its precursors, is that its rapid popularity quickly leads to misinterpretation. Segments of its market developed, then spread, then shared essential misunderstandings of the term:

\- 'BPR is a free floating concept that allows everyone to grab on and associate it with what they're doing'.

Because the term BPR is losing its original reference, it has begun to iconify a body of more loosely associated ideas, assumptions and motives, and is starting to take on a broader relevance. BPR is now an icon for a broad web of ideas, an inadequately defined school of thought. Because its original definitions ignore this iconification, some of BPR's widely assumed features are, in fact, never openly attributed to it. Its followers take such assumptions with almost religious faith, which cannot be substantiated in practice:

\- 'Can we view BPR as the ideology of the IS area in the same way that customer service is to marketing, and statistical quality control is to production and operations management?'.

\- 'BPR addresses a crisis in business due to pressures from customers, competition and change and does so by empowering workers, but ... none of these factors hold up on inspection'.

Important new techniques like BPR are subject to the bandwagon effect and, perhaps unfortunately, each passenger boarding the bandwagon is bringing their own baggage. It quickly becomes difficult to separate 'pure' BPR from everything else that it iconifies. It could be argued that it is perfectly normal for such approaches to develop through practice as problems and opportunities are encountered in the real world. However, it also becomes difficult to isolate BPR's successes and failures from those caused by labelling every organizational change as BPR for the sake of fashion and status.

## Finding 2

IS researchers seem to be searching for the ‘correct formula’ that will universally improve organizational performance and quality. They quickly overlook the fact that IT creates its own set of new problems.

There are three aspects to this finding that deserve discussion. The first aspect is the idea that a panacea exists that will transform the struggling organization into a revitalised competitive powerhouse. The second aspect concerns the incorrect assumption that a package of changes that revitalises one organization will similarly improve different, or even similar, organizations. The third aspect considers the disregard for the new set of problems that are typically created on the introduction of new information technologies into an organization.

Perhaps the first aspect, this ‘no silver bullet’ caution, is so well-known that we could deem it an ‘old saw’. However, in many areas, this adage is being ignored, and current research and practice are howling louder than ever for its restatement. Brooks (1987) made his famous analogy of a silver bullet (a single development in technology or management) that would instantly slay the nightmarish werewolf (missed schedules, blown budgets and flawed products) with reference to software engineering, but it seems also to fit our purposes:

\- 'Be wary of gurus bearing supposedly new cure-all solutions to life, the universe and new emergent organizational forms. Stay away from fast technology fixes; we'd only have to go cold turkey when we finally realize that technology is only part of the answer, not an answer in itself'.

Considering the second aspect, it seems equally evident that a close examination will reveal that every human organization, being a higher-order biological system, is unique in many senses. Managers and developers should be prepared to adapt every management technique or information technology to match the culture of the unique organization. Such adaptation should consider not just markets, formal structures, policies and resources – but also the organizations' traditions, political patterns and social system:

\- 'If it always worked, there would be no need for an expert to help apply the technique. The failures are always attributed to the improper application of the ideas (rather than the ideas being wrong), again stressing the need for expert assistance'.

\- 'There are no “recipes” for an organization to follow'.

The final aspect points out that the introduction of new information technologies into an organization may bring an altogether new set of problems for management. Often, promoters of newer organizational forms and techniques with a heavy emphasis on technologies will fail entirely to prepare managers and developers for these new complications. Sometimes the new problems (such as increased stress levels for key managers) are predictable (endemic to the new techniques), but brushed aside carelessly and improperly as minor flaws in the wheels of great progress. Sometimes the new problems arise unpredictably out of the particular interaction between the unique organization and the new techniques:

\- 'The optimistic perspective was that IT would enable the development of a new organic corporation to replace the hierarchically layered bureaucracy. The pessimistic view was that this technology would lead to a distortion of the organization in which the importance of face-to-face relationships bounded in space, time and memory become subordinate to more impersonal ones, which are structured and facilitated through the use of networked computer-based systems'.

\- 'Network organizations have to cope not only with the traditional organizational problems, but at the same time electronic nets have to cope with the new emerging incidents, issues that seem to be even more urgent and therefore are getting more attention'.

Our second finding calls for improved awareness that dramatic improvements achieved by the innovation of any single organization deserve close study. These represent opportunities for learning about successful organizational change. But the steps taken in such unique cases cannot be transported wholesale into other organizations, particularly where a greater dependence on newer information technology is involved. The successful adaptation may require further innovation and considerable modification of the approach. In addition, the adaptation may reconstruct the organizational problem set. The absolute best that innovators can hope for is that the subsequent problem set will be somehow less onerous than the antecedent problem set.

## Finding 3

IT is too often portrayed as either the key element, or even the most important element, in organizational transformations.

Perhaps it is the role of the IS researcher to approach human problems from the perspective that the organizational universe revolves around IT, but there is a worrisome trend to elevate this perspective to an arrogation. Such hubris is unforgivable, even considering the ubiquitous rush of technology in industrialised societies. Much of our research and practice into IT and new organizational forms really makes quite unreasonable assumptions about the degree of pre-eminence of IT in organizational development. Certainly IT is important, certainly IT is a factor, but changes in IT alone can never transform a human organization:

\- ‘Frank [Land] challenged the idea that IT should be the central theme around which success is built. Technical competence or technical innovation is one variable of many that may spell success’.

\- 'The physical network and the social network are only loosely coupled, although computer networks tend to dilute some of the effects on the social network'.

\- 'Theories of different forms of communication, which include telecommunication and information systems theories are restrictive in the way that they are only addressing formal information flows and requirements'.

Disarrogating the role of information technology in organizational success brings a number of important misconceptions into sharp focus. Among these are the presumption that IT professionals are not only the organization's ordained leaders in technological innovation, but are also the only inherent organizational instigators of technological innovation. In practice, of course, the IT managers are sometimes dragged by their users into new technological forums like prisoners in chains. Another important misconception is that the impact of major decisions by IT managers will surge powerfully across the entire organization, triggering extensive changes in the nature of the entire organization. In practice, major changes by IT management may go entirely unnoticed beyond the close circle of senior IT managers:

\- 'The expectation had arisen that IT leaders would become powerful figures, but now it is the users who are in some cases the leaders for technology'.

\- 'A robust management program in a global, multinational company is like a flea, on a hair, on the tail of a dog, trying to wag the dog . . . in reality, there is no dog, only lots of fleas. To use Habermas' terminology, there are many, many lifeworlds that have durable relationships with each other; organizations are relatively stable relationships among [work] practices, organizations are not things'.

This third finding is important in suggesting that IS research may be missing its own point by fastening so intensely on the impact of technology (and the management of technology) that the interactive nature of these elements with other organizational elements is entirely lost. The study of IT and IT management in isolation from their environment could yield highly misleading and constricted theories.

## Information technology and new organizational forms: winning with chaos

The final two findings extend the thinking in the previous section toward a new basis for approaching research and practice regarding new organizational forms and IT. These findings, together with the previous three, constitute an improved set of assumptions on which to base research directions and practical planning in this field.

## Finding 4

Organizations and management are not predictable, categorically controllable machinery; these things are organic, with inextricably intertwined elements – they are messy things.

Management theorists sometimes suggest that the most difficult task confronting management is forecasting a future environment and planning the organizational response. There seems to be an assumption that the mechanics of implementing the plans are straightforward and obvious. This assumption does not hold under close inspection. A penchant for instrumentality has left IS researchers with a faulty understanding of the organizational and management processes. Human organizations are biotical, not mechanical. The human organization is an entangled mass of individuals and individual activity – a mass that is in motion.

The ‘organizational direction’ is a trend or tendency among the activity of the individuals that sets the entire mass on a discernible course. This course may be desirable, undesirable or erratic. The motion is autogenous regardless of whether it is set and stimulated by management or just happens as a result of the random mass of individual activity:

\- ‘An organisation is messy, and an electronic network organisation even more so’.

\- ‘The emergent organization is not really the emergent organization as a machine-based form. It is the emergent human form. Afterwards comes the (information) needs of our human activity systems, and among a combination of needs includes the need for integrating IT into our human organizations’.

\- 'The three panellists presented detailed case studies and conceptual schemes which focused on the difficulties of capturing, at both a micro- or macro-level, the processes which constitute “management”. The challenge then would be to better explain what we mean when we talk about managing emergent organizations. One suspects that the data is there, what is missing is a conceptualisation of what data to collect and analyze'.

This ‘messy’ perspective causes us to regard all of the causal links between management directives and organizational response more cautiously. This incredulity extends to the role that IT might play as an instrumental tool in the hands of management for purposefully transforming the organization. In reality, the typical role of IT is little more than that of providing communication between those individuals that choose it, and of suggesting characteristics of the current and past locations of the ‘random mass of activity’ that we described earlier. Changing the IT might stimulate favourable changes in the patterns of communication, and consequently the organizational direction. Changing the IT might have no effect, or stimulate unfavourable changes. In the final finding, we consider the factors that modulate the effects of IT changes.

## Finding 5

Some critical factors modulate the interaction of changes in IT and emergent organizational form: organizational learning, structural premise and power.

Once IT managers yield to the premise that an organization is biotical and its direction and emergence are autogenous, then how do we plan effectively to use information technology to improve its condition? There are three factors that clearly emerged from the discussions.

The first factor is organizational learning. This seems to be critical from two perspectives not far removed from Argyris and Schön's (1978) double-loop learning concept. Under the single-loop learning perspective, an enlightened management in an emergent organization can turn to IT as a helpful tool in learning about the organization's current activity. This is a considerably more intense use of IT than its previous role as a reporting medium. Managers can use IT to extend new or richer communications links (e.g. email and groupware) into segments of the organization that were previously impossible owing to formal communications channels or geographic separation.

Under the double-loop learning perspective, the organization as a whole can use IT to improve its internal communication. However, this learning process will also be emergent in the sense that individuals will adapt new IT elements to their own learning style. The availability of new or richer communication links may reduce the randomness in the individual activity and increase the likelihood of a favourable direction in the motion of the entire organizational mass. In this sense, IT may serve to adjust organizational entropy. Improved organizational learning does not necessarily guarantee that the organization's eventual direction of motion will closely match the direction desired by the management component of the organization. It could serve to increase organizational entropy and eventually compel a change in its management component:

\- 'Organizational learning is vital. It is not possible to order learning and a more chaotic stance is preferable'.

\- 'It was generally agreed that information technology provided a framework for learning which was not seen as detrimental or overly restrictive'.

\- 'It is not the processing or communication capabilities of the system, but access to a collective memory that can build the learning organization'.

\- ‘The lesson that is drawn from this is that “work processes” and “management processes” need to be simultaneously redesigned if an organizational transformation is to be successful’.

\- ‘Perhaps most intriguing to the audience, as a result of this constant betting and shifting, was the organization’s ability and willingness to discard prior successes and competencies and also to forget prior failures’.

The second factor is the structural premise underlying IT management and organizational management. The key seems to lie in a careful co-ordination between the structures encoded in IT and the configuration of the organization. As we have argued that the organization is a biotic mass of individual activity, the organization will autogenously choose its configuration. Indeed, the configuration will be in motion much of the time. There is no persistent organizational structure. However, IT presents the organization with a structure, but a structure that can be incongruously persistent. IT managers must develop the IT such that it is adaptive. Wherever possible, the IT elements should be easily reconfigured by the individual users or user groups themselves:

\- ‘In keeping with one of the pervasive themes of the conference, Klenke suggested that the term structure be replaced by plasticity. Stamping of a “rational” structure on flexible forms may be regarded as a retrograde move’.

\- ‘They moved to an entirely project-based organization in a completely open office, so project teams can be put together quickly. All paper was scanned so people can move around easily (since all they move is a workstation) and information can be more easily shared. The team then picks a spot to meet and within a few hours can be working together’.

\- ‘The change involved a holistic vision rather than re-engineering; was employee-centred rather than process-centred; involved commitment rather than processes; was participative rather than expert-driven; and was culture-driven rather than [driven by the threat of an] execution squad’.

The final factor concerns the importance of organizational politics in the success of both IT and organizational form. The issues of politics with regard to IT management are difficult to study and highly complex. As a consequence, our study of the political effects is badly neglected. Despite this neglect, power is plainly a crucial warp thread in the theoretical fabric of IT and new organizational forms.

The political factors centre on the presumed management use of IT as an instrument for organizational control. Under the premise that organizations are biotic, then IT is placed in a role antithetic to more humanistic ideals of autonomy, empowerment and access. This political use of newer information technologies also places them in an intrusive role with regard to existing political patterns. The changing IT may disrupt political patterns by adjusting individual access and autonomy. Empowering one individual in this way typically implies the redistribution of power formerly exercised elsewhere in the organization:

\- 'Little attention had been paid to politics'.

\- 'There is a tension between “control” and “autonomy” that is solved by the “Information Age” organization'.

\- ‘One of the organizational design challenges is a move from “autonomy” to “empowerment”, which the author equates to the “distribution of decision rights”. The key to making this change is the information infrastructure and a key design problem is how to use information to tighten control in the organization of the 1990s’.

\- 'Power and politics as related to IT appears to be the emerging issue. Political barriers may be the most challenging barrier to the success of “team telework” (or to the ability of team telework to lead to new organizational forms)'.

It is also clear that our assumptions about organizational politics must shift in tandem with our assumptions about the basic nature of organizations. If we assume that management plays a deterministic role in organizational direction, structure and culture, then we must also assume that management also determines the distribution of organizational power. If we assume that organizational forms are emergent, then organizational power is configured and reconfigured according to a mutating set of chaotic, interactive processes embodied by the mass of individual activities of organizational members:

\- ‘Thinking of power in the singular, implies a politics of access, thinking of many powers implies a politics of struggle’.

As a final point regarding political factors, there is a still-broader view that considers the political impact of IT at the societal rather than the organizational level. Here we can find the same tension between control and autonomy, but exercised broadly as the distribution of power between individuals and organizations:

\- 'One can visualise the use of information technology in two ways (in terms of their relationship to freedom):

a. For coordination: here information technology fosters diversity. Examples could be cooperative work systems (CSCW), electronic mail, computer conferencing etc. Here the freedom obtained is related to the social conditions that govern the concrete form and access to the technology.

b. For control: here the task of information technology is to prevent disorder. Examples are police systems, credit checking systems, and management control systems. Here clearly the issue of freedom is severely jeopardised. What control does one have over the data gathered by government and other agencies as part of the apparatus to control society?

We must ensure that society is significantly protected from the use of information technology as sophisticated apparatus for control. However, the area for opportunities is in the field of information technology to enable, to realise potential (personal, socio- and economic development).

These three critical factors, organizational learning, structural premise and power, are modulating factors in the management of IT in new emergent organizational forms. An understanding of these factors could lead IT and management researchers and practitioners to discover effective ways to integrate the technology into the organization. For example, an understanding of the nature of IT in relation to organizational learning may help to achieve highly effective IT elements. At the practical level, this might lead an IT planner to ask 'How can I enable our new email system to be more effective at both levels of organizational learning?' In this way, the consideration of organizational learning will modulate the planning of the new email system.

As a second example, a better grasp of the need for congruence between IT and an emergent organizational form may lead to appropriately flexible and adaptive IT elements. At the practical level, this might lead an IT designer to consider how to enable a user to declare computer file directories or database tables with temporary, limited public access by file transfer programs. This would permit a user to authorise temporary access to other users in response to fast-paced changes in organizational needs and communication lines. In this way, the organization can adapt quickly in response to the need for multifunctional special project teams. The consideration of structural plasticity modulates the way IT is structured.

As a third example, consideration of the impact of changes in IT on organizational politics may lead to more successful integration of IT into the organization. At the practical level, this might lead an IT planner considering a new query management system to look for fluid, yet powerful power centres that would be affected by the changes in IT. The designer might choose to limit certain queries to those in the power centre in order to reinforce the existing politics. Alternatively, if the organizational motion calls for changes in the politics, the designer might purposely extend access to a wide population, thus diluting the power of those in the centre. This conscious decision shows how political factors might modulate the IT plans.

However, while many researchers and practitioners would agree with these proposals, it is not clear how best to proceed. Some would argue that ‘good management’ supported by traditional research is sufficient, while others would argue that an acceptance of these ideas implies that many of the traditional theories and associated research methods are less relevant to the field of new organizational forms and their constituent information systems. Concepts from post-modernism and the theories of Heidegger, Foucault and Giddens would seem to be appropriate, but it is by no means clear how to apply these notions:

\- ‘For some, the theoretical focus of the papers was seen as something of a diversion from what was felt to be the essential practical issues of IS design and use. For others, in contrast, the theoretical analysis did not go far enough. Simply picking a concept from some “fashionable” theorist did not guarantee its relevance or interest’.

\- 'The review of academic work had been disappointing and the practitioners had not managed to find a rich seam of theory that they could use in addressing their concerns'.

\- 'There was by no means a consensus on the proper use of theory in IS research'.

## Conclusion

New emergent organizational forms are an important development for those involved in IT. It is clear that there is a critical relationship between IT and these forms. However, this relationship is not well understood. There are five main aspects of this relationship that are important to IT researchers.

Successful experiences in the integration of IT and organizations are often quickly corrupted as unrelated ideas, concepts and techniques are attached to the basic episode. The meaning behind the original success can be quickly smothered by attempts to elevate the process into an organizational strategy, a philosophical school or a professional theology.

An unsettling trend in management has been the unbridled promotion of universal panaceas that promise to transform organizations into completely different and more appropriate forms. These approaches are typically unclear as to how the approach can be adapted for different and unique human organizations. Most recently, IT is seen as a critical element in these transformations with little regard for the new set of problems that accompany the IT changes. Because of the pre-eminence given to IT in these new approaches to organizational transformation, IS researchers sometimes attribute excessive significance to the impact of changes in IT alone. This excess is leading to distorted expectations about the role of IT in stimulating new emergent forms of organizations.

IT exists in the context of human organizations, and a considerable portion of the IT management literature fails to recognise this context. The impact of this failure is the perception of human organizations as mechanisms that can be adjusted, restructured, triggered and directed in highly instrumental ways. New emergent forms of organizations, perhaps more so than earlier forms, are extremely organic and messy. An organization is biotical and its direction and emergence are autogenous. The biotic nature of these organizations may cause them to react in unexpected ways to attempts to impose management direction. We can plan effectively to use information technology to improve its condition by recognising the role of IT in the provision of elective patterns of organizational communication.

Some critical factors modulate the interaction of changes in IT and emergent organizational form: organizational learning, structural premise and power. The organizational learning factor leads to the use of IT in a highly appropriate role of improving the self-awareness of the organization as a biotical body-in-motion. The structural premise helps IT designers recognise incongruities between the very real structures of IT and the shifting patterns of organizational communication that sometimes pass for organizational structures. The power factor leads IT planners to recognise the important interaction between IT and the emergent organization's political patterns. IT planners have to consider the potential shifts in power that may anticipate, accompany or result from proposed changes in IT. However, as yet, there is no consensus among IS researchers as to how to take advantage of some of the newer social theories that could potentially provide considerable insight into the opportunities and problems of new organizational forms and their associated information systems.

Each session in the conference was attended by an appointed reviewer who sat with the audience. The reviewer's role was to ‘capture and interpret’ the learning that publicly took place during a session. This required reflection on and reporting of the discussion in the room, with the option to conduct follow-up interviews with participants (including the audience). After the conference, the reviewers formulated and submitted a short commentary. We chose the term ‘reviewers’ rather than ‘reporters’ or ‘recorders’ to indicate the intent to encourage the reviewers to contribute their individual interpretation of what was learned by the participants in the discussion.

Fifteen review texts were submitted and can be found in the April 1995 issue of ACM SigOIS. These review texts were then analysed and coded to produce the findings described above. To a large extent, these findings are the work of approximately 125 scholars, some of whom have worked for two years on this project. Readers are encouraged to refer to the full text of the reviews and the proceedings papers (Baskerville et al., 1994) to develop more fully the ideas in this paper.

Steve Smithson, as well as being editor of EJIS, has worked as a Lecturer in Computing at the London School of Economics for the last ten years. Before this, he worked for twelve years in industry, mostly in transport and distribution. His main research interests are in information systems management and the application of information technology in organizations. He has published two books and numerous journal articles and conference papers in these fields.
