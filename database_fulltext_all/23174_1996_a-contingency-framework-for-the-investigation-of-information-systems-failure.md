# A contingency framework for the investigation of information systems failure

A Poulymenakou and A Holmes

Information Systems Department, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE

Despite the increasing sophistication of information technologies, information systems (IS) continue to fail either during development, or at the points of implementation and use. This paper argues that effective practices to deal with, or prevent, such failure require wider organizational awareness and a deeper understanding of the nature, phenomena and implications of IS failure. The paper suggests that the social nature of IS needs to be a central feature in the study, analysis and action to mitigate failure. Following this view, context plays a significant role in the development of an understanding of failure incidents and in the adoption of relevant action. These concerns can be accommodated within a contingency perspective for the investigation of IS failure. The paper proposes a framework of concepts that support this perspective and then moves on to provide examples of how such a perspective could inform study of, and action upon, IS failure.

## Introduction

The information systems community faces a paradox: despite impressive advances in technology, problems are more abundant than solutions; organisations experience rising costs instead of cost reductions, and information systems misuse and rejection are more frequent than acceptance and use. (Lyytinen, 1987)

Despite some notable successes, information systems (IS) projects can, and still do, fail dramatically. News of such failures very often makes spectacular headlines in the popular press while instances of project overruns, disgruntled users and unusable systems feature almost on a weekly basis in the computing press. IS failure is discussed predominantly in terms of monetary loss. For example, it is estimated that in the UK alone, problems with projects within the public sector over the last twelve years have cost the taxpayers over £5 billion (Collins, 1994). Such data exacerbate organizational concerns for the risks of IS failure since organizations are clearly becoming more dependent on IS for their day-to-day operations as well as for the provision of sustainable products and services (Adams, 1988; Wise, 1990; Aldrich, 1994).

Increasing organizational dependence on information technology (IT) is confirmed also by investment trends. Recent statistics claim that the world's largest economies spend on IT on average $4\%$ of their gross national product (GNP) and this figure is expected to double within the next 8 years (Coombs, 1993). In 1993, company expenditure on IT stood at 1.5% of annual turnover, itself a 15% increase on 1992's figure (Wilcocks and Lester, 1993). In the UK this represents an annual spend in excess of £12 billion. In this climate of intensifying organizational involvement with IT, in the financial sector alone, one in four IT managers believe that most of their projects have failed (Aldrich, 1994), which suggests that 'famous' failures such as the London Stock Exchange Taurus system are merely the tip of the iceberg.

Project failure in IS is by no means a new topic for debate. Camier (1958) discussed failure in the very first issue of the Computer Journal where he argued that system success could only be achieved through the automation of the whole office function. We may no longer necessarily subscribe to this view but it is important to note this early sign of concern. Within the software engineering community, failure has featured as a concern since the late 1960s: at a North Atlantic Treaty Organisation (NATO) conference on software engineering in 1968, there was discussion about the ‘software crisis’. Even then, system development projects were characterised by major delays, cost overruns and operational problems ranging from persistent bugs through to complete disasters. Almost thirty years later this still continues, but the financial implications are far greater: the Westpac CS90 \$A150 million failure and the \$125 million CONFIRM project failure (Oz, 1994) are but two examples. The search for ‘silver bullets’ in software technologies and practices (Brooks, 1987), that would help mitigate the risks in systems development, has not yielded thus far any major successes.

Moreover, ‘success’ of an IS assumes much more than the successful development of a computer based system (Land, 1992; Avgerou & Cornford, 1993). This still appears elusive, with up to 70% of IS projects failing (Hochstrasser & Griffiths, 1991). The headline failures, which usually run into the tens, if not hundreds of millions of pounds, suggest that the scale of the problem is bigger than most organizations believe. In this context, information systems failure is becoming a concern of considerable urgency and complexity for organizations in both the public and the private sectors.

Most publicity on failure focuses on particular cases of projects. While failure incidents proliferate and information on them becomes more detailed and accessible, we believe the IS discipline still lacks the detailed understanding of the nature, issues and factors affecting failure which would enable us to capitalise from experience and suggest more appropriate courses of action. We believe, that in order to meet effectively the challenges that systems failure presents for professionals and users alike, we need more than anecdotal recounts, or empirical evidence based on individual incidents of failure. We need a framework of understanding which should be sufficiently rich and expressive to make the most of our knowledge and experiences concerning the relationship of IT with its organizational context. In other words, we need to apply aspects of the study of IS to the analysis of IS failure.

In this sense, this paper has two distinct aims: the first is to enrich the current debate on failure with relevant themes from the study of IS as ‘social systems that use technology’ (Land & Hirschheim, 1983). From the considerable variety of such themes, we have selected two that in our view touch upon topics pertinent to the study of failure. These are the relationship between IS and organizational change, and the impact of sociotechnical thinking and of participative approaches in IS development. The second aim of this paper is to make proposals towards the development of a deeper understanding of failure phenomena by putting forward concepts that could be used in the study of IS failure, as well as by pointing to relevant organizational action in the mitigation of such failure. In our effort to develop a rich and relevant conceptual framework for these purposes, we build upon, amongst others, earlier studies related to IS failure (Lucas, 1975; Markus, 1983; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987; Davis et al, 1992).

## Recognising the social and organizational dimensions of failure

For the software industry, the single most important response to the software crises since the 1970s has been to increase the rigour of the development process through the adoption of engineering principles. Despite such efforts, little has improved (Fenton, 1993) and there is an increasing awareness that the development of IS cannot be addressed in engineering terms alone because they are social, not purely technical systems (Allen, 1988; Angell & Smithson, 1991).

The need for joint development of social and technical elements of a system has been captured by the sociotechnical school of thought in organizational theory (Trist and Bamforth, 1951). The ETHICS methodology (Mumford & Weir, 1979) clearly extends the sociotechnical rationale into the IS discipline. The reasoning behind the creation and adoption of this methodology evolved from the work of the Tavistock Institute and the view that both technologists (in this case systems professionals) and managers look primarily at efficiency gains from the introduction of technology. In doing so they focus on the structural elements of the change process and often use machines to replace people. Yet because people are an integral part of all work systems a neglect of their interests can throw a change situation into a state of disequilibrium. So, instead of increasing efficiency the new system operates more inefficiently than was anticipated and gains may not outweigh the costs of innovation (Mumford, 1983).

Sociotechnical theory has provided the IS discipline with considerable insight into the reality of systems in their organizational environments. The central premise of sociotechnical theories is that if systems are regarded in purely technical terms the result, in sociotechnical terms, may be suboptimised, organizationally and socially deficient systems. This helped explain why the promises made about IT seemed so rarely to materialise. Some areas related to IS failure which lend themselves to a sociotechnical analysis are now reviewed.

IT has been seen as the best way for introducing major productivity and other competitive gains within organizations; the particular ‘vehicle’ may change, the pressure for achieving such gains through IT remains. A current example is the intense organizational interest in groupware systems. However, the productivity payoff from IT has been exceptionally poor with annual productivity increases during the 1980s ranging from 0.7% (Landauer, 1995) to a paltry 0.28% (Forester & Morrison, 1994). This poor productivity when related to the enormous investment in IT has been termed the productivity paradox as it seems that for those who invest heavily in IS a fall in productivity usually follows (Landauer, 1995). Further evidence comes from a CBI survey which showed that only a third of managers could identify gains in productivity because of IT; 38% did not even know which criteria they used to quantify these benefits and half were less than satisfied with the performance of the systems (Merrill, 1993). Similar difficulties in evaluating IT investments have been highlighted by Targett (1993) and Lang (1994).

The productivity paradox is not the only source of concern regarding IT in organizations. There is a growing interest in IT as the main vehicle for implementing change within organizations (Wilcox, 1994). A prominent manifestation of this trend is captured in business process reengineering approaches (eg Hammer & Champy, 1994). However, to achieve change requires much more than technology; there is a significant but often neglected social driver. Certainly, IT often acts as an enabling force (Earl, 1992), but the ability to accept and foster change should be the main concern here; an ability often embedded in the cultural dimension of the organization (Sauer, 1993). Organizations often experience difficulties in managing change through their systems projects because they fail to understand the role of individuals within the change process. Moreover, it seems that the IS profession, has currently only a marginal role in guiding organizations through IT enabled change programs (Avgerou et al, 1995).

Uncertainty about the actual role of IT as a productivity lever and the ways in which this can be assessed, coupled by disproportionate reliance on the technological dimension of IS within organizational change processes is manifest in managerial perceptions of IS projects. Earl (1992) contrasted successful and unsuccessful change projects and found that in the successful ones, where IT was seen as the key enabler, it was perceived as a means to an end, not the end itself. In the unsuccessful ones, the project was managed as a technology project, with the IT director standing to lose. Earl concluded that an IT project can often lead to failure because of the lack of human considerations (the social system). Roos (1993) drew very similar conclusions. From a study of two General Motors plants with two radically different strategies to automation, both were found to be highly unsuccessful at implementing change. Roos suggests that focusing too much on the technological aspects results in:

● The automation of existing mistakes;

● The purchase of wrong solutions;

\- The failure to implement non-technical solutions, even if they are essential;

\- The implementation of solutions that make sense only at departmental level;

● The creation of organizational chaos;

• The alienation of staff.

It seems that although people are a major component within IS development, they are often regarded as being secondary to technology. Technical determinism may, in part, be embedded within the psyche of systems developers who, because of the assumptions they hold about technology, tend to occupy themselves with the technical aspects of problems alone believing that the organizational and political dimensions are difficult to resolve (Davenport et al, 1992). Most of the techniques employed for systems development may precipitate this situation in so far as rigidity and bureaucracy, which are implicit within these techniques, could become imposed upon the organization's social system (Angell & Smithson, 1991).

The view that IS are sociotechnical allows us to consider factors which would allow the joint optimisation of the technical and social systems, rather than one above the other. Mumford & Weir (1979) recommend that the systems professional should regard him/herself as a facilitator supporting the users in defining desirable and sustainable systems for their environment. They suggest that such systems need to 'fit' within the organizational context along a number of dimensions. Task fit ensures that the redesigned work system around the new IS offers the users task variety and enriches where possible their work content. Knowledge fit requires that the new IS offers the user the opportunity to learn and advance professionally through using it. Psychological fit requires that the new system offers the user the satisfaction of 'doing his/her job well' and being able to appreciate the results. Ethical and social fit require that the new system does not contradict the prevailing social and ethical norms in the organizational environment.

A central feature of sociotechnical approaches is user participation in systems development. Although in strict terms the sociotechnical perspective does not require or indeed ask for user involvement, the two concepts have been married up within the IS literature and especially the ETHICS methodology (Mumford & Weir, 1979). Participation is seen as an important mechanism for introducing change leading to more successful systems (Cale and Curley, 1987). Participation is also a means of reducing resistance to the change process (Coch & French, 1948; Mumford, 1983; Checkland, 1981).

Participation itself, however, has produced mixed results. Some empirical studies have found that user involvement leads to more successful systems (Cale & Curley, 1987), whilst others have found no obvious correlation (Ives & Olsen, 1984). The type of system being developed seems to affect decisions on user involvement. For example, systems which are of a more critical nature tend to require more user participation (Hirschheim, 1993; Kim & Lee, 1986; Tait & Vessey, 1988). There also appears to be a strong cultural link to participation as it tends to be the Scandinavian countries that adopt such approaches more readily (Clement & Ven Den Besselaar, 1993), although not always successfully (Bodker et al, 1987). There is also more to participation than the act itself. Finally, a recent study by Amoako-Gyampah & White (1993), suggested a strong positive relationship between perceived user involvement and the degree of satisfaction with the implemented IS. It is the value which is attached to this involvement by the development teams that appear critical, not the participation itself. Such empirical evidence suggests that the effectiveness of participative approaches to system development is contingent upon cultural and other contextual factors in a particular project.

There is research to suggest that, despite the cries from the professionals that software development is an extremely complex and intellectually demanding job, thereby making it inherently unpredictable, very few projects fail because of technology alone (O'Connell, 1994; Stahl, 1994). Similar evidence is available in other disciplines such as civil engineering: a survey of 500 civil engineering companies by Sowers (1991) found that $75\%$ of failures were attributed to human factors either in the ignorance of prevailing knowledge, failure to use prevailing knowledge or conditions beyond prevailing knowledge.

The sociotechnical perspective accommodates the realisation that any software system is an artefact of a human thought process. Therefore, all software developments should be considered as the combined result of human and organizational elements (Norman, 1988). As Whitley and Darking (1994) argue, the 'black box' view of technology still predominates: users and managers are not concerned about how the system works, as long as it does work. From empirical research they show how differences in prior IT acknowledge together with social norms can result in totally different interface designs: those that have been previously involved with IT produce designs that reply heavily on their past development skills; those new to IT provide designs that relate more effectively to the problem at hand. Norman (1988) suggests a human centred approach to systems design as the convergence of two distinct modelling activities: on the one hand there is the design model which reflects the system designer's understanding of the problem at hand. On the other hand there is the user's model which is created to reflect the user's understanding of the operation of the system. These two should ideally be equivalent. As Norman points out though, it is important to remember that the user and the designer communicate chiefly through the system image: the physical appearance and operation of a system, the ways it responds and the documentation and procedures that accompany it. Thus, this system image needs to reflect a number of quality criteria.

\- The principles of system operation need to be observable;

\- All system actions need to be consistent with the conceptual model (design model) of the system;

\- The conceptual model which is developed for the system should be appropriate for the particular user, should capture the important parts of the operation of the device, and should also be understandable by the user.

Such criteria suggest a design process which, apart from making effective use of technology in a particular project, approaches the whole question of what is an appropriate design for an IS in a manner contingent upon the perceptions of the human actors of the situation at hand.

The emphasis on the social nature of IS provided by sociotechnical thinking and human centred design approaches seems to reveal a rich and complex set of variables affecting the process of systems development and the ultimate ‘success’ of a system’s use. The question now becomes one of how to combine such variables within a comprehensive framework which would enable a deeper understanding and a more relevant analysis of failure phenomena. The nature of concerns raised thus far suggest that study of failure should move away from attempts to define ‘silver bullets’ in the sense of fail-safe approaches to IS projects that would somehow ‘guarantee’ success. We propose that failure should be considered from a contingency viewpoint. Our argument is that failure is highly situational in nature and consequently dependent upon a number of environmental factors which, although common to the study of failure, manifest themselves very differently in different circumstances. Our objective therefore has been to develop a model for understanding failure which can support both further empirical research and organizational action in this area.

## Elements of contingency in IS failure

Contingency approaches have been adopted in various areas in IS, particularly in the study of methodological aspects of systems development (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990; Poulymenakou, 1995). In a similar vein, our work hypothesises that, in the study of IS failure, it is possible to uncover contingent variables which affect the outcome of a given development project. And, as with organizations, these variables are likely to impact differently by project and organization. The contingency perspective is proposed here as an alternative ‘reading’ of situations regarding failure; it should allow for the development of richer descriptions of failure phenomena, and through these the development of a better understanding of ways in which context plays a role in the success or failure of an IS.

For the purposes of establishing a framework for understanding IS failure, we have modified the contingency model from organizational theory (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967), as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c c c}\text {Contextual}&\rightarrow \text {Project}&\rightarrow \text {Project}\\\text {Factors}&\text {Processes}&\text {Outcome}\end{array}
$$

Fincham and Rhodes (1992) have reviewed the effects of contextual factors in managerial decision making and they have concluded that context may vary over time and from firm-to-firm in an almost endless variety of ways. The same may very well be seen to apply to the case of IS failure. We restrict here the scope of our search by using evidence from other studies on IS failure and their recommendations.

Lucas (1975) suggested four types of reasons for IS failure: technical and operational reasons, organizational reasons, individual reasons and environmental reasons. Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) proposed a much more refined typology of reasons for failure, classified under four broad areas: the IS (technical and operational reasons), the IS's environment (user related, work related, planning related) the IS development process (method, decision making, politics of implementation and systems assumptions), and the IS development environment (cognitive and behavioural limitations of system developers, user education reasons). Finally Davis et al (1992) study failure along social and technical dimensions of an IS. The technical dimension includes the technology used, user interfaces, information requirements, and the organizational fit of the system, while the social dimension includes the user reactions to the technical system, the particular measurements of system adequacy used, the development processes followed, and the ideas, objectives and beliefs held by those involved in the building of the technical system.

## The macro contingent variables

## Culture

Organizational culture is the pattern of basic assumptions that a given group has invented, discovered or developed in learning to cope with its problems of external adaptation and internal integration, and have worked well enough to be considered as the correct way to perceive, think and feel on those problems. (Schein, 1984).

People who share a common culture see the world in a similar way; hence the significance of organizational culture for the ultimate effectiveness of IS. A strong culture can have many benefits, not least a shared vision (Liebenau & Backhouse, 1991; Von Werssowetz & Beer, 1985). It can, however, introduce intransigence during times of major change which can be damaging. For example, both IBM and Hewlett Packard suffered as a result of their strong cultures preventing them from anticipating and reacting fast enough to the shift in the IT market during the late 1980s. In addition, various stakeholder groups in organizations may develop their individual subcultures, often with equally negative effects for the organization as a whole. A dominant subculture of an IS department can be imposed, through the technology and systems developed by them, onto an organizational culture with which it is not always compatible.

The different sets of beliefs held about technology and systems by different stakeholders, and the tensions that arise from them can create a culture gap within an organization. This culture gap is an important situational element for IS failure:

An IT system starts out for its users as an entity, created, in its details by an alien people – techies – and posing the problem of whether it really will be workable, cost effective, adequate, secure, upgradeable, compatible, etc. Techies are always moving the technology goalposts, increased penetration of IT continually increases an organization's exposure to risks of disruption. Thus for users, an IT system is always a problem. (Hale, 1991).

The culture gap between users and developers (Clement & Van Den Besslaar, 1993; Peltu, 1993; Snell, 1994) has in part been attributed to the allure of technology which can be difficult to resist because of its image of efficiency. When the underlying assumption within IS departments is that technology will resolve all problems (Aldrich, 1994), organizational and political issues are perceived to be non-existent or unmanageable, thereby creating a situation where technocratic utopianism dominates and projects fail. The manager–developer relationship creates equally as many culture clashes, particularly at board level (Evans, 1993; Grindley, 1991).

Culture gaps create tension among the users of systems, the developers, and those who expect a return on the investment from these systems. Tension is manifest in extreme reactions of non-specialists who use computers and either have a deep-seated mistrust of them, or believe that anything is possible at the press of a button: the pessimistic or dystopian view vs the optimistic or utopian view (Clement, 1994). Senior management increasingly find it difficult to justify the escalating cost of computerisation, particularly when it is virtually impossible to see any return on investment (Brooks, 1987; Rymer, 1993). Indeed, many chief executives are tiring of the IT hype and many believe it has been oversold and under-delivered (Earl, 1992); the proponents of IT are often so persuasive that the non-specialist finds it difficult to analyse their proposals in terms of return on investment (Aldrich, 1994).

## Planning

Strategic planning for IS has been considered as an essential means for organizations to gain the best from their systems (King et al, 1989; McFarlan, 1984; Porter and Millar, 1985). The development of a strategy for IS in an organization however is fraught with problems. Singh (1993) summarises well the drawbacks of current strategic approaches:

\- Models tend to be fragmented, narrow in their approach and lack general applicability.

\- Those models which have evolved within specific organizations invariably require modification when applied within another.

\- Most organizations have to operate in an environment characterised (and constrained) by their history.

\- Most models by themselves are of little help in deciding how to get started, how to adjust the plans to incorporate the last technology and what organizational changes to make.

Singh believes that such models over-emphasise the use of techniques to the detriment of organizationally based solutions. The issue of stability is important here, both in the external environment in which the enterprise exists and in its internal situation. The less internal and external stability an organization faces, the less it is meaningful to engage in top down planning. This has been recognised by researchers such as Ciborra (1994), who suggest that most approaches to strategic planning are limited because they are based upon a top-down rationalistic analysis. We can build on Ciborra's 'bricolage' perspective, and strive for an approach to IS strategy which moves away from dysfunctional and rigid models of the process and instead puts emphasis on those aspects of IS that make them a resource of strategic importance for organizations. Systems planning needs to be concerned with the empowerment of users through the introduction of meaningful and useful systems. In this sense, Fergreus (1993) calls for strategies that strive to provide the end users with access to data, the power to analyse it and the connectivity to share it. Following this view, we need to address issues of integration of systems strategy with other strategic initiatives in the organization, issues related to the cultural and behavioural issues of strategy implementation, and we need to identify the factors that facilitate and foster the development of partnerships between systems specialists and top managers in organizations (Rock-Evans, 1993).

## Accountability

We have placed this factor amongst the macro variables primarily because it appears to be common to most failed projects and, more importantly, it is organizationally precipitated. Accountability is an issue for the entire organization, not just for IS projects.

Accountability is an important factor both in the analysis of cases of systems or projects that failed, as well as for the deployment of failure mitigation practices in the organization. It is, however, difficult to create a climate of accountability for systems. Nissenbaum (1994) has identified the major problems in this area:

\- The problem of too many hands. Most information systems today are the result of collective action. Consequently, when a system gives rise to concern, the identification of who is responsible is obscured. Moreover, how and where the problems or errors within the systems were introduced, is often very difficult to identify.

\- Software error. There can be no denying that software bugs constitute a major reason for failure, but it is not so obvious how they are related to accountability. Software bugs are considered to be an unavoidable side effect of systems development, it seems that they do remain in systems however rigorous the testing may have been. Consequently, the prevailing view is that it is unreasonable to hold programmers, systems engineers or systems designers accountable in this case, which contributes to the accountability deficit for systems.

\- ‘It’s the computer’s fault’. It is quite common to blame the system for anything that goes wrong with the processes around it. Such claims may be hiding an individual’s inability to carry out their job, which may or may not be their fault, thus introducing barriers to accountability and the analysis of failure. Such a tactic is partly created by the organizational culture and/or the individual’s perception of the systems in question.

\- Ownership without liability. The software industry is setting an alarming trend by demanding maximum property protection for commercially available software, whilst denying accountability for any problems caused by its use.

We would like to add two more:

\- Dishonesty. Typically, when failure occurs, there is rarely a self-examination, but systematic dishonesty about the nature of the failure. Effort is generally directed towards the punishing of whistle-blowers within the organization who have identified the people and procedures responsible for the failure.

\- The instrumental nature of systems design. A systems designer is not neutral, or seen to be neutral; he/she is seen as an agent of authority because the design is seen to be instrumental. Consider, for example, the introduction of a computer-based project management tool in an organization. Such an action can be perceived as a way of increasing the control of management over systems personnel and a way to introduce performance measurement.

Accountability is dependent upon perceptions of ownership of a project. This can be a major problem for organizations, particularly when there are a number of different stakeholders involved. Some of the problems associated with culture gaps, resistance to change and issues of accountability can be largely related back to the ownership of projects and the extent to which they were ‘bought-in’ from the users. In fact many authors have recognised the importance of management and user commitment in the IS delivery process (Erskine, 1994; Land, 1992; Snyder, 1981).

## Irrationality

Irrational behaviour is common to all people, but within organizations the effects can be quite dramatic. Irrational behaviour affects all aspects of a project from initial cost justification right through to implementation. It is, however, particularly evident during a project which is experiencing problems and usually manifests itself collectively in what Drummond (1994) terms as escalation.

Escalation refers to a predicament where decision makers find themselves trapped in a losing course of action as a result of previous decisions. Costs are incurred; there is an opportunity to withdraw or to persist; and the consequences of withdrawal or persistence are uncertain. Typically, the response of such dilemmas is irrational persistence. (Drummond, 1994)

Drummond (1994) identifies the four areas of concern which contribute to escalation:

\- Project factors are concerned with the perceived benefits of the project and the costs of withdrawal. There is also a tendency to hype project benefits when they start to go wrong. Such a tactic is designed to ensure the continual flow of funds.

\- Psychological factors emphasise the role of self-justification which may be accompanied by information distortions, particularly in the over-estimation of an individual's capability. Similar trends have been identified by Schmaltz (1993) who showed that managers often hide vital change-related information from their staff for the same reason. In analysing the failure of the London Stock Exchange system, Taurus, Erskine (1994) showed that the project was severely put off-course at a crucial stage by the over-optimistic opinion of the chief executive.

\- Social factors indicate that escalation may be motivated by the desire to keep face. Others include competition; job security; imitating the behaviour of apparently successful colleagues and the cultural norms of consistency.

\- Structural factors. Escalation may be perpetuated by the organization through political pressure, corporate pride, entrenchment and administrative inertia. For example, Erskine (1994) illustrates this point using the London Ambulance system failure which, despite containing eighty known bugs, was implemented on the original implementation date. The Chief Executive was concerned he might lose face, unfortunately he lost a lot more.

Escalation phenomena in decision making create many barriers which prevent the termination of projects, even when disasters become obvious (Meredith, 1988).

## Evaluation

As the amount of money that is invested in systems increases, the need to determine whether these systems offer value for money is of interest to all stakeholders. However, as with the process of development, evaluation is dependent on the organizational context (Farbey et al, 1993). The evaluation process has been found to be highly variable in the methods and outcomes (Sauer, 1993) and to be highly political in nature (Angell & Smithson, 1991).

Attaching value to information is a very difficult and subjective task (Wiseman, 1992), and organizational factors may have a large part to play in its understanding or even recognition (Sauer, 1993). The timing of evaluations is another issue for debate. Farbey et al (1993) believe that such evaluations should map onto the traditional life cycle and, taking this further we believe should provide a key to understanding and learning, particularly where failure occurs. Despite the many methods open to those that undertake evaluation, there is an emphasis on methods such as return on investment. The promotion of organizational learning, however, requires the use of formative, multi-objective, multi-criteria evaluation methods (Farbey et al, 1993) that extend the search for appropriate measures and indicators far beyond the monetary value of the investment.

## The micro contingent variables

## Methods and methodologies

The management of projects is closely linked to the approaches taken for systems development. The systems development life cycle, a concept in use for over thirty years, is characterized by its rigour, simplicity and general applicability, regardless of product size or other contextual factors. The various shortcomings of the life cycle have been highlighted (Avgerou & Cornford, 1993; Gabig, 1991) and include the problem of adapting to changing needs of the organization, the need to increase the speed of the development process, the need for closer user involvement, and the need to manage development risks more effectively. Process models that address some of these shortcomings have been proposed. For example, Boehm's Spiral model (1988) put emphasis on the management of various types of risk within systems development, whereas Land's future analysis (1982) provides a means for overcoming some of the uncertainties surrounding changing user requirements in systems development.

Development methodologies were introduced to improve the software development process through the simplification and standardisation of the steps required to create a (successful) IS. Early methodologies were based upon corporate development standards, while today they might be commercial, in-house creations or academic (Wynekoop & Russo, 1995). Yet despite this impressive array of techniques, methods, methodologies, manual as well as computerised, they have failed to provide a comprehensive answer to the problems of systems development. We are still facing the persistent problems associated with poor communication among project stakeholders, and we still lack techniques that can address effectively the problem of reliably specifying user requirements without becoming either inconsistent and irrelevant, or over-bureaucratic. The high maintenance overheads associated with IS, typically exceeding the cost of development many times over, has largely been attributed to the inappropriate usage of methods (Fitzgerald, 1986). Finally, the contribution of the methodologies movement is scarce when it comes to issues of organizational change (Rymer, 1993), yet this is the very stuff of IS.

For a meaningful analysis of the impact of development methods on the success or failure of IS, we would argue that instead of searching for optimal ‘solutions’, it is far more important to understand how methods are being selected and used in a specific organizational context (for a similar argument see Poulymenakou, 1995). However, there is recent evidence to suggest that we do not even know if methodologies are used, whether they are successful, how they are selected and which ones might be obsolete (Wynekoop & Russo, 1995).

## Resistance to change

An organizational situation which is often put forward as the major obstacle in the successful implementation of IS, is the users' resistance to change. Resistance to change is not the simple rejection of a proposed or newly implemented IS. It is a complex combination of factors which can be organizational or individual, but primarily situational (Hirschheim et al, 1984). Projecting resistance to change as a negative practice which the IS professionals need to 'eliminate' is indicative of a technocentric mentality which puts technology before people in organizations. Resistance to change ought to be recognised as action (rather than reaction) conveying important messages for systems professionals. Resistance should also be recognised as being a normal course of events for organizations, particularly successful ones. Rapid technical change can lead to resistance through technology fatigue (Szathmary, 1994) for the users who have to continually learn new techniques and systems. IS professionals also have problems with rapid technological change as they are expected to apply the latest techniques, tools and technologies, often without any formal training (Rzevski, 1994).

Managing the technical aspects of change is a problem, partly because it is so rapid, but also because it affects the very way in which business is carried out. Strom (1993) highlights the inability of managing directors to handle rapid change; his empirical data reveal that most chief executives do not have a change management programme and only half of them consider themselves capable of planning for change. This suggests an attitude of ‘throwing money and technology at problems’, rather than tackling them in organizational terms. The boards of directors are under increasing pressure to take advantage of each new wave of technological developments because they are led to believe that the more advanced and sophisticated the systems, the better the business results achievable (Grindley, 1991); the notion of the silver bullet, one best way, something which the contingent model rejects. In this climate, when systems failure hits the headlines, a blame culture prevails in organizations resulting in spasmodic reactions that often create more damage than failure itself.

Resistance to change is fuelled by a lack of communication between the developer and user (Wilcox, 1994) but also by a lack of recognition by them that systems are agents of change (Lederer & Nath, 1991). Techniques advocated as enablers for the change process, such as building prototypes, involving the users, and enlisting a sponsor should be recognised as part of a wider strategy to deal with the management of change, which in itself recognises the social and political nature of the process (Hirschheim & Newman, 1988).

## Power and politics

It seems that still today most IS departments approach information management from a technological perspective; in such a context there is no room for politics which is regarded as too difficult to understand and model. Angell and Smithson (1991) argue that it is important to recognise that pluralism and politics are essential aspects of the nature of organizations. Stakeholder groups should be expected to exhibit attitudes which support not only the organization, but also the group norms and their own personal aims and ambitions. It therefore becomes critical how this pluralism is managed to aid, rather than inhibit the change process.

Markus (1983) clearly shows that IS frequently embody a distribution of intraorganizational power among the key factors affected by its design and that this power is an attribute of individuals, subgroups, departments, etc. The contingent nature of power and politics is clear especially during, or at implementation. It is here where power struggles can be particularly severe, because it is possible to withhold funds, resources and even decisions, all of which can adversely affect a project. Waiting until implementation also allows the most damage to be done. The balance of power and the effects of politics will vary considerably within organizations and also by project, because each project will affect each group differently, because of the perceived effects of computerisation.

## Some implications of the contingency perspective: a discussion

A contingency approach to understanding IS failure requires a fresh look at how we manage and learn from projects (both successful and unsuccessful). We believe that there are a number of areas which can help the adoption of the contingency perspective, including the adoption of learning principles, understanding the issues of implementation and improving the project management discipline.

## How failure may breed success: support for organizational learning

The IS department has traditionally been blamed for organizational failure of systems. The current ‘feeding frenzy’ on the IS department, as Young (1993) argues, prevents individuals from turning negative results into learning opportunities. This combined with the lack of power vested in the IS director, who is often excluded from the board, leads to very low morale within the IS department (Horne, 1992). People are discouraged from taking risks, thus curtailing the creative and innovative potential of the organization.

The failure of any IS has a longitudinal dimension that is often overlooked in the crisis which usually breaks out when this failure is publicly acknowledged or exposed. In every organization, there is a continuity in practices, experiences and knowledge, which are shared resources feeding into organizational culture. By acknowledging the significance of this continuity, a more constructive perspective for the understanding of failure in organizations can be developed. Our perspective puts emphasis on the role of organizational learning in the understanding and mitigation of failure. Senge (1990) uses systems thinking as the discipline which allows us to see the ‘structures’ that underlay complex situations, such as the ones surrounding failure. DeGeus (1988) proposes proposes scenario planning as a means for refining organizational understanding of the implications of future developments in the organization's environment. The development of a new system could well serve as the object of such exercises.

Organizational learning is a profoundly cultural phenomenon. Awareness of the systemic nature of failure for all stakeholders, users, developers and managers, erodes the blame culture surrounding failure which is a symptom of poor understanding of the problem situation. Systems thinking puts emphasis on communication and the process of problem identification (Checkland, 1981) and allows for the expression and accommodation of various stakeholder views in the context of a system (Lyytinen, 1988). The analysis of failure becomes a quest to identify the source of the problem, not to punish the person responsible for it. In such a climate, people feel less vulnerable in expressing their views and perhaps opening themselves to criticism.

In the face of continuous and radical change, organizations have come to rely heavily on a very different set of resources as compared to that of thirty years ago. As Zuboff (1991) argues in the informed organization of the 1990s, ‘learning is the new form of labour and ultimate source of value added’. The new organizational paradigm is characterised by changes in intellectual skills, roles, authority and the development of structures and systems that foster learning. Thus, IS should be viewed in terms of business purpose and vision of the organization’s mission and goals as an holistic approach and not in terms of an individual project. The primary contingent variable reflecting the need to support organizational learning as a part of a strategy for the mitigation of failure is evaluation. Farbey et al (1993) argue that evaluation of IS is in fact an opportunity to capitalise on past experiences, develop and communicate a common understanding which ultimately becomes a shared organizational resource guiding the future IS developments. Other contingent variables that can be seen to play a role here include accountability (in the sense of removing the obstacles to the development of a learning culture discussed in the section on accountability) and irrationality (as the containment of escalation phenomena discussed in the section on irrationality).

## The critical stage of implementation

The introduction of any new system in an organization implies organizational change. Land (1992) stresses the importance of creating the right organizational climate through the development of a shared vision. Users and other stakeholders need to be involved extensively to achieve such a vision. Involving people within the change process can be beneficial (Coch & French, 1948) because it reduces uncertainty which incites resistance to change. Real commitment to this involvement is required from the management who should make their intentions clear through communication and willingness to share in any pay-offs from the system's successful use. It is important to ensure that all stakeholders are aware of their roles, how the new system will affect them and the reasons why such change is being implemented. Job redesign should be a major concern during systems development.

The smooth transition from the old to the new ‘way of doing things’ as well as the transfer of the data, processes and people from the old to the new setting created by the system requires the ‘set-up of an organization to manage change’ (Land, 1992). The type of approach adopted should be contingent upon the type and structure of the organization, but general guidelines which include setting up appropriate communication channels, and appointing a legitimate project champion are common to all. Land also argues that it is critical to ensure that the appropriate staff of the right quality are employed on the project and are provided with specific terms of reference. The implementation strategy for any system should address in detail issues such as the level of user involvement, testing and training strategies and the degree of help that will be available during the change process. Putting such a strategy into action is largely the concern of project management. Contingent variables that reflect relevant concerns here are power and politics, and resistance.

## Project management

The implementation of any IS requires a project management approach capable of coping with change. Classical approaches to project management advocate that a project must have clearly defined objectives with careful planning and budgeting laid down at the start of the project. Adhering to this plan yields a project that finishes on time, within budget and to the required quality. Quite clearly, such an approach is difficult to follow in software development. Williams (1992) suggests that many of the problems that beset projects cannot be seen or anticipated. He believes that although making plans is crucial to any project, it is important to recognise that it is often very difficult to plan beyond the first stage without bringing in an element of the unpredictable. Uncertainty creates problems for the managers of systems projects who have been found to have a more restricted perception of project management, which focuses on the planning and control elements of the projects (Raz, 1993). Moreover, many managers of systems development projects appear to be easily seduced by technology, relying on automated project management tools rather than personal involvement for project control, approaching team building as an issue of analysing of skills and profiles of project personnel, and preferring training courses for the development of their staff rather than coaching them on the job (Williams, 1993).

The development and implementation of an IS is surrounded by uncertainty and requires project management practices that tolerate and work around this situation.

One such practice is risk analysis which should be applied to the individual stages as well as to the overall project (Charette, 1989). Risk management recognises the need to identify and prepare for problems before they appear, and acknowledges the importance of allowing plans to change (Ward, 1994). In recognising that plans have to adapt, it is necessary to be able to analyse the effect of changes that are introduced in a project which is under way (Snyder, 1981). For example, when a user asks for an extra feature and, assuming the basic project criteria remain the same, functionality, schedule and effort will increase while quality may suffer unless mitigating action is taken.

Planning and controlling projects and managing risk should be complemented by negotiating, staff motivation and development skills for project managers (Raz, 1993). Negotiation skills are important for the clarification of requirements from the project and the management of expectations of users and other stakeholders (Berkely et al, 1990). Project managers need to understand how the four basic project variables of function, effort, quality and schedule interact as the negative effects of this interaction can drive the project to disaster (Ward, 1994). Human resource management skills are important for understanding the impact of staffing decisions in a project on the progress of work. Brooks' Law strongly suggests that there is an optimum staffing level for every project which explains why adding more people to a late project may result in further delays (Thompson & Mayhew, 1994). Such advice seems to be easily forgotten in the hectic world of software development.

The development of meaningful project management practices depends also on the existence of an organizational culture that does not end up rewarding those who 'play the system' by making the right political moves in times of trouble, but supports those who focus on delivery, even when dealing with problems necessitates unpopular decisions for action (Williams, 1993). In conclusion, the contingent variables that reflect these concerns are those of culture, planning, and methods.

## Conclusions

The breadth of the discussion reflects the breadth of the topics within the IS discipline and beyond that based on the observations made in this paper are relevant to the study of failure phenomena. The review of the issues raised in this paper can be summarised in three main areas requiring further, empirical research as well as organizational action:

\- Linking information systems evaluation to failure. Much can be achieved through the effective monitoring of projects through rigorous project management. However, as identified within this paper, IT project management leaves much to be desired and can really only be considered as task based. What is required, we believe, is a sufficiently rich evaluation framework of which project management is a part. Thus evaluation should become a continuous exercise through the project life cycle and beyond that.

\- Creating a sufficiently open culture to permit failure to be recognised, openly discussed and resolved. We believe that the principles of the learning organization (Senge, 1990) may help us focus our empirical research on the factors that facilitate the creation of such a climate in an organization. To provide such a culture will require significant upheaval for the traditionalists within the IS profession, but it is a necessary step to provide the basis for reducing the effects of failure.

● Refining and enriching the contingency perspective presented in this paper through empirical research. Future work needs to concentrate on how particular contingent variables manifest themselves in projects; for example, identifying for irrational behaviour within systems development and establishing how such behaviour affects project outcomes.

Abdel-Hamid and Madnick (1990) add optimism in their analysis of failure by arguing that every incident of failure has a silver lining because, through experience and costly errors, managers develop the effective intuitive judgement needed to prevent future failures. We hope that the IS perspective on failure developed in this paper will help in transforming this optimism into designs for meaningful empirical research, as well as effective organizational action.
