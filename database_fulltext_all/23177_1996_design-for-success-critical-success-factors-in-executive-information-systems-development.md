# Design for success?: critical success factors in executive information systems development

J Nandhakumar

University of Southampton, Department of Accounting and Management Science, Southampton SO17 1BJ, UK

Much of the literature on executive information systems (EIS) development provides lists of factors critical to the successful EIS development. This gives only limited insights into the relationship between the success factors and the development process. By drawing on an in-depth study of EIS development in a large manufacturing company, this paper seeks to provide a theoretically-based explanation of the process by which the relationship between key success factors and the EIS development operated. The rich insights gained from this empirical study are used to critique the main assumptions of the factor approach. This paper argues that a better understanding of the relationship between key success factors and the EIS development is required if success factors are to be of any guidance to the practitioners to develop effective information systems.

## Introduction

Specific executive information systems (EIS) were first identified over a decade ago (Rockart & Treacy, 1982) and there have been many prescriptive papers providing checklists and guidelines aimed at developing EIS. Much of this literature provides lists of success factors as shown in Table 1, claiming that these are present in ‘successful’ EIS in specific organizations studied. Many surveys on EIS in organizations (eg Fitzgerald, 1992; Watson et al, 1991; Watson et al, 1992) also examine the presence of these factors. Other studies such as Rainer and Watson (1995) attempt to rank the factors in the order of importance to successful EIS development and on-going operation. Newman and Robey (1992) claim that this factor approach of identifying the potential success factors for information systems (IS) development is widespread in IS research in general. This approach however, as noted by Newman and Robey, fails to explain how and why these factors and outcomes are related.

We can identify three frequent assumptions in the literature prescribing success factors. First, the literature largely assumes that a causal connection exists between factors such as the presence of the executive sponsor and EIS success. However, it provides no explanation of how the assumed causal connection operates in practice. It is therefore unable to explain, as noted by Cottrell and Rapley (1991), why some factors commonly thought to be critical to developing effective systems are not present in ‘successful’ EIS. Second, the literature assumes each factor as an independent variable and overlooks the interaction between them and the interaction with other elements in the social and organizational context.

Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) claim that the relationships between these factors are crucial to understand the effect of these variables on IS. Third, it provides a static view of the factors assuming that the influences of these factors are frozen in time. Walsham (1993) argues that the factor approach gives no consideration to the dynamics of the process of organizational implementation.

One reason for these limitations could be that these factors are often presented in the literature without much in-depth empirical evidence and research (Ives & Olson, 1984; Walsham, 1993). Although some of these factors have emerged from empirical studies, these studies are predominantly anecdotes of EIS vendors' or consultants' experiences and cross-sectional surveys, which in turn have many limitations. For example, the quality of the 'evidence' in much of the anecdotes is sometimes questionable because the authors have a personal interest in the results. The cross-sectional surveys provide only a snap-shot view of the process and disregard different organizational and historical contexts within which EIS are developed and used. Therefore, there are great differences among the studies with respect to the factors which are critical to EIS development.

Further, the studies prescribing success factors lack any theoretical basis which could draw attention to important issues of IS/EIS phenomena and help to understand and describe these issues. In recent years, richer frameworks and theoretical concepts have been developed in the IS literature. For example, 'web models' (Kling, 1992; Kling & Scacchi, 1982) assume broad boundaries around IS and examine how their development and use depends upon a social context. The layered-behavioural model developed by Curtis et al (1988) shows the complexity of the contextual influences by drawing attention to the various layers of context, rather than treating social context as undifferentiated. The factors may therefore be seen as relating to social context at various levels such as IS design team, division, company and environment. In addition, these factors may also be seen as continuously influencing the development activities, while human actions in that context may be seen as influencing the social context. A number of authors such as Orlikowski and Robey (1991); Jones and Nandhakumar (1993); and Walsham (1993) draw on Giddens' structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) to investigate the interaction between human action and social context. Despite the development of richer theoretical concepts in IS literature, the research on EIS and the factor approach in general fails to draw on this work.

In this paper we seek to analyse the relationship between critical factors and the EIS development process by drawing on an in-depth study of EIS development in a large multinational company. The study will be presented later in this paper to describe the chronology of development activities and the social and historical context. The situation will then be analysed: to consider the appropriateness of the factor approach as a way of understanding the relationship between key factors and the EIS development process; and to discuss an alternative explanation of the process by which these influences operated, by drawing on theoretical concepts. The rich insights gained from this study will be used to offer a critique of the assumptions of the factor approach and to outline some implications for practice.

## Research approach

The research approach adopted in this paper involves describing and analysing an in-depth study to develop rich insights and specific implications. Walsham (1996) argues that this approach is particularly suited to studying the complex processes of IS development. As Shneiderman and Carroll (1988) argue, this approach does not aim to produce information suitable for conventional statistical analysis and hypothesis testing, but rather adopts an interpretive epistemology (see for example Walsham & Waema, 1994), that seeks to understand the complex social process of IS development practice.

The case description is based on participant observation by the author who worked full-time as part of the EIS team at LMC Europe (a pseudonym), a large multinational company, for six months. During this period a log was kept of the activities of EIS team members (including the researcher's) and observations and notes of team meetings, discussions and conversations. These were then transcribed into a study describing the EIS development process and important aspects of the real-life context. The writing convention adopted to present this case description is often used in ethnographies (eg, see Van Maanen, 1979; 1988; Kunda, 1992). This ethnographic convention to write up qualitative data has increasingly been used by many authors to develop what Geertz (1973) terms ‘thick descriptions’ of the research context. Walsham (1995) argues the need for thick description in trying to understand what is happening in connection with a complex IS involving many key players in organizations.

In contrast to some field studies such as Curtis et al (1988), we focus on a longitudinal study of a single group rather than a cross-section of several projects. While this enables us to gain insights into the complex interactions of the key players through participant observation, it also means that we must be cautious about generalising from this single case. As Walsham (1995: p 79) argues, the generalisation from the research reported in this paper should be seen as ‘explanations of particular phenomena derived from empirical interpretive research in specific IS settings, which may be valuable in the future in other organizations and contexts’.

The description presented in the next section of this paper focuses on the chronology of development activities and on the historical context, which helps to explain these activities. For brevity, the case description is limited mainly to the development of one EIS project (known as STOCK-EIS). Although it is possible to characterise the process in terms of critical ‘encounters’ and ‘episodes’ as presented by Newman and Robey (1992), it does not address the detailed chronology and social dynamics of the development process which are our primary concern. We therefore adopt a continuous, rather than discrete model, seeking to track a range of activities in which the different team members are engaged over the particular period.

Although the company name and some details about individuals' jobs have been disguised, we have provided as many details as possible, as recommended by Walsham (1996), within the ethical limits and confidentiality agreement to illustrate our arguments in this paper.

## EIS development at LMC

## The company

LMC Europe comprises the European businesses of a large multinational company (LMC), which is a volume manufacturer of high-value consumer products. It has a multibillion dollar turnover and employs over 100 000 staff throughout Europe. The headquarters (HQ) of LMC Europe, the site under study, formally co-ordinates the strategic planning and provides advisory specialist support services, including computing (systems), for its national companies. The management of the affairs of each national company is the responsibility of the board of directors or other equivalent body of that national company. An extract of the organizational chart of the HQ of LMC Europe is depicted in Figure 1 which indicates the manner in which LMC's European activities are formally coordinated. It also shows the existence of a clear management hierarchy with several layers of senior executives and a divisional structure within the company. The main operational functions are co-ordinated by the president and by the vice presidents (VPs) of the support functions such as finance and manufacturing.

## Origin of the EIS in LMC

The EIS concept began to evolve in 1986 in response to concerns raised by a divisional vice-president (VP) about the timeliness and accuracy of daily performance reports (DPR) for the company's different sites. At that time these were manually compiled using information sent from sites in five countries and were hand-delivered to executives. A manager of an area of the systems division, was asked to investigate the use of spreadsheets to address this problem. He recruited an analyst working in the same area to help him. While the analyst was developing the spreadsheets the VP asked for them to be made available using a touch-sensitive screen system, after he had seen one in use in a finance company. A commercial EIS software package was therefore purchased to develop a prototype for the VP.

In mid 1987, a year after the original request, the first EIS prototype was presented to the systems executive and executives from the VP's division. Most of them reacted positively and it was decided to establish an EIS programme in the systems division to develop a fully operational EIS by the start of 1988. Despite initial resistance from a few systems analysts who regarded this new system (DPR-EIS) operating outside their control, the EIS was installed as planned. This system was based on company-standard PCs, which provided a touchscreen, colour, graphical interface through which executive reports could be accessed, and a mainframe-based system which managed the distribution of the reports to the PCs.

## First two years of EIS evolution

Following the first EIS installation for the divisional VP, all executives who reported to him wanted to have access to the EIS so they could answer his queries as it enabled him to get performance reports for their sites even before they did. Hence, during 1988 the number of executive users grew rapidly. The VP who had formally become the sponsor of the EIS programme, began to demand that all his reports should be available on the EIS. In order to ease the staff shortage, another analyst from the finance area joined the systems analyst and the area manager and they became known as the 'EIS team', headed by the area manager. Both analysts individually developed EIS applications for each new report (referred to as 'EIS projects') and they subsequently maintained them. As the EIS became established many new EIS-PCs were installed for executives outside the original division such as VPs of other functions. As LMC began to disperse its operations to a number of different European countries the EIS network was extended to executives at these sites.

During the first two years of EIS operation, the team was concentrating all its efforts on new EIS projects and adding new users, so very little work was carried out on refining existing EIS reports and keeping up with the developments in the data provider divisions. For example, after the original STOCK-EIS was launched during the early period, to provide monthly data on inventory performance to executives, a new PC-based graphics package was introduced in the production finance division (the data providers), without the knowledge of the EIS team. This package enabled the production finance division to prepare their reports in a graphical form rather than the tables used in the EIS. Later, a major strike led to a large adverse stock variance, so many ad hoc reports and briefs had to be prepared for executive meetings, for which the graphics package was used. A low priority was placed on updating the STOCK-EIS reports as the data provider officers were pushed for time (preparing and inputting the data to the EIS took three days each month). As a result, the STOCK-EIS was suspended and the reports prepared on the graphics package were hand-delivered to executives selectively.

In addition to refinements to existing systems, there were increasing requirements for maintenance and support activities such as debugging, documentation, software upgrades and improved security as the systems became more widespread and formalised. As a result an extra analyst from another systems area and an industrial trainee were taken on during 1990. The author later joined the team to provide temporary support before another member was recruited.

Towards the end of the second year, the EIS team members noticed that they were not getting any new requests from executives. It was claimed that this was because the EIS sponsor, who had been the driving force behind most of the new EIS projects, had moved on to manage a company newly acquired by LMC. As news of the EIS spread, however, managers from other sites and from HQ took the lead to initiate new EIS projects. For example, in a meeting a manager from product development (PD) asked the EIS team leader to include PD reports on EIS. An EIS team member took on the development of this prototype (PD-EIS) in association with an analyst from the PD division. In another meeting the systems executive requested a system to report staff movements in the division directly to him. This prototype (STAFF-EIS) was developed by one of the analysts and subsequently demonstrated to the systems executive. The executive however rejected this prototype and asked for a new prototype which involved development of new reports from the vast amount of data from head office. The team members noticed that this would be a large project and that the data providers should be given new modelling software in order to develop the new report. It was decided that in the prevailing economic climate, new resources would not be allocated for this project. So it was agreed to postpone STAFF-EIS project.

While some new development work was carried out, team members were mostly dealing with support activities such as software upgrading, systems security and documentation during that period. For example, during that period, many EIS PCs were upgraded with the new release of EIS software. Systems security was improved by implementing a new, more secure logon procedure for EIS. Although the EIS team followed an elaborate password allocation procedure, the passwords were known to each member in the team and password controls were sometimes taken off executives' PCs on request.

## Origin of the new STOCK-EIS project

The suspension of the old STOCK-EIS was considered to be bad for the image of the EIS in the company. The EIS team leader therefore made several attempts to talk to the production finance division to identify a new project which might get it reinstated. Eventually a meeting was held with the analyst responsible for preparing the inventory data in the production finance division. It was discovered that, since the original STOCK-EIS had been launched, the production finance division had changed their reporting procedures so that they could no longer be represented using the existing screen layouts. The production finance division was also not aware of the graphics capability of the latest EIS software. Early systems consisted of tabular EIS reports to meet the original sponsor's preference for the report format favouring numbers and analysis rather than graphs. A new screen layout was therefore developed, which also offered the potential to view the inventory levels for several different items simultaneously.

The new STOCK-EIS was further developed by one of the team members in association with the inventory analyst. They had to decide which reports to show and their sequence by guessing how executive users would view the reports. The analyst agreed to carry out the necessary modifications to the reports from the graphics package to match the new format. It had been hoped that the new STOCK-EIS prototype would be ready by the end of the week to be demonstrated to managers in the production finance division, but due to high priority work on the PD-EIS project, the prototype could not be completed in time.

The same team member then spent a week on developing the PD-EIS prototype with the PD systems analyst. When the prototype was refined it was installed in the conference room for the presentation to the PD project steering committee. Although the committee debated keeping control of PD-EIS development, it finally agreed to allocate resources for further development. The development of PD-EIS was affected by the ban imposed later that month on using contract workers for system projects resulting in restructuring the PD project.

## Development of the new STOCK-EIS

By the time the new STOCK-EIS prototype was completed, the inventory analyst had requested further modifications to take account of organizational changes which were planned as part of a new manufacturing strategy. After these modifications had been carried out, the STOCK-EIS prototype was tested by the team members and demonstrated to the EIS team leader and the data providers. It was then loaded into the mainframe EIS database to give the production finance executive access to it as he would have the final 'go-ahead' on the new STOCK-EIS. His PC, however, was found to be still using the old version of the EIS software, so it was agreed to upgrade it urgently before the executive was given access to the prototype so that it could be demonstrated with the new version. The earliest available day to update the PC when the executive was away turned out to be a week later (EIS team members were not permitted to work in an executive's office when they were there).

## Arranging a demonstration of STOCK-EIS for the executive

When an EIS team member finally tried to upgrade the executive's PC, he encountered technical problems, so there was a further delay while this was sorted out. The following week, the prototype was tested on the production finance executive's PC to make sure that it was appearing correctly now that the software had been upgraded. The EIS team leader arranged a demonstration for the executive, but it did not go ahead as the executive was engaged in several meetings. The manager responsible for the inventory data expressed concern about leaving it too long as he thought the other people in the division might demonstrate the system to the executive before he had the chance to do so. The EIS team leader therefore asked the executive's secretary to arrange another time.

Despite efforts to refine existing EIS, the team was uncertain about what the executives actually wanted. One reason for this was the departure of the original EIS sponsor, which meant that those executives who had especially got the EIS to answer his queries had become less enthusiastic. The new divisional VP was seen by the team as a ‘travelling man’, who did not make much use of the EIS and was unlikely to require new EIS projects. The team members therefore held several meetings to discuss their next move.

At one of these meetings, the team leader reported that the systems executive had rejected a proposal to send an evaluation questionnaire to executives as it was against the established practice in the systems division. He suggested that team members should use informal discussions with executives when they had the opportunity. The team leader therefore asked the team members to use every opportunity such as during demonstrations, maintenance and talking to executives' secretaries to find out their needs. One of the team members commented that the executives' secretaries treated the EIS team members like maintenance workers and would not talk to them, although this view was not shared by other members.

In meetings the team leader continuously emphasised the company's adverse market position as the cause for many of the problems. The team leader also insisted in meetings that the team should concentrate more on the business issues rather than technical ones as he found that the team members frequently diverted their attention to work on technical features. For example, in an EIS meeting a team member requested that the EIS regulating program should be rewritten as it had grown in an ad hoc way since the launch. As no one in the team was fully familiar with this software, it was suggested to hire a consultant, but this was disallowed by the team leader.

Demonstration of the new STOCK-EIS for the executive When the demonstration was finally held, the production finance executive requested some changes to the screen layout. Sample printouts of the new screens were prepared and the manager responsible for preparing the inventory data showed them to the executive and got a quick, positive response so the prototype was suitably modified.

At an executive meeting, there was a general complaint about there being too many paper reports. An executive from the production finance division stated that they were considering putting inventory reports on the EIS and the divisional VP told them to arrange this at the earliest opportunity. The EIS team considered this as the official acceptance of the STOCK-EIS.

Following this evidence of high-level support for the EIS, the team leader tried to encourage other parts of the production finance division to improve their reports by circulating screen printouts of the STOCK-EIS as an example of the improved EIS screens available. The inventory analyst also sent all users a memo explaining the new system and enclosing samples of the screens and reports. Later that week, the production finance executive asked the inventory analyst for an additional summary screen in the STOCK-EIS which the EIS team had originally proposed but had abandoned at the analyst's request. It was agreed to add the new screen and the analyst promised to bring the reports as quickly as possible and helped with the necessary changes.

## Preparing for the STOCK-EIS launch

The team members made preparations for the launch of STOCK-EIS once the live data became available. Later that week the inventory analyst requested yet further changes. After carrying them out, the team had to redo all the preparations for the launch. Although it was carried out in time, the new data for the STOCK-EIS had not become available as the inventory analyst had promised, because of the organizational changes and resulting report changes. The data providers had to create several new procedures for data collection and to change the spreadsheets used to produce these data. After more changes to the colours of the graphs to improve the contrast in the printouts, the STOCK-EIS was finally launched one month late. Printouts of the STOCK-EIS screens were sent to the executives who were not on the EIS and data providers subsequently stopped using their graphics package and spreadsheet reporting and relied solely on the EIS for inventory performance reporting.

## Analysis

This study indicates that there are many interrelated elements in the social and organizational context that influenced the LMC EIS development process. In this section we will therefore examine the process by which these influences operated. This analysis will be in two stages: first we will investigate how critical factors are seen in the EIS development at LMC Europe and then examine the nature of their influence.

## Critical factors

In the introduction to the paper we identified five factors commonly seen in the literature as critical to the successful EIS development: executive sponsor; technical and other resources; link to business objectives; plan for development and spread; and management of data problems and resistance. We will now consider how each of these was seen in the EIS development at LMC Europe.

## Executive sponsor

The presence of a sponsor was certainly seen as being critical for the EIS development at LMC Europe. For example, the EIS programme was seen as originating in response to the functional VP's request for timely data, which was inadequately served by the reporting mechanisms then available. Once the initial prototype was installed, the VP who had formally become the sponsor of the EIS programme demanded that all his reports should be available on the EIS and thereby help to sustain the continuity of EIS programme.

Although LMC Europe's strong hierarchy prohibited direct contact between EIS team members and executives, the team members' informal knowledge of executive practices guided their assumptions about the executive's data requirements and usage behaviour. For example the sponsor's preference for the report format, favouring numbers and analysis rather than graphs influenced the team members involved in the initial EIS development to produce tabular reports. The expansion of the EIS was influenced by other executives' needs to have access to the EIS in order to answer the VP's queries.

The stories in the company of how executives used the EIS, such as when the sponsor called his subordinate for an explanation for adverse performance figures, helped to reassure the team that the EIS was used. In the later stages however, the team members viewed the new divisional VP as a ‘travelling man’ and therefore he would not give much support for the EIS. This led to concerns within the team about high-level support for the EIS and gave rise to several team meetings and negotiations for a user survey to assess the situation.

## Technical and other resources

The use of appropriate hardware and software technologies was seen as critical to the LMC EIS development.

Systems features such as touch-screen colour graphical interfaces and menu and icon driven software enabled team members to produce what they perceived as an impressive EIS interface, which was seen as getting more acceptance among executives. When a graphical interface for STOCK-EIS was developed, the team leader therefore tried to encourage other parts of the production finance division to improve their reports by circulating screen printouts of the STOCK-EIS as an example of the improved EIS screens available.

The availability of other resources such as financial, personnel and data was also seen as critical. This was however mainly influenced through company policies. For example, the relaxed management attitude towards resource allocation for systems projects during the beginning of the EIS programme was seen as enabling the team members to obtain staff and technical resources to expand the EIS. The tighter control over staff and hardware and software expenses in response to poor market conditions was seen as adversely affecting development work on many EIS projects such as PD-EIS and STAFF-EIS.

The quality of IS staff in terms of the right mix of managerial as well as technical skills was constantly emphasised in LMC Europe. The team leader constantly had to direct team members to concentrate on business issues because of their tendency to focus on technical improvements, which was their main interest. However, the lack of technical expertise in some areas delayed work. For example, no one could understand the program written for regulating EIS reports in the mainframe.

The data for most of the EIS projects were available electronically, such as in the form of spreadsheets, and only small changes were required to adopt them for EIS. Unavailability of the data in the required format was often seen as a drawback. For example, the development of the STAFF-EIS project never went ahead because it involved the development of new reports from the vast amount of data and required obtaining new modelling software.

Team members' resources of authority were also seen as critical in the EIS design. Although the team members lacked authority over data providers' activities they could draw on the authority of the sponsor to obtain more staff and technical resources to initiate new EIS projects.

## Link to business objectives

The EIS at LMC Europe was seen as helping to solve a business problem. For example, when the first EIS project was installed, it was claimed that it addressed the problem that the sponsor had to wait a long time to receive the daily performance reports for the company's different sites which were manually compiled, often inaccurately. Many other EIS projects aimed to provide some additional features which were not otherwise available such as timely reports, colour graphics, and touch screen menus. Although structured approaches to define data needs in EIS were not employed, a series of prototypes were developed through which the EIS reports evolved. For example, many of the new STOCK-EIS reports evolved through prototyping with the data providers.

## Management of data problems and resistance

The management of data problems such as availability, ownership and security were frequently discussed in EIS team meetings. During the early period of EIS development the data availability and access were seen as major problems. For example, the systems area's perception of the first DPR-EIS project as operating outside their control caused problems in installing this system.

Commencement of other projects such as PD-EIS was also viewed as a threat by data providers which led to less cooperation during development. In contrast, the data providers' positive attitude towards the project enabled better co-operation. For example the inventory analyst pushed for a speedy development to enable him to get credit for the STOCK-EIS.

Tight security was seen as essential to safeguard the confidential reports and win the support of data providers in LMC. Although in practice security was compromised due to many issues, such as the reluctance of executive users to use passwords, the team members followed an elaborate password allocation procedure in order to project an image of a secure EIS. Although these formal procedures satisfied the data providers that the EIS complied with company norms about safeguarding sensitive data, there were informal norms within the team to make the passwords known to each other.

## Plan for development & spread

Although there was no evidence of a high-level design plan at LMC Europe, the team members were working to committed deadlines, such as delivering a working STOCK-EIS prototype in a week. These plans were often changed because of changing priorities as in the case of the development of PD-EIS for the presentation to ensure continuity rather than developing STOCK-EIS as previously planned. Most of these changes were not expected and the team could not have foreseen, for example, the repeated requests for modification after preparations for the launch had been made. For example, the need to upgrade the production finance executive's PC and the subsequent discovery of the technical problem held up developing work for STOCK-EIS. The team members' commitment to EIS projects however meant that they sought to fulfil these requests to get the project launched.

The team initially planned to restrict the systems to top executives in the headquarters. This was abandoned as other executives required access to the EIS in order to answer the VP's queries. As LMC began to disperse its operations to a number of European countries the EIS network was extended to executives at foreign sites. This unplanned expansion also increased the pressure on technical and staff resources to deal with problems such as slow response and refinements.

## The nature of the influence

Although these factors were seen as critical in EIS development, it was difficult to identify the possible contribution of each because many factors influenced developers' activities simultaneously. For example, designing DPR-EIS was influenced by the team's evolving standards on EIS report format and screen design; systems division's norms of systems security; LMC Europe's policy on resource allocation, company hierarchy and relationship with executives; market conditions and technological innovation in the business environment and so on. These factors may be seen as relating to different layers of social context in a layered-behavioural model (Figure 2).

These factors also influenced a range of actions rather than limiting a single cause of action. For example, the lack of authority of team members over data providers' activities can be seen as limiting a range of possibilities such as their inability to insist that the production finance division should update STOCK-EIS and so prevent its suspension, to insist that executive users got their reports only through EIS, to prevent data being distributed by e-mail and so on.

Some of these influences however were not direct but acted through factors in other layers. In the above example, while DPR-EIS design was influenced by factors at various levels such as relationships with executives, these factors were in turn influenced by many different layers of social context. For example, the technological development in the external business environment such as the use of touch-screen technologies by another company indirectly influenced the EIS development through executives. This also indicates that these layers of social context or the factors within them were interrelated.

The case also illustrates that the different factors varied in their strength of influence at different times as circumstances changed. For example the design of PD-EIS in its initial stages was largely influenced by the design standards, during prototyping more by the data providers' views on formats and in the later stages, by the recession and by management policies. Another aspect of these influences was that even when conditions were relatively stable, such as the continuing influence of the recession, their effect on EIS development varied between individuals. The strength of these influences therefore appeared to be largely subjective and dependent on individuals' perceptions. These were continuously changing and subject to influence by others. For example, the team leader's continuous emphasis on the adverse market condition affected other team members views of its effect. The varying strength of the influences of these factors shows that the influences of the multilayered social context or the factors within were not frozen in time.

Further, these influences may work in both directions, for example, the EIS developers' activities in LMC were said to have influenced the vendor's design of the upgraded software package. Within the company, there were also many norms relating to EIS team practices which were reproduced as the team members were influenced by each others' actions.

## Discussion

In the introduction to the paper we identified some limitations in the literature prescribing success factors in three major areas: providing no explanation of how the assumed causal connection operates in practice; overlooking the interaction between the factors; and providing a static view of the factors assuming that the influences of these factors are frozen in time. The above analysis illustrates the nature of these influences. We may now highlight some empirical understanding gained in EIS development from our study by reflecting on these three areas. We then outline some implications for practice.

## Causal connections

Much of the EIS literature focuses on factors critical to the outcomes of EIS development. Although this may indicate some of the contextual factors relevant to the development process, it only implies that a causal connection exists rather than explaining the process by which these influences operate. For example, the presence of a sponsor or champion is usually seen as being critical for the success of EIS (eg see DeLong & Rockart, 1992; Barrow, 1990; Fitzgerald 1992; Cottrell & Rapley, 1991; Watson, 1992; Rainer & Watson, 1995). We found that this affected the developers and development work in many ways. For example, at LMC Europe the developers were able to draw on the authority of the sponsor to obtain more staff and technical resources to initiate new EIS projects, to obtain access to restricted data, and to amend company norms.

The presence of these factors however did not necessarily ensure the intended outcomes. For example, the tighter security control for EIS which is recommended in the literature as critical to protect sensitive data, had unintended consequences in our study such as delaying development work and allowing an informal system to develop. This means, as Orlikowski (1992) argues, even where the actions are directly intended to preserve or change some key features of the social context, the result is not guaranteed.

We found in addition however, that even in the absence of the sponsor, once the EIS had become established developers were able to draw on institutionalised authority. The presence or absence of these success factors alone, therefore does not give the complete picture of how these influences affect design and implementation outcomes.

## Interrelation of the factors

EIS literature treats critical factors as groups of independent variables relating to EIS development without recognising the interactions between them. Our findings suggest that these factors or the elements within them are interrelated and were influenced by the developers' actions. For example, factors such as the availability of resources (eg see Houdeshel & Watson, 1987; Paller & Laska, 1989; Watson, 1992) are not independent of other factors. In our study, the executive sponsor reinforced the resources available to EIS development by demanding more reports on EIS and by using EIS as a monitoring system to exercise control over other managers in the division. Moreover, many technical features critical to the success of the EIS, in turn were dependent on other factors such as the financial and technical resources available to the developers, and design conventions embedded in the social context. This means that understanding these interactions is crucial to explaining the reasons for EIS success and failures.

## Dynamic nature of the influences

The complexity of the social context and their influences are inadequately conceptualised and explained in the EIS literature. Our findings suggest that the different factors varied in their strength of influence at different times. The strength of these influences is largely subjective and dependent on EIS team members' perceptions. The varying strength of the influences of these factors indicates that the influences of the social context or the factors within were not frozen in time. This also means that the multi-layered context is not fixed as the Curtis et al (1988) model implies, but dynamic. Further, the social context is not independent of individuals as the Curtis et al model implies, but exists in their minds as perceptual layers. Contextual influences may work in both directions rather than unidirectional as implied in the literature. The limitations of the models of social contexts of IS development also mean that more theoretical development is needed to represent the dynamics of the social context for IS development and use.

## Implications for practice

Our findings illustrated that developers drew on various factors in the interacting conceptual layers of social context to design EIS. This would suggest that approaches to support developers' understanding of the importance of social context may be beneficial. One approach would be to train at least a few developers to have a better understanding of the factors of the organizational context, not only of those factors regarded as 'critical' in the literature, but also of contextual elements such as assumptions, beliefs, shared norms and perspectives. Once acquired, this knowledge of the context may be shared with other developers within the project team when they work on the same project. In the absence of greater access to users, understanding of the social context could improve EIS design through developers' better formal and informal knowledge of the organization which they can build into EIS.

The changing nature of the social context would suggest a need for appropriate approaches to facilitate better communication between participants to help to share knowledge and assumptions of the social context. Although formal communication and documentation can be valuable, it was evident from the LMC case that these might not be workable because of the informal nature of development practices. Further, the value of documentation might be restricted only to developers. Documenting is also time consuming and difficult to maintain under deadline pressures. Different approaches may therefore be needed for promoting communication with different participants. Informal review of each others' work, as shown in the LMC case helped to communicate and reinforce or change developers' assumptions and views. Job rotation policies enable participants to establish many informal links with data providers and systems staff which facilitate communication. On the other hand staff rotation may also cause problems of continuity and may lead to loss of knowledge and loss of time because of the need to learn the new job.

Although traditionally participative design is seen as improving communication between participants, especially between developers and users, we illustrated however that the executive-user involvement was not practically feasible due to the effects of cultural norms such as hierarchical structure and limited time. One way to overcome their effects could be to support mechanisms to improve communication with intermediaries such as data providers. As was evident from the LMC case, participative design with data providers appeared to improve interface design to deal with the continuously changing conditions.

## Conclusions

In this paper, we have sought to illustrate the limitations of EIS literature which often provides lists of success factors aimed at developing EIS and to provide a broader perspective on the relationship between the critical factors and development process by implicitly drawing on theoretical concepts of human action and social context. We have used an in-depth study of EIS development in a large multinational company based on participant observation to illustrate our arguments.

We have sought to show how critical factors are seen in the EIS development at LMC Europe and to examine the nature of their influence. A number of insights were identified: the process by which these influences operated; the interrelation of the success factors; and the dynamic nature of the influence. The theoretical insights developed on the social context such as multiple interacting perceptual layers can be of value to other researchers in EIS development or IS development in general, as a basis for further investigation. We also illustrated the limitations of the context models in IS literature to represent the dynamic nature of the social context. Further theoretical development to deal with the dynamic nature of social context would therefore seem to be needed. By recognising the nature of the influences we may be better able to support EIS development. The theoretical interpretation developed in this paper on the contextual influences may therefore be of some guidance to understand the nature of these influences.
