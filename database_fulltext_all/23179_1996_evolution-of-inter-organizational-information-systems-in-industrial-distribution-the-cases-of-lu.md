# Evolution of inter-organizational information systems in industrial distribution: the cases of Luna and Pappersgruppen

O Fredriksson and M Vilgon

Stockholm School of Economics, Box 6501, S-113 83 Stockholm, Sweden

Many of the best known and most successful examples of competitively advantageous information systems are those that link the focal organizations with their suppliers, distributors or end-users; so called inter-organizational information systems. There is little published research describing the evolutionary processes of such systems. This article presents two, earlier unpublished, longitudinal Swedish case studies of successful evolution of inter-organizational information systems. On the basis of the comparative analysis of the cases, future research is proposed to focus on dynamic aspects and applying relationship (or industrial network) perspectives.

## Introduction

New ideas about organizational design have received increasing interest in recent years. These new types of organizations have been given labels such as: networked organizations (Miles & Snow, 1992; Powell, 1987), learning organizations (Drucker, 1988), team-based organizations and virtual organizations (Davidow & Malone, 1992). A driving force behind these new organizational ideas in many industries is the belief that increased competitiveness demands increased cooperation. A fundamental building block for increased cooperation is to consider the trading counterpart as a partner and stakeholder in goal achievement and value creation. This means setting the research focus on the relationship between firms rather than on the individual actor and a perspective where the organization spans beyond the focal firm. This perspective can be labelled inter-organizational relationship (IOR) research.

One of the premises in this article is that an inter-organizational information system (IOS) is an important enabler, at times even a necessity, to realise increased competitiveness through increased cooperation with certain trading partners. Customers demand quality goods, (ie goods meeting expected quality) delivered on time. An IOS does not contribute to on time delivery directly, but indirectly. An order-entry IOS normally speeds up order data processing, lead time for placement of own orders (regardless of whether this is done manually or electronically) and the generation of delivery/shipment data. In addition, an order-entry IOS is often based on an inventory-control internal information system (IS), which can be used concurrently for optimising the inventory, ie minimise inventory value without incurring stock-out situations for goods perceived as important by the customers.

Some recent trends are affecting the need for IOS applications: (1) information technology (IT) development has dramatically increased the capacity of the distribution channel to store, process, and transmit information (Glazer, 1991) at dramatically lowered cost-performance ratios (Scott Morton, 1992); (2) the degree of specialisation has increased, which in turn increases the need for co-ordination and integration of adjacent activities (Gadde & Håkansson, 1993); (3) competitive pressure has increased, becoming a driving force for closer, less adversarial trading relationships (Cunningham & Tynan, 1993).

However, the IOR literature seldom examines the IOS phenomenon and the IOS literature seldom takes an IOR perspective. A relationship approach, taking into consideration individual trading partners' profiles, is often neglected within the IOS literature. For example, Meier and Sprague (1991) only indirectly base their discussion on a relationship perspective, by stressing the need for modular IOS architectures as different organizations participate in an IOS. The overall message proposed in this article is that an integration of ideas between the IOS literature and the IOR literature would be mutually beneficial.

Researchers aiming for understanding, explaining or predicting IOS evolution choose different perspectives and methods, but different perspectives and methods tell different stories about IOS evolution. For example, some IOS studies are more general and inter-disciplinary, focusing on the context (Venkatraman, 1994) or taking a longitudinal case approach (Mason et al, 1991); other IOS studies are more specific, focusing on ‘source firms’ (Ciborra, 1991; 1994) 'target firms' (O'Callaghan et al, 1992) or on product attributes (Malone et al, 1987) in order to understand, explain or predict specific or general cases.

There is research on how to implement and design IOS, as well as research on their possible effects. However, there is relatively little reported on the evolution of IOS. Some examples are Benjamin et al (1990), who stress the importance of focusing on the evolution of IOS applications over time, Cooper and Zmud (1990), who propose that IT applications evolve according to a distinct diffusion model, and Meier and Sprague (1991) who touch upon the evolutionary aspects when they predict that IOS are likely to evolve as IS have done.

The purpose of this article is to contribute to the understanding of how IOS evolve. The purpose has been approached through:

\- describing two cases of successful IOS development, which is interesting per se given the scarcity of published cases;

● conducting a theoretically based comparative case analysis;

\- proposing a research agenda.

Like Benjamin et al (1990), we use the IOS concept as an umbrella term for both transaction processing applications and non-routine task-support applications, regardless of whether they are applied in an electronic hierarchy or in an electronic market setting. The most common type of IOS is electronic data interchange (EDI) systems (Benjamin et al, 1990; Meier & Sprague, 1991), which can be defined as inter-organizational transaction processing applications in established buyer-seller relationships.

As a research method we have chosen case-studies (multiple data sources) since it gives high ‘currency’ with a medium level of ‘data integrity’ (Bonoma, 1985). The task of evidence gathering has been conducted through extensive interviews with key managers and personnel and through ample access to written information. In writing the case descriptions we follow the guidelines formulated by ‘The Harvard MIS History Project’ in that we identify ‘a time line with critical events’ as a methodological tool when describing the IOS evolution. In addition, we try to write case descriptions which in themselves form ‘credible stories’ (Mason et al, 1991). The case descriptions are thereby results by themselves in that they reveal some of the dynamics involved in the IOS evolution.

A highly developed competence in relationship management, ie the ability to differentiate between the exchange relationships along with the importance and needs of their business partners, is especially important for middlemen. Studying how successful industrial distributors, that are (likely to be) highly advanced in relationship management, may be affected by use of IOS is therefore assumed to provide valuable insights. Thus, the first selection criteria was to choose IOS source firms taking positions as successful industrial distributors. Another selection criteria was that the focal firms should have a long and successful history of IOS utilisation. Since IOS are such a relatively recent phenomenon, a relatively experienced firm is needed in order to obtain interesting results (cf Hagström, 1991; Holland et al, 1992).

Luna and Pappersgruppen are two early and successful – in terms of adoption and utilisation – Swedish industrial distributors and developers of IOS. Hence, they are referred to as source firms. Both firms, distributors of tools and fine paper respectively, were the first to implement IOS in their respective distribution systems. They are also among the most profitable distributors in Sweden overall.

In the parts of the distribution channels where the selected IOS cases were initially implemented the degree of IOS utilisation is substantial. Currently, Luna receives 75% of its orders through IOS links, while the paper mills receive all orders via their IOS links with the distributor Pappersgruppen, which in turn receives 50% of its orders by IOS links. Thus, in these parts of the distribution systems the IOS have become a strategic necessity (cf Meier & Sprague, 1991).

Thus, to summarise, in this article two successful examples of industrial distributors implementing IOS, Luna and Pappersgruppen, are presented. In the following section the cases are analysed along selected dimensions of the 'MIT90s framework' and also related to IOS research and some well-known cases. Thereafter, the most important findings are presented as conclusions and finally, some IOS evolution research directions are proposed.

## The case of Luna

Luna, founded in 1917, is the leading wholesaler in the Scandinavian market for tools, machines and workplace equipment for professional industrial use. In each of these product sectors, Luna supplies high-quality brands. Sales are mainly to the hardware and machinery retail trade. In the 1950s, Luna made the policy decision to sell exclusively to dealers. The rationale was that they did not want to compete with the dealers due to their closeness to, and knowledge of, the local markets. Today, Luna markets and distributes an assortment of more than 100 000 articles, of which 34 000 are kept in stock. Five thousand of the 34 000 articles are classified as high-value products, accounting for 90% of Luna's sales. Eighty-five percent of the products are maintenance, repair, and operating (MRO) goods, ie, goods supporting production.

In 1993, Luna had about 6% of the total Swedish market. In general, Luna accounts for 5–30% of the dealers' purchase volume. Luna's core customer group consists of about 150 industrial dealers. End-users consist of engineering and building industries, public administration and the do-it-yourself market.

The industrial organization of the hardware and machinery supply system to the Swedish market consists of: 2000–3000 suppliers; forty primary distributors of which five are full-range wholesalers; 400 industrial dealers and 400 consumer-oriented dealers; and, finally, about 100 000 industrial end-users. Since the beginning of the 1950s, there has been a dramatic decrease (approximately 50%) in the number of dealers.

Two characteristics of the goods flow in this supply system are that average unit values are relatively low and handling costs are relatively high. The hardware and machinery trade is therefore, distribution-intensive with large transaction volumes. In combination with the broad product range, this implies that inventory management is a key skill in this supply system.

This is the case in which an industrial distributor, medium-sized and profitable (see Table 1) and with a strong market position, made its IOS an integrated part of its strategy. The IOS development was initially internally driven by a co-alignment between the business logistics and IS units and externally driven by the major customers, which integrated themselves vertically with Luna. The IOS of Luna has enabled it to expand its market scope, its virtual product range, increase its service levels, thereby contributing to maintaining its high profitability. ## Distributive needs driving IOS formation and development

By 1973, most internal routines of major importance were computerised. The first terminal was installed that year, and in 1978, terminals were used in all functions within the Luna organization. The foundation for IOS implementation was laid.

In 1977, Luna tested an on-line customer order-entry system in cooperation with three dealers who were among its most important customers. Thus, Luna opened up the catalogue database and the inventory database for its customers. After the tests had proven to be successful, forty customers were connected to Luna's mainframe. In 1979, there were already eighty customers (corresponding to 10% of the customers of that time) connected via 'dumb' terminals. The rapid diffusion can be explained by the low degree of adaptation that is needed for online systems on the buying side. Renting fixed telephone lines was, however, fairly expensive at that time; 25 000 SEK for each line annually. The connected customers were given a 1% registration discount by Luna.

Besides the electronic ordering capability, the IOS of Luna, called the LAGER (Wholesaler–Dealer AB Delivery) System, contained information about prices, stock levels, terms of delivery, etc, for all inventory-held products (A and B articles) within Luna's product range (see Figure 2). A and B articles have high and medium annual requirement values (ie unit value times annual usage). As an alternative to the online terminals, dealers were also offered electronic ordering in the form of handheld (off-line) computers.

The primary objective from the Luna perspective was to reduce the order-entry costs, ie, increase internal efficiency. The second objective, also aimed at increasing the comparative efficiency of Luna, was to increase customers' ease of ordering and receiving information. An increased comparative efficiency was believed to contribute to increased competitive advantage.

In the early 1980s, three customers were connected online to Luna during office hours from their internal information systems. The IS was developed by Luna in close cooperation with two of these three major customers and was called the OLAF System. OLAF was designed as a set of administrative information modules (invoicing, accounting, accounts receivable/payable ledger, statistics). Up to 1987, Luna took the responsibility, and the costs, for the development and maintenance of the customers' OLAF Systems.

In 1981, Luna had about 300 terminals connected to its mainframe. About a third of them were used internally (in Sweden, Norway, Denmark and former Western Germany), about fifty terminals were located on the three OLAF-customers' premises, while the rest were placed at ninety-four other customers' premises.

Luna's capability of offering a broad product range to its customers was expanded in 1982 when it designed the SALDO (Supplier-Dealer BC Delivery) System in cooperation with six of its suppliers. Luna thereby increased the available product range from 34000 to 100000 articles. Articles with medium and low annual requirement values (B and C articles) from these six suppliers are represented in this IOS sourced by Luna (see Figure 2). The customer sends its electronic purchase order to Luna, where the individual order items are automatically sorted and channelled to the respective supplier. Thus, goods can be ordered both from Luna's (LAGER) and the suppliers' stock, whereupon transport is synchronised to the customer (see Figure 2). Typically, the suppliers' stocks are up-dated daily. Luna has found that the degree of accuracy in this information, which varies across the suppliers, has significant effects on the degree of utilisation (Fredriksson, 1992). Even though sales via the SALDO system at present only account for $3\%$ of Luna's turnover, the service offered to the customers is thought to be of vital importance because it supports recurrent needs.

In 1993, Luna had direct electronic links with about twenty of its 800 suppliers. Almost all these twenty IOS links to the SALDO suppliers are connected in different ways.

A system designed for those AA articles (ie, articles with high annual requirement values and that are considered to be very important by the customers) which have fairly even consumption patterns over time was implemented in 1989; the TRANSIT (Supplier–Dealer AA Delivery) System. If the customer's order meets specific minimum criteria regarding order value, and order line value, and if the customer can wait somewhat longer for the delivery, a discount on the order is received in return. Orders via TRANSIT account for 2–3% of Luna's turnover at present.

In line with Luna's recently adopted strategy for getting closer to the industrial end-users, the TRANSIT concept has been extended to a system where a few groups of members from a wholesaler – dealer – end-user chain cooperate to reduce the total distribution costs; this IOS is called the EXAKT (Supplier–Wholesaler–Dealer–End-user Delivery) System. Luna and the individual end-user jointly agree on three limits of value for each end-user article, along the parameters of quantity demanded and delivery time, which determine whether the goods are sent from the dealer, from Luna or from the supplier. The system contains a database which matches the three partners' article numbers.

The operation of Luna's IOS is reported to have been a necessary prerequisite for Luna's successful establishment in the Norwegian market, which started in the early 1980s.

## Channel organization dynamics: the shift from proprietary IOS (1977–1987) to open network IOS (1987 to present)

Until 1973, Luna was a family-owned firm. Then the dealer-owned and partially competing wholesaler, Järnia, bought 50% of the firm. At the same time, a formal agreement was made between the two firms not to offer competing products. The remaining part of Luna was bought by Järnia in 1984.

In the mid-1980s, there was uncertainty among Luna personnel about the future; sharply decreasing profitability, three CEOs between 1981–1987 and the buy-out by Järnia contributed to this. The decreasing profitability (see Table 1) was partly caused by high IT costs. One of the measures in order to reduce Luna's (and Järnia's) escalating IT costs was the formation of a separate and jointly owned value added network (VAN) firm.

In 1985, four employees left Luna (among them the IS manager) and started a competing supplier-initiated

VAN firm. Since the former Luna employees had online VAN competence, the main emphasis was put into this mode of IOS communication. Backing them were the local marketing organizations of five large Swedish multinational corporations, including SKF (Hagström, 1991) and Esab (Konsynski & McFarlan, 1990).

A standstill situation among the dealers arose. At the request of the Järnia dealers, as customers and owners of one of the VANs, the two competing VANs merged in 1987. This meant that a new electronic network was formed, which became open for anyone interested in connecting to it. Thus, the platform for an open IOS network was created out of two proprietary ones. However, this has not evolved into an electronic market (Malone et al, 1987), but more as electronic hierarchies (Johnston & Vitale, 1988). The number of firms connected to the network has continually and steadily increased since 1987.

After the VAN merger, the customers' online connection to Luna's mainframe regained momentum. In 1993, the number of dealers with active IOS links to Luna amounted to 400 (50% of the potential).

## IT-outsourcing and IT-infrastructure redesign (1990 to present)

Starting in 1987, a number of strategic decisions were made concerning Luna and Järnia as a preparation for a planned launching on the Stockholm Stock Exchange: (1) the Ferro Group was established as the parent company of Luna and Järnia; (2) management for the newly-formed Ferro Group was recruited; (3) new Chief Executive Officers were appointed in both Luna and Järnia, and (4) some joint group functions/firms were established. Among these, a business unit called FerroData was established, with the purpose of cutting costs by providing joint data-processing operations for the Ferro Group.

In 1990, Luna (and Järnia) formed a partnership with the most advanced Swedish forwarding company in terms of IT, namely Bilspedition. The estimated development potential regarding IT was one of the major determinants for the choice of Bilspedition as a strategic partner. Close cooperation and mutual adaptation in terms of IOS-supported logistic solutions has resulted in increased customer service levels. Mainly due to the partnership with Bilspedition, Luna soon received marked improvements in two key delivery quality ratios (1992): a service-level of 93.5% (92% in 1989) and an on-time delivery score of 99% (97% in 1989).

In 1990, the Ferro Group management decided to design a joint group communications platform/system with three kinds of applications: Ordering, Distribution and Invoicing (ODI). The ODI system is expected to yield significant benefits. For example, the delivery lead time is expected to decrease from two to one day. The group perspective was imposed upon Luna which, at that time, wanted to design and implement IOS links on an increasing scale with its major suppliers. It was decided to postpone this until the implementation of the ODI system was finished. However, the complexities in the order system turned out to have been heavily underestimated, which resulted in down-scaling of the original plans. In early 1995, the ODI system, heavily delayed, was brought into operation. The outcome of the new IT platform was disappointing during the first half year, due to problems arising when logistical activities and a complex IT platform were integrated.

In 1994, the Ferro Group, including Luna, merged with a profitable trading group of high technology products, Bergman & Beving. One of the trading group's rationales for the merger with Luna (and Järnia) was to complement its own competence in sales to industrial end-users with Luna's competence in distribution, logistics and 'more mechanical selling'. Therefore, some dealers are apprehensive that Luna will start selling directly to the industrial end-users. If this happens, the IOS of Luna may also play a significant role in its future development in this respect.

## Performance and IOS link development

The extent to which the IOS have influenced different key performance indicators is unclear. However, the IOS source firm Luna has been successful over the last decades (see Table 1). Luna has long been a profitable firm. Between 1983 and 1992, Luna's total profits were 350 million SEK which corresponds to 4% of its total turnover during that period. ROCE has been within the range of 11–30%, with an average of 18% during this period.

The inventory turnover rate has been within the range of 4–4.7 during the last fifteen year period, which is an improvement compared to the 1970s (see Table 1).

The IOS of Luna has been an integrated part of its strategy since the late 1970s. The number of IOS links has increased at a fairly steady rate since the start in 1977. About 75% of the orders were received electronically in 1993: via handheld terminals (40%), via online terminals (30%) and via EDI (5%).

## The case of Pappersgruppen

Fine paper (ie paper with a low content of mechanical pulp) is the sort of paper found in copying machines, offset prints and promotional brochures. The production is process-oriented and capital-intensive. The process follows three major steps (forest and wood industry, pulp mill and fine paper mill) whereafter wholesalers or distributors supply the fine paper to the customers, who are mostly in the printing industry.

One of the largest paper-producing companies in the world is the Stora group that sells fine paper worth about 12 billion SEK annually. Today, Stora's distributor in Sweden is Pappersgruppen, a medium sized firm with a market share of over $50\%$ . It was Papersgruppen which developed the IOS supported distribution system of Stora that, together with an advanced distribution policy, propelled Stora to become one of the major players in the fine paper industry in the world.

In this case (cf Vilgon, 1996) an industrial distributor within a company group developed a concept consisting of a product range, a logistic model and an IOS. With this concept, Pappersgruppen first consolidated its national distribution, thereby integrating backwards (1970s), then expanding horizontally, ie, internationally (1980 to present), and finally integrating forward (1986 to present). In Figure 3 a time line with critical events for Pappersgruppen is presented.

## The phase prior to the IOS (1960s)

In the 1960s, there was one fine paper wholesaler in every region throughout Sweden. At the same time, there were many small paper mills, each producing its own paper quality. The wholesalers bought paper in large quantities in times of recession to get the lowest prices on the market and then sold it, under their own wholesalers' brands, during prosperous times. Each producer had its own (technical) quality and the product range offered by the wholesalers differed from region to region. Some of these wholesalers prospered while others did not, which resulted in a turbulent period with a large number of mergers and acquisitions.

## Redesign of the distribution and formation of the IOS (1970s)

In 1967, the owners of three major paper mills decided to cooperate in production as well as in distribution. They bought a dozen regional wholesalers and, in 1970, jointly formed Pappersgruppen, a national wholesaler with the right, but not the obligation, to distribute the owners' products. The new top management team was a strong and vigorous group of individuals: the chief executive officer, the chief product manager, and the chief information officer with his technical team. Together they had unique knowledge of the market, production and wholesale sides of the business. Backed by solid support from the owners they devised and implemented three interdependent and radical changes in the production and industrial distribution: a revision of the production, an investment in a co-ordinating IS, and a redesign of the distribution (each described below).

Concerning the revision of production, the producing companies, the three mills, agreed to restructure and focus their production on a specific range of paper within the product range offered by the wholesaler. The objective was to reduce each paper mill's production to a small number of products, ie, focusing their effort on just one or two paper machines. Each mill revised its production and investment strategy and increased the volume produced by each paper machine. Through the concentration of the mill's production into just a few grades, they could secure a stable quality under one brand. During this process the group established a handful of brands, one for each paper machine. This adaptation of production to the product range of Pappersgruppen was later followed by other producers, without ownership ties.

On the wholesale side this revision reduced the number of articles but increased the (technical) breadth of the range. To be able to offer the customers a sufficient range of products, other mills, nationally and internationally, were affiliated. Together with these producers, Pappersgruppen could offer a full range of fine paper to core customers.

The second major change concerned the co-ordination of handling information. The IS from one of the merging wholesalers, later to become the new wholesaler, Pappersgruppen, had been in use for two years. This was a mainframe-based batch system that was unable to keep track of stock during working hours. An article held in stock in the morning could be sold several times, by different salespersons, at noon. The management of Pappersgruppen decided, in terms of online processing, to rethink the use of computerised ISs in the distribution of paper. A visionary plan was launched, in which it was decided that the IS was to become the major controlling device for the distribution chain. It would initially cover paper mill to wholesaler, but in the future would include both affiliated distribution companies and customers.

Two years were spent on defining the functions that the IOS should perform, whereafter the chosen system supplier developed the proprietary application software. The effort put into the system development was massive; the initial investment amounted to three years' profit in the firm. In 1975, the accounting routine and the 'wholesaler warehouse application', an inventory control function were taken into use. The IOS containing these applications (and several others which were later developed and implemented) was called PISTOL (Pappersgruppens Informations SysTem via On-Line).

From the start, the system supplier used dumb terminals to an IBM mainframe. The initial system, designed for 7000 transactions per day, which was considered sufficient at the time, consisted of two databases: a complete product database, with a wide range of data about every article, and a corresponding customer database.

The first application, the wholesaler warehouse application, connected the five, geographically dispersed warehouses. The wholesaler was thereby able to reduce its stock by transporting goods from one warehouse to another when an article was demanded. The three mills were involved in the planning and development of the IOS and given the option to connect to it. Within a year, all of them were connected with full access even though one was not connected online. The mills thereby had control over their sales through the wholesaler in addition to their own sales outside of the wholesaler's market, ie, outside of the Swedish market. The wholesaler, in turn, received accurate up-to-the-minute information on the level of stock for each article, both at its own (five) and at the mills' (three) warehouses.

The third major change concerned logistics, where a radical option was chosen. The three main paper mills were offered an extra commission on their sales, through their warehouses with direct transport to customers, for keeping a guaranteed stock of articles most frequently sold. This left the wholesaler to provide the articles less frequently sold in its warehouses, and so reduced the inventory held in the distribution chain as a whole. Pappersgruppen also reduced its number of warehouses to five with the capability of delivering articles from the wholesaler's warehouses within 24 h, and from the mills' warehouses within two days. This innovation was made possible by co-ordination through the IOS and was called the mill warehouse application. In 1979, more than 500 articles were provided from the mills' warehouses, the equivalent of 20% of the total volume sold in the market by the wholesaler (see Figure 4).

In 1978, all articles sold by Pappersgruppen were listed in the new price-application in the IOS. Each article was given a base-price connected to a newly invented article number. On this base-price there were mark-ups for differences in type, packaging and method of transport. On top of this, the prices were differentiated by volume. Depending on the volume ordered, measured in kilograms, the customer was given six alternative prices. The cost of transport was thus transferred, in close correlation with the forwarding company, to the price. The pricing was said to follow an honest principle, ie, to reflect to some extent the subsequent costs incurred for each order. The new prices were therefore widely accepted by the customers. The IOS was then complemented with several other applications, among them one for statistics, one for purchasing and a planning routine for the mills' operation.

## Extending the IOS use horizontally - Internationalisation (1979 to present)

In 1981, one of the three owners had to sell its part of the wholesaler, Pappersgruppen, and chose to sell to the other two owners. This indicated a decreased integration but did not interfere with their cooperation with and sourcing of paper to Pappersgruppen.

The good performance of Pappersgruppen and its owners, the three supplying mills, gave the financial strength to expand Pappersgruppen's business operations horizontally. In 1979, a relatively small Danish wholesaler was bought, later followed by another. The whole concept from Pappersgruppen was implemented in the acquired companies, ie, the product range, the logistic system and the IOS. The acquisition of a third wholesaler (including several small paper mills) with market dominance was planned for a long time but was not executed until 1987.

In 1979, an agent was set up in Norway, since the major wholesaler could not be bought. The Norwegian market had a situation similar to the Swedish market a few years earlier, ie, many small paper mills in the hands of independent wholesalers. After some initial complications, the major Norwegian wholesaler, through the agent, adopted the concept of Pappersgruppen including the adoption of the PISTOL IOS. Thereby the wholesaler outsourced some of their own operations, eg, the product range composition and the price structure. At the same time, they received a full fine paper range and deliveries within a day. In order not to open up the market for competition, a Finnish firm, already present with a special paper quality, was offered minority ownership in the agent. Within a few years, half the volume sold in the Norwegian market came from Sweden, and the expansion continued, without major new investments.

During the 1980s, considerable efforts were made to internationalise fine paper distribution. In England, a wholesaler was bought and restructured according to the Pappersgruppen concept. As the wholesaler was small and quite distant from the Swedish mills, this investment has not proved to be the same success as the other international activities. In Finland, a small stake in a minor wholesaler was bought. In the contract signed between the seller and Pappersgruppen, the usage of the IOS was one of the major clauses. This was a strategic move, since the seller, a British paper group, had some insight into the IOS, as the strategic competitive tool it had become. This resulted in plans for major cooperation in distribution in the whole of Europe, but this plan was never implemented. Similar efforts were planned in many European markets but not realised. The development of the IOS in this phase was mainly focused on extending its use. Several national and international companies wanted to purchase the system and implement it in their businesses in other industries.

All the international affiliates and several suppliers to

Pappersgruppen were connected to the IOS which placed considerable demands on the personnel at Pappersgruppen. The usage of the system expanded heavily throughout the 1980s from 30 000 transactions to 150 000 transactions per day by the end of the decade.

A firm responsible for the IOS was established as well as a parent company responsible for all the international companies, including Pappersgruppen, that once again became responsible for the Swedish market. In the 1990s, after the mergers and acquisitions by the Stora group, there were demands on the IOS management and staff to connect new companies.

The content of the IOS has, over time, been improved and developed; for example, the first EDI connection was installed with the former third owner. In 1985, the first standardised application was installed. This application, though, was given a new interface. Several dialouts, ie, gateway applications, to other IOS were installed; for example a connection to a bank and another to a major credit control firm.

## Extending the IOS use forward (1986 to present)

When the IOS was implemented in the 1970s, the idea to broaden the product range began to take form. Given that the customers were satisfied with the work done by the wholesaler, it was believed that they might be willing to purchase products other than fine paper from Pappersgruppen. Thus a system selling concept was introduced. Over time the product range grew (the number of articles offered almost doubled within fifteen years) and new customers were approached.

These new customers, who did not belong to the printing industry, consisted of public administration, large private firms and retailers of office material. They were not content with the price application in the IOS. Therefore, an ‘application for contracted customers’ was developed in 1982. Although, it had been planned for a long time, this was the first move to connect the customers to the IOS. The task confronting the personnel of Pappersgruppen was two-fold: first, to open up a necessary part of the IOS to the customers, and second, to provide the required hardware and software to the customers. As a response, a videotex application and a PC application were developed.

In 1986, the first customer was connected to the PISTOL IOS. It was called ‘the partner system’. From the start, the personnel at Pappersgruppen did the training through seminars, courses and direct support. Several of the early partners were the tightest linked customers to the wholesaler. In order to assist adoption of the partnerships, Pappersgruppen at first supported the purchase of hardware, but later modified its support to give a rebate on orders placed through the customers’ terminals. All the customers in the printing industry, and some of the customers in the new areas, were approached. In the beginning it was hard to convince the customers, but as the PC gained wide acceptance, some of the need for customer education vanished and the number of users increased.

## Performance and IOS link development

The extent to which the IOS has influenced various key performance indicators is unclear. However, the IOS source firm, Pappersgruppen, has been successful over the last decades (see Table 2). Pappersgruppen has, since the establishment in 1970, been a profitable firm. The turnover has increased more than ten times and most of the time it has had a high level of profitability. There has been, though, a drop in sales in the 1990s, which is said to be an effect of the national recession. In 1980 there was a temporary drop in profitability, mainly caused by a major re-organization of the company group. The inventory turnover rate has improved over time.

The IOS of Järnia has been an integrated part of its strategy since the early 1970s. The number of IOS links has increased dramatically during the last decade. About 50% of the orders were received electronically in 1993, while the mills receive all orders via their IOS links with the distributor Pappersgruppen.

## Case analysis

Since the IOS literature contains strategic, organizational and environmental perspectives on IOS development, the MIT90s framework, proposed by Rockart and Scott Morton (1984), Benjamin & Scott Morton (1988) and Scott Morton (1991), with its eclectic nature has been chosen for the case analysis. Taking a relationship approach to IOS development would bring about a combination of two MIT90s frameworks.

The inherent cross-functional and cross-organizational character of IOS calls for an eclectic approach in order to explore comprehensively the adaptation processes that are necessary, both within and between firms participating in IOS supported activities.

When applying the framework we agree with MacDonald (1991, p. 162): 'the problem [is] what to put in the boxes and what is implied by the linkages'. In the following analysis an attempt is made accordingly to match the most important insights from the cases to appropriate dimensions in the framework: external socio-economic environment, source firm strategy, management processes and individuals & roles.

## External socio-economic environment: channel organization and industrial logic

The existing overall structure of a distribution system influences the prerequisites for establishing IOS links, since IOS decisions for individual exchange relationships are contingent upon decisions for other exchange relationships. Holland et al (1992) propose a generic model for the major factors of importance when evaluating the prerequisites for implementing EDI in dyads within distribution channels (ie, sourcing EDI links towards selling firms and sourcing EDI links towards buying firms respectively).

## Market position

The market positions of the IOS source firm and the IOS target firm respectively are likely to affect the source firm's abilities to influence its trading partners regarding trading terms, ie buyer and supplier power. Luna had relatively more supplier power than buyer power which arguably is an important explanatory factor underlying Luna's mainly forward IOS integration. Both Luna and Pappersgruppen have strong market positions. Luna and Pappersgruppen have broader and deeper product ranges than their competitors.

## Strength of exchange relationships

The extent to which a trading partner's buying needs or supplied product range is of commercial interest for the individual firm, affects the propensity to establish an IOS link in each specific case. As noted in the case description, the IOS sourced by Luna have been developed to some extent in cooperation with its major customers.

Vertical (ownership) integration implies a strong relationship, which facilitates any technological implementation. In both cases, ownership ties were present, which supposedly facilitated the IOS implementations. In the case of Pappersgruppen, there was joint forward integration from three producers, while Luna was the object of backward integration from a voluntary chain of approximately 300 dealers. This difference in direction of ownership integration is reflected in the difference in main purpose of the established IOS; in the former case the focus is on selling, including subsequent diffusion to customers in export markets, while in the latter case the main purpose is buying, including importing. What the two have in common is that their IOS were initially adopted and subsequently adapted and routinized (cf Cooper & Zmud, 1990) with firms that were their owners. In addition, both source firms had relatively weak owners, since their equal voting powers were offsetting them, which implies more relative authority for top management.

The later IOS development of Pappersgruppen, the forward IOS links, has taken place without any ownership ties. Through IOS links, vertical integration is achieved without ownership (cf Konsynski & McFarlan, 1990). Such integration is not, however, necessarily technology driven. The closer integration may have existed before the IOS links were adopted (Johnston & Lawrence, 1988).

IOS can be classified on an organizational continuum, ranging from IOS links established between totally independent firms to IOS links between firms with a 100% ownership-relation. IOS close to the latter end-point of the continuum, which initially and partially was true in our cases, can be denoted as ‘quasi-IOS’.

## Type of product/service

The nature of the product or service being traded, including aspects such as volume, frequency and type of information, determines the need for IOS (Holland et al, 1992). In addition, the amount of information needed to specify the attributes of a product is suggested by Malone et al (1987) to influence the chosen co-ordination form; whether coordination will be obtained through a hierarchical relationship or through a market relationship.

The significance of the supplied product range, as perceived by the buyers, most likely affects the willingness to invest in IOS. In general, original equipment manufacturer (OEM) products are perceived as being more critical than maintenance, repair, and operating (MRO) products.

The bulk of products within the Luna product range is not of strategic importance for the professional end-users. The total value of the range that Luna is representing amounts to only 3–4% of the end-users' purchase value. At the same time this range has high relative distribution costs (indirect costs). In the case of Pappersgruppen the range is of great importance to its customers, since the distributor provides a large share of its customers' needs.

## Distribution channel characteristics

The degree of structure, eg the concentration ratios at each distribution system level, has over time developed to be higher in the distribution system of Pappersgruppen, than in Luna's case. In conjunction with the more narrow and more OEM-oriented product range, this partly explains why Pappersgruppen provides its customers with a significantly higher proportion of their buying needs than Luna does. Thus, room is provided for Pappersgruppen to establish and maintain a proprietary IOS link to its trading partners to a larger extent than in the Luna case.

## Interaction effects explain development paths

The broad product range, the numerous suppliers and end-users, recurrent end-user needs and (on average) low value/weight imply a transaction- and distribution-intensive market system, and thus a relatively strong need for middlemen. Luna's position as a supplier to its customers has been strong, but not dominant, as Luna caters for between 5–30% of its customers' buying needs.

The industrial logic and industrial organization situation was in many respects the same in the Pappersgruppen case, but with some distinctions. In the first phases in the Pappersgruppen case, there was a need for restructuring the product range, due to increasing capital costs in the whole distribution system. The actual conduct in the Pappersgruppen case was in line with ‘the organized behaviour system’ approach proposed by Alderson (1957). The main building blocks for Pappersgruppen, in reducing the capital costs within the channel (Gustafsson & Svensson, 1983), were its logistics concept and the IOS.

## Source firm strategy

Business relationships are often long-lasting (Arndt, 1979; Gadde & Mattsson, 1987). Strategy formulation is therefore often embedded in these long-lasting relationships. Thus, exchange relationships are investments and should be treated accordingly.

Both case firms have significantly reduced the number of suppliers from whom they purchase. The terminated relationships clearly imply less cooperation. Even though this development is not extraordinary per se, it indicates an increased dependence on the remaining suppliers and a concurrent shift towards partnership relationships. To exemplify this, the establishment of the SALDO IOS cooperation with some suppliers was an important strategic move by Luna; and in 1986 when Pappersgruppen began to connect its customers it was marketed as ‘become a partner with Pappersgruppen’.

Since the 1950s, Luna has chosen to sell exclusively to the dealers in the distribution system. This pledge from Luna not to bypass the dealers has arguably increased the trust between them, which in turn has made the dealers more inclined to invest in IOS. A manifest example of this trust is that the largest customer of Pappersgruppen in Norway has adopted the whole IOS of Pappersgruppen, which means that much of its pricing and product range decisions are allowed to be made by Pappersgruppen.

## The strategic role of IT

Venkatraman (1991) stresses the importance of managers understanding their context before choosing an IT development path for achieving business process redesign. The manager should therefore understand whether the context favours a strategy of seeking efficiency or enhancing capabilities. In the former case, the business network and scope is considered as given, while these conditions are carefully analysed in the latter case (Venkatraman, 1994).

The histories of Luna and Pappersgruppen can be described as those of distributors with strong market positions which have proactively made important decisions about IT in the planning mode, ie highly ordered, neatly integrated decision processes by a purposeful organization (Mintzberg, 1978). Also, the entrepreneurial mode, ie when a leader takes bold decisions toward his/her vision of the organization's future, has prevailed for some important IT decisions (the ODI and PISTOL platforms/systems).

The four strategic information system cases that Mason et al (1991) studied were all initiated by a crisis. In the Luna case the IOS development was not crisis-driven, but instead regarded as a tool for gaining competitive advantage (cf Applegate et al, 1993). Luna's IT development can be characterised as being ongoing evolutionary over time, like AHSC/Baxter (Short & Venkatraman, 1992).

The large number of actors at all levels and the transaction-intensive and -extensive character of the information flow in the tools and machinery distribution system implies opportunities for increased efficiency with IOS implementations. Given the industrial organization with two levels of distributors, wholesalers and dealers, and given that Luna had the necessary financial resources for building a large-scale IS, Luna did choose to be pro-actively involved in the field of IS/IOS.

The early IOS development of Luna was to a large extent driven by the internally influential business logistics unit, which advocated a total systems integration approach. Over time, Luna has continued these efforts (cf Monczka & Carter, 1989). In the Pappersgruppen case the supplier level integrated forward. In the process of restructuring the distribution system an IOS was launched. Designed ‘free from history’ and with many new and creative features, the system can be labelled as a process innovation (Davenport, 1993) or as a revolutionary business transformation (Venkatraman, 1994). Pappersgruppen's IT exploitation initially was revolutionary, followed by an ongoing evolution. The revolution consisted of a redesign of activity-chains, thereby making them more efficient and effective (cf Gadde & Håkansson, 1993).

The integration of business processes with the customers was supported in both cases by electronic order-entry systems, which were different from the systems used by their competitors at that time. In both cases, the IOS were considered to be integrated parts of their strategies. Accordingly, the IOS have been developed and improved regularly over time.

## IOS strategy and design

Most current IOS are considered to be electronic data interchange (EDI) systems. However, in the two cases presented another IOS category is the dominant one, namely electronic data access (EDA) or online systems. In general, EDA systems are easier to implement since adaptations to the internal information systems on the user/target side are not required. If an EDA system is integrated with the internal purchasing and inventory control systems, benefits and costs are assumed to increase.

Both IOS were initially proprietary, transaction-oriented and highly centralised as in well-known American cases such as AHSC/Baxter (Cash et al, 1988; Short & Venkatraman, 1992) and American Airlines (Copeland & McKenney, 1988; Copeland, 1991; Hopper, 1990).

Initially, Luna and Pappersgruppen offered the same mode of IOS, namely online communication. To stay competitive, both source firms have been driven to implement EDI by the IT-sophisticated firms on the dealer and end-user side respectively. In addition, Luna introduced off-line file transfer early on through handheld computers to enable order-information exchange with its customers.

Pappersgruppen has consistently stuck to a dedicated, proprietary IOS linkage strategy, which has been an enabling tool for integrating suppliers and, particularly, affiliated distributor firms into electronic hierarchies (Malone et al, 1987) and value-adding partnerships (Johnston & Lawrence, 1988). Initially, Luna also followed a proprietary linkage strategy. Later on, however, producers and dealers contributed independently of each other to the establishment of an open, industry-wide IOS network. This hardware- and software-independent system, run by a third-party VAN operator, has gradually become multi-industrial; an electronic quasi-market place has been established (cf Malone et al, 1987). This EDA system is hierarchically structured and so it is not possible to receive price information across suppliers. Hence, since 1987, IOS-related competition has taken place between the value-added services provided by the connected IS. However, in recent years Luna has built up its own VAN competence, through the investment in the new ODI platform.

## Individuals & roles

Keen (1988; 1991) and Venkatraman (1991) have argued that IT has to become a strategic issue and thereby a question to be handled by top management, including the board of directors. McKenney (1995) found in his research, based on two longitudinal case studies of IS implementation (Bank of America and American Airline), the existence of a triad of parties occupying specific roles necessary for a successful IS development. These roles were found to be an insightful CEO, a dynamic maestro and an excellent technical team. Their importance varies within different evolutionary phases of an IS for driving the development as well as responding to crises. Zuboff (1988) found in her research that IT makes it possible to ‘informate’ the workers; this is an alternative to the view that new technologies represent a threat to the workers’ existence and hence alienate them.

In our cases, support can be found for all these researchers' findings. From the start in both cases, it was explicitly declared that IT was a central part of the business concept. In Pappersgruppen, a large amount of venture capital was initially allocated by the board of directors for the IT investments. The existence and importance of triads are striking in both cases. There was a visionary CEO and a chief technician with a good staff in both cases. The role of maestro was assumed by the business logistics manager and the chief supply manager respectively.

## Management processes and individuals & roles

Ciborra (1991; 1994) proposes that some of the most cited implementations of IOS are the results of bottom-up processes:

SIS (strategic information systems) applications are those that are developed close to and serve the grassroots of the organization, where its core competencies and skills are (1994, p 21).

Throughout the Luna case most of the initiative and driving force for the IS development came from the business logistics unit and in the Pappersgruppen case the initial initiative for the development came from top management. The role of the IS unit was in both cases subordinated to the wholesale business activities. The co-alignments of different functional units were of vital importance for the innovation process of creating the IOS. The skills and competencies from different units were simultaneously resources and constraints for the innovation processes (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990). The holistic view taken by Alderson (1957) in his proposition concerning an ‘organized behaviour system’ indicates a shift in perspective from the firm to the distribution channel. In each case management’s cognition of the wholesaler’s role in the distribution channel was that the wholesaler was only one part of the total distribution channel. They were thereby convinced of the value of installing IOS well beyond their own firm’s boundary.

## IT enabling a shifting of roles

The main purposes of an IS for a distributor are to increase availability, reduce costs, support administrative tasks and to keep control of stock. There is evidence in the cases for this as well as for emerging new uses of the IOS. Prior to the IOS adoption by Luna's customers, the sales representatives of Luna in practice assumed the responsibility to replenish their stock. As the customers resumed control over their stock, due to the IOS implementation, the Luna personnel stopped handling the customers' inventory management. Thus, the utilisation of Luna's IOS resulted in a relocation of the stock replenishment responsibility.

As noted above, there were also changes in the offers made by the distributors. Glazer (1991) and Davidow and Malone (1992) propose that information itself is becoming an asset in its own right. In the Luna case, the IOS gave the opportunity to increase 'its' product range three times (from 34000 to 100000 items) and in the Pappersgruppen case, the IOS gave the opportunity to internationalise to different foreign markets. In the Pappersgruppen case this information is used in different parts of the chain. The producer, for example, uses the information gathered is used in different parts of the chain. The producer, for example, uses the information generated in the IOS as input to operations planning, the distributor uses the information for statistical decision support to change its product range and customer base, while the end-user uses the information as an aid in their budget or accounting handling. In the Luna case there has been a development of an electronic market infrastructure and in the Pappersgruppen case the IOS has been complemented with additional information services. These services, for example credit control and banking, are provided by independent actors. Thus, the IOS enables the distributor to assume new roles as in the SABRE case (cf Hopper, 1990). This development is concurrent with the reduced capital accumulation (ie, the separation of goods and information flows or reduced levels of stock keeping) in all parts of the distribution channel.

The existence of well-functioning IOS have, in both cases, been necessary prerequisites for the internationalisation of the firms. The IOS was used by Pappersgruppen for central control of its affiliated companies, as in the case of Mrs Fields Cookies (Haeckel & Nolan, 1993).

## Conclusions

Is the IOS development process random? We do not think so. When seeking explanations for IOS evolution processes, we argue for considering concurrent factors. The descriptions of the successful IOS development cases, which we argue are noteworthy per se, have shown that different determinant factors can affect the evolution of IOS at different times.

The task of management can be described as balancing counteracting requirements, and transforming these into chains of activities, in a way that best promotes the firm as a whole. This, in turn, requires balancing of the available resources, within the firm and outside the firm. Some of the traditional dichotomies or choices that managers face are central control vs local autonomy, economies of scale vs economies of scope, top-down vs bottom-up approach, functional orientation vs process orientation, low inventory levels vs high (delivery) service levels, and outsourcing vs insourcing of IS resources.

These dichotomies can be resolved partly by sharing information, both within the firm and between firms in the distribution systems. IS and IOS are tools facilitating such exchange of information. O'Callaghan et al (1992), discussing EDI specifically, stress that EDI has the potential to provide both cost savings and service enhancements to the source firm's channel partners. Although history is important, (earlier choices affect current ones), new possibilities enabled by new IT, emerge. Often changes require ex ante investments in IT, on the part of individuals and the organization as a whole, in order to attain as many of the potential benefits as possible. IS developments are often constrained by the organizational structure for which they are designed. Thus, preceding improvements of the organizational structure may leverage the impacts of an implemented IS. Continuous, individual learning and process oriented teams are examples of other significant antecedents, which are illustrated in the case descriptions.

How are changes best conducted? Through ‘clean sheet approaches’, ie what Davenport (1993) labels as process innovations, or through continuous process improvements? How should the systems be designed? By investing in proprietary software or package software? These, and many other questions, cannot easily be answered. It depends, on the external technological and socio-economic environment, on the specific position in the network of the firm, on the capabilities of the firm, on previous choices and present resources, and on many other factors, which may contribute to the understanding of IOS evolution.

As illustrated in the cases, benefits do not originate from the sole use of the IOS per se, but arise from interaction with human, organizational, industrial organization, networking and competitive aspects. Two seemingly similar cases of IOS developments may turn out to have rather different characteristics, when examined more closely. We therefore propose holistic approaches in order to understand similarities and dissimilarities across firms which are implementing IOS. We conclude that the understanding of the evolution of IOS can be enhanced by applying dynamic approaches and relationship (or industrial network) perspectives.

## Proposed research agenda

The normal research approach when investigating an IOS development is either to focus on the specific firm, often the source firm, or on the industry in which an IOS is developed. To take the research one step further, it would be necessary to set the focus either on the exchange relationship between the firms in which the IOS is implemented (ie, both the source and the target firm) or on the whole system of firms within the IOS network (ie, connected chains of source and target firms).

In addition research models are often static in nature as one specific factor is studied and tested regarding its correlation to another factor, ceteris paribus. Dynamics, on the other hand, is concerned with motion (cf Hicks, 1985) that is a process that takes place over time where a change cannot easily be predicted from the initial conditions (cf Klein, 1977). A dynamic approach thus, reflects feedback mechanisms from one factor to another factor where the effects depend on the total set of factors (see for example Forrester, 1980).

A shift in the focus of research from a static to a dynamic approach as well as a shift from an actor to a relationship (or industrial network) perspective is therefore proposed in order to enhance the understanding of the evolution of IOS (see Figure 5).

From an actor to a relation or network perspective
We ask ourselves what new insights into the development of IOS a shift from an actor perspective to a relationship or network perspective would give. It can, for example, be hypothesised that a source firm implements an IOS link firstly with a target firm where there exists a strong business relationship. However, the judgement of strong exchange relationships is not only based on economic aspects but also on (more subjective) personal factors. This is easily understood if an IOS link is perceived as an integrated part of an exchange relationship between firms. Empirical evidence suggests that IOS as a rule are implemented in existing relationships and not the other way around (see for example Gadde & Håkansson, 1993). A possible explanation for this is that it is necessary to have a stable exchange relationship in which the source firm is willing to induce the change, ie, the IOS link.

When three or more exchange relationships are connected, they together form a system of relationships, an industrial network (cf Johanson & Mattsson, 1993). A specific exchange relationship has arguably some influence over other relationships, which implies that a network is more than the sum of several dyadic relationships; there exist positive and negative synergy effects. Such industrial networks may, for example, explain why at a point in time there is resistance to further IOS development since there is a specific industrial logic in the network. Furthermore, the adaptation of an IOS link in one relationship may depend on, or be dependent on, another exchange relationship, thereby, inducing ‘second order effects’ (cf Cunningham & Tynan, 1993) from the implementation. Another related problem is when one IOS network needs to be connected to another, which requires a dominant design, eg an EDIFACT standard.

As Cunningham & Tynan (1993) point out, the individual exchange relationship, with its potential of cooperation for mutual benefits (Bowersox, 1990), is important for explaining effects of IOS. Thus, we propose that the individual relationship constitutes the focal unit of analysis, complemented with indirect effects between the focal relationship and other relationships. Such studies are performed within the ‘market-as-networks tradition’ (cf Johanson & Mattsson, 1993). Within this tradition, the emergence of industrial networks has been studied by Lundgren (1994) and technological development in industrial networks by Håkansson (1987, 1989). There is, thus, a useful platform in place, for conducting studies of the emergence and evolution of IOS in industrial networks. Once again, we call for an integration of ideas between the IOS literature and the IOR literature.

## From a static to a dynamic perspective

We ask ourselves what new insights into the development processes of IOS a shift from a static to a dynamic perspective would give. It is, for example, believed that an IOS link will change the content of an exchange relationship; eg bonding the buyer tighter to the seller. If this is the case it would be logical for the actors involved to adjust their respective activities and commitments of resources to each other. These mutual adaptations (cf Hallén et al, 1991) would probably give rise to further adjustments, which in turn will trigger increased or decreased bonding, ie more of a hierarchical or a market setting.

Thus, the evolution is path dependent in the sense that a choice in an earlier period will affect the opportunities to make choices in a later period. These choices are made both by the source and the target firm but also by actors in the environment, which means that the source firm may be in control of its own development but is also affected to a large extent by other actors as well.

## About the authors

Odd Fredriksson received a licentiate degree in business administration in 1992 from the Stockholm School of Economics. His licentiate thesis focused on the relationships between driving and impeding forces and perceived effects as a response to the implementation of a many-to-many inter-organizational information system into an industrial distribution system. Fredriksson is with the Foundation for Distribution Research at the Stockholm School of Economics, which deals with research and education concerning marketing and distribution issues.
