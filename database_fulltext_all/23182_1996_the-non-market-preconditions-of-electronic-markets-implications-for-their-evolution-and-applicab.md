# The non-market preconditions of electronic markets: implications for their evolution and applicability

K Reimers

University of Bremen, Germany

This article asks which institutional structure is required for electronic markets to emerge. It proposes the adoption of a rather narrow concept of electronic markets, one which requires that electronic markets support the negotiation phase of a business process in order to identify those preconditions that are specific to electronic markets as compared to other inter-organizational systems. A theoretical framework is elaborated which distinguishes between the institutional regime shaping market participants' transaction behaviour, a set of meta-activities which establish and maintain the institutional regime, and a generative regime, which is the group of actors performing or promoting meta-activities. All three components constitute the notion of non-market preconditions of market systems, whether electronic or conventional. This theoretical concept is then used to identify the specific non-market preconditions of electronic markets in each of the three distinct categories. In the concluding section, the findings are related to the way in which electronic markets actually evolve and the areas of economic activity in which they provide a useful coordination instrument and in which they do not.

## 1. Introduction

The promise offered by the concept of electronic markets is fascinating. First, the limiting factors of time and space seem to have been overcome. Electronic markets are ubiquitous and available 24 hours a day (Schmid, 1993; Weber, 1993). Second, electronic markets seem to be more 'just' and 'democratic' as well as more competitive and decentralised (Himberger et al, 1991; Malone et al, 1987). Third, it seems that some types of market dysfunctions (such as negative externalities) can be avoided through programming (Miller & Drexler, 1988), although, of course, the 'classical' negative externalities, such as pollution, will persist as long as there is a physical counterpart to the property rights traded on electronic markets. Following these arguments, electronic markets could be considered manifestations of the neoclassical market ideal, reducing transaction costs to a negligible minimum (Bakos, 1991a; Schmid, 1993).

Thus, it would seem desirable to have as many electronic markets as possible in order to improve economic efficiency and social justice. However, they first have to be constructed (Schmid, 1993, p 468). Therefore, we need some basic knowledge about the conditions under which they are likely to be constructed. Only then is it possible to determine whether or not electronic markets are a useful instrument for coordinating economic activity, ie, given these conditions, for which kinds of economic activity it is actually desirable to have them established, and for which kinds they should rather not be used. Thus, we have to analyse: (1) the institutional rules which must be provided to make electronic markets work; and (2) the process which establishes these rules. The analysis of these two issues constitutes the topic of this paper.

## 2. The definition of electronic markets revisited

Up to now, discussions of electronic markets have always required a definition since there is no widespread common understanding of the concept. A state of the art discussion can be found in Schmid (1993). He suggests defining the concept of electronic markets according to the different transaction phases which have been elaborated in transaction cost theory. Electronic markets, in a narrow sense, are then assumed if all possible transaction phases are supported electronically. Yet, Schmid prefers using the term electronic markets in a wider sense, that is if at least one phase is supported electronically (Schmid, 1993, p 468).

The question arises as to whether this definition of electronic markets suffices to exclude a sufficiently large enough number of phenomena that it remains a meaningful theoretical concept, unless we wish to call any inter-organizational system an electronic market (for the concept of inter-organizational systems see Swatman & Swatman, 1992). Therefore, the electronic transmission of invoice data, for example, does not constitute an electronic market, although it is definitely part of a certain transaction. Thus, we have to identify a 'kernel' of activities which constitute the notion of an electronic market.

Using the transaction phase scheme of Schmid, we can distinguish between inter-organizational systems that support or automate the initiation, negotiation or processing phases (Schmid, 1993). Electronic systems which support the initiation phase would focus on two tasks: the identification of possible transaction partners and the search for product alternatives. Examples of such systems are electronic browsers which contain a more or less standardised description of potential transaction partners and products. After having identified attractive offers, one has to contact those who made them for further bilateral negotiations. $^{1}$

At the other end of the spectrum, we find systems which support the processing of a transaction which has already been agreed upon, ie, the transaction partner and transaction object (including the terms of settlement) are already fixed. Examples are the electronic transmission of an invoice or an order, whereby the latter presupposes that the terms of business have been detailed elsewhere (eg in a general contract). This type of inter-organizational system is generally termed electronic data interchange [EDI] (see Swatman & Swatman, 1992).

Finally, there are electronic systems which support the negotiation phase. Since the result of any (successful) business negotiation process is a transaction which specifies a certain transaction object (product or service) at a certain price, an electronic system automating the negotiation phase is essentially a price-determination or price-generation mechanism. There are basically two ways of accomplishing this task. (1) A number of offers and bids which are linked to a fixed price or a price range are collected and then automatically matched according to predefined rules, eg the maximisation of total transaction volume. $^{2}$ (2) In a continuous offering and bidding process, the ‘best’ available price at any moment, ie the lowest for buyers and the highest for sellers, is determined and this price is then used as the price at which transactions are executed. $^{3}$ Trading partners no longer negotiate in the true sense of the word. Instead, the negotiation phase is replaced by the statement of whether the conditions under which contracts will be concluded are given or not. If so, a contract will be concluded. Examples are automated stock or futures exchanges and commodity markets, such as NASDAQ for stock trading, Globex for futures and TELCOT for commodities. $^{4}$

According to Schmid's broad definition of electronic markets, all of the above described forms of inter-organizational systems would qualify as electronic markets. However, one essential criterion, as formulated by Schmid himself, is still lacking. Schmid requires that electronic markets make use of the market mechanism (Schmid, 1993, p 469). $^{5}$ This means that only those systems which support the negotiation phase electronically could be called electronic markets. Systems which support the initiation phase electronically improve the information distribution of those using it, thus contributing to the conditions under which the market mechanism guarantees efficient allocation of resources (complete and immediate distribution of information). However, the information distributed via electronic systems only suffices to decide with whom it is worthwhile to start bilateral negotiation. If this were not the case, bargaining would become superfluous; the system would take over this function, thus qualifying as a system automating the negotiation process. On the other hand, inter-organizational systems supporting transaction processing do not presuppose that the bargain in question has been struck on the market place as opposed to a hierarchical coordination mechanism. Electronic transaction processing systems, ie EDI systems, could be used in a an intra-firm context for the recovery of costs as well. In fact, they first appeared between auto manufacturers and their suppliers, which are characterised by rather hierarchical relationships (see Holland & Lockett, 1993; Horlück, 1994; Reekers, 1994). $^{6}$

The distinction between electronic systems which support each transaction phase in an isolated manner is, of course, an analytical one. Real systems typically support two or more transaction phases, $^{7}$ eg Reuters' Dealing 2000-System for trade in foreign exchange provides participants with market data, automatically matches buy and sell orders, and supports subsequent transaction processing by electronic funds transfer (see Weber, 1993, p 8). Furthermore, electronic markets may evolve from EDI systems or initiation phase-supporting electronic bulletin boards (Schmid & Zbornik, 1991). In fact, most automated trade execution systems evolved from transaction processing systems on financial exchanges $^{8}$ However, the purpose of this paper is to single out those institutional requirements which are specific to the establishment and operation of electronic markets. I contend that these elements of an institutional framework are found in the environment of electronic systems which qualify as electronic markets defined as price-generating systems, irrespective of their historical evolution path.

## 3. A simple framework

In order to analyse the non-market preconditions of electronic markets, the notion of ‘non-market preconditions’ will be disaggregated into three categories which can be dealt with separately. Each of these categories describes a subset of preconditions which must exist if an electronic market is to function.

Since the framework is designed to explain the emergence of inter-organizational institutions, eg rules governing electronic markets, it sets out with the basic inter-organizational process, the business transaction. $^{9}$ We can think of a business transaction as consisting of several distinct activities which are organized in a specific way, whereby the characteristics of a business transaction are partly due to the individual organization and thus account for a large part of organizational behaviour. In fact, Nelson and Winter develop the notion of routines, which can be interpreted as just such typical, organization-specific combinations of distinct activities, to explain the behaviour of organizations (Nelson & Winter, 1982, p 124). However, these internal rules do not completely determine the way transaction activities are organized. There is a set of other external rules which must be observed as well. The sources of these other rules are manifold. There is, in the first place, the legal framework within which business partners interact. There are other, formal and informal rules, for example generally acknowledged business principles, general terms of trade, etc. I call the sum of all these rules institutions, as distinct from Nelson and Winter's routines, since they are not specific to any particular organization. Thus, the practice of issuing one invoice per order instead of one invoice per delivery becomes an institution if it is part of a regulatory framework which applies to a number of organizations. It is, however, not an institution if this choice is left up to each individual organization, which may have elaborated definite procedures, ie routines. It is thus possible to identify a set of institutions which partly determine the way distinct activities are organized as part of a business process. I call this set of institutions the institutional regime under which business processes take place.

Since the institutional regime ‘explains’ the way in which activities within and between trading organizations are organized, for example in the form of an electronic market, one could call for those institutions which provide the necessary conditions for the emergence of electronic markets. However, as already mentioned, there are institutions on many levels and only a few of them have clearly defined actors responsible. In states ruled by law, the legislative body and the jurisdiction provide for a large part of the institutional regime by means of the legal framework. But there are many other institutions which must be created by the economic system itself. I label the process which brings forth these institutions meta-activities and thus distinguish between this process and the actors driving it. We can identify, for example, the activity ‘standardisation of data formats’, which is required in many instances of emerging electronic markets, without assigning it to a specific organization which may carry it out, since there are many potential organizations for that purpose (for example national and international standardisation organizations, industry associations, chambers of commerce, informal working groups of companies etc). Yet it should be noted that there are many meta-activities which must operate continuously in order to maintain certain institutions, as in the standardisation of products which often requires continuous testing and certifying if these standards are to have any normative impact. Thus, it would seem as if the organization which carries out the meta-activity becomes the institution itself.

Finally, we need a theoretical category for describing the actors performing or promoting meta-activities, ie invest resources in their implementation or furnish them with legitimacy. I call a group of these actors a generative regime. A generative regime could be one single business organization or the whole legislative body. It does not necessarily create institutions directly but also indirectly via decisions about meta-activities. Thus, the International Standards Organization (ISO), for example, would be called a generative regime although it does not produce standards itself but establishes or designates working groups with the sole purpose of developing the desired standards. The proceedings of these working groups would then be called meta-activities.

Of course, we could imagine one powerful actor who designs an electronic market, creating the institutions and the electronic system simultaneously. In this case, the three categories – institutional regime, meta-activities and generative regime – would fall into one. Nevertheless, I think it useful to distinguish among these categories, since on the one hand, there are several empirical possibilities of their combination as hinted at above, and on the other hand, it is thus possible to locate much more precisely where hindrances to developing electronic markets might exist. Indeed, we can be quite sure of the necessary institutional regime for a specific electronic market but there might simply be no actor powerful enough to perform and support the corresponding meta-activities which would produce this type of institutional regime. Similarly, we can identify powerful generative regimes which, however, fail to define the correct set of meta-activities.

Figure 1 describes how the three categories are related. This graphic representation indicates that these three categories form a closed model of how institutions emerge. Of course, this model does not reveal any information about what the institutions, in the case of a specific problem, should look like. It only states a general functional mechanism. I want to illustrate this model through a fictional case of how an institutional regime could have been modified to accommodate the new requirements of EDI. $^{10}$ The story sets out with the management of an apparel manufacturer analysing its internal organization of activities. The analysis reveals that there would be a huge rationalisation potential if orders were keyed in automatically. Order processing could be much faster, decreasing stocks and improving market performance. The manufacturer asks his retailer to transmit orders electronically and in a specific data format. Since the retailer does not want to lose this manufacturer (who enjoys the success of a powerful brand name), he complies with the manufacturer's demand. In a similar fashion, other manufacturers approach our retailer until he eventually refuses to meet individual demands any longer. Instead, the retailer asks the manufacturers to agree upon one single data format for orders. If the retailer is not powerful enough to do so, he might seek a coalition among several retailers who share the same problem. The manufacturers set up a committee which designates a working group for the purpose of drafting a proposal for a standard order data format. After this has been accomplished, they publish the standard order data format and agree to use it when negotiating with retailers.

In this case, the desire to automate some activities within several organizations (set of activities in Figure 1) started a process which resulted in a modification of the institutional regime (by adding a new standard to it). The independent demands of several manufacturers did have external effects among themselves in that they motivated the retailer to change his negotiation strategy. Thus, the manufacturers had enough incentives to constitute a generative regime which promoted the meta-activities necessary for the amendment of the institutional regime. The automation effort could be considered a cause of the new institution working through the mechanism of generative regimes and meta-activities. There are other classes of possible causes, such as privacy and security issues connected to automation and rationalisation. Besides standardisation, certification and coding might prove essential for providing the necessary institutional regime. There are many other possible inputs and outputs of this process.

## 4. Applying the framework to electronic markets

Using the above described framework for the derivation of normative rather than positive hypotheses, we have to read it ‘backwards’, ie proceed from the desired institutional regime necessary for the functioning of electronic markets to the meta-activities which establish and enforce these meta-activities. I will deal with each of these issues in turn.

## 4.1. The institutional regime

The discussion of the necessary institutional regime of an electronic market, defined as a price-generating mechanism (see section 2), can be guided by two questions. (1) How does a potential participant enter an electronic market, ie which constraints on the behaviour of participants on electronic markets are required? (2) How does a product enter an electronic market, ie which properties of a product are required if it is to be traded on an electronic market?

(1) There are two main problems in markets with a price-generating mechanism, depending on which of the two basic principles is being used (see section 2). A price-generating mechanism, which uses limit orders, that is buy or sell orders with an attached fixed price or price range, are confronted with the problem of extra-market dealing. On the one hand, there must be someone giving a firm bid or offer; on the other hand, this bid or offer must not be available to others who have not bound themselves by firm bids or offers. Firm bids or offers are necessary to determine the market-clearing price. If a market clearing price is in the interest of all participants, it can be regarded as a public good, its contributions being firm bids. However, everyone can profit from this without having contributed his/her own share by using the price as an indicator for his/her own offer and, thus, be able to simply undercut firm bids or offers (see Grossman, 1989, p 15).

The second basic price-generating principle is that of a continuous quotation process with some rules as to which quotes are regarded as the market price at which a market order will be executed, typically the 'best' available price (ie lowest for buyers and highest for sellers) at any moment. The main problem of price-generating mechanisms built on this principle is that of keeping the quotation process going on, otherwise there will be no prices which can be used for price determination. This requires that there are participants who will counter offers or bids if no one else does. However, if no one counters offers or bids, it is because prices are either too low (for offers) or too high (for bids) to derive any profit from the transaction. Therefore, fulfilling this requirement generally means taking losses. Again, the main problem is that of providing a collective good, as a continuous quotation process is in the interest of every participant but may inflict individual losses.

Thus, the price-generating mechanism represents a classical collective good. Applying Olson's analysis, we can conclude that price-generating mechanisms will be provided only if either coercion or selective incentives are used (Olson, 1965). Clearly, restricting trade to members only represents a coercive solution. Theoretically, the second possibility would thus be to use selective incentives. Selective incentives must at least compensate for the gains or losses which could be made or avoided through free-riding (ie off-exchange trading when using limit orders or no-trading when using market orders). Considering the possible quantitative dimensions of these profits or losses, it seems rather impossible to devise an incentive mechanism which would accomplish this. I therefore conclude that restricting membership is the only possible means of creating a price-generating mechanism. $^{11}$

(2) The only input a price-generating mechanism then needs is a number of offers and bids. An offer or bid consists of a product or service and the price (or price range) at which one is willing to buy or to sell. $^{12}$ Obviously, homogenous products or services are the simplest to trade on electronic markets, eg equities. However, this is not a necessary condition as long as products can be specified through a number of attributes which, in turn, are standardisable. $^{13}$ Therefore, memory capacity, processing speed and other technical details of computers could be priced separately. $^{14}$ Another important issue is quality. Participants in electronic markets could, for example, rely on brand names, since they serve as an indicator of quality (Barzel, 1982). Alternatively, guarantees could be required.

In all these cases, participants depend on an external institutional environment, for example standardisation and certifying organizations or branded goods or guarantees. Even then, there is a role for a centralised administration which defines further aspects of contracts to be established on electronic markets – for example terms of settlement – since a price generating mechanism would be of little use if the parties, after having agreed upon an exchange, started haggling over details of transferring property rights. This role could be extended. Instead of relying on a specific and contingent external institutional environment, a central administration could internalise some functions. The stock exchanges' listing of equities is an example of putting a quality stamp on a product. Financial derivatives are an example of the generation of totally new products by stock exchanges which essentially consist of a standardised contract.

In the case of auctions, the central administration becomes a trade party by means of monopolising the supply side. Since competition is restricted to buyers, this form of electronic market is, in efficiency terms, inferior to those which also allow for competition among sellers. However, the idiosyncratic nature of products generally traded on auctions rules out this option. $^{15}$

## 4.2 The set of meta-activities

All these institutions have to be provided in one way or another. In some cases, this is a matter of a group of people coming together to draft a document and publish it. The published document thus represents the institution. However, in most cases, this is not enough. Even the incorporation of the most simple standards, for example, has to be checked. If no one else does, the consumer or buyer has to do it. If he/she does so before concluding the purchase contract, the seller has an incentive to see that the standard is properly incorporated. If there are many buyers checking proper standard incorporation, the incorporating of this standard becomes a public good. Any buyer can rely on the other buyers' checking efforts, thus avoiding having to do so himself/herself. Therefore, we might expect the emergence of some certifying organizations (for the moment I will not deal with the sort of incentives which would make them emerge) that control proper standard incorporation, providing incentives for sellers to properly incorporate standards. We can thus state that there must be some sort of certification necessary to make a standard effective. In other words, the publishing of a certain standard could hardly be called an institution, if it requires certification for being effective but no certification is actually done. $^{16}$ The activity ‘certification’ is thus necessary to constitute the institution of a certain standard.

Similar cases could be made for other institutions, a stamp of quality, for example, which immediately implies a quality control checking, and the establishment of membership rules which must be enforced as well. In considering electronic markets, there is a whole class of activities related to standardisation of data formats, devising codes for qualifiers and others, which are specific to electronic communication. I do not want to deal with this class of meta-activities here, since they are dealt with extensively in the context of EDI (see Kubicek, 1993). Instead, I want to sort out those meta-activities which are specific to electronic markets.

Most importantly, membership rules must be enforced. Membership rules include, as described above, the ban on off-exchange trading and the provision of firm bids, ie members must be willing and able to execute those contracts which they offer. This might imply that members have to deposit a certain amount of money and further security mechanisms. Alternatively, if a continuous quotation process is used for the generation of prices, at least some participants $^{17}$ must be forced to keep the quotation process going on if bids or offers are not met by counter offers since this means making losses.

In the second place, a meta-activity must cover the operation of a price-generating system since price-generating is automated on electronic markets. This usually requires the installation of rather expensive equipment for order routing, quotation display, high speed telecommunication lines, etc (see Keith & Grody, 1988 for details). Third, classification and quality checking of traded products might be necessary, for example in the case of auctions or the listing of equities. Fourth, standardisation of contracts, which implies a continuous adaptation to new business procedures and new products, is essential in any electronic system with a price-generating mechanism. Fifth, although not essential, mechanisms for transaction processing might be provided (such as financial clearing and transfer of property rights including risk sharing mechanisms). $^{18}$

## 4.3 The generative regime

For discussing the question who will be the one performing or supporting these meta-activities, it is helpful to think of the meta-activities described above as some form of centralised negotiating. Every single meta-activity which is necessary for establishing electronic markets via the respective institutional regime has a counterpart in bilateral forms of negotiating. The establishment and enforcement of membership rules correspond to bilateral trust relationships, while the operating of a price-generating mechanism corresponds to negotiating bilateral contracts. Thus, if we consider the negotiation process as being built up from many basic bits of distinct activities, we can identify a number of these bits of activities which are shifted away from the partners of a bilateral business to a central agency. This process of centralising negotiation activities can be a gradual one. A sequence of different exemplary forms of negotiation patterns is represented in Figure 2.

As negotiation patterns change from bilateral and unilateral to multilateral, new organizations representing the generative regime might emerge through the centralisation of negotiation activities. By definition, in a bilateral monopoly, the details of a contract must be specified in bilateral negotiation processes. If one party is powerful enough, it may be able to unilaterally dictate its terms of business to several business partners simultaneously, for example in the automotive industry (hub and spokes case). In a next step, several business partners might coalesce in order to gain enough negotiation power to dictate terms of business. A trade association, for example, may formulate general terms and conditions which automatically become part of every contract any one of its members concludes, or a purchasing agency may conclude general contracts with manufacturers on behalf of its members to achieve better prices. In response, the other party to the trade might form a coalition as well. One example of this negotiation pattern is the negotiation of the European Article Number between the trade associations of consumer goods manufacturers and the organization of food chains and department stores (see Kubicek & Seeger, 1992). Finally, the negotiation of terms of business can be centralised in such a way that direct negotiation between business partners is largely eliminated, as in the case of stock markets and other organized markets such as futures exchanges (and, as we have seen, electronic markets).

By definition, any organization carrying out meta-activities is called a generative regime. What I mean by this term is simply that there must be an organization generating the institutional regime. By default, the generative regime is the state. However, in developed economies, there is a large variety of organizations which contribute to the institutional regime, ranging from customer organizations to standardisation organizations and from trade associations to chambers of commerce. If we consider our examples of negotiation patterns, in cases I and II, no additional organization emerges. Most activities specifying the institutional regime of the business (the governance structures as Williamson (1987) would term it) are carried out by the transaction partners themselves. However, in cases III-V, new organizations emerge, doing part of the job of negotiating the terms of business. The question is: why? Put in economic terms, what are the incentives for these organizations to come into existence? I do not want to delve into the political economics of trade associations and the like. However, it seems crucial to the understanding of the emergence of electronic markets to know what the incentives for a ‘central negotiator’ could be. Mulherin et al (1991) argue that stock exchanges can be viewed as firms whose products are prices (and which earn revenues through fees). Thus, in order for them to exist, it is necessary to establish ‘property rights to price quotes’ (ibid p 592). They demonstrate, in great detail, how the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and the Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT) evolved on parallel lines to the establishment of property rights to price quotes. One big issue in the history of these exchanges is the impact of the telegraph on their development.

Telegraph technology made it possible to use the price quotes of the NYSE on the CBOT for trading purposes without implementing an extra price-generating mechanism (and carrying the costs of it). Accordingly, they comment on the Congressional plan to establish a ‘National Market System’ through ‘communication and data processing facilities’ (ibid p 627): ‘... the proponents of the National Market System beg the question of who will pay for the technology to link exchanges’ (ibid p 633).

I do not want to forecast the development of the National Market System either. However, this discussion demonstrates that electronic markets cannot be assumed to be provided freely. Instead, there must be actors regarding it worth their while to engage in the establishment of an electronic market. Up to now, most stock exchanges, for example, have been reluctant to integrate (Mulherin et al, 1991, p 636). This might cause some authors to think of them as monopolies which try to defend their privileges. However, if one regards stock exchanges as firms, it is clear that they compete. $^{19}$ Similarly, electronic markets would emerge in several places simultaneously and initially would be unwilling to merge. Only as competitive pressure increases would they integrate horizontally, probably forming a national market. Thus, I agree with Schmid (1993) that electronic markets are (or better, can be) open systems technologically (ibid p 470). However, there are serious non-technical reasons why computerised exchanges are unwilling to integrate horizontally, forming a ‘global, open stock exchange’ (ibid).

## 5. Conclusion

Starting with a rather narrow definition of electronic markets which requires that the negotiation phase be electronically supported thus rendering electronic markets price-generating systems, I have developed a simple framework which decomposes the otherwise somewhat obscure concept of institutions into three elements which sequentially constitute one another: the generative regime, the set of meta-activities, and the institutional regime. The non-market preconditions of electronic markets, ie their institutional requirements, could then be spelled out for each of these components separately. It turns out that, counter intuitively, the institutional requirements of products to be traded on electronic markets are not as restrictive as those of market participants. This implies that electronic markets are closed systems to a certain degree. A further aspect of non-market preconditions of electronic markets adds to this characteristic of electronic markets: required meta-activities are expensive; a generative regime thus needs strong incentives to establish and operate an electronic market. These incentives could be provided by the markets for electronic trades, which, however, will lead to competition among electronic markets, thus precluding the establishment of one unique global electronic market.

Although these conclusions only apply to electronic markets defined narrowly as automated price-generating systems, I think that they are also indicative of systems which only support the initiation phase. These systems have to provide some sort of product classification in order to support the search phase and enforce some restrictions on participants, for example precluding commercial traders from private advertisement browsers. The more restrictive these rules are, the higher the value added in terms of saving transaction costs for market participants becomes (Bakos, 1991a) and the less scope remains for bilateral negotiation.

This also seems to provide the severest point for a critical view of electronic markets. The less scope there is for negotiation, bargaining and haggling, the less scope there is for discovering new product opportunities and new utilisation opportunities, too. This seems to be important, since the function of markets is not only to guarantee the efficient allocation of available resources but also the creation of new knowledge (von Hayek, 1945) and thus new resources. Therefore I think that this new coordination mechanism should only be used if this aspect is of minor relevance.

Bakos (1991b) distinguishes between two basic possibilities of supporting business transactions through electronic means, namely electronic links and electronic marketplaces, depending on whether they are based on ‘bilateral integration’ or ‘multilateral information sharing’ (Bakos, 1991b, p 33). However, it is implicit in his use of these two concepts that electronic marketplaces provide benefits in the early phases whereas ‘any benefits from information technology that are generated after a bilateral relationship has been established . . . are in the realm of inter-organizational information links’ (ibid, p 34). Thus, Bakos’ concept of electronic marketplaces resembles the definition of electronic markets as used in this paper.
