# The configuration of inter-organizational relations

S Klein

Institute for Information Management, University of St Gallen, Dufourstr 50, CH-9000 St Gallen, Switzerland

Based on recent literature on the impact of inter-organizational information systems (IOS) on industrial organization, this paper proposes a framework for research into the interdependencies between IOS and coordination strategy. The elements of the framework are: (1) market structure; (2) governance forms; (3) relationship dimensions and (4) enterprise resources. It is suggested that these four elements at the same time affect and are affected by coordination strategy which focuses on inter-organizational relations. The research proposition is that inter-organizational arrangements have to be interpreted as complex, multilayer configurations of organizational parameters. The composition and flexible adaptation of these configurations – rather than the selection of one efficient governance form – and the related design of IOS are the major tasks of coordination strategy. The design parameters are related to the elements of the framework: (1) the position of the enterprise in inter-organizational relations, be it a network, groups of networks or markets; (2) combinations of governance forms with different partners; (3) combinations of governance forms within a given network of relations referring to the different layers in inter-organizational relations, namely the institutional, operational and technical layers and (4) combinations of (inter-) organizational arrangements for the use and development of resources. EUROSELECT, a horizontal network of European grocery wholesalers, is used as an example to illustrate how different contingencies affect the inter-organizational design and why the complex configuration of relations is chosen.

## The impact of inter-organizational information systems (IOS) on governance structures

While there is a long tradition of research on the organizational impact of intra-organizational information systems (IS), the development and proliferation of IOS has brought forward a separate stream of literature as IOS affect dimensions like the boundaries of firms and the inter-organizational division of labour as well as decisions about governance structures and industrial organization.

## A literature review

The dominant research question in this literature concerns the impact of IOS on governance decisions: do IOS foster a trend towards (electronic) markets, (electronic) hierarchies or (electronic) networks as hybrid or intermediary forms of governance? Institutional economics, especially transaction cost economics, provides the theoretical foundation for most of the authors.

## The proposition: from electronic hierarchies to electronic markets!

... the result of reducing coordination costs without changing anything else should be an increase in the proportion of economic activity coordinated by markets. (Malone et al, 1988, p 591)

In an influential article (written under the auspices of the MIT's Management in the 1990s Research Programme) Malone et al (1988) hypothesise the influence of emerging information and communication technologies on the execution and coordination of economic transactions. Their claim is that IT will increasingly be used to coordinate economic activities and will consequently increase the share of market coordination over hierarchical coordination. Their argument is based on the analytic framework of transaction cost theory which compares relative production and coordination costs of markets and hierarchies. As information technology (IT) reduces coordination (and information) costs, market transactions become more attractive. In greater detail, they focus on asset specificity and complexity of product description as transaction features, which determine the choice of coordination mechanisms. If both are high, hierarchical coordination is most efficient; if both are low, market coordination is preferable.

Malone et al argue for two trajectories towards electronic markets, one is based on biased IS which have become economically feasible through the emergence of IT, the second is based on an electronic hierarchy where standardisation opens the system up to competitive pressures (Benjamin et al, 1990). There are different starting points as far as the functional scope and organizational setting of the systems are concerned, however competitive pressure and to some degree governmental regulations sustain the shift towards electronic markets in both cases.

Ebers (1992, p 2) follows up on the transaction cost line of argument and tries to fill some voids of the theory, namely the choice of asset specificity and the interdependency among transactions. His hypothesis is that the extended scale (the frequency of transactions and the number of participants) and scope (diversity of transactions) of electronic transactions, reduce the optimal asset specificity of investments and hence explain the development path from electronic hierarchies towards electronic markets via these two trajectories.

## The rebuttal: move to the middle!

This hypothesis states that the lower cost and better monitoring capability of IT and the lower relationship specificity of IT investments will cause firms to engage in a greater degree of outsourcing; moreover, this increased outsourcing will be from a reduced set of suppliers with whom the firm has long-term cooperative relationships. (Clemons & Reddi, 1993, p 809)

The ‘shift to the market’ hypothesis has recently been challenged by Clemons and Reddi (1993, p 810), who – again based on transaction cost theorising and a limited amount of empirical evidence – propose the ‘move to the middle’ hypothesis. The move to the middle is a double move: away from the hierarchical vertically integrated organization to a higher degree of outsourcing, and at the same time away from faceless market relations towards a situation where the firm relies on a few cooperative partners. The first part of the move towards outsourcing is based on a reasoning similar to Malone et al (1988): IT lowers transaction costs, the costs of coordination and monitoring and the relationship specificity of IT investments, and hence makes outsourcing an advantageous option. The role of IT in this process is to at least partly offset exogenous factors that usually increase transaction costs, such as relationship-specific investments, small numbers bargaining, and demand uncertainty.

The second part of the argument, however, gives reasons why companies increasingly choose long-term cooperative relationships instead of market relationships: long-term relationships provide higher incentives to invest in IT and in the requisite organizational adaptations and learning processes (Seidmann & Wang, 1992). At the same time long-term relationships provide some protection against the risks of opportunistic behaviour and especially the loss of critical resources (Clemons & Row 1992, pp 646–648).

In order to explain which conditions favour a ‘move to the middle' vs a 'move to the market', Clemons and Reddi (1994) propose a model of three variables that affect the price and the transaction risk. These variables are product complexity and the variability of product prices over time and among suppliers; the reducing relationship-specificity; and the increasing cost-effectiveness of IT. If both the price and the transaction risks are low, a partnership solution is most likely; if the transaction risk is low but the price risk is high, market transactions are more efficient.

Bakos and Brynjolfsson (1993) focus on the phenomenon of increasing outsourcing and challenge the hypothesis of an increasing number of suppliers which is based on the assumption that IT reduces search and coordination costs. Empirical findings show, on the contrary, that firms in many industries are reducing the number of their suppliers. Their explanation is that although coordination costs are decreasing, this effect is often offset by an increasing demand for quality. As quality and non-contractible actions of suppliers become more important, the buyer has to provide incentives for the supplier to invest in underlying relationship specific assets. A limited number of suppliers is such an incentive as it increases the bargaining power of the supplier and reduces the risk of buyer defection. Their argument is that the salience of non-contractible dimensions of a supplier-buyer relationship determines the optimal number of suppliers. The buyer relinquishes part of his power in order to create an incentive for the supplier to invest in the relationship. It is a balancing act of control over gains from investments and quality improvements. Non-contractible properties of the relation are dealt with through contracts concerning the exclusiveness of the relation and a barter of power concerning the division of revenues. What we see is a growing reliance on (specific) institutional arrangements to provide the right balance of interests in the area of non-contractible and intangible aspects of inter-firm relationships.

## The synthesis: 'anything goes' and mixed-mode hypothesis

In essence, Information Systems enable organisations to do what they want much more efficiently and flexibly ... It is therefore suggested that in practice the outcome will be a mixed mode of operation in which elements of both markets and hierarchies are evident simultaneously. (Holland & Lockett, 1994, p 409)

Holland and Lockett (1994) propose what may be described as the ‘anything goes’ hypothesis, stating that whatever governance form a firm chooses, IT can be used to accentuate the effects of the chosen governance structure and to improve its efficiency. In hierarchical relationships IOS are designed to foster the organizational integration, in market relationships IOS reduce information and transaction costs and facilitate the ease of switching among several suppliers.

In response to the primary transaction cost oriented approaches, Holland and Lockett (1994) have developed an extended theoretical framework and have included business marketing, manufacturing strategy and organizational behaviour literature. Based on these literatures and in contrast to the prevailing contextual determinism, they develop a research framework for coordination strategy and propose the ‘mixed mode hypothesis’: firms develop different forms of market and hierarchical relationships that are maintained simultaneously. The interrelations and interdependencies of governance structure, asset specificity, market complexity and coordination strategy are analysed in order to explain inter-organizational arrangements. Holland and Lockett (1994, pp 410–413) use five cases to illustrate different examples of vertical relationships on a continuum from a high level of organizational integration and hierarchy to fragmented business relationships and a market system of trade. All but the market system example show a combination of hierarchical and market arrangements. These mixed-mode arrangements combine the benefits of markets, such as high incentives to lower costs and improve quality, with the benefits of hierarchies, namely the efficient integration of operations.

## A configuration framework

While there is a strong trend in the current literature to – in terms of economic logic – argue that ‘the contingencies of the economic rationale determine the selection of governance forms’, Holland and Lockett (1994) have emphasised the role of strategic calculations and have given empirical evidence that usually companies do not go for an either/or decision but design mixed-mode forms of governance. We therefore propose that strategic management should be augmented by coordination strategy, which covers all aspects of the design and maintenance of inter-organizational relations and arrangements. Although inter-organizational relations have traditionally been subject to strategic considerations, coordination strategy recognises that the concept of the firm is changing as firm boundaries become increasingly blurred and emphasises the management of inter-organizational relations and in particular the development of closer and more cooperative relationships like alliances or partnerships. In addition, the boundaries of the firm themselves become subject to coordination strategy. Coordination strategy recognises questions of the (inter-) organizational architecture as well as decisions about the design of IOS which are both strongly interdependent.

Inter-organizational arrangements have their own dynamics that affect the participants' strategies and cannot be (fully) controlled by any single participating organization, ie the autonomy of strategic decision making is limited. It is recognized that coordination strategising is embedded in bi- or multilateral inter-organizational relations, in encompassing industry and market structures as well as in governmental/public policies and regulations (Best, 1990). However, we suggest that coordination strategy, despite these restrictions, should be seen as an integral part of a firm's strategic management.

Like any other strategy, coordination strategy has to take restrictions and contingencies into account. At the same time, strategy is not only reactive and adaptive but also actively shaping its own environment, ie the (potential) impact of strategy has to be considered. In order to depict coordination strategy rationale, we will discuss the duality of contingencies and impact of coordination strategy. Given the plurality of inter-organizational arrangements and the multiplicity of inter-organizational relations, we propose a research framework that comprehends elements that at the same time affect and are affected by coordination strategy (see Figure 1, the arrows indicate contingencies and impacts of coordination strategy):

1. Market and industry structure reflects the external orientation of strategic management.

2. Governance structure refers to different types of institutional arrangements or regimes between firms.

3. Transaction and relationship attributes reflect an organizational design perspective on strategy, which focuses on the inter-organizational relationships and their underlying business transactions. At this point qualitative and social aspects of relationships such as trust are also included.

4. Resource based strategy emphasises the unique competencies of firms and thus reflects a more internally oriented perspective which, however, complements market strategy.

Each of these elements encompasses options for strategic design such as the position of a firm in a network, the selection of governance forms in relation to different business partners, the shaping of different layers of inter-organizational relationships and the choice of forms of resource usage and development. 'Configuration' refers to the arrangement of complex combinations of design options in relation to the four elements of the framework. The research proposition is that inter-organizational arrangements are the result of complex, multi-layer configurations of organizational parameters. This framework extends the idea of mixed-mode arrangements with respect to governance decisions (Holland & Lockett, 1994) and applies it to the positioning of companies, to the different layers within inter-organizational relations and to the different modes of resource usage.

## Contingencies, impact and configuration options for a coordination strategy

The development of coordination strategies can be divided into three steps. The initial step scrutinises contingencies of market and governance structure, of transaction attributes and the resource base for coordination strategy. The second step focuses the impact of coordination decisions on these four elements. Finally, different options for the configuration of inter-organizational relations are distinguished.

The concept of configuration alludes to Mintzberg's (1979, see also Miller & Friesen 1984) work, who has emphasised the salience of an internal fit within organizations in contrast to a permanent adaptation to environmental changes. While Mintzberg focused on ideal-type organizational forms in his early work (1979), he later (1991) expanded his argument to combinations of forces that characterise organizations. This later notion is applied here to describe combinations – or rather compositions – of inter-organizational arrangements within a field of market and inter-organizational forces.

... we have two views of organizational effectiveness. One, for the lumpers, concentrates on a portfolio of forms, from which organizations are encouraged to choose if they wish to become effective. The other, for the splitters, focuses on a system of forces, with which organizations are encouraged to play in order to become effective. (Mintzberg, 1991, p 765)

The hypothesis is that firms form multiple, multi-layer relationships with different partners and groups of companies in order to sustain their competitive position. Designing, flexibly adapting and carefully balancing these configurations – rather than selecting one efficient governance form – are the major tasks of coordination strategy. The design (or configuration) parameters are encompassed by the elements of the framework: (1) the position of the enterprise in inter-organizational relations, be it a network, groups of networks or markets; (2) combinations of governance forms with different partners similar to the mixed-mode hypothesis; (3) combinations of governance forms within a given network of relations referring to the different layers in inter-organizational relations, namely the institutional, the operational and the technical layer; and (4) combinations of proprietary, shared and pooled resources.

## Market and industry structure

Market and industry structure define the fundamental setting of a business: products and services as well as the structure and intensity of competition.

## Contingencies

Market complexity, dynamics, uncertainty, degree of concentration and dominance are the most frequently quoted structural features of markets and industries that affect governance decisions. Price and product volatility and the complexity of product descriptions are the most important determinants (Bensaou, 1993; Clemons & Reddi, 1994).

## Impact of coordination strategy

Governance forms like mergers and acquisitions or other forms of vertical integration are chosen in order to improve the relative market position of a firm. By the same token the design and initiation of inter-organizational arrangements like cooperative networks affect the market position of the participants (Bakos, 1987). The introduction of IOS can fundamentally change the market structure, especially if it is combined with a change of the governance structure like the establishment of an electronic market where previously hierarchical structures have dominated. If IOS are deployed within given governance structures, they affect attributes like the transparency and volatility of markets or the standardisation of products.

## Configuration options

The configuration options on this level refer to different aspects of a firm's position within the market, possibly within networks and the position of the network in relation to other networks (Gomes-Casseres, 1994). The position of a firm can be described in terms of the definition of its business and its role in complex value chains. The development of electronic markets with new entrants and major challenges for traditional intermediaries like wholesalers, underscores the salience of the (re-)positioning of firms (Benjamin & Wigand, 1995).

## Governance structure

The governance structure refers to different types of institutional arrangements between firms. The generic inter-organizational coordination forms are market, hierarchy or network.

## Contingencies

Governance regimes, ie existing patterns of inter-organizational arrangements such as a high degree of concentration in the grocery retail industry, influence governance decisions. Within various sectors a trend to adopt dominant governance forms – so called isomorphism – has been observed (DiMaggio & Powell, 1991).

When firms are part of existing and successful networks within an industry, individual firm's decisions are determined by these networks. It is increasingly difficult to compete against groups of companies, and with every competitor joining one of the existing networks, the scope of action becomes more and more restricted (Gomes-Casseres, 1994). The phrase ‘contract-oriented market economy’, coined by Tietz (1992, p 53), describes this constellation.

## Impact of coordination strategy

The main task of coordination strategy is the selection of governance forms for various inter-organizational relations such as R&D alliances or close vendor–supplier relationships. The prevalent governance decisions of companies will have an impact on the governance regime in a sector and will subsequently influence other companies' decisions.

As Holland and Lockett (1994) have shown, IOS by themselves do not bias governance decisions; however they have the potential to make chosen governance forms more efficient.

## Configuration

The configuration options refer to combinations of inter-organizational arrangements with different partners. Kambil and Short (1994) have used the roles and linkages model to describe the spectrum of options within a given business case. In most industries, firms try to leverage market mechanisms in relation to suppliers and closer network relations on the customer side. Another typical pattern is to have one preferred supplier and several second layer suppliers in order to benefit from the advantages of a closely coupled, trust-based, cooperative relationship and at the same time uphold competitive pressure on the preferred supplier. Holland and Lockett (1994) have summarised a number of typical governance patterns.

## Transaction and relationship attributes

While governance structure refers to institutional arrangements, the concrete design and pattern of relationships within these arrangements can vary considerably. There are multiple forms of network arrangements or market mechanisms.

## Contingencies

Transaction cost theory hypothesises that the choice of governance forms is contingent on the transaction attributes, frequency, asset specificity and uncertainty of transactions (Williamson, 1975). Further relational attributes have been proposed in the literature such as dependency vs autonomy and opportunism vs trust or centrality and integration of transactions, information complexity, defection risk and switching cost. Oliver (1990) has proposed a framework that covers additional determinants for the formation of inter-organizational relationships:

necessity, asymmetry, ie the desire for autonomy and control, reciprocity, efficiency, stability and legitimacy.

## Impact of coordination strategy

Inter-organizational arrangements are designed and implemented in order to affect transaction attributes, eg inter-organizational networks reduce risks of opportunism and at the same time maintain some of the incentives of competition.

The design of IOS, in particular the degree of standardisation, affects relationship attributes. Solutions based on standards reduce asset specificity and related costs and risks, such as switching costs and the risk of one-sided dependencies, for the participants; they increase, however, the defection risk for the initiator of the IOS.

## Configuration

Several layers can be distinguished within the set-up of inter-organizational arrangements. (1) The institutional layer relates to boundaries of a network, to rules for participation and coordination forms. It refers to the specification of the governance form within a business relationship. (2) The operational layer deals with organizational integration and alignment of procedures. It encompasses, for example, the selection and combination of coordination mechanisms. (3) The technical layer refers to design parameters of IOS, such as the intra-organizational integration of applications or IOS-related governance decisions. In order to reduce risks, different arrangements can be maintained on different layers, such as a market-like institutional arrangement combined with a hierarchical governance of the IOS.

## Resource base

The resource-based paradigm of business strategy has focused on the individual competencies of firms and their development and maintenance. However, a resource-based rationale is also an important element of coordination strategy since competencies can be acquired and developed within inter-organizational arrangements. Referring to the ongoing discussion about the impact of IT on industrial organization, we will argue that the resource-based theory augments and partially complements the transaction cost focused theorising (Klein & Kronen 1995).

## Contingencies

The relative resource position of a firm or of a group of firms (eg resources that are facilitated and enhanced by sharing) affects coordination strategising. Weder (1989, p 117) has shown that a complementary resource position of firms favours the development of cooperative coordination forms. In times of volatile technological developments, the joint development of resources reduces the financial risks for the participants.

## Impact of coordination strategy

While the resource-based strategy literature focuses – with few exceptions – on the intra-organizational development and isolation of firm specific resources, inter-organizational arrangements are often chosen in order to get access to complementary resources or to pool resources and develop them within a network. Hamel (1991) for example has shown the potential of inter-partner learning.

IOS facilitate and accelerate the exchange of resources such as marketing or partner information and they provide a platform for resource sharing and pooling within so-called value added or information partnerships (Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991).

## Configuration

Depending on the relative resource position and the quality of the resources, different strategies such as protecting, pooling, sharing or exchanging of resources can be chosen (see also Moss Kanter, 1989). Some of the resource strategies can be combined with different governance forms; details are presented in Table 1.

Table 2 summarises the elements of coordination strategy framework that are subject to the three steps of configuration analysis. The elements reflect the complexity and multiplicity of inter-organizational relations. Coordination strategy in its attempt to align and balance, eg the technological and organizational arrangements or the benefits of markets and hierarchies, thus mirrors the hybrid structure of inter-organizational networks (Best, 1990).

The following example of EUROSELECT illustrates the analytic potential of the proposed research framework, which is used to reconstruct the strategic rationale of the complex configuration of EUROSELECT.

## EUROSELECT: the configuration of a network

Multi-organization service alliances, or consortia, are groups of firms that band together to create a new entity to fill a need shared by all of them. They are generally limited in purpose, such as research and development, and offer the benefits of larger scale through resource pooling. (Moss Kanter & Myers, 1991, p 332)

EUROSELECT, a network of 12 European grocery wholesalers, is built around arbitrage operations that benefit from the existing price differences among the member states of the European Union (EU). A shared strategic vision is the basis of the cooperation among the wholesalers, who have combined their respective competencies, decentralised knowledge and business connections into a value adding partnership. The EURO-SELECT network provides the organizational framework for an EDI-based product/price catalogue and ordering system. IT is used as a lever to set-up a Europe wide procurement information and order management network of medium-sized wholesalers, who thereby gain the virtual size required to gain (information) economies of scale and to operate effectively in a unifying Europe, and to compete successfully in their regional markets (Klein & Kronen, 1993; Cavaye, 1995).

## Market and industry structure: the competitive environment

The grocery industry in Europe is a highly concentrated industry with small profit margins and numerous links along the value chain. While take-overs and cooperation among producers in the grocery industry lead to a decreasing number of producers of the same articles, the remaining producers integrate forward by taking over distribution channels, thus enhancing the European concentration process in the industry (Gorter de Vries & van Leur, 1991). At the other end, a few large, international wholesalers increasingly dominate the market. In addition, supermarket chains, originally operating in their home country, have started to expand into other European countries, either by opening branches in other countries (eg Aldi or Tengelmann, Germany: Carrefour or Auchan, France) or by cooperating with other chains (eg Rewe in Germany) (Zentes 1992).

Price volatility in the industry is high, not least as a result of seasonal influences. In addition, remarkable price differences exist throughout the EU, especially for a large number of basic goods, even between identical products supplied by different national branches of the same producer.

Squeezed between dominant national suppliers and growing supermarket chains, Van Eerd whose core business is wholesaling and who also owns a supermarket chain, sought an opportunity to extend procurement to a European level without becoming a multinational company. In the late 1980s he established EUROSELECT, a peer network of wholesalers throughout the EU that faced a similar situation, and were prepared to exchange procurement information. By cooperating horizontally, the position of the wholesalers is strengthened and dependency on suppliers is reduced in an indirect manner. Although existing wholesaler–supplier relations are not immediately affected, the participating wholesalers get new procurement opportunities through the network and can expand their (virtual) procurement market Europe wide. An IOS is the backbone of this network and enables them to sustain the arbitrage operations and the size of the network at reasonable costs.

The horizontal network is carefully positioned within the highly concentrated market. The main impact of the arrangement is the extended virtual size of the participating organizations which gives them leverage towards competitors and suppliers and at the same time yields benefits for their customers.

## Governance structure: configuring multiple relationships

EUROSELECT establishes what comes close to a virtual marketplace where prices from all over Europe are compared. However, given the structure of the industry, producers are reluctant to post their prices in an electronic market as prices not only vary regionally but are also partner-specific, the supplier side is represented by the national partners who post their supplier's prices. This implies that on the customer side as well, some restrictions apply in order to secure the interests of the national partners who share their procurement prices.

EUROSELECT is set-up as a three layer network (see Figure 2):

1. The first layer consists of the EUROSELECT central office in the Netherlands and national offices in the EU member states. It is the core part of the operational network: the offices collect information and exchange it electronically. They are responsible for the classification of products and quality control.

2. The second layer consists of wholesalers from the EU. There is only one partner for each country in order to avoid conflicts of interest within the network. These national partners share their procurement information on a daily basis. They get privileged access to the buying suggestions and get a share of the profits generated by sales that are based on their buying suggestions. As a hypothetical example, a Spanish wholesaler will get the information from EURO-SELECT, that cans of Coca Cola are cheaper in Greece than in Spain or Portugal and will be able to submit his order to EUROSELECT. The national partners maintain the relationship to their suppliers. EUROSELECT, however, buys directly from the suppliers. Better prices and an extended assortment of high quality products provide an opportunity for the wholesalers to strengthen their relationship with their customers. The network of national partners is essentially a closed user group in which wholesalers participate to strengthen their competitive position. The boundaries of the network are thoroughly maintained. EUROSELECT combines information barter and a platform for order management among the national partners with market relations towards suppliers and participating wholesalers.

3. The third layer consists of participating wholesalers who only get selected offers and can place their orders with EUROSELECT. This third layer poses some risk as the participating wholesalers are competitors to the second layer group. However, they contribute to the volume of sales and thus improve the bargaining power of the network, and the national partners get a certain percentage of the revenues. EUROSELECT has to balance these conflicting interests. It maintains a strict regime and insists, for example, that the price lists be kept confidential.

## Transaction attributes: configuring multiple layers within a cooperative network

The design of the relationship between Van Eerd Holding (VEH) and the national partners reflects the balancing act between the need for an open exchange of procurement information and the fear of losing critical assets to (potential) competitors.

## The institutional level

On an institutional level, the EUROSELECT network is a focal network. EUROSELECT is a subsidiary of Van Eerd Holding (VEH) for information services. VEH is the hub of the network, it initiated EUROSELECT, holds a dominant position with high centrality and keeps a close hierarchical control over the IS. The dominant position of VEH helps to avoid long discussions about procedures and technical arrangements, but it requires a high level of trustworthiness, reliability and integrity on behalf of EUROSELECT. As soon as the trust in VEH is undermined for whatever reason, the entire network is endangered.

The national partners operate in different regional markets and cooperate only in a part of their procurement activities. They retain a high level of independence and there is no direct competition among the national partners. The selection process has been challenging and time-consuming as it was necessary to align different interests and to bridge the gap between national cultures and business practices. Selection criteria have been personal ties and a shared strategy. A careful selection of partners secures the delicate balance of the social fabric of the network.

## Operational level

At an operational level there is a clear distinction between the exchange of price information and the subsequent information and order management.

The exchange of prices is governed by a barter mechanism: each party provides its share and gets the best offers from all over Europe. For the participants it is a mutually beneficial situation of information pooling. Every night, prices of more than 5000 products are collected and compared in order to generate a daily updated price list and a list of buying suggestions. VEH has developed and harmonised organizational procedures for price quotations and product classification in order to ensure the validity of the comparison.

Most of the ordering is done through the EURO-SELECT head office in order to take advantage of scale economies and to coordinate logistics efficiently. Yet again, by this means VEH maintains a high degree of central control over the entire operations although it depends on the input from the network partners.

## Technical level

This pattern is repeated at the technical level: information technology provides the backbone to handle the size and the extension of the network efficiently. Electronic data interchange (EDI) is used for the purpose of communicating product, price and order information which is stored and compared in a database. Efficient transfer and processing of information alone keep transaction costs low and assure the viability of the arbitrage across Europe. However, the EDI network is only maintained on the first layer of the network, while communication with the national partners is based on traditional communication media like fax. This keeps investments on the side of the national partners low and their independence is correspondingly high. On the other hand further potential for rationalising the information exchange with the national partners is foregone in order to maintain a higher level of control over the critical IT infrastructure.

## Resource base

VEH maintains a careful balance between pooled or shared and proprietary resources. Thereby the advantages of the cooperation are obtained and the risks of losing proprietary resources are minimised.

## Proprietary and shared resources

The core competencies of operating EUROSELECT are market and product knowledge. Each partner not only retains this competence but also expands it through the exchange with peer organizations.

At an operational level, a reliable classification scheme is needed in order to compare multiple product features. Van Eerd has designed the network so that the product classification know-how is kept within the first layer of the EUROSELECT offices. Know-how for the evaluation of prices and the coordination of logistics as well as the profile of the partners are all kept centrally. However, part of the classification know-how is shared among the partners in order to improve the efficiency of information collection and exchange.

## Pooled resources

The success of EUROSELECT depends on the input from the national bureaus and – most of all – their national partners. The efficiency of their intelligence operations, the extension of their business connections to suppliers and their assessment of offers (in combination with the central office's judgement based on specific knowledge for the classification and evaluation of products) largely determine the attractiveness of EURO-SELECT. The daily suggestions of best buys are the result of pooled resources.

## Resource building - cooperative learning

The cooperative arrangement has two effects on the resource position of the participants:

1. Individual market knowledge is enhanced through exchange. For the price of sharing information about their own market, each of the partners gets an overview of the EU market.

2. Organizational routines for ordering and logistics are harmonised among the partners in order to improve efficiency.

In sum, EUROSELECT is a hybrid solution. On the one hand it resembles an electronic marketplace in its function with a focus on price and product information operated by a cooperative network of wholesalers. Market and arbitrage mechanisms are used as incentives among the participants, the network provides economies of scale, virtual size, learning opportunities through resource pooling and sharing as well as an improved competitive position for the participants. However, each of the partners maintains a high level of independence and autonomy.

The network, on the other hand, though cooperative in spirit has a dominant hub-spoke structure with VEH in the centre. The infrastructure is governed hierarchically in order to protect critical resources on behalf of VEH and to reduce the defection risk. The configuration of EUROSELECT, the fit among different functional components, shows a delicate balance between the hub-spoke governance structure and the cooperative spirit of the arrangement. This fragile balance of market, cooperative and hierarchical structures within the arrangement is a potential source of conflict among the participants. However, for the time being the advantages of being a member of EUROSELECT outweigh the objections against the governance structure at this level. The combination of a centrally controlled infrastructure and a cooperative network of autonomous partners ensures stability as well as flexibility but depends to a high degree on the leadership of the hub.

## Conclusions and propositions

A framework for the analysis and design of coordination strategy which focuses organizational and technical arrangements on an inter-organizational level, has been proposed and illustrated by using the example of EURO-SELECT. The following propositions summarise the underlying assumptions and future areas of research:

1. Configuration decisions reflect a complex, multidimensional set of contingencies that encompass market, industry, inter-organizational and firm related factors. At the same time coordination strategy is directed at affecting some of the contingencies and at changing the firms' and the network's competitive position.

2. Governance decisions and decisions about the design of IOS are interrelated and constitute together the coordination strategy. However, complementary arrangements can be chosen in order to combine the benefits of more open, market-like arrangements and more controlled hierarchical arrangements. Different governance forms on different levels of a network relationship can be used to balance conflicting interests among the partners. As networks can be interpreted as dialectic arrangements that combine the benefits of control, cooperation and competition, the organizational configuration of networks mirrors this dialectic relationship. Hierarchical arrangements are put in place in order to offset the risks of losing proprietary resources, cooperative arrangements are developed in order to pool resources and to utilise the benefits of virtual size.

3. Rather than a singular decision or a simple choice among alternatives, coordination strategy faces a complex set of interrelated configuration options. The multiplicity of design and decision parameters mirrors the complexity of inter-organizational arrangements. IOS not only make existing governance forms more efficient, they also – and even more importantly – facilitate changes and combinations of governance forms that make firms more flexible and more fluid.

Further research is needed to operationalise the proposed framework and to show how it can be used to facilitate the strategic planning of fluid organizational arrangements. Configuration analysis is seen as a method to study the emergence as well as success and failure of inter-organizational arrangements.
