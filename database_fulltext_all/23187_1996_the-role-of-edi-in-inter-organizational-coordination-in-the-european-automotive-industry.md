# The role of EDI in inter-organizational coordination in the European automotive industry

N Reekers and S Smithson

London School of Economics and Political Science, Information Systems Department, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is an important prerequisite for inter-organizational coordination in the automotive industry. It not only affects the efficiency of coordination but also power-dependency and structural aspects of inter-organizational relationships. This study assesses the impact of EDI use on the trading relationships between car manufacturers and their suppliers based on a series of case studies in the German and UK automotive industries. The analysis is based on a multi-perspective research framework integrating three major approaches: transaction cost analysis, resource dependence theory and the network perspective. Findings suggest that EDI enables both parties to rationalise their operations. However, manufacturers still optimise their production at the expense of their suppliers. This may have a negative impact on cooperation with suppliers preventing long-term ‘true’ partnerships.

## Introduction

The automotive industry has seen a steady movement towards more complex inter-organizational structures and coordinative mechanisms between car manufacturers and their suppliers. Increasingly, manufacturers are engaging in long-term stable relationships with their suppliers as opposed to the more traditional market relationships, characterised by conflicting interests.

Changes in the competitive environment drove car manufacturers to restructure their supply chains and rely increasingly on effective coordination of their logistical activities. EDI can be seen as a prerequisite for these externally focused systems with its ability to provide fast, frequent and reliable information exchange (Golhar & Stamm, 1991; Womack et al, 1990). But EDI does not only affect the efficiency of coordination, its impact may be even more fundamental on the power-dependency relationship. EDI can shift the balance of power and control between the supplier and the manufacturer, making for a possibly unhealthy dependence on either side. As EDI can increase the efficiency of an entire network of organizations, conflicting interests between the organizations will often need to be resolved (Delbridge et al, 1990). The organizational literature suggests that inter-organizational networks are likely to experience more conflict when organizations with different perspectives work together (Alter & Hage, 1993).

The objective of this paper is to assess the impact of EDI on the trading relationships between car manufacturers and their suppliers. The goal is to identify the extent of the changes that are currently taking place in the European automotive industry that can be attributed to EDI use. The basis of this research rests on three dominant theoretical perspectives which are normally applied separately. These are transaction cost analysis, resource dependence theory and the network perspective. The study moves beyond focusing on single organizational relationships to an examination of a range of pairwise relationships. Taking a network perspective, the unit of analysis becomes a network of organizations rather than individual dyadic relationships.

The following section of the paper describes the recent major developments in the European automotive industry. This context has affected the use of EDI and its consideration highlights the conditions under which car manufacturers and their suppliers have adopted electronic trading. Before presenting our research framework, we define EDI and discuss the various levels of EDI use in the automotive industry. The framework draws on a strong theoretical foundation and identifies a number of key concepts which guided the data collection and analysis. This is followed by a description of the research design and the discussion of our findings, which are summarised in the conclusion.

## Changes in the automotive industry

During the 1980s, increased competition from Japanese manufacturers in terms of price, quality and model range forced European manufacturers to change radically their production methods (Delbridge et al., 1990; Wells & Rawlinson, 1994). In a buyers' market, customer demand forced a change from mass production to a need to make individualised automobiles in small lots, while at the same time increasing efficiency through strict inventory controls and the precise coordination of parts manufacturing and delivery, sub-assembly production, and final assembly.

This radical change in the market forced most European car manufacturers, such as Ford, Opel, Vauxhall and BMW, to adopt certain closely related strategies which significantly changed their relationships with suppliers (Wells & Rawlinson, 1994). Production increasingly crossed national boundaries in a search for improved efficiency and competitiveness. Customisation of products became essential, leading to modular sourcing where key suppliers were entrusted with the supply of complex sub-assemblies (modules). The manufacturers themselves outsourced more operations, reducing their own depth of production as part of a general rationalisation of operations. The number of direct suppliers (mostly of sub-assemblies) was reduced with other suppliers being relegated to second-level suppliers serving the first-level modular suppliers. Just-in-time manufacturing, involving the synchronisation of production with suppliers, improved efficiency considerably by dramatically reducing inventory and work in progress.

Manufacturers introduced the concept of lean production (Womack et al, 1990) to shorten production cycles, reduce work and space expenditure required for assembly, and lower inventory levels while increasing product variety and improving quality. An important aspect is materials management and the coordination of the supply chain. To ensure flexible production with minimal stock and minimal bound capital, better planning and continuous information exchange between suppliers and manufacturers is required.

The increased complexity of components supply led the manufacturers to recognise that the efficiency of external suppliers largely determined retail prices, the quality of the final products and ultimately, their own competitiveness. Therefore, manufacturers began forming supply networks by establishing long-term joint programmes to increase productivity and quality, subcontracting more components and final assembly at unusually high levels to outside suppliers. Within this close cooperation, manufacturers can shift more responsibility for quality control and research and development to their suppliers. On the other hand, long-term contracts allow better long-term forecasts for the suppliers. The reduction of the total number of direct suppliers means increased volume and more intensive cooperation with manufacturers for the remaining suppliers.

In the rationalisation process, the design of material flows between suppliers and manufacturers becomes more important. Manufacturers require low logistical arrangements that provide high flexibility in materials management (Wildemann, 1988). Backward coordination of the supply chain is regarded as an enabler for synchronous and low-stock production. As a result, information cannot be handled in the traditional way if the organizations are to achieve an overall cost reduction and increased efficiency. Deterministic material requirements planning (MRP) systems from the 1960s and 1970s were unable to provide sufficient coordination because their prediction of requirements was based on previous production and sales data rather than on actual consumption. Changes in sales and production affected planning relatively late, so to ensure continuous production and to maintain a minimum of flexibility expensive buffer stocks were needed. In the early 1980s, the more flexible Japanese kanban system began to replace MRP systems (see Cusumano, 1985, for a description of the kanban system).

As the manufacturing techniques changed, so an increasing reliance developed on computers to coordinate sub-assembly production, final assembly and parts supply. EDI links between suppliers and manufacturers become more and more important because of EDI's ability to transmit fast, error-free information. Developments in the automotive industry to call-off materials at short notice and to reduce purchasing lead times start with improvements of information and communication links between manufacturers and suppliers. Daily or even hourly delivery frequencies of parts that are ordered at short notice require a rationalisation of data exchange. Thus, most European manufacturers increasingly rely on integrated EDI systems to synchronise production and deliveries of parts and material.

## Electronic data interchange

Most EDI definitions (cf Pfeiffer (1992) for EDI definitions) distinguish EDI from other inter-organizational systems because:

\- it facilitates the seamless exchange of information and data between computer applications with minimal or no human intervention rather than mere computer-to-computer communication.

\- it utilises electronic networks (normally value-added networks) rather than data carriers (such as magnetic tapes or disks) for regular transmission of large quantities of documents.

\- of the use of structured, formatted messages conforming to standards.

The need for far-reaching inter-organizational adjustments makes EDI different from internal information technology (IT) investments, causing difficulties when assessing its effectiveness. While the direct gains from EDI, such as reduced personnel or mailing costs, are relatively easy to measure, they tend to be unimpressive (Swatman & Swatman, 1992). Equally, the direct costs of setting up an EDI link are relatively low. The major costs and benefits are normally indirect; for example, inventory control, improved trading relationships or

EDI-induced business process re-engineering. To appraise the qualitative changes (eg a change in the governance structure), the organizational context needs to be fully understood. Similarly, the real monetary values of changes in organizational dependence are difficult to estimate (Wey & Gibson, 1991). Normally, organizations do not conduct post-implementation evaluation studies for EDI as they regard it as a strategic investment to remain competitive (Reekers & Smithson, 1994).

The level of EDI use varies considerably between suppliers, not only between the preferred suppliers and sub-suppliers but also within these groups. However, nearly all suppliers receive at least the trend, schedule and delivery ‘call-off’ message, ie a message which ‘calls-off’ goods forming part of a blanket order previously placed. Some orders are still transmitted through telephone or fax but manufacturers expect all their suppliers to implement EDI in the very near future. Considerably fewer suppliers use the invoice message. Shipping data and quality data interchange are at a very early stage of implementation, with only a few pilot projects. Similarly, EDI use for electronic payments is also only beginning to be used within the industry. Depending on the value and volume of the product and its predictability of consumption (ABC analysis), three call-off methods can be distinguished that reflect the level of EDI use, as shown in Table 1. The level of EDI use is defined by the number and frequency of messages exchanged and is related to a number of important variables considered in this study, namely efficiency, dependence and structure.

## The research framework

This section discusses the three perspectives which form the theoretical foundation and their applicability to the study of EDI: transaction cost analysis, resource dependence theory and the network perspective. Each of these perspectives has a unique emphasis that contributes to an understanding of the benefits and drawbacks of EDI use for inter-organizational coordination. Transaction cost analysis emphasises the organizational concerns with efficiency, resource dependence theory highlights the degree to which an organization is dependent on other organizations for important resources, and the network approach makes the web of relationships more explicit.

## Transaction cost theory

Transaction cost theory (Williamson, 1975) provides insight into the variety of institutional arrangements available for the fundamental economic exchange relationships between organizations. These can be described in terms of a spectrum, from loose to tight, from arms-length bargaining to total integration, from spot market transactions via standing relations to hierarchies. With the introduction of 'lean production' (Womack et al, 1990) and advanced electronic trading in the automotive industry, these issues become important. Organizations are seen as networks of contracts which govern exchange transactions between members having only partially overlapping goals. Buyers and sellers need to choose an appropriate coordination mechanism in order to economise on transaction costs. These transaction costs include environmental factors, such as asset specificity, uniqueness, uncertainty and complexity of the exchange, and behavioural factors, such as opportunism and bounded rationality. The cost of transactions is critical for the choice of an optimal governance structure. Thus, the focus of transaction cost theory is on the circumstances under which one might expect transactions to be externalised (taking place in the market) or internalised (hierarchical transactions).

In many ways, transaction cost theory can be considered as an extension to the resource dependence perspective, discussed below. While focusing on efficiency aspects of a resource exchange, it introduces constructs of risk due to potential opportunism and transaction monitoring costs that are not found in resource dependence theory. The theory has great power to discriminate between firms that are more or less vertically integrated. Its view of organizations as constantly shifting and changing their boundaries provides insight into the evolution of inter-organizational cooperation.

However, the model does not make any universal claims that would apply to all organizations, nor does it accurately predict what will happen in a specific situation. It fails to take into account the different positions of organizations regarding their strategic choices, their ability to adopt electronic trading, or the general structure of their industry. Therefore, varying impacts of EDI adoption on the inter-organizational structure need to be examined on a case by case basis. There are also weaknesses in operationalising the concepts of transaction and transaction costs (Lyytinen, 1991). Its key limitation is that it only considers the economic dimension and neglects the political and other non-economic aspects of inter-organizational relationships. Thus, it cannot adequately recognise the complex interdependencies between manufacturers and suppliers. While transaction cost theory provides a fruitful springboard for understanding the emergence of vertically integrated and horizontally integrated firms, it is less applicable for understanding emerging organizational arrangements. It is useful to explain the movement towards vertical integration but it is not helpful in understanding the movement away from vertical integration under precisely the same set of conditions.

For the purpose of this research we use transaction cost theory to conceptualise the impact of EDI on the efficiency of inter-organizational coordination and to study firm boundaries. EDI acts as a mediating technology that can lower transaction costs and improve information handling. Consequently, changes in information processing technology can affect the choice of governance structure and result in a different level of vertical integration.

The primary theoretical construct of transaction cost theory is cost efficiency. It focuses on the economic motivations behind EDI adoption decisions and the impact of EDI on the costs for information exchange. As the total transaction costs are often difficult to quantify, three additional constructs are considered in the analysis: transaction type (frequency and specificity of transactions), production complexity and uncertainty. Parallels of these constructs are also considered by the other two theories from a dependency and structural perspective rather than efficiency considerations.

Technological change, such as the adoption of electronic trading, has two main impacts on the total economic efficiency (Lyytinen, 1991). First, it can reduce the costs associated with the operation of transactions because of the automation of information handling. Second, the cost of developing and maintaining such a system may also increase transaction costs, especially when the system is dedicated to specific transactions. These additional costs may not be distributed equally among trading partners. EDI can also reduce external or market transaction costs in other ways: it can help to reduce the costs of market search and reduce opportunity costs related to uncertainty due to the availability of effective monitoring schemes. The reduction of transaction costs due to the availability of cost-effective information technology should allow manufacturers to subcontract more than before and reduce the level of vertical integration while improving the management of the heightened operating risk (Clemons et al, 1993; Johnston & Vitale, 1988).

## Resource dependence theory

Resource dependence theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) complements transaction cost theory by adding a process dimension concerned with economic decision making and behavioural aspects of an organizational dyad. This reflects a more holistic approach, with an explicit recognition of the economic and political dimensions of organizational interaction that addresses the whole relationship over time and across its economic/political as well as structural/behavioural dimensions.

Dependence can arise due to limited supply alternatives, or it can arise from an imbalance of power between supplier and car manufacturer caused by differences in their relative importance to each other. Resource dependence can be a response to problems of managing complexity and uncertainty in the environment. Therefore, organizational interdependence becomes a function of the distribution of both control of scare resources and the constraints on the acquisition of resources controlled by others. Assuming that organizations are rational actors that strive to optimise their self-interests, the objective of a firm is to minimise its dependence on other firms and to maximise the dependence of other firms on itself.

However, the resource dependence perspective recognises that firms are open systems, and that, consequently, they must continuously engage in boundary interactions with other organizations in their environment. No organization is totally self-sufficient, rather each must enter into inter-organizational relationships with other organizations to secure those resources that it needs and to dispose of its output. Although organizations strive for functional autonomy, it is a state that is never fully realised (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978). With increasing environmental complexity, organizations will try to stabilise inter-organizational linkages through formalised inter-firm linkages, such as EDI links, that allow them to react more flexibly to market changes (Van de Ven & Walker, 1979).

According to this theory, EDI can be regarded as a control mechanism to expand influence and status. This view allows for the analysis of asymmetric power and dependence relationships and the consideration of such aspects of inter-organizational relationships as conflict, struggle for power, exploitation and protection of autonomy. These aspects are mostly neglected by other approaches. However, the resource dependence perspective is limited by its lack of conceptual and operational definition of concepts, such as opportunism or dependence, as well as a lack of empirical validation.

The use of EDI can lead either to reduced or increased dependence on trading partners. Partner-specific investments create substantial switching costs and, once made, make the two parties highly interdependent. In just-in-time environments, increased interdependence is likely if EDI leads to higher coordination needs and mutual adaptations between manufacturer and supplier. Single and modular sourcing increase the dependence of manufacturers on suppliers as a manufacturer makes investments to adjust to a single supplier who is responsible for a module; for example, cutting back their own production may mean that a manufacturer cannot resume production at short notice. In this sort of case, the manufacturer will wish to monitor closely the relationship with preferred suppliers as the failure of a supplier would have far reaching consequences for the manufacturer.

These interdependencies can create potential contractual problems if the parties do not completely trust each other. Contractual problems may arise because these investments are not redeployable; thus one party may act opportunistically since it, in effect, has a monopoly on the customised investments. Alternatively, EDI should reduce transaction monitoring and enforcement costs because, with a nearly equal stake in the relationship, both parties are less likely to behave opportunistically. Reciprocity motives are likely to be stronger when the bargaining position of the two partners is balanced. This is less likely to occur when one partner makes irreversible, asset specific investments or when the contribution of one partner would be difficult to replace (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978).

## Network perspective

While the above perspectives provide much insight, they are limited by their focus on dyadic relationships between trading partners. In order to understand the dynamics of inter-firm coordination fully it is necessary to study ‘several party’ relationships, extending the analysis to networks of interrelated firms (Axelsson & Easton, 1992). The inter-firm network has been largely neglected as an area of serious economic research (Clark & Staunton, 1989) due to the simplistic assumptions of traditional economic theory (Barney & Ouchi, 1986). In practice, firms operate within an historical context, characterised by direct relationships with a range of partners and indirect relationships with a further level of partners with whom their direct partners have, in turn, direct relationships.

Network analysis focuses on the relationship or linkages among two or more organizations that interact in a wider network. It has been widely used in anthropology, sociology, economics and organizational behaviour (Fulk & Boyd, 1991; Galaskiewicz, 1979) to study various social networks but its application to industrial systems is relatively new (Cunningham & Tynan, 1993; Hakansson, 1990). Fulk (1991) identifies two complementary approaches within network analysis. First, relational approaches focus on links among members of a network, based on frequency and/or intensity of communication, thus providing information about cliques of organizations and their patterns of communication. In the automotive industry this would correspond to the suppliers and manufacturers as a clique and the interactions between them. Second, structural approaches focus on network members occupying similar positions but who have no direct relational connection. A structural analysis yields 'blocks', or collections of individual organizations with similar communication patterns. This approach considers individual organizations to be less important than the roles or positions they occupy. In the automotive industry, such an analysis would show different manufacturers and different suppliers and their roles and positions in the network as different blocks.

Thus, the application of network analysis methods to the complex phenomena of inter-organizational systems has several advantages. First, the structural analysis synthesises data across different blocks of organizations, ie manufacturers and their suppliers at different levels of the supply chain. Second, it permits the assessment of individual company strategies in the context of other relationships rather than in isolation. Third, the network model offers a broader context for assessing the implications of EDI. However, the approach also has its limitations. The separation of network methods from network theory has created a body of literature described by some critics as relatively ungrounded theoretically (Nelson, 1989). Also, within network analysis, the development of concepts and data gathering tools has been slow and because of its background in sociology and anthropology, the analytical techniques have, therefore, been largely concerned with 'social' aspects of network relations rather than business aspects (Tichy et al, 1979).

From a network perspective, EDI is seen as a structural enabler to improve flexibility, exchange and adaptation processes. The network concepts of inter-organizational coordination, cooperation and power become relevant when considering the complex and sophisticated relationships between manufacturers and suppliers in the automotive industry. An organization facing a high degree of uncertainty may become increasingly dependent on an organization that can more effectively cope with the external uncertainty. Thus, EDI systems can change the power balance of a relationship towards manufacturers who are better able to cope with environmental uncertainty than their suppliers. An EDI system tends to shift centralisation of planning, coordination and decision processes towards manufacturers and it provides better control and monitoring for manufacturers. EDI use can therefore increase the level of friction and perceived conflict between manufacturers and suppliers, perhaps becoming an indirect cause of environmental uncertainty. Both the desire for more control (manufacturers) and the reluctance to lose control (suppliers) reflect asymmetrical motives. Furthermore, the interconnected environments in which these organizations operate are assumed to represent political or negotiated arenas that are characterised by injustice, information distortion, manipulation, exploitation, inequality or conflict (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978).

EDI may be adopted initially by bargaining and negotiation processes as each organization attempts to secure power and control, and then they may develop a reciprocal relationship when a mutually acceptable compromise is achieved (Oliver, 1990). Trust is a concept only recently brought into the focus of research into inter-organizational relationships (Dwyer et al, 1987). Whereas the market and the hierarchy are characterised as low-trust coordination mechanisms the climate of trust is the hallmark of inter-organizational networks. EDI is an important element in those hybrid coordination structures because it facilitates comprehensive information exchange and mutual adjustments, through the stepwise adjustment of schedules from continuous updates of production forecasts; thus, contributing to more trust in the trading relationship.

One way for organizations to protect themselves from environmental uncertainty (and complexity) is by being better organized. In this context, EDI systems can facilitate better planning, more coordinated actions and greater competitive strength. As the uncertainty and complexity of the environment increase, organizations are likely to make efforts towards increasing the level of vertical coordination. These efforts could be made through more intensive interaction and a higher degree of integration through EDI. However, the vision of highly integrated manufacturing processes depends on agreement upon a common data infrastructure with common data standards and network protocols. Nevertheless, individual organizations have their own preferences that might cause them to choose systems based on their own criteria rather than on their ability to integrate with business partners. This is particularly likely where large firms with a variety of internal systems optimise on their local needs rather than their ability to communicate with the outside world.

## Research design

Drawing on the three theoretical perspectives, we extracted three key concepts for the analysis, described in more detail in Reekers and Smithson (1995). These are efficiency, dependence and network structure. The concepts were used to generate interview questions and to interpret the findings. Although each theoretical perspective has its distinct focus, most concepts have parallels in the other two theories. For example, transaction cost theory shares an interest in vertical integration and opportunism with resource dependence theory. The network perspective considers power and conflict as network characteristics that parallel the concepts of influence and power in the resource dependence theory.

To evaluate the effect of EDI beyond the impact of other exogenous factors, such as market dynamics, competition or culture, we considered organizations that use different levels of EDI and determined the correlation between the perceived change and the level of EDI use. Manufacturers in the UK and in Germany were selected as focal firms together with a number of their suppliers. Data collection involved a series of seventeen in-depth interviews with representatives from two German and two British car manufacturers and six supplier organizations. All the interviews followed the same protocol, lasting on average approximately one and a half hours. Semi-structured questions were used to elicit information on specific issues, and ample opportunities were given to allow participants to recount their own experiences. The interview data were supplemented with information gleaned from annual reports and internal documentation. An informal triangulation was achieved by using a variety of sources while focusing on the same events.

## Discussion of findings

The following discussion examines our findings from the interviews, based on the above theoretical framework. During the discussion we highlight the effects of EDI on the efficiency of inter-organizational coordination, the dependencies between car manufacturers and their suppliers, and the structure of trading relationships.

## Efficiency

In all the cases, suppliers claimed to have realised operational or first order benefits from the use of EDI, in addition to the major benefit of staying in business with the car manufacturers. Frequently interviewees reported quantifiable benefits such as the reduction of costs and an increased speed of day-to-day communication with trading partners:

Large supplier: ‘EDI frees up time from routine transactions for more challenging and more important activities. We have got more time and more information in problem situations. There are much less benefits from CAD/CAM applications’.

Supplier and advanced EDI user: 'Quick, paper information exchange across boundaries is essential between customers, suppliers, production plants, shipping agents, external depots, banks and the authorities. Without EDI we would not be able to manage huge amounts of data any more'.

Suppliers as well as manufacturers were able to reduce the number of personnel needed for order processing and saved money on mailing costs and paperwork. One supplier cut 18 jobs in the sales department and created only two positions for EDI administrators. Although interviewees reported some measurable benefits, no company conducted any post-implementation analysis to measure the actual benefits in monetary terms. The direct investments in EDI systems, such as hardware and software, are not a major concern for suppliers or manufacturers. However, the indirect costs when integrating EDI into existing internal systems and the running costs can be very high, as pointed out by one interviewee:

Large supplier: 'The technology to start with EDI is relatively inexpensive. The real cost occurs somewhere else. The indirect costs to reorganize our internal data processing, maintain the link and to handle errors are very high. EDI customers require more programming effort to process their data with our complex production planning systems and to automate the synchronisation of production'.

From a manufacturer's perspective, EDI is regarded as a productivity enhancing tool while, for suppliers, the service enhancing capabilities are more important than the cost savings. To stay in business with car manufacturers, who often account for a large portion of their turnover, suppliers have to be continuously innovative and meet the demands of these important customers. As a result, suppliers have to spend more resources on EDI functionality which helps reduce the transaction costs of the manufacturer:

Manufacturer: 'EDI is absolutely necessary for rationalisation. Traditional paper based transactions are too expensive. The market forces us to cut personnel costs because people get too expensive! Paper has to be eliminated first'.

On the other hand, the extra service provided by the EDI system is likely to increase the transaction costs of the supplier:

Large supplier: 'Our additional service is not for free. Partnership with the manufacturers' hand in our pockets cannot work in the long-term'.

Higher level benefits from EDI use seem to be more difficult to both achieve and measure. These are mainly strategic or second order benefits. The speed of the communication and the sharing of information makes suppliers more flexible to react to unforeseen changes in the production programme of manufacturers. It allows suppliers to provide a better customer service which may give them a competitive edge over competitors. The following statements illustrate EDI's potential to improve cooperation and coordination:

Medium supplier: 'EDI encourages the sharing of information. The same information for all minimises misunderstandings and inefficient action. When talking to someone on the telephone both parties have the same numbers on their screen'.

Large supplier: 'We are no longer dependent on purely our own forecasting. There are fewer surprises and we are developing a better understanding of each other's business. Over time, EDI helps to build trust and more open partnerships'.

Manufacturer: 'EDI enables us to keep our suppliers informed about changes. We update our orders continuously so that suppliers can act accordingly'.

The ability to receive orders immediately highlights the supplier's ability to deal with the orders efficiently but it places considerable stress on their internal response systems. The absence of slack forces them to react much more quickly. A great deal of attention has to be placed by suppliers on inventory management to maintain service levels, while manufacturers can reduce their inventories easily by relying increasingly on the flexibility of their suppliers.

Since the introduction of EDI, suppliers have noted significant variances of up to $\pm15\%$ between initial orders and actual quantities delivered. In many cases EDI data insufficiently reflects reality. Typical problems reported by interviewees are: lack of manufacturer's input control, double ordering, use of similar older parts or lost products. Instead of trying to make a few exact forecasts, manufacturers simply make frequent changes without considering the confusion these changes might cause for their suppliers:

Medium supplier: ‘Sometimes deliveries seem to be on their way for years because the manufacturer does not book the receipt’.

Large supplier: 'EDI reduced the planning moral of manufacturers. It makes it easy for them to constantly change their orders and difficult for us to plan our production properly. EDI makes the transmission of unnecessary information quite easy. One side has trouble to generate that information and the other side doesn't really need it'.

Supplier: ‘EDI is a world of illusion. It is useless as long as manufacturers’ production processes are not sufficiently planned and stable. If customers neglect input controls, EDI data does not reflect reality’.

Another complaint is the unreliability of production information and the large number of production changes at short notice, which makes it very difficult for suppliers to stabilise their production. The need for ‘helicopter missions’ (special flights to transport urgent parts) and the infeasibility of suppliers to stick to their own production plans illustrate that these flexible production systems do not work as well as manufacturers may claim. Sometimes chaotic situations can be observed:

Supplier: 'First we get the three month schedule, then the one month schedule, then the one week schedule, then the daily schedule, then the fax message, and finally the telephone call desperately seeking urgent delivery of the component'.

One interviewee reported that efficient communication does not necessarily mean effectiveness.

Supplier: ‘We have got larger amounts of quicker and more accurate data but not necessarily better information of what’s really going on’.

He pointed to a Japanese manufacturer who still sends orders through fax messages:

Supplier: 'We receive a few fax messages a month from a Japanese customer with all the information we need. And this is reliable information that is hardly changed afterwards'.

This indicates that the stability of underlying production processes is an important prerequisite for effective cooperation between manufacturers and suppliers.

## Dependence

As relationships in the automotive industry become closer, interdependencies become more important. All suppliers agree that they are dependent on car manufacturers who make up a large share (in some cases, nearly all) of their turnover. Depending on their importance to car manufacturers, suppliers mention differing degrees of dependency.

Small supplier of standard parts: 'We are completely dependent on manufacturers. Customers dictate what to do and force us to rationalise'.

Medium supplier: 'We are directly dependent on manufacturers. If a major customer catches a flu we start coughing'.

Large supplier: 'We are an important supplier and our customers can't switch to another supplier easily. But we can't afford to lose a car manufacturer as a customer. Therefore, we are always trying to fulfil our customers' requests but we don't want to commit ourselves too much. If the customer has got a works council meeting we don't want to interrupt our production'.

However, through the reduction of the number of direct suppliers, manufacturers also become increasingly dependent on their preferred suppliers.

Manufacturer: ‘We can’t simply switch to another supplier. A synchronous supplier is like one of our plants and very important to us. We depend on our suppliers’ know-how. Complex and innovative components are difficult to get because it takes a long time to build up a good relationship with important suppliers’.

Dramatically increased information sharing would provide manufacturers with direct influence on the suppliers' production plans, giving them increased control over their suppliers. However, suppliers are reluctant to give customers instant and direct access to their internal production system. In one case, however, a supplier provided a read only access for the manufacturer to read its own orders. Even this limited facility caused some conflict between the supplier's IS department and its production planners. As mentioned by a production planner:

Large supplier: 'We don't want to give away too much information. Nobody likes to be looked over the shoulder'.

An EDI system is designed with the expectation of meeting the manufacturer's inter-organizational needs. If the system does not solve the manufacturer's transaction cost and resource dependence problems, it is not likely to be approved. Reduction of transaction costs emphasises the efficient linkage between manufacturers' internal operations and its external communication and coordination with suppliers. But EDI can also reduce a manufacturer's resource dependence and emphasise the service of preferred suppliers so that they are locked into the system. On the other hand, suppliers can provide their customers with features that they want, or cannot do without, thus making important customers more dependent, eg sophisticated logistical services. With high dependence on the preferred supplier, the manufacturer may not be able to replace the supplier easily because of the rigidity of the inter-organizational relationship formed after the implementation of EDI. Therefore, an EDI system can increase the interdependence of both parties.

## Network structure

The automotive industry is an excellent example of the importance of inter-organizational networks. Rather than manufacture the whole car themselves and practise extensive vertical integration, car manufacturers purchase many parts from suppliers. Some of the most complex partnerships have taken place in the automotive industry where strategic alliances and partnerships are used as a means to spread the risks. Manufacturers pursue cooperative agreements to reduce uncertainty, get fast access to information, technologies and know-how, achieve reliability and responsiveness, share the risks, and achieve economies of scale in joint research and development or production (Powell, 1990). As manufacturers move towards more time critical supply chain operations and real-time planning systems, more coordination and information exchange is needed within a production network.

Such a network can only function if the suppliers meet specific organizational and structural requirements and connect their production planning system with their customers' systems. In addition to EDI implementation, the manufacturer may demand specific technical and organizational changes that affect the freedom of suppliers to make investment decisions. Forced rationalisation investments are the result of the continuation of the manufacturer's internal rationalisation of material flows across organizational boundaries.

According to manufacturers, these investments are a business requirement. But they also reduce the planning autonomy and increase the dependency of suppliers on their customers. Not only do the manufacturers prescribe the way information is exchanged but they also influence the suppliers' production by 'suggesting' internal technological and organizational adjustments. Once these initial and specific investments are made by the supplier, the manufacturer's influence shifts to the production sequencing itself. A 'production dependency' (Nagel et al, 1990) on the manufacturer is likely to evolve as it is the manufacturer which centrally coordinates the production network. As a result, deliveries are determined by the following unit in the supply chain (ie the manufacturer) and cannot be influenced by the preceding unit (the supplier). Therefore, the supplier is left with little control over its own production and is put under pressure to fulfil material call-offs even if orders are changed at short notice. This requires drastic organizational and technical adjustments to rationalise and to ensure flexibility. Especially if delivery cycles are shorter than production cycles, suppliers have to make externally determined adjustment investments. EDI is an important tool to integrate and connect production planning systems that ensure optimal subsequent processing of material call-offs.

## Conclusion

The empirical results derived from case studies in the German as well as in the British automotive industry provide insights about EDI use in two particularly advanced user communities in Europe. The level of EDI use could be identified as an important contributing factor for a number of changes that are taking place in the automotive industry. However, it has to be seen in connection with other developments that are taking place, such as demands for zero-defect quality and high reliability of production processes, global sourcing or increased competitive pressures. The results of our analysis and the effects of EDI use are summarised in Table 2.

For suppliers as well as manufacturers, efficiency gains can be achieved. At a low level of EDI use, companies benefit from faster and more secure flows of information and materials that allow them to reduce capital tied up in stocks. EDI provides up-to-the-minute information for decisions about production and deliveries. Higher levels of EDI use enable organizations to develop and rationalise their operations and achieve closer integration of production processes across organizational boundaries. EDI is a potentially significant investment to enhance competitiveness and raise profitability but also requires organizations to make significant adaptation investments.

From a power-dependency point of view, however, the supplier's decision is virtually restricted to whether or not to stay in business with the manufacturer. If the supplier decides to continue the business relationship, and in many cases suppliers have little choice but to do so, their planning autonomy in terms of investment and production is considerably influenced by the manufacturer with increasing use of EDI. Due to their size and power, manufacturers can usually get their way. It can be observed that the manufacturers' influence over suppliers increases quantitatively as well as qualitatively and that decision making is centralised and shifted to manufacturers. This creates a dilemma for the supplier: to optimise production within the network, planning is more and more synchronised with the manufacturer's production by closely integrated information flows and organizational and technical adjustments. But, due to frequent changes of call-offs, suppliers cannot achieve the desired optimisation. Instead, external planning errors are directly transferred into their own system making the supplier's production unstable and difficult to predict. As planning is outside the suppliers' influence, they have to adjust their planning frequently.

Before the introduction of EDI, rationalisation processes were internally focused and followed different patterns for manufacturers and suppliers. With the introduction of extensive information links, however, production structures and sequences are increasingly integrated. Within the emerging production networks the suppliers' decisions are more and more replaced by systemic requirements that restrain their entrepreneurial autonomy. Car manufacturers give detailed instructions for rationalisation and the introduction of process and production technologies to their suppliers who are included in their 'systematic rationalisation' strategies. Such a broad rationalisation and structural change is enabled by EDI which provides control and domination of material and information flows across organizational boundaries, thus extending the existing economic dependence and mediating additional organizational and informational dependencies for the supplier.

However, if manufacturers still try to optimise their own production at the expense of their suppliers, this may have a negative impact on cooperation with suppliers, perhaps preventing long-term ‘true’ partnerships. Possible reactions suggested by suppliers could include delaying deliveries which would slow down the manufacturer’s production and reducing support when the manufacturer is in trouble and needs extra parts. Ultimately, if an important systems supplier terminates a business relationship, it may be very difficult to replace this supplier.
