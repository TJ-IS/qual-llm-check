# The formation of a virtual global bank

AG Lockett $^{1}$ and CP Holland $^{2}$

$^{1}$ School of Business and Economic Studies, University of Leeds, Leeds LS2 9JT; and $^{2}$ Manchester Business School, Manchester University, Manchester M15 6PB, UK

Banks are undergoing dramatic changes to their organization structures, operations and information systems. The banking market is also developing because information technology (IT) is enabling the banks' business customers to create new forms of organization and trade, and there is a clear trend towards the globalisation of markets and competition. Banks are responding in a variety of ways, each of which is designed to increase their ability to act internationally and more quickly. In this paper the international payments and foreign exchange markets are analysed and a case study of an international bank is presented. The traditional methods of foreign exchange dealing and subsequent payment transfer using the Society for World-wide International Funds Transfer (SWIFT) are coming under close scrutiny, and new and better ways of operating are being devised. This paper describes one approach to redesigning the international payments process. It describes the partnership strategy which is being implemented by a large international bank in cooperation with a number of regional banks, resulting in the creation of a virtual global bank. An outline is given of the evolution of the strategy, showing how a vision of the way transactions should be carried out has enabled the bank to combine its various internal strengths and develop its market network. The virtual bank system and its implementation are presented, and issues of organizational and market network change are considered. Finally the results are compared with network theory and the inter-relationships between strategy, network structure and IT are analysed.

## Introduction

The broad trends of globalisation of markets and competition can be observed in most industries, although the specific impact on particular organizations is difficult to predict, and even within the same industry sector, there are large variations. The globalisation of markets is leading to novel strategies and structures in both manufacturing and service based industries (Dicken, 1992). For example Ford have recently announced a bold restructuring of its operations which combines its North American and European arms to create a global automotive division which is charged with creating global cars that will sell in all the world's major markets with only minor local modification (Treece et al, 1995). In the service industry professional organizations such as accountancy companies and advertising agencies have also reorganized themselves so as to be able to offer their services on a global scale in order to reflect the outlook of their business customers (Morgan, 1992).

The global innovators were mainly large companies in capital intensive industries such as automotive, electronics and airline industries. Companies such as Motorola and Ford have used their internal resources to establish a geographic presence on a worldwide scale. For smaller companies this is not viable in a short time scale and alternative strategies have been developed such as strategic alliances with competitors. An example of horizontal integration to achieve global reach, as an alternative to expanding their own operations, can be seen in the airline industry where code sharing between separate computer systems of different airline companies allows a passenger to travel around the world using several carriers with just one ticket (eg KLM's strategic alliance with Northwest Airlines, Toy et al, 1995).

## Globalisation of markets

A cursory examination of recent research into globalisation in both the academic literature and contemporary business journals reveals that it is a complex phenomenon which is having a dramatic effect on product-markets, strategies and organizational forms (eg see Clemons & Row, 1992; Drucker, 1988; Hamel & Prahalad, 1989; Holland et al, 1994; Ohmae, 1989). For most companies a national focus for managing is no longer appropriate because the combined effects of deregulation, improved transportation, globalisation of market demand, and the availability of cheap and powerful information systems (IS) mean that the relative advantages of an organization with a global reach and outlook far outweigh those of a company with a national or regional presence. However the problem of determining the appropriate strategy in response to these external pressures is complex and there is no single solution. This is evident by the wide range of strategies being pursued by close competitors in the same industries, and by the debate in the academic literature concerned with the issues of strategy choice and implementation in response to the pressures of globalisation. Even on what one might assume to be fairly straightforward issues, such as to what extent are global markets homogeneous, there is considerable debate and confusion (eg see Ohmae, 1989 for an alternative strategy perspective on the globalisation of markets and Treece et al, 1995 for a discussion of widely varying global business strategies by the large automotive companies).

The research problem of interest is to understand and explain the formation of a particular type of market network to support international payments. The most useful literature concerned with market networks and the impacts of information technology (IT) is in the strategy, marketing and IT subject disciplines. In his seminal article, Levitt (1983) argued that national markets are becoming more homogeneous, which has the effect of creating global markets and that companies should therefore organize themselves to take advantage of global economies of scale by developing manufacturing, distribution and marketing activities on a worldwide scale. This basic model of global markets has been tempered by the need to recognise local differences in tastes, culture, climate, environment and other factors which are often specific to a geographic region. For example, Ohmae (1989) argues that fast information flows through telecommunications links, makes geographic barriers irrelevant and therefore creates a borderless world for competition. However he explicitly recognises the need for local optimisation and it is not possible to simply average the requirements from different markets to specify one global product. This issue is of paramount concern to most companies because it creates a fundamental tension between the achievement of global economies of scale and central coordination, and the requirements for local innovation and adaptation in order to meet the specific needs of the local market. However, for international payments the problem is a lot simpler than in say manufacturing or retail industries because the basic product, cash, is totally homogeneous throughout the world. One would therefore expect very efficient systems for managing international cash flows for both retail and business customers. However experience in the European Union (EU) shows that it is common for international payments between member countries to take an average of 10 days to be fully processed. Related to the globalisation of product-markets organizations are restructuring both their internal operations and their external market networks to achieve some form of global coordination with suppliers and customers.

## Market networks and IT

An important development in industrial marketing is to conceptualise markets as networks of interconnected organizations where the relationships between separate organizations and the structure of the market network are recognised as key properties of the market (Ford et al, 1986). This network view of markets is fundamentally different from the atomistic view principally represented by economists and also some strategists (eg Williamson, 1991; Porter, 1979). The network perspective has been applied successfully at different organizational and geographic units of analysis and also has the advantage of being intuitively correct to managers. For example Cunningham and Tynan (1993) investigated the impact of electronic trading on buyer–supplier relationships in a retail supply chain, and Ghoshal and Bartlett (1990) employ the network paradigm to model the multinational corporation. Describing the organization of economic activity both within the firm and between firms as networks appears to have both face validity and to provide a method for modelling and comparing different economic systems. However the interesting and difficult questions are concerned with explaining why market networks develop into particular structures and what is the nature of the individual relationships? Globalisation in whatever manifestation is clearly one important variable among several which determine and influence the shape of market networks.

Based largely on transaction cost economics Malone et al (1987; 1989) proposed that the principal effect of IT applied to inter-organizational processes would be to make hierarchical style structures (including close relationships such as sole-supply arrangements) comparatively less attractive than more market style relationships. The basic logic is that the search and evaluation costs associated with managing multiple suppliers and multiple customers would decrease dramatically and combined with the market advantages of lower production costs the overall outcome would be proportionately more use of markets than hierarchies. However research conducted using the network paradigm (eg Lorenzoni & Baden-Fuller, 1993; Holland & Lockett, 1993; Bahrami, 1992) and operations management research (see Bakos & Brynjolfsson, 1993), question the results derived from simple transaction cost economics and instead identify the emergence of much more integrated and stable network structures. (This is not a criticism of transaction cost economics per se but an observation that the results from its theoretical use do not correspond with empirical evidence.) These stable structures are based on an entirely different strategic logic, specifically one in which the quality of the final product, trust between organizations and a history of long-term relationships, and an ability to adapt in a coordinated manner to market changes, are the key factors in determining the formation of the market network. In the next section the characteristics of the banking industry are outlined before presenting the case data.

## Banking industry

Banking is an information intensive industry and should therefore be at the forefront of the application of IT. In some companies such as Citibank this is the case (Holland et al, 1994) but most banking systems are still concerned with the basic simple data handling of transactions rather than the leading edge use of information in a strategic manner. One possible explanation for this apparent lack of innovation may be the amount of resources allocated to modernising legacy systems such as the investment of £500 million by National Westminster bank into its basic IT infrastructure (Clemons & Weber, 1992). Another viewpoint is that the banking industry has enjoyed protection from open competition by national regulatory regimes. However recent innovations in retail banking such as telephone-based home banking, internet marketing supported by electronic retail payments and novel use of mixed technologies such as satellite and terrestrial networks suggest that European banks are beginning to exploit the competitive potential of IT. When one looks at other industries the literature has suggested that the most effective way to reap the benefits of IT is to take a more outward looking approach. Cash and Konsynski (1985) outlined how IT could redraw competitive boundaries. Other authors discussed the creation of inter-organizational IS as a competitive weapon (eg see Johnson & Vitale, 1988; Malone & Rockart, 1992; Holland et al, 1992). A novel case if IT cooperation has been documented by Clemons et al (1992), in which a group of travel agents combine their IS to form a virtual global corporation. However most of the case examples which are discussed do not heavily involve the banking industry. This is now beginning to change and this paper reports one detailed case which shows the releasing of the potential of the technology by new forms of working, both internal and external to the organization. The project involves partnerships between Barclays bank and a series of smaller banks across the world. The paper will outline the history of the partners and the associated IT systems, and how they are now being combined for their mutual benefit. In the next section an overview of the international payments and foreign exchange processes is presented. This will be followed by a detailed description of the case.

## International payments and foreign exchange problems in banking

The problem of trading in foreign currencies has been with businesses for many years and banks introduced systems to streamline the process. This led to the growth in the use of correspondent banks, where individual banks in various countries hold correspondent accounts with banks in other countries which enables them to transfer foreign currency relatively easily. Although this approach is now well developed, it is fairly costly and can take more than 10 days to transfer the money. Moving foreign currency around the world can still take many days, even though banking is supposed to be electronic. Some organizations are large enough to set up their own global systems (Holland et al, 1994), but for most companies this is not viable and the traditional methods are still very slow. Therefore the problems associated with foreign exchange and international payments still remain. This can be costly because of the need to hold balances in foreign currencies, manage the exposure of funds, handle the credit risk and manage operational relationships with the correspondent banks. The banks' customers are looking for a much better service, as international trading increases, especially in what were the more remote parts of the world.

For the large international bank this may mean them implementing their own global system. This can be very costly, especially maintaining the numerous number of correspondent relationships that may be necessary. It needs access to a large customer base to provide a scale payment processor and a dealing room with adequate liquidity to make it economic. This is the approach being taken by some of the large banks, which are mainly concerned with the major corporate market. They are implementing very detailed systems for handling the most frequently used currencies, but they still find it a costly exercise to deal in their more exotic currencies. In an effort to compete but using less resources, some of the smaller national banks are joining forces on a regional basis and sharing facilities (eg see the Economist Intelligence Unit's report 1994 for a critical review of IBOS). IBOS is in essence a form of cooperative where the expectation is that each bank will be the primary actor in its own particular region. The sharing of costs will be mutually beneficial for all the parties, and such a strategic alliance will lead to other forms of business relationship. This case describes a different approach, where a large bank is creating partnerships with smaller and more localised banks. They can make use of Barclays' global systems, yet give their own stamp on dealing with their own local customers. They are able to act as a global bank, ie acting as a virtual global organization while retaining their individual identity in their regional marketplace. The overall system has implications for all the partners, financially, organizationally, and strategically (see Harrigan & Newman, 1990; and Kanter, 1989 for an organizational perspective of strategic alliances). These issues will be discussed in this paper, following a detailed history of the creation of the current networks.

## Barclays system development

There are two main components involved in the international payments system; Multi-Currency Payments System (MCPS) and a treasury system called TAG. The two systems have very different development paths. For nearly 10 years Barclays has been implementing global systems. This comes from two distinct sources of pressure. The first one is the history of the bank, resulting from its position as a leading British bank which was well represented throughout the developing world. A legacy of its history is that Barclays' range of trading currencies is probably unmatched. The second source of pressure is the stated ambition of the bank to become an even larger international player. These two combine to make it almost imperative that the bank is a key player in this new application of technology. The two components will be initially described separately, and the steps leading to their integration will be analysed.

## The international payments system - MCPS

There have been a number of attempts to provide a truly electronic payments system. It is ‘obvious’ to most people concerned with the banking industry that it is a logical outcome of the use of IT to streamline the process. This is the situation in many banks but most of them have found it difficult to implement their ideas. It is only the larger institutions which have been able to have enough resources to develop the necessary IS and cope with the concomitant organizational change. Barclays are probably no exception, and have been experimenting in this area for a long period. There have been two previous major attempts at developing an international electronic payments capability. Both of these were internally driven, with most of the system development work being done in-house. Considerable sums of money were spent on systems which were apparent failures in that they were not eventually implemented. It is not the purpose of this paper to review the reasons for the lack of application, but to look at the more recent success. It would probably be almost impossible to give a true picture of the events, given the changing nature of the personnel concerned and the technological advances that have taken place. However what is quite clear is that the organization learnt from its earlier attempts at solving the problem and that all the effort was not wasted. The first system, started in 1984, that took on a life of its own was called the New Payments System. It was designed by the bank using their own internal systems development staff. However although the concept behind it may have been sound, its realisation was fraught with difficulties.

The system grew like an octopus and had to be scrapped in 1989. (Senior Systems Manager, Barclays 1994)

The organization did not give up on its objective and looked at other ways of solving the problem. When it was originally looking at potential solutions, Barclays UK considered an American system called AFTS (Automatic Funds Transfer System), but discounted it because it was only a single currency system. However the Barclays New York division bought AFTS because they mainly dealt in dollars and it coped easily with their payments. AFTS worked very well but lay dormant as far as the centre of the bank was concerned. During the same period the sterling system in the UK was seen to need upgrading. AFTS brought the core functionality to cope with the bank's customers' needs and could handle the high volume with appropriate resilience. It was therefore customised and implemented in a short lead-time to deal with sterling transactions.

After implementing AFTS in the UK, the bank also bought another completely separate system for dealing with queries relating to international payments. It is a US system which is used by the majority of the top US banks. The package allows the bank to pull all its databases into one place so that they can be interrogated without having to go into active systems and thus be disruptive. It has been extremely successful and what once could take over an hour to sort out can now take less than a minute. The overall result is a much more efficient system for dealing with queries, a far less costly system, and a greatly enhanced customer service. One important consequence was that it showed that the information contained in their processing systems could be accessed and interrogated without the need for complicated system development. IS success had been shown to be possible in a very short time period with major organizational benefit and without operational disruption. These changes were taking place during the next attack on the international payments problem and their impact on the thinking of the organization was important. It led them to a view that the best strategy would be to take AFTS and use its excellent functionality as a basis for further development and adapt it to handle multiple currencies. During this phase the company who developed AFTS had gone into liquidation and Barclays, NY had become the sole owner of the software. Therefore Barclays now had a good internal system which had to be developed themselves if it was to give them the ability to deal in a variety of currencies. This was the chosen course of action, a middle ground between purchasing a proprietary product and developing something which they had great trouble in achieving during the last few years. The result was a new system that was delivered in about a year and which covered all the important requirements that had been specified by the businesses. The final system allowed the bank to coordinate a variety of existing systems within a single framework, and is shown in the Figures 1 and 2 which were used on a promotion exercise.

In essence AFTS allows the integration of a number of operating systems. Some of these are legacy systems and will probably be updated, but their age is no longer a huge barrier to overall system flexibility. There have been much fewer difficulties in introducing the new way of working and the project implementation went very smoothly, given the enormous size of the everyday data handling problem. By using AFTS they can now aim to maximise the amount of automatic straight-through processing:

Electronically received payments will be processed without the need for any manual intervention. (Barclays Global Payments Manager, 1994).

The internal success of the project suggested that they could make use of the product in the marketplace by offering to handle multi-currency payments for other organizations, both corporations and medium sized banks.

## Foreign currency dealing system - TAG

BZW, Barclays investment bank, are also one of the leading foreign currency dealers in the world. They have dealing operations in London, New York and Tokyo with their headquarters in London. BZW are regarded as one of the top organizations in this area of banking. While most of the dealing is done in the 20 or so most frequently used currencies, BZW actively offer prices in over 80 currencies. However as trade has become ever more international and most companies have to export to and import from a growing number of currencies, the need to exchange relatively small amounts of currency in what may be exotic denominations has grown steadily. For this type of foreign exchange deal the profitability is low and risky and the foreign exchange dealing rooms are not set up for this small scale and intermittent type of request. This has been picked up by nearly all the major dealers, and there are now a series of products that are offered to help businesses to cope with this problem. Barclays (BZW) have developed a system called TAG, which allows users to directly carry out foreign exchange deals via a simple computer terminal. It contains features which are too numerous to mention in this article, and they will be discussed in a forthcoming paper. Here only a brief overview will be given so that the broader case characteristics can be put in context. A summary of the attributes of TAG is given in Figure 3.

It has SPOT and Fixed Forward currency contracts at the core of the service, but it allows for many more types of transaction. Customers are given direct access to the system through a PC, and once a company or bank has registered an account they are then free to deal. It has the speed and accuracy necessary for precise foreign exchange transactions with the flexibility and convenience to cope with any kind of settlement requirements. It currently trades in over 80 currencies, and is one of the leading systems of this type, is very easy to use, and is well liked by the user community:-

The automated system is beautiful and response time is great. For small amounts TAG stops the need of using up favours. You don't need phone calls and there is automated confirmation. It is just a whole lot cleaner and smoother. (Signet Bank, 1994).

## The combined system - TAG and MCPS

The approaches to the two separate problems of payments and foreign exchange took place in parallel in different parts of the organization. Although they had aspects in common, they were originally focused on different audiences, MCPS as a payments system for the bank, and TAG for corporate customers for the specialised foreign exchange service. The potential for synergy between the two systems had been appreciated but not realised. The bank next decided that it would offer its MCPS system to other smaller banks in partnership. It had undertaken a large amount of market research on the use of international payments and had isolated regional banks as being in need of their services. A trial campaign to develop such relationships was undertaken and a series of banks began to make use of MCPS. There was a close working between the partners which allowed some of the initial teething troubles to be quickly ironed out, and trust to grow between the various actors (Smith Ring & Van de Ven, 1994). For both of the systems there was success. In the case of TAG it was mainly with their large company treasury clients, and for MCPS it was mainly in partnership with other banks. It was due to the reaction in the market place that the integrated system became a live issue. Bank clients who began to use MCPS also found out about TAG and similarly clients who used TAG then needed to make an international payment. The two systems fit neatly together as far as some of the customers are concerned, and their combined appeal is greater than the sum of the two separate parts, especially to other banks. It is this part of the market that is under discussion here.

The bank originally discussed MCPS with TAG being mentioned in passing. But the response from the banks was very encouraging. Many of them were having great difficulty in handling small amounts of foreign currency, especially the more exotic ones. Through the use of TAG they are able to offer their business customers a much wider set of currencies with which they can get an instant foreign exchange rate, receive confirmation of the rates and set the payments systems in motion. They are able to offer their customers via MCPS access to a large number of foreign banks across the world without having the cost and problem of maintaining a whole host of unprofitable correspondent bank relationships. In essence medium sized banks can appear to their customers as a global bank by hooking into the systems and business networks of Barclays. It was expected that there would be resistance in customer banks from the currency dealing room. However, once it was installed for foreign exchange and third-party payments it was found to be extremely useful for inter-bank dealing. It enables them to gain access to comparable market rates to phone dealing direct with the London market. This outcome was an unexpected bonus for both parties. The partnership with Barclays enables them to act as a virtual global bank. They can offer a much enhanced and more attractive service to their customers, concentrating on satisfying their needs, while Barclays obtain increased revenue from a much greater throughput of their international payments system. From each of their perspectives the relationship is mutually beneficial. The systems are very flexible, and the banks can operate a variety of methods at the same time. This is illustrated in Figure 4 which shows the main information and decision routes along which currency and payments travel. A bank may use all or part or none of the system, depending on the characteristics of a particular transaction. This is not a partnership which excludes other ways of working, but relies on a trust relationship between the actors.

## Organizational adaptation

The organizations described in this paper are in the throes of tremendous organizational change. Banks are having to adapt to an increasingly competitive environment which is being altered by the development of the power of information and communication technologies. Because of their information intense nature, banks will be affected more than most other types of commerce. This paper has described one attempt to use the power of IT by a series of organizations in partnership. The history of the project clearly indicates organizational learning and adaptation are at least as important as any of the technical aspects of the problem. Barclays succeeded when they used their undoubted strengths in banking, rather than simply their IT skills. They customised packages in a way that enabled them to deliver a product in a short time and of such a quality to be able to market it.

The new international payments system is an integration of their internally developed and well tried separate information systems. Although the strategy has been unclear at times, systems often take a long time to evolve and the apparent failures are usually not wasted. In many cases although the concept may be excellent, a combination of organizational resistance and technical complication leads to slow or non implementation. Even with IS, it is very important that there is a coincidence of events as well as strategic thinking. The international bank in this study has had a vision of where the future of payments systems should lie. By cooperating with both technical organizations and other banks, they have been able to produce a system that provides mutual benefit for the partners. In the case of Barclays they get access to the payments and foreign exchange of a large number of medium sized corporate customers which would be difficult to obtain otherwise. In essence it allows them to market their IT products on a virtual basis to a whole range of new customers. For the partner banks it has a number of novel advantages. For many of them they are competing with much larger national banks which have much greater resources. The access to Barclays' systems, both TAG and MCPS, allows them to offer their customers a much enhanced service without the need to invest a large amount of management time or resources. They are now seen to be much more of an international bank which can provide these types of service for their regional customers. It allows them to compete with much larger banks in their own country. For countries to which they trade on a very regular basis, they may decide to keep a correspondent relationship, but for the infrequent trade, use of the MCPS is obviously advantageous.

There are significant organizational and strategic implications for Barclays and their partners. For the regional banks it means becoming partly dependent on a larger partner for foreign exchange deals and international payments and involves a much enhanced level of trust. However, given the developments in IT it is highly likely that the risks are not huge. For Barclays it implies that they will not attempt to compete in these regions for a certain group of customers. They will also have to commit themselves to providing these services for a considerable period. In order to keep the trust in the partnership they will have to continue to develop the products in line with the competition. They have become an IT service provider. The internal organizational implications for Barclays and their customers are similar. The cooperation between the foreign exchange and payments departments will have to become even greater, and the dividing line between the functional boundaries may well blur. There is common information between the two departments, and the need to make paperless transactions will grow. For the organizations concerned it is the beginning of a long-term relationship where many other forms of cooperation may develop.

## Comparison of virtual bank with network theory

The network organization is now established as a generic concept in the management and IT literature (eg see Nohria & Eccles, 1992; Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1990; Hakansson & Johanson, 1993; Snehota, 1993) but there are still relatively few empirical analyses of their formation, structure and use of IT. One would expect the banking industry to be at the forefront of the network organization because its products are information based and therefore have the potential to be revolutionised by the novel application of IT systems to redesign banking processes and products. However in practice the banking industry as a whole has evolved fairly slowly when compared to say manufacturing or retailing. However there are some significant exceptions, for example First Chicago's Windows 2000 cash management, Motorola's global netting system and Midland Bank's First Direct retail banking product which provide examples of innovation which indicate a step change is about to occur in banking as a whole.

In the payments and foreign exchange markets, Barclays' strategy takes into account that as innovation is introduced by the leaders the profit margins will inevitably fall. Given Barclays' huge investment in internal IT systems the only way to protect profit is to expand the volume of payments processed, which increases the revenue at only a marginal cost. Barclays' history of international market networks and IT systems combined with its experience and knowledge of foreign exchange and payments have led it to use inter-organizational systems (IOS) to link into small and medium sized banks who do not compete directly with it, but who require access to an integrated global payments system. This novel application of IOS to create a virtual global bank is compared with relevant network theory from the IT, marketing and strategy literature.

The virtual global bank form of organization is obviously similar to documented examples of network organizations but it has several important features concerned principally with the structure of the network, strategy implementation through structural change and the associated use of IS which make it theoretically important. Two major structural changes have occurred to create the virtual global bank. These are: (1) a reduction in the number of correspondent relationships; and (2) the formation of new relationships with small regional banks. These structural changes are similar in concept to other well documented cases such as Baxter (eg see Short & Venkatraman, 1992; Johnston & Lawrence, 1988) but it is occurring on a truly global scale and with two types of structural change being implemented simultaneously.

The first type of change is a reduction in the number of correspondent relationships which is equivalent to the reduction in the number of suppliers evident in manufacturing industries (eg see Bakos & Brynjolfsson, 1993). The reason for the reduction in banking is to make it possible to offer guaranteed service levels and prices to corporate customers. In network terms the efficiency of the correspondent network has been increased by decreasing the redundancy of the correspondent, that is, where more than one correspondent relationship existed for a particular country.

The second change is to form relationships with small regional banks through IOS. The strategic rationale is to increase the volume of payments while only incurring marginal extra costs. The traditional strategy to increase the level of business is to set up more correspondent relationships with the expectations of reciprocal trading but it has been shown that this strategy is no longer viable because it is too costly to maintain a large number of redundant correspondent relationships. In network terms Barclays has used IOS to connect to small regional banks and leverage Barclays' existing investment in its correspondent network, IS and staff. The small banks can then offer a global service to their own customers who need not be aware that it is actually Barclays who are supplying the service. The connection between Barclays' correspondent network and the customer networks of small regional banks is an empirical example of the theoretical concept termed structural holes (Burt, 1992), ie it has established a set of non-redundant relationships between its own global correspondent network and the trading networks of small regional banks to improve the performance of Barclays' payments and foreign exchange systems.

The correspondent network is very similar to the strategic web concept proposed by Lorenzoni and Baden-Fuller (1993) but the inclusion of customer banks and their own corporate customers makes the virtual bank structure more complex than a strategic web. A strategic centre coordinates a web of partners to market a final product and takes advantage of the flexibility afforded by a web of partners to maintain innovation in design and competition to control costs whilst ensuring continuity through a relationship mode of operation rather than using an adversarial strategy to deal with trading partners. The virtual bank concept is similar to the strategic web concept because Barclays are the strategic centre and coordinate their web of correspondent relationships to form a global market network for cash disbursements and foreign exchange. However there are some important differences. In the strategic web concept the end product in all of their examples is a physical tangible product and the product flows are in one direction. In the virtual bank example the network itself constitutes part of the banking product. The small regional banks who connect to Barclays are actually buying the market network capability of Barclays through an IOS. This means that any small bank can have global capability which it can sell to corporate customers under its own name – Barclays' customers are therefore a much more integral part of the strategic web than the customers of say Apple or Toyota. (There is also a further complexity because some of the correspondent banks are also in turn customers of the virtual bank.)

The connection to small banks who are not direct competitors of Barclays exploits the capabilities of Barclays' network to give the small bank global payments and foreign exchange capabilities. This use of IT makes it possible to leverage Barclays' correspondent network and effectively replicate it for small banks. This application of IOS has a greater effect on the structure and operation of the network than in manufacturing supply chains where the IOS impact is typically to improve the effectiveness of dyadic relationships or relatively simple supply chains. Based on this case study, it seems that the overall effect of IOS on network structure in the banking industry may be even more dramatic than in less information intensive industries because the structure of market networks between banks can be changed extremely quickly simply by exchanging data on a global scale.

In summary it has been shown that changes to the competitive strategy have been implemented through structural changes to the market network. The impact of IOS on network structure has been contingent on the competitive strategy of the bank and the market environment. This is evident by a reduction in the number of correspondent relationships occurring in parallel with the formation of a new customer network of regional banks. The inherent complexity of such structural changes in the same company suggests that simple theoretical IT impact models which predict a move towards different forms of industry structure are inadequate (eg see Holland et al, 1992; Malone & Rockart, 1992; Clemons & Row, 1992). The virtual bank concept to organize international payments and foreign exchange is just one approach to this market and other organizations such as IBOS, Citibank and Microsoft are pursuing fundamentally different strategies and market network structures which are discussed in detail in a further paper (Holland et al, 1996).

## Conclusions

This paper has presented an outline of a novel form of inter-organizational IS between two kinds of bank. Its success is dependent on the cooperation between departments within the larger bank as well as that between the banks. The two distinct businesses, ie the investment bank and the commercial bank's payments processing operation, have used their core strengths to maximise the

benefits to the end customer. The result is one approach which allows Barclays to interact more easily with customers' foreign exchange and payments departments. The combined system allows the smaller partner to become a global bank in the eyes of its customers without increasing its IT systems. It becomes a virtual global bank. Although potentially of great importance to both the partners in the alliance, its technical attributes are not the vital ingredient which has led to the success, rather it is the vision which has enabled all the parties in the agreement to evolve strategies which are mutually beneficial. It is important for organizations to have personnel who can adapt quickly to the new form of business partnerships that are being created. Inter-organizational IS have an important part to play in this kind of strategic alliance. The use of systems in a cooperative fashion is still in its infancy, but as organizations' experiences in this area increase it is likely that their use will grow. Many organizations have both complex systems and large databases whose potential remains largely untapped. Their construction will still be a technical exercise, but their evolution and exploitation will be dependent on business vision and the growth of cooperation which enhances competitive ability. In the future, IS will have an even more enabling role in the formation of business relationships and the development of complex, global network structures.
