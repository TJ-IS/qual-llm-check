# A critical review of research on electronic mail

I.A. Rudy

University of Cambridge, The Judge Institute of Management Studies, Trumpington Street, Cambridge CB2 1AG, UK

This paper reviews the literature on email (including computer conferencing) and discusses the most important current issues in the field. Though it will be of interest to researchers working specifically in the email field, it is intended mainly as a summary for IS researchers in other areas. Email research is part of a wider field, computer-mediated communication (CMC), which includes some areas not covered here, such as the Internet, video conferencing and EDI. Most research on email has concerned itself with the issues of media choice and media effects. Despite a great deal of published work though, the field still has an unsatisfactory, piecemeal feel to it. Much of the work that has been published is unsatisfactory in being based upon unrealistic, laboratory-like simulations, and a positivist epistemology. Most of the work concerns the use of email and the effects of using email on individuals and groups; little has been done on the effects at an organizational level. The paper concludes with some suggestions for future avenues of research.

## Introduction

This paper reviews the literature on email research and discusses the most important current issues in the field. It includes some coverage of the wider field of computer-mediated communication (CMC) within organizations, but does not cover topics such as the Internet, video conferencing or electronic data interchange (EDI). It is aimed principally at those not working in the field who nonetheless wish to be aware of advances in it. A substantial review provided for such an audience has been notably absent since those of Steinfield (1986) and Culnan and Markus (1987). The paper will also be of interest to a second audience, those with a direct interest in computer-mediated communication, both as a critical summary of past research and as a discussion of important issues that are as yet unresolved. For this second audience, the most recent substantial literature review of research is Rice (1991).

The use of computer-mediated communication has grown markedly within the past twenty years. The growth has been driven mainly by two factors: the increase in the number of desktop computers and the increase in the number of data transmission links able in principle to connect them together. These two factors have allowed the development of communication protocols (such as TCP/IP), essentially defining a language with which two computers can 'talk' to each other. This in turn has allowed the development of software which conforms to the protocols (such as electronic mail, ftp, Usenet News and World Wide Web software) allow individuals to exchange that messages and access huge amounts of data far more easily than in the past.

CMC in its broadest sense covers any kind of human communication involving the transmission of electronic signals between computers. The focus of this paper is email (including computer conferencing). CMC also includes the Internet and the software tools to navigate it, as well as video conferencing, voice mail, (EDI) and other areas. These topics are not discussed here in any great depth; a good starting point for reviews and sources of information on them is John December's (1994–) archive. Around the field of CMC are a number of closely related fields which share some common ground with it. These include computer-supported cooperative work, group decision support systems, and communication standards (e.g. ISDN).

The major features of email are apparent to anyone who has used it. They include:

\- discussion can occur asynchronously: sender and receiver do not have to be present simultaneously;

\- data can be shared more easily, as individuals can be sent data more rapidly than via ordinary postal mail, and messages or data in electronic form can be copied easily to many individuals;

\- individuals have time to think before replying to a message;

\- it is unintrusive compared with face-to-face conversations or the telephone: the receiver of messages can choose when to read them;

\- a possible drop in face-to-face contact and building of relationships and consensus;

\- possible information overload;

\- the extra time required to type in and read messages compared with face-to-face and telephone conversation.

A large number of studies have addressed the issue of what factors tend to lead to the successful implementation and use of a new CMC or groupware system. A distillation of the original studies can be produced, but this is given in the Appendix rather than here, since many of the points are intuitively reasonable and probably not of more than passing interest to most readers.

It can be a surprise to individuals new to email research (and to CMC research more generally) that a considerable amount of work has already been done in the field, as is demonstrated by Rudy's Bibliography of Organizational CMC (Rudy, 1994–). (Organizational CMC covers the topics discussed in this paper, as well as voice mail and video conferencing; it excludes inter-organizational topics such as the Internet.) However, a problem in the past has been the huge range of journals in which this work has been published. It is therefore encouraging, and a sign of the maturing of the field, to see the recent establishment of a specialist journal in this area (Journal of Computer-mediated Communication, 1994–). Appropriately, this is an electronic journal, though with a rigorous refereeing process to ensure academic quality.

The structure of this paper is summarised in Table 1. Despite the existence of a substantial body of work on email, an attempt to review and categorise it is hampered by the rather piecemeal nature of the field. There has been a general lack of theoretical grounding (Steinfield & Fulk, 1990), and many of the theories that do exist are far from being conclusively supported or refuted. In the face of this difficulty, the two main sections in this paper categorise the bulk of the research into media choice (how individuals choose a medium for a particular message) and media effects (what effects different media have once they have been selected for a message). Clearly some issues cut across this categorisation, as media choice depends to some extent on perceptions of media effects. After these sections, the paper discusses the methods used in email research, including the issue of ethnographic/interpretive studies versus laboratory/positivist studies. There then follows a discussion of some neglected but arguably interesting avenues of research. The final section draws conclusions and makes suggestions for some issues that future research on email should address.

One topic that the reader should particularly bear in mind when reading the next two sections is contextualism, which in this paper is discussed in the section on research methodologies. A characteristic of much past email research is that it implicitly assumed that general rules about email could be found from studying just one or two systems. This characteristic will be seen in the research discussed below on the theories of information richness, social presence and reduced social cues. A major limitation of this research is that it neglects how the use and effects of email depend on the context into which it is introduced.

## Media choice

Media choice is, quite simply, the issue of how users choose a medium to send a message when more than one is available to them.

A newcomer to the email field would probably expect efficiency (in the sense of time required by the sender to compose the message, and the transmission time) to be a candidate to explain media choice. However, in past email research, efficiency has not received much attention, and where it has been considered (Lea, 1991) was concluded to be unimportant in media choices. Indeed one could be forgiven for thinking that many researchers think it is of negligible importance in media choice. I would argue that while efficiency is far from being the only important criterion, it is often one of the important ones, being present 'in the background' before other (perhaps more interesting) factors come into play. For example, one would seldom consider a trip across the Atlantic to discuss an issue if it were possible to communicate acceptably well by telephone or email, simply because of the time and expense involved. If this point is accepted, the work described below then becomes a search for other important criteria to complement efficiency rather than to replace it.

## Information richness

One of the most studied and influential theories of media choice is that of information richness, developed by Daft and Lengel (1984, 1986). They defined information richness as the ability of a medium to facilitate shared meaning or convey information, and proposed that media can be ranked in ascending order of information richness, independently of situation or context. Their original ranking was unaddressed documents, written addressed documents, telephone and face to face; subsequent researchers have extended this to include email. The ranking is made on the basis of a blend of four criteria:

feedback richer media have instant feedback, allowing a more interactive discussion;

multiple cues richer media have more cues, such as words, voice inflection and body gestures;

language variety richer media are able to cope with a wider variety of language and hence are better at conveying meaning, for example natural language can convey a broader set of ideas and concepts than, say, numbers;

personal focus richer media allow personal feelings and emotions to be conveyed.

The theory of media choice that Daft and Lengel developed predicted that good managers would choose a medium for a particular message on the basis of the equivocality of the message/situation. In this context the term equivocality has a precise meaning, and is distinguished from uncertainty. Uncertainty means not having the data you need to answer particular question(s); equivocality means not knowing which questions to ask. It is often argued that upper managers tend to deal with situations of high equivocality, whereas people lower in the organization tend to deal with situations of high uncertainty. Daft and Lengel's theory predicted that good managers would choose an information-rich medium for messages/situations of high equivocality (where a medium of low richness would not be capable of conveying the necessary information), and an information-poor medium for messages/situations of low equivocality (where a medium of low richness is quite capable of conveying the information required, and to use an information-rich medium would be overkill).

Support for this theory came through a study of managers in a large petrochemical company (Daft et al., 1987). Here, a number of incidents of managerial communication were generated from interviews, and were rated on an equivocality scale of 1 (low) to 5 (high) by an ‘expert panel’ of judges. The incidents were then presented to a different sample of managers, who were asked which media they would use to communicate in each incident. There was statistically significant support for the hypothesis that managers prefer information-rich media for incidents involving high equivocality, and information-poor media for incidents involving low equivocality. Furthermore, managers who were rated by the company as particularly good displayed this tendency to a greater extent. Similar findings have been obtained by others (for example Trevino et al., 1990b). However, there is also conflicting evidence for and against the theory provided by studies of whether email is used less by managers than secretaries (since, it is argued, the former ought to be dealing more often with situations of high equivocality than the latter). Consistent with the theory are the findings of Rice and Shook (1988), who found that secretaries in a particular company used email more than managers. Counter-findings are provided by Markus (1978) and Rice et al. (1989), where the reverse was true. As noted by one of the anonymous referees, there is room to doubt that the portions of equivocality and uncertainty differ much between managers and lower level staff. This might explain the contradictory findings. Studies such as Orr (1991) show how relatively low status engineers have essential skills and procedures that their managers are unaware of. However, lower level staff are not involved in, for example, strategy formulation, and it is probably this kind of 'rich' situation that media richness theory should be being tested on.

Markus's (1994b) paper seems to conclude that media-richness theory is not a good model. Instead, the individuals studied chose media on the basis of being seen to be ‘behaving appropriately’, that is, conforming to a set of social norms established within the organization. While this does strike a blow, it does not prove that information richness theory is useless: it could still provide a useful perspective on media choice. Also, as Markus notes, some of the data reported early in the paper in fact give empirical support to the theory of media richness.

Lee (1994) argues that the richness of a medium is not just a property of the medium itself, but also of the organizational context in which the medium is used. This view is similar to Markus's, and both are consistent with the social influence theory, discussed below.

Regardless of its validity, the theory of information richness has had a great influence on the email field. Much subsequent research has tried to build upon it or identify shortfalls in it, as discussed below. It is interesting, though, to note two points about the original work: email was not actually included, and the managers did not actually have to carry out the communication they were being asked about. The latter point may be a weakness in the empirical support for the theory: individuals do not always behave as they would like to behave, and may consider other factors in a decision on media choice in real situations.

## Social influence models

An important criticism of the theory of information richness is that it neglects the effects on an individual's media choices of the social influence of colleagues, and of organizational norms. Fulk et al. (1990) made a number of criticisms of the theory, notably that it assumes that a medium has fixed properties, that individuals make choices independently of the people around them, that choice-making is a purely cognitive, prospective and objectively rational process, and that it is efficiency-motivated. They described an alternative model, the social influence model. This proposes that media choice depends on an individual's evaluation of media, the evaluation of the task in hand, the media skills and experience, and a host of situational factors (such as individual characteristics, accessibility of media and time constraints). Their key point is that the evaluation of media and of the task is partly objective and partly socially influenced. Empirical support for this theory comes from Schmitz and Fulk (1991), who found that the media choices of an individual are influenced by co-communicators and immediate superiors. Further, and very convincing, support comes from Markus (1994b), who reports on an organization where email was heavily used, and where much of the reason for this was the enthusiasm of the chairman. His influence spread down the organization: if anyone did not use email, the boss pressured them to do so. Markus relates (although she was unable to corroborate the story directly) that when the chairman and several senior managers later left, the new chairman was less enthusiastic about email and its use dropped.

Another way of framing the criticism that media do not have fixed properties is to say that different individuals have different perceptions of media richness. Finholt and Sproull (1990) obtained empirical evidence of this. Schmitz and Fulk (1991) found that individuals' perceptions of richness are moderately positively correlated with keyboard skills and negatively correlated with computer experience of the individual. They suggested that a better model of media choice is that individual skills and media properties will be positive predictors of an individual's perceptions of media richness, which will then be positive predictors of media choice. (The authors use the phrase 'positive predictors' presumably to imply a positive correlation between the variables involved, without wishing to suggest a totally deterministic relationship between them.) However, if this is correct, the use of the word 'richness' in the intermediate step seems redundant, and it appears simpler just to say that individual skills and media properties determine an individual's perceptions of media, which then determine media choice.

## Critical mass

The concept of a critical mass in an email implementation was developed by Markus (1987, 1990). She points out that it is not worth any individual switching from traditional media to email until a reasonable number of the people they want to communicate with are also using it. An existing and well-used email system therefore attracts new users and grows; a new email system may never get going because nobody uses it. It is therefore worthwhile for companies to encourage email use in the early stages of implementation. Tactics to achieve this include not charging individuals for email use, ensuring the software is easy to learn, and involving a relatively small number of individuals who are likely to make considerable use of the system and who will continue to use it even though few others do. Steinfield (1992) suggests a refinement of the idea involving a critical mass of services which can be accessed via email rather than a critical mass of users.

## Interactivity

Zack (1993) studied electronic mail and face-to-face communication during the work periods in which editions of a newspaper were created. He found that face-to-face communication tended to be used at the start and end of a work period, and helped to establish a shared, agreed context. Electronic mail tended to be used in the middle of a work period, once the context had been agreed. While this is consistent with the idea of information richness, Zack also discusses his findings in terms of interactivity. Interactivity is a concept which comes from Goffman's work on the importance of interaction in communication and human life (Goffman, 1981). To some extent a clear definition of the concept has never been agreed, but there is general agreement on its main characteristics. Rafaeli (1988) describes interactive communication as having the property that each message (or statement) can refer not only to the immediately preceding message but also to the way that the previous message referred to even earlier ones. Zack (1993) describes several key characteristics of interactive communication, including the continuous exchange of information by both parties simultaneously, the use of non-verbal cues, the unpredictability of the path of the conversation, and the ability to interrupt. Goffman was the first person to emphasise the importance of these characteristics in everyday conversations, which distinguish real conversations from the uninterrupted and non-interactive 'perfect talk' of newsreaders, or written communication. Zack notes that although in some ways interactivity is similar to media richness, in that it is multidimensional and shares some of the dimensions of media richness, it goes beyond it in also emphasising the structure of the conversation. Clearly, the structure of an email conversation can be quite different from that of a face-to-face conversation and this could therefore be fruitful territory for investigating differences between the media. Zack's particular work shows how face-to-face was preferred when conversations requiring high interactivity (to establish the shared context) were occurring. Once this shared context was established, email could be used.

## Symbolic meaning in messages and media

Several researchers have pointed out that particular media have a shared, symbolic meaning within a group or organization, and that selecting such media therefore conveys this symbolic meaning to the recipient. For example, in some organizations, a manager might choose to use email because it has an image of 'hi-tech', and they want to associate themselves with this image. Implicit in some of this work is a flavour of structuration theory (Giddens, 1984; Contractor & Eisenberg, 1990; Banks & Riley, 1993) to explain how the symbolic meaning is established and changed. Trevino et al. (1990a) described a symbolic interactionist perspective, which proposed that media choice is determined by a combination of equivocality of the message (an information richness model), contextual demands (such as time pressure and whether the intended recipient uses a particular medium) and symbolic meaning. Sitkin et al. (1992) described a dual-capacity model of media choice, in which they distinguish between the data-carrying capacity of a medium and its symbol-carrying capacity, and divide the latter into the intrinsic symbol-carrying capacity and the symbolic meaning attached to the medium. The model is quite sophisticated, but a simple description of it is that the attractiveness of a medium is determined by the medium's data-carrying capacity, its symbol-carrying capacity, task contingencies (characteristics of the task in hand), normative contingencies (cultural norms and rules within the organization), and constraints on the capability of particular media to convey the message to the recipient (such as whether the intended recipient uses a particular medium).

## Other ideas in media choice

Some authors have studied the idea that individuals have a ‘preferred medium’, and will default to this medium unless there are good reasons to use another. For instance, Komsky (1991) found that people who used email a lot tended to express a situation-independent preference for that medium over the telephone; people who used email only a little preferred telephone over email. Markus et al. (1992) found that individuals tend to use one medium more heavily than others. While these arguments have some force, Reder and Schwab (1988) point out that if we look at users in real organizations we will observe different genres of work, and that media use will depend heavily on the genre.

Genres include one-to-one meetings between supervisee and supervisor, project status meetings, brainstorming meetings, and unscheduled and informal meetings to maintain social links. In some genres, one particular medium will be preferred; in others several media will be used, with users switching from one to another, and perhaps choosing a particular medium for strategic reasons. Reder and Schwab criticise the bulk of media choice research as neglecting to study genre effects, and instead aiming to test a null hypothesis that two media are equivalent.

A unique and particularly interesting paper on media choice is Lea (1991). He interviewed users in a real organization using a repetitive grid technique to discover what criteria they used to distinguish between various media. The aim of this was to overcome the problem of researcher preconceptions influencing the list of criteria: in most studies the interviewees are asked to rank or evaluate a set of criteria constructed by the researcher. Lea's main conclusion was that efficiency was not mentioned by any of the users; instead they distinguished media in terms of spoken versus written communication, spontaneous versus planned communication, inconsequential versus important, emotionally poor versus emotionally rich, and needing versus not needing technology. The fact that efficiency was not mentioned anywhere is indeed notable; however in Lea's interviews, users were asked to compare and contrast media in a general sense, rather than in a specific example context or situation. This might have made it difficult for them to think of all the criteria that they use in choosing a medium, and of the efficiency criterion in particular.

Zmud et al. (1990) provided another perspective on media choice, namely how it can be influenced by the direction of communication. They found differences in the criteria used by managers to choose media for communication downwards from a manager to a subordinate on the one hand, and for communication laterally along a single status level on the other.

Email can be used for social as well as work (task) messages (see Sproull & Kiesler, 1988; Ord, 1989; Weedman, 1991). The percentage of messages which is social rather than work-related will vary from system to system, but can be as high as 40 per cent. The existence of non-work communication disproves an old idea that CMC is very work-based, and unsuitable for socio-emotional communication. Therefore while improving work-based communication is often the quoted reason many organizations give for installing email systems, they may well find that a good deal of non-work communication takes place. The authors above suggest that rather than look upon this as wasted communication, they might regard it as useful in establishing for the individual a sense of belonging to a group and hence increasing motivation. Other studies have shown that email can make otherwise isolated individuals feel a part of the larger organization, and therefore have more commitment to the organization (Huff et al., 1989; Bikson & Eveland, 1990).

Different individuals make different assessments of email. Several studies have shown how a small percentage of users often account for a large percentage of the messages sent in an email system (for example, Eveland & Bikson, 1987; Komsky, 1991). Many studies have been carried out attempting to discover links between email usage patterns and individual characteristics such as gender, age and computing experience. This work often goes under the heading of individual differences. For the most part, the work is prone to the criticisms of lacking generality and neglecting contextual issues. Perhaps a better approach is to study well-established psychological characteristics, which have deeper (cognitive) significance than the characteristics listed above, and not expect them to provide a comprehensive, context-independent model of media choices. Some work has attempted to understand differences in individuals' liking for and use of email in terms of, for example, writing apprehension or Myers-Briggs characteristics such as introversion/extroversion and judgers/perceivers. Studies in this area (for example, Trevino et al., 1990b; Alexander et al., 1991; Mabrito, 1991) have had mixed results, but have identified some statistically significant patterns.

In summary, there are several theories of media choice, but no single theory that is generally accepted. Social influence theories have gained considerable ground in the past five years, at the expense of rational choice theories such as media richness. While this is desirable in many ways, it should not be regarded as completely invalidating rational choice theories, which still have their place as useful perspectives to help us understand media choices in some situations.

## Media effects

The large body of work on media choice discussed above is complemented by an even larger body of work on the nature of the subsequent communication, and in particular, how email communication differs from face-to-face communication. Much of the work in this area consists of controlled, laboratory-like experiments, which study group effects such as polarisation (the tendency of the mean of individual views to move towards a pole as group discussion takes place) or persuasion (in, for example, union-management negotiations) when only one of a number of media is available. A criticism of much of this work (Culnan & Markus, 1987; Fulk et al., 1990; Mantovani, 1994) which should be borne in mind is that these laboratory-like experiments are often intrinsically unrealistic, and quite different from the processes that occur when real managers use email in real organizations.

## The concept of social presence

Much of the early work on teleconferencing, phone conferencing and face-to-face communication was carried out by the Communication Studies Group at University College, London (Short et al., 1976). They introduced the concept of social presence, intended as a measure of the extent to which one feels aware of the person one is communicating with. The social presence for a given medium is operationalised by judging it in terms of several scales between pairs of poles such as cold-warm or impersonal-personal. These scales were generated by asking individuals to rank media on a large number of scales, of which the key ones were subsequently selected. Experiments suggested something that seems intuitively reasonable: that one can rank the various media they studied into order of increasing social presence – business letters, telephone, multi-user audio conferencing, teleconferencing and face-to-face communication. Social presence was intended to explain how different media made it easier or harder for one party to persuade another of their point of view. It is often grouped with media-richness theory, and has similarities; one difference though is that social presence is more concerned with media effects whereas media-richness theory is more concerned with media choice. Although the concept of social presence seems intuitively attractive, and has contributed to many of the subsequent ideas, it has not itself been a major explanatory force. Short et al.'s book does not actually discuss it very much in the context of their empirical work, and support for it there is rather lacking. The lack of empirical support for the social presence idea is disappointing, given the clear sense most people have that there is something unique about face-to-face conversation, and the physical presence of the other person.

## Reduced social cues

The major candidate offered to date as a theory to explain differences in media is the idea of reduced social cues. Although this idea, or a variant on it, has been suggested by several authors, the most notable proponents and popularisers of the idea are Sproull and Kiesler and their co-workers at Carnegie–Mellon University (Sproull & Kiesler, 1992). They point out that the recipient of an email message does not obtain from the message some of the social context cues, such as information on the status, gender and race of the sender, that they would obtain in face-to-face conversation. They propose this as an explanation for a number of behaviour patterns observed in their research on email use, including an increased tendency for individuals to focus on themselves rather than others, an increase in irresponsible behaviour (known as ‘flaming’), and an increased tendency of groups to take extreme positions.

The thinking behind the reduced cues approach is that when people send or receive email messages, they are less aware of the social norms and rules which govern communication, and tend to be more self-centred and/or information-centred as a result. This view has some attractiveness, but has received some criticism recently, most notably from Lea and Spears (Spears et al., 1990; Lea & Spears, 1991; Spears & Lea, 1991), who argue for an alternative model which they call the SIDE (Social Influence DEindividuation) model. They argue that although email undoubtedly does not convey some visual information or the physical presence that would be available in face-to-face communication, the recipient of a message can still perceive and be influenced by a social dimension. This debate is one of the more important and interesting debates in email research at present, and therefore the SIDE model will be discussed further.

Lea and Spears distinguish between an individual's personal identity and social identity: the latter results from the individual feeling he or she belongs to a social group, whose norms have to be followed. Depending on the situation, one might behave more according to one's personal identity, or one's social identity. For example, while at work, a teacher will be strongly influenced by how he or she thinks teachers should behave. At home, a teacher will not be influenced in this way, and might behave according to his or her personal identity, perhaps showing quite different behaviour. Lea and Spears speak of the individual or the group being 'salient' (i.e. emphasised as being important in a given situation). Which of the group or individual is salient can be varied by external factors: for example, in a controlled laboratory experiment on email, the researcher might describe the experiment to the subjects as studying group or individual behaviour. Lea and Spears argue that when using email, even though visual and other information about the person one is communicating with is not available, individuals still have a sense of belonging to a social group (in the above example, you do not have to see the person you are communicating with to know that you are a teacher). Furthermore, if the group is salient while they are communicating via email, they will actually shift towards their social identity and away from their personal identity. Conversely if the individual is salient, he or she will shift towards a personal identity.

This theory predicts that one will tend to see self-centred behaviour in email when individuals feel group identity is salient, and when group norms imply self-centred behaviour is appropriate. Lea and Spears' arguments are supported by a laboratory-like experiment which found that when the group is salient, communication over email leads to greater group polarisation (i.e. the mean view of individuals shifts towards a group norm). They also offer an explanation of the results of the various Carnegie–Mellon studies using their model. Interestingly, in an earlier paper (Hiltz et al., 1989), a similar theory was proposed to explain why groups of managers in a controlled experiment made more conservative decisions when using anonymous (pen-name) email than when using normal email or face-to-face communication. In that study, the managers were from a company with a conservative culture, and therefore the SIDE model would explain why they became more conservative when using anonymous email.

Other authors have also criticised Sproull and Kiesler's approach (notably Mantovani, 1994). However, there has as yet been no published response from the proponents of the reduced social cues theory, and therefore the debate remains unresolved. The mere existence of alternative views on this issue makes one aware that the email research still gives us only a primitive understanding of how email differs from face-to-face communication.

## Information overload

While the possibility of access to huge amounts of information has its attractions, it may bring the unattractive feature of information overload. Palme (1985) pointed out that, since writing a message is slower than reading it, a key feature of email which makes information overload a possibility is the opportunity to send a message from one person to many people. To this we should add a well-discussed idea (see, for example, Sproull and Kiesler, 1992) that email increases the number of communication connections in an organization. Past work on information overload can be divided into that which seeks to discover exactly what it is, and that which seeks practical ways of alleviating it.

Beginning with the question of what information overload actually is, it has been pointed out (see for example Stohl and Redding, 1987) that overload is usually not the simple arrival of large amounts of data. Rather, it seems to relate to what Stohl and Redding call the internally experienced message: you have to process a message (or at least, contemplate doing so) for it to be able to contribute to a sense of overload. But it still remains to be established whether what matters more is a fall in satisfaction through being overloaded or a fall in actual performance. There may be an important distinction between them, suggested by O'Reilly's (1980) study. In a non-CMC situation he studied personnel in navy aviation units and compared those who perceived themselves as having less information than they needed with those who perceived themselves as having more information than they needed. He found that the former performed better but were less satisfied with their performance. Satisfaction and performance are therefore not necessarily the same thing. Most authors seem to have studied satisfaction rather than performance, perhaps because performance is so difficult to measure objectively. For example, Taha and Caldwell (1993) discuss the idea of an ‘appropriate’ level of interaction or contact, which is situation dependent: more contact than one desires leads to what they term ‘invasion’; less contact than desired leads to isolation. Although this and other work has helped our understanding, further work is needed to gain a satisfactory understanding of what information overload really is.

The other main block of work on information overload is on practical ways to alleviate it. Mackay (1988) reports a fascinating study of users' differing strategies for obtaining information from email and dealing with overload. She divided the users she studied into those who feel electronic mail is 'OK', those who feel on the edge of information overload, and those who feel overloaded. The first type did not try to read all of their electronic mail, tended to remove themselves from voluntary electronic distribution lists, and archived only a few of the messages received. The second and third types both tried to read all of the mail that came in, and remained on voluntary distribution lists. The feature that distinguished the second and third types was that the second types tended to get to the end of their electronic inboxes whereas the third types did not and became demoralised by an ever-lengthening inbox.

Before we become gloomy about future problems of information overload, it is worth noting that there are a number of studies which suggest it is ultimately not a problem. Hiltz and Turoff (1985) found that feelings of overload would generally reach a maximum in the early stages of using email, when users had gained enough familiarity to cause a large number of messages to arrive, but not enough familiarity to know how to deal with them. Thereafter they learnt how to self-organize to solve the problem. They noted that in their studies, high-volume users of email tended to feel less overloaded than low-volume users. Finholt and Sproull (1990) found that subscribers to electronic distribution lists in a real organization simply dropped out if they felt the number of messages arriving was too high.

Better software is likely to be a significant factor in alleviating information overload. Anyone who has tried different Usenet News readers will know this very well. In the academic research on new software, the key contribution is the work of Malone et al. on the Information Lens. The Information Lens is a software system which forces writers to structure their messages, and forces them to indicate explicitly which one of a number of types their message belongs to. Malone et al. found that this enabled the recipients to process the messages more rapidly, and sometimes have them processed automatically by the system. Rules for filtering email messages automatically are an important idea in this area. Malone et al. (1987) discuss a number of ways of filtering: in social filtering one filters on the basis of the sender; in economic filtering the sender has to pay a 'price' to get the message through the recipient's filter, and is therefore discouraged from sending junk mail; in cognitive filtering one filters according to the message content. The last of these is obviously particularly attractive but also the most difficult to achieve. Obviously it is possible to use keyword searches to some extent, but this is unlikely to be even a reasonable match for human judgement.

## Unforeseen effects

A number of authors have pointed out that the effects of technologies in general, and email in particular, are often quite different from what was expected (Hiltz & Turoff, 1981; Ord, 1989; Finholt et al., 1990). Sproull and Kiesler (1992) proposed a useful categorisation of the effects of CMC into two levels. First-level effects are relatively easy to foresee, are concerned with efficiency, and usually provide the rationalisation for the installation of a CMC system. First-level effects include being able to get data to someone more quickly than would be possible with postal mail, and being able to send a single message to many people very easily. Second-level effects are those which are often not foreseen, and are concerned with social impacts on the groups and organizations using CMC. Second-level effects include each individual being more aware of other parts of the organization, and new possibilities for geographically dispersed work-groups.

Although the first-order effects are very important, their predictable and relatively constant nature across the range of types of organization makes them less interesting than second-order effects. Sproull and Kiesler (1992) discuss the types of second-order effects they saw in organizations, or expected to see as a result of their laboratory-like simulations. They expect contributions in CMC organizations to be much more evenly spread than in traditional organizations, since individuals lower down the status scale will be more willing to contribute ideas. They note that this increased democracy may be a good or a bad thing, depending on the context. It may be bad in that consensus may be harder to obtain, but good in that individuals who would normally feel somewhat isolated from the rest of the organization may benefit significantly from the availability of CMC to keep them in touch and make them feel part of the organization.

Huff et al. (1989) observed the latter effect in a group of shift workers in a city government. Other writers have disagreed with the idea that CMC automatically means greater democracy. Mantovani (1994) suggests that CMC will not necessarily overcome social barriers in organizations, but will tend to reinforce the existing lines of communication, and that users who find technology hard to use will tend to be excluded.

Some other unexpected effects of email are apparent in the study by Phillips and Eisenberg (1993). Because email allows messages to be copied to many people quite easily, and stored in an archive, it allows a number of tactics and strategies which would not normally be available to individuals. Phillips and Eisenberg observed a number of interesting tactics and strategies of this kind employed by uses of email in one organization. Individuals were observed sometimes to send a request for action to an individual and copy it to the recipient's boss in order to gain leverage over the recipient. This could be particularly effective when the message was a verbatim copy of an earlier request, with a note of 'have you done this yet?' added at the beginning. Other individuals would copy to their own boss in order to let him or her know that they were getting on with their work, or would pull an old message out of an archive and send it to say 'yes, look' in response to a query about whether some work had been done.

Markus (1994a) discusses the unexpected effects of email in an excellent paper which assesses the merits of three different perspectives on the impacts of email on social life in organizations. The three theories are: technological determinism (that social outcomes following the introduction of a new technology are determined solely by the technology itself), the rational actor perspective (that individuals determine the outcomes, and will sometimes take actions even if they have negative social consequences) and the emergent process view (that positive and negative effects sometime occur for totally unforeseen reasons and are not attributable to either human choices or material properties of the technology). In the organization she studied, there was support for the last two theories in effects such as people avoiding social contact, and using email in a highly interactive way, leaving their computer on all day and getting it to beep immediately when a message arrived.

One could interpret the above works on unforeseen consequences to mean that we cannot ever predict very well what the effects of email will be. However, it is clear that to some extent we simply have not yet done enough research in this area. The relative lack of non-quantitative studies and surveys of real organizations is a hindrance here. Another hindrance is that the use of email in organizations is very fluid at present. New email systems are being installed all the time, and users are learning how they can be used. Further advances can be expected in email links between companies rather than just within companies, and this will open up a further tranche of new possibilities and unforeseen consequences.

## Email research methodologies

This section considers a number of issues concerned with the approaches to and assumptions made in email research. The first two of these concern theoretical issues; the last two (network analyses and thread structure) are practical tools used to analyse email communication.

## Epistemology

The issue of epistemology has been discussed a great deal in IS research in recent years. Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) divided the underlying epistemologies of 155 research papers in IS into positivist, interpretive and critical, and found that the positivist approach was overwhelmingly dominant, with just a few interpretive studies and no critical studies at all in the sample. The situation in email research is very similar, but with the difference that there has been very little discussion of epistemology. The work of Markus (for example, 1994a, 1994b) referred to elsewhere and Lee's (1994) paper are exceptions to this rule and show how interpretive approaches can be very valuable.

A great deal of the literature on email research (including many papers not discussed here but included in the bibliography by Rudy, 1994–) consists of laboratory-like experiments, usually with only one medium available at a time. Often the subjects were students, whose behaviour will probably be quite different from that of real managers in real organizations, who have worked together in the past and have to work together after the 'experiment' is over. These limitations have been noted by several authors (such as Culnan & Markus, 1987; Reder & Schwab, 1988; Fulk et al., 1990; Mantovani, 1994). Another problem with past studies is that they often attempted to measure a large number of variables (such as age, gender, computer experience and job type) and see which were correlated with media-use decisions, or see whether the variables could be grouped together (via a statistical factor analysis) in determining media use. This kind of statistical approach assumes that humans can be measured and described by numbers, and fails to gain much understanding of the great richness and complexity of individual people. It also neglects contextual issues, discussed below.

Ethnographic approaches provide an excellent antidote to laboratory experiments. Though little work of this kind has been done on email, there are studies in other areas which make the limitations of the laboratory approach very clear indeed. Examples are Harper and Sellen (1995) and Orr (1991, 1992), and researchers in CMC would benefit greatly from reading a few such papers.

## Contextualism

A criticism of a lot of the past work on media choice and media effects is that it assumes that we can produce a set of rules about how users choose media which are context-independent. The approach often involved a good deal of statistical analysis, testing a large number of hypotheses involving correlations between a large number of pairs of variables. While several studies did find patterns in individual organizations, the patterns often differed from organization to organization. Similarly, a great deal of work has been done on whether the availability of email leads to a drop in the use of other media (through substitution) or creates new communication that would not otherwise occur. Taken as a whole, the numerous studies can only be said to show that the answer is both, and that it depends on the individuals and the culture of an organization.

To those who are familiar with theories of contextualism, this will come as no surprise. However, in CMC research the idea of contextualism has gained attention in only the past five or so years, and many researchers perhaps still ignore it. A number of papers on email have pointed directly to the importance of contextualism. Erlich (1987) noted how important it is to understand the local rules, taboos, expectations and roles when trying to understand features of an email system in any organization. Fulk et al. (1991) specifically addressed the problem of the mass of conflicting evidence from email research, and attributed it to differing contexts. Markus et al. (1992) found that there were significant differences in media use between companies that could be explained by contextual factors. Sproull and Kiesler (1992) and Reynolds (1994) also note that the effects of new technology depend heavily on the culture and policies of the organization into which it is introduced. Manotovani (1994) and Reynolds (1994) argue that the idea that email means greater democracy (a claim often made for email) is over-simplified: in practice there tend to be winners and losers, and actual outcomes are context-dependent. Perrin (1991) describes how electronic systems supporting collaboration may be suppressed by management because they conflict with existing bureaucratic norms and practices.

In a very interesting paper, Perry (1988) takes the theory of contextualism developed by William McGuire and considers its implications for media research. McGuire's theory proposes that all theories and hypotheses are a priori both true and false, in different situations. Therefore it is foolish to search for hypotheses which are always true. Instead, one should look for a ‘main’ hypothesis, which is true in some contexts, and then study which variables limit the validity of the main hypothesis to these contexts. If such a context variable is found, then one can imagine what might be called a second-level hypothesis, describing when the main hypothesis is true, dependent on the context variable. The theory of contextualism would then of course note that this second-level hypothesis is true in only certain situations, and is limited by other variables. Obviously one can apply this argument infinitely many times, but after a few levels the exceptions tend to become sufficiently uncommon to be of negligible importance. Although aimed primarily at mass media research rather than email, Perry’s paper provides much food for thought for email researchers.

## Network analysis

Network analysis is a set of practical methods for quantifying and studying the amount of communication that goes on within a group. The definitive book in this area is Rogers and Kincaid (1981). Network analysis can be used, for example, to identify individuals within a group who are well or poorly integrated with other people in the group (from an email viewpoint obviously), or individuals who act as important links between pairs of people who do not communicate directly. Although this description of network analysis is framed in terms of individuals, Contractor and Eisenberg (1990) have noted that the idea really focuses on the links between individuals rather than the individuals themselves. Eveland and Bikson (1987) and Bikson and Eveland (1990) used network analysis methods to study the communication between new email users and between two distinct types of email users (employed staff and retired staff) respectively. They produced sociograms, which are two-dimensional graphics on which each individual is represented by a point, and the closeness of the points for two different individuals conveys how often they communicate with each other.

An obvious issue here is whether it is valid and useful to try to quantify communication in such ways. The methods often concentrate solely on CMC, and neglect face-to-face or telephone communication. If this is the case, an attempt to interpret them as representing ‘communication’ within an organization is likely to be seriously flawed. This aside, the methods can be a useful way of conveying the nature of CMC communication in a group.

## Thread structure

A thread is a collection of messages with a common subject. The importance of the concept stems from the fact that email allows several discussions (threads) to be going on simultaneously. There is some interest in the structure of threads, and how their structure can be represented. Although most of the interest in this concept results from Internet-related services such as Usenet News and electronic distribution lists, it can also be applied to email within organizations. Faf-champs et al. (1989) describe how email discussions on a topic may be divided up into islands, dialogues and webs of communication. An island is a message which receives no reply; a dialogue is a series of communications between a pair of individuals on a single topic; a web is a complex structure of messages, where a single message may receive replies from many users, some of which are replying to issues raised in more than one message.

## Neglected avenues

This section discusses three interesting but rather neglected avenues of research in email: the effects of email at an organizational level, email from the recipient's viewpoint and the concept of face.

## Organizational issues

Most of the work reported in previous sections concerns the effect of email on and use of email by individuals and groups. By contrast, very little has been done on the effect of email at an organizational level.

Culnan and Markus (1987) note the lack of linkages between theories of media and theories of organizational communication and information processing, citing this as a weakness of the theories of media. They suggest the use of O'Reilly and Pondy's (1979) framework for organizational communication to alleviate this problem. The framework proposes that communication outcomes (such as consensus or leadership emergence) in an organization are influenced by properties of the communication (such as cues being filtered out, or information overload), which in turn is influenced by the structure of communication in the organization (such as the availability of new channels of communication provided by email or modifying the networks of communicators), which in turn is influenced by organizational variables such as the structure of the organization.

One of the few papers to discuss the organizational impact of CMC directly is Huber (1990). His general view is positive, and is based on the idea of CMC increasing the speed and ease with which data are transferred within the organization. He suggests this will lead to more rapid information processing and more rapid and better quality decision-making. Fewer intermediate human nodes will be required to process information, since each individual will have better access to data. Among a number of other effects he suggests is one that CMC will lead to a larger number and variety of people participating as information sources in decisions (as a result of the reduced effort required to contact a source for information), but that the number of people actually making the decision will be smaller than before. This idea is based presumably on a core group of decision makers being able to obtain data and opinions from many individuals without having to involve them in face-to-face meetings where they would become part of the core. This view does not seem to be one of increased democracy: in some ways it is rather disturbing since those who are sources of information may not be aware of what their information is being used for. Huber suggests that organizations which are currently centralised will become less centralised, as lower managers become more informed of company strategies, and that organizations which are currently decentralised will become less decentralised, as upper managers become more informed of detail.

In contrast to the generally positive view of Huber, the paper by Perrin (1991) describes how electronic collaboration tools may conflict with the norms and practices of bureaucratic organizations. She uses the term ‘electronic social fields’ to refer to the invisible networks set up, and gives examples of how real organizations suppressed their electronic communication systems because they conflicted with the need of managers to observe and check the performance of their employees.

One interesting paper on the effects of email on organizations is that of Feldman (1987). She proposed that email enables the ‘weak ties’ suggested by Granovetter (1973, 1982). Granovetter’s model of organizations was of groups, with a lot of internal communication, complemented by much weaker ties between individuals in different groups. These weak ties are important for the organization as a whole: they have the effect of transferring data and ideas from one group to another from time to time in a very beneficial manner. It is easy to see how email can enable this in organizations, since as Culnan and Markus (1987) have pointed out, email links people by interest rather than geography, social position or prior acquaintance.

## Email from the receiver's point of view

Past research on email is heavily biased towards the sender of messages, and has almost completely neglected the recipient. This seems odd given that usually the purpose of sending a message is to communicate something to the receiver. This lack of attention to the receiver has been pointed out by a number of authors (for example, Contactor & Eisenberg, 1990; Sitkin et al., 1992; Mantovani, 1994). Some of the issues in this area relate to information overload on the receiver (see Denning, 1982). Others relate to how much success the sender will have in influencing the receiver. For example, Mantovani (1994) criticised the prevailing view of email as being more democratic in allowing low status people to send messages where they would not do so face to face, since although they can indeed send messages, there is no guarantee that they get read. Matheson and Zanna (1989) set up a controlled experiment with student groups where the students within a group were led to believe they were of a common view on an issue and were arguing against a group of students with the opposite view. In this situation, individual students were provided with arguments on the issue from two types of person: strong counter-arguments from a person of supposedly same-mind; and moderately strong counter-arguments from people of supposedly opposite-mind. They found that the former were more influential than the latter. This suggests something which seems entirely reasonable: that the receiver's perception of a message will be influenced by their prior knowledge of and opinions of the sender.

Lee's (1994) paper applies hermeneutic concepts to email, and very valuably confirms that receiving and interpreting a message is far from a simple, passive process. He notes how the receiver appropriates social constructions and joins the hardware and software system as coprocessors of the message data.

The lack of attention to the receiver of a message is made all the more surprising by the existence of a considerable body of research in the 1960s and 1970s in the field of mass media which addresses the receiver directly. The term ‘uses and gratifications’ was coined to describe this research: its aim was to understand why people watch television, read newspapers and books, and use other mass-media. Katz et al. (1974) provide a very readable summary of the uses and gratifications work up to that date. Although their context was the mass media, much of their paper is of relevance to today’s research on email. There is clearly much to be done on the issue of the recipient of an email message.

## Face

Rather isolated from all other research in CMC is Hiemstra's (1982) fascinating paper which describes the application of ideas of 'face' in CMC situations. Face is a concept which comes from the work of Goffman (1967) and is defined as 'the positive social value a person effectively claims for himself by the line others assume he has taken during a particular contact'. Goffman regarded maintenance of face as a key part of communication episodes. This work was developed by Brown and Levinson (1987) to suggest that any communication interaction between individuals is potentially face-threatening, and that an individual contemplating such an interaction will use one of four high-level strategies. These are:

\- bald on record – for example, ‘your trousers are on fire’ – maximising efficiency;

\- positive politeness – for example, ‘what a beautiful garden, could I have some lettuce?' – a desire to come closer to the converse;

\- negative politeness – for example, ‘is there any chance that you could help me with this’ – not a negative threat, but showing a concern that the converse is being disturbed by the interaction;

\- off record – for example, ‘gosh, it’s drafty in here’ – hinting at something but allowing denial of the real motive if necessary.

Some preliminary work done by Hiemstra suggested that more than 75 per cent of communication in a CMC system was non-bald-on-record, and almost none of this was off-record. Clearly, in email it is more difficult to employ the off-record strategy since as soon as you send a message you cannot deny that you wanted to say something. It is not like the ‘gosh, it’s drafty in here’ situation where the two people would have been in the same room anyway, regardless of whether the speaker wanted the window closed or not. Further studies in this area would be useful.

## Conclusions and suggestions for future research

It should be clear from the discussion above that a lot of work has been done on email, even if one ignores many of the numerous quantitative, laboratory-like studies. While this is encouraging and should be noted, the field still has a distinctly piecemeal feel to it. There is no single clear theory explaining how individuals make media choices or respond to information overload; there is no agreement on how email discussion differs from face-to-face discussion; and there is very little work on the effects of email at an organizational level. The quantitative studies that have been carried out generally explain only a small percentage of the variance in media use from individual to individual. In this section, suggestions are made for future avenues of research.

It is clear that some of the traditional approaches used in email research need to be questioned. In particular, controlled, laboratory-like experiments may have some advantages, but they seldom reproduce the context in which managers use email in real organizations. The recent increase in studies of real managers in real organizations, aiming to understand their motivations, tactics and strategies in using email, is therefore important. As discussed above, a major element of any future work on email should be an appreciation of issues of contextualism.

Information overload is becoming a more and more important area for study, partly in the context of electronic mail in organizations but also in that of individuals trying to use the Internet (and in particular the World Wide Web) to find useful data. Although some organizations have had email systems for many years, many have only recently installed email or some other CMC system. The latter organizations often have simply not yet learnt how to use these systems in a social sense, even if they know how to use them in a mechanical sense. For traditional tasks in organizations, most people have a fairly clear idea of what is expected of them in terms of number of tasks to be done, and the timescales in which they are expected to be completed. By contrast, in organizations with new email systems, the large number of incoming electronic mail messages is a new phenomenon, and individuals often do not know what is expected of them in terms of response time and what portion of incoming messages they are expected to respond to. Studies of the different strategies they use to alleviate this problem will be valuable. Such studies might also throw light on a general organizational issue, namely the process by which groups and organizations develop the rules and norms which govern the behaviour of a mature technology (which email will presumably eventually become). When faced with a new technology, are individuals capable of working out for themselves what the best response is, or do they need significant guidance from colleagues and the organization as a whole? In this sense email, and in particular, the information overload issue within it, might be seen as a probe which we can use to discover something about individuals and organizations.

The lack of attention to the receiver of a message has been highlighted as a neglected area. Past work on uses and gratifications could serve as useful input here. What is needed in the first instance is to ask recipients of messages how they felt about them, whether the sender had the desired effect, and how the message might have been improved (perhaps including whether it could have been sent using a different medium). If we regard the purpose of sending messages as to make the senders feel satisfied that they have done so, then the traditional emphasis of email research on the sender is justified. But if we are at all interested in the overall effects of the message being sent, we must consider the recipient at least as much.

We still do not really understand what it is about face-to-face communication that gives it a character quite different from email. Studying the structure of an email conversation compared with that of a face-to-face conversation would be worthwhile. This work would need to take account of discussions on other media, on which the email conversation depended for its meaning, and on the presence of social norms that affected the interpretation of email messages, but would otherwise focus on the structure of email messages alone. It would draw upon Goffman's and Zack's work on interactivity. But it also seems likely that there is something more to the difference between face-to-face and email than this, perhaps something to do with face to face being synonymous with physical presence, and perhaps the potential of physical contact and/or threat. Although now regarded as unacceptable in many societies, using force to resolve arguments used to be the norm. In playgrounds, groups of children often establish pecking orders on the basis of force. We therefore have had an evolutionary and a cultural inheritance in which physical contact is probably a major element in determining the social rules and norms by which we live. This suggests a return to the concept of social presence, or one similar to it, for analysing email use.

The lack of a theory base has also been pointed out as a problem for the field (Steinfield & Fulk, 1990). One way of alleviating this problem is to make more use of theories from other related fields, such as communication theory and social theory. Workers in these fields have already spent some considerable time and effort studying humans and how they interact with technology and with each other. Examples of this are in the work referred to above by Hiemstra (1982) and Zack (1993). Rather than think of email as a totally unique technology, we ought to think of it as just another way for humans to interact, and bring to bear relevant ideas from other fields.

Mainstream information systems research surely also has much to offer the field of email. Readers from other areas of IS research will probably see patterns in the sections above that have occurred in their own fields. Such patterns include the move from a belief that the effects of technology could be predicted objectively, to an understanding of the importance of the culture and context in which the technology was introduced. This is not to say that the culture and context are all that matters and that email technology will not have new and significant effects on organizations and society. As with the introduction of personal computers, it is likely to change them significantly.

## Appendix

Factors encouraging successful email systems

The importance of these factors will depend heavily on the details of the organization concerned, but all have been seen to be important in at least some types of organization.

## Training

As with all IT systems, many individuals will need help with (new) email software, and are likely not to use the system if it is not provided (Uhlig, 1977; Grieve & McCabe, 1986; Bullen & Bennett, 1990).

## Critical mass

There is no point in using email if the people you wish to communicate with do not use it. Some systems fail because they never reach a critical mass of users early on, and there is therefore no incentive for new people to switch to using the system. This critical mass idea has also been expressed in terms of a critical number of useful services which can be accessed via the system, rather than a number of people (Markus, 1990; Steinfield, 1992).

## Champions

As with all IT systems, the presence of champions (from either top or middle management), to encourage others to use the system, makes success more likely (Fafchamps et al., 1989; Bullen & Bennett, 1990).

## Pressure

Pressure, either informal of formal, has been shown to encourage system use. This might come from a supervisor, or be a requirement of a group working together on a project (Golden et al., 1992; Bikson & Law, 1993).

Proximity/accessibility of computers from which email can be sent/received

This is obviously a requirement, though perhaps less important nowadays given fairly widespread availability of computers (Culnan, 1984; Steinfield, 1986).

## Ease of use of the software

This is often, but surprisingly not always, shown to be an important factor in successful systems. Clearly someone who is dubious about email is likely to be further put off if the user interface is poor (Steinfield, 1986).

## Time to type/lack of keyboard skills

Email is intrinsically slower than face-to-face communication for the sender of the message. For people who do not have time to type out their views, this can discourage email use (Bikson & Law, 1993).

## Comparability with existing work styles

There is conflicting evidence on whether the new email system should allow people to work as they did before, or encourage them into new modes of working. However, clearly some people will be reluctant to adopt a new system if it disrupts their working patterns (Grieve & McCabe, 1986; Erlich, 1987).

## Observability

An idea, borrowed from innovations research, is that an innovation is more likely to succeed if its use is visible. Email is not visible in the usual sense of a physical object, but can be made more ‘visible’ by key individuals sending out useful information via email (Grieve & McCabe, 1986; Contractor & Eisenberg, 1990).

## The presence of an existing group identity

Although the evidence is conflicting, email seems to be less good than face-to-face communication for establishing a group identity or 'spirit'. Both email and other types of communication are therefore more likely to succeed if a group identity has already been established by face-to-face communication (Zack, 1993)

## Flexibility

Different people will require different features from the system. Designing it to be flexible is therefore better than constraining its use in particular ways. This applies as much to the types of messages that get sent as it does to the number of functions available. For example, some organizations ban social messages from email systems, but there is considerable evidence that these serve a valuable role in making people feel part of a single organization (Hiltz & Turoff, 1981; Grieve & McCabe, 1986; Mackay, 1988)

## Ease of integration with other packages

Email is becoming increasingly viewed as a medium for transferring many different kinds of information, rather than just text messages. For example, spreadsheets or drafts of reports might be transferred. This requires the email system to be well integrated with other software that is used (Hjalmarsson et al., 1989; Markus et al., 1992).

## Absence of a charging system

With new media, use is sometimes charged to the user. This invariably discourages users to switch from existing media to the new one. Even if such charging systems are essential, they should certainly not be introduced until the critical mass of users or services has been passed (Markus, 1987).
