# Personality's impact on information management strategy formulation

T Reponen, J Pärnistö and J Viitanen

Turku School of Economics and Business Administration, PO Box 110, 20521 Turku, Finland

This study aimed to examine the impact of a decision maker's personality on the ways the decision maker formulates information resource strategies. The basic hypothesis is that different personalities make different decisions in the same environment. An empirical test was made with a group of 20 managers participating in an Executive Education Programme. Using the results of the Myers and Briggs Type Indicator test the managers were divided into two groups of intuitive-extrovert and sensing-introvert types. Both groups were given the same assignment – creating an information resource strategy for a case company – and conclusions were drawn from their behaviour during the process and the results of their work. This test shows that the personality has a strong influence on both the working methods and results of the planning process. The main conclusion is that the decision maker's personality should be taken into consideration in the strategic planning processes.

## Description of the research problem

The research problem of this study is to discover the influence of a decision maker's personality on that person's decision making. The application area is the deployment of information technology (IT) in business processes. The viewpoint taken is that of general management, and the specific objective is to clarify how their personalities influence their decision making on information systems. The rationale for studying this subject stems from the increasing role of general management in relation to IT. Information is a vitally important resource for organizations, and thus integration of technology with business operations is a key to success.

General management plays a central role in making alignment decisions, but their competence in making such integrative decisions varies considerably. Their education and experience in the field is very heterogeneous. However, they know both the strategic goals of the organization and the needs for organizational changes best, and their contribution is urgently needed in generating plans for IT development.

The main hypothesis of this study is that the decision maker's personality has a noticeable impact on the way he formulates an information management strategy. This hypothesis is based on the differences in the way that people perceive situations and environments. This diversity refers to perceptual and actual differences among individuals, and is evident in a variety of forms including gender, age, physical abilities, cognitive styles, national origin and so on (Jamieson & O'Mara, 1991). The personality of any individual builds on the interaction of all these elements and thus exerts a powerful force in developing values and perceptions. This hypothesis is examined by trying to find the links between the personality types of general managers and their cognitive maps of information resource management.

The research methods used in this study are conceptual analysis and a laboratory test. First, the concepts of mental and cognitive maps are discussed, and then the framework for information resource management is introduced. These elements are then put together to form a research framework. Finally, an empirical experiment is presented to illustrate the ideas created. Conclusions are drawn from both the conceptual analysis and the empirical test.

## Mental models, cognitive maps and personality types in decision making

The notion of mental models comes from the idea that humans are processors of information and have limited processing capabilities (Simon, 1955; March & Simon, 1958; Johnson-Laird, 1983; Ramaprasad et al, 1993; Reger & Huff, 1993). Owing to people's limited capacity, knowledge structures (eg mental models) have been advanced as a plausible explanation of how we go about handling decision situations. In other words, an individual's perception and understanding of a problem can be captured in a cognitive structure that consists of interconnected sets of elements representing implicit views of one's own interests, concerns and tasks (Lee et al, 1992). Synonyms for such a cognitive structure include cognitive maps, cause maps and scripts. For management studies, the concept of these cognitive structures provides an explanation as to how meaning is given to data.

To make sense of the world and of decision making processes, managers usually rely on quite simplified representations and mental models. Defined by O'Keefe and Nadel (1978) as an aggregate of inter-related information, mental models consist of concepts and relationships that an individual uses to understand various situations or environments. They serve as maps allowing individuals to perceive environments on a larger scale, beyond the range of immediate perception (Weick & Bougon, 1986; Huff, 1990).

Eden's (1988) specific technique for problem solving, strategic planning and modeling qualitative data is summarised in the following definition: 'The cognitive map is a model designed to represent the way in which a person defines an issue. It is not a general model for someone's thinking, neither is it intended to be a simulation model of decision making, but it is a network of ideas linked by arrows: the network that is coded from what a person says.' This technique is aimed at structuring an individual or collective discourse in such a way that participating actors are left with a problem construction they are likely to feel as their own, either individually or collectively.

The relationship ultimately chosen for mapping depends upon the map's purpose. It is possible, however, to group the purposes of mapping (and the relationships that help fulfill that purpose) into at least five generic families that tend to fall along a continuum demanding increasing interpretive input from the researcher (Huff, 1990).

\- Maps that assess attention, association and the importance of concepts;

\- Maps that show dimensions of categories and cognitive taxonomies;

\- Maps that show influence, causality and system dynamics;

\- Maps that show the structure of argument and conclusion;

\- Maps that specify schema, frames and perceptual codes.

It is also possible to discover the dynamics of the strategic decision making situation and to obtain information concerning the influential determinants of that process. These so-called causal maps are based on somewhat different assumptions about cognition than cognitive mapping, including the ideas that causal associations are the major way in which understanding about the world is organized and that choice among alternative actions involves causal evaluations. The basic idea is that in a world of incomplete data, individuals nonetheless make causal inferences that allow interpretation. Even though decision-makers' causal assertions about a given situation can be very complex, the evidence to date indicates that internal inconsistencies are quite rare (Axelrod,

1976; Bonham & Shapiro, 1976). Furthermore, this internal inconsistency and stability of an actor's cause map, and its usefulness in both predicting future actions and explaining those in the past. are clearly areas of great interest to strategic management and argue for the continued use of causal mapping in strategic management research (Huff, 1990).

But there are also weaknesses in the causal mapping method, since decision-makers' ability to perceive feedback loops is limited, even when such loops are deliberately designed into the settings used in experiments (Huff, 1990). Another problem is that the causal relations are at the same level of certainty. In other words, there is no way to distinguish among speculative, experimentally fitted relations, or those taken on faith. Perhaps the most realistic maps of strategy related materials should also show some points of uncertainty and exhibit contradictory forces that have an effect on how decisions are made.

The potential contribution of cognitive mapping to strategic management lies in the field of strategy formulation and in gathering an increasing understanding of strategic options. Mapping can be used to reveal the dimensions of key alternatives in, for example, strategic decision making. On the other hand, the mapping method can be used to interpret the competitive assumptions that could lead to competitive advantage. We believe that management researchers should pay more systematic attention to the broad range of research problems that can be approached from a cognitive perspective, since there is a growing need for executives to extract more useful information from often fuzzy strategic decision-making situations.

One characteristic of decision makers that involves both cognitive and motivational orientations, and thus may be particularly significant as a source of influence on decisions, are decision maker beliefs about the causes and effects of the strategic decision (O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978; Bobbitt & Ford, 1980; Beyer, 1981). On the one hand, such beliefs act as filters to how decision makers see the world, by serving to explain the hows and whys of events (ie cognitive orientation). On the other hand, beliefs about the causes and effects of a strategic decision also influence action (ie motivation orientation). Beliefs rationalise the decision maker's understanding of the world and influence perceptions by focusing attention on particular events and providing an interpretation of those events (Bayer, 1981). These ideas are very close to personal construct theory, which is predicated on the belief that individuals act on their perceptions of the objective world filtered through their construct system. In other words, the individual actively constructs perceptions of his or her environment (Reger & Huff, 1993). It can therefore be stated that beliefs and personality have a potentially important impact on decisions. Cognitive mapping may be used to determine these relationships (Axelrod, 1976; Hall, 1978; Roos and Hall, 1980; Eden, 1988).

As Chidambaram and Kautz (1993) have stated, the primary measure of cognitive diversity and personality classification can be chosen from several alternatives, but one of the most promising is the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), (Myers & Briggs, 1976; Keirsey & Bates, 1984). Based on the ideas of the Swiss psychologist Carl Gustav Jung (1971), the MBTI measure provides a well grounded device with which to analyse personality. MBTI has been validated across a broad spectrum of groups and is widely used to describe and differentiate people according to the way they prefer to 'use their minds' (Murray, 1990). It does not measure personality per se, but merely registers certain respondent preferences. The score consists of four attributes, each of which has two possible values. The dimensions are (Keirsey & Bates, 1984).

Usually, a person's MBTI value is presented as a combination of these four attributes (eg ENFP, ISFJ, INFP), which describes the potential managerial nature and preference structure of that person. By using these values, psychologists have been able to identify and name the so-called ideal managerial types, and analyse (sometimes even predict) their behaviour and beliefs in a given context and situation. The cognitive mapping method relies partly on these differences between decision maker personalities, since such differences strike at the source of the whole research problem. According to cognitive theories, if the personalities did not differ, the outcome of the process would be quite similar.

MBTI tests can be used to classify individuals on the basis of their personality characteristics. The personality is assumed to influence the way individuals acquire and process information and make decisions. For example, sensing type people typically emphasise efficient decision making procedures, control and hierarchical structures. Such people are very effective decision makers in situations where decision outcomes can be optimised on the basis of some well-defined criteria. Intuitive-type individuals tend to emphasise free exchange of opinions and rich conversation. They feel themselves more comfortable in an unstructured environment than sensing-type individuals.

## Information resource strategy

Several papers have argued for a cognitive view of strategy formulation (see Lenz, 1981; Dutton et al, 1983; Fahey & Narayanan, 1989; Huff, 1990; Narayanan &

Fahey, 1990; Reger, 1990; Stubbart & Ramaprasad, 1990) since the cognitive maps used usually refer to the structure of causal inferences made by strategists to make sense of their environment. For example, Lenz (1981) has suggested that organizations reformulate their strategy in response to environmental change, primarily through a revision of strategic decision makers' cognitive maps. In this way it has been argued that strategic choices reflect the decision makers' personalities and subjective constructions of the environment and organization (see Narayanan & Fahey, 1990).

Research on managerial knowledge has drawn on cognitive science work on systematic knowledge structures known as schemas or maps (Stubbart & Ramaprasad, 1990). In many cases the research settling is predetermined, at least partly, through a hypothetical strategy schema drawn from previous theories or practical experiences. These general descriptions usually reflect supposed modes of managerial thinking about strategy attributes or situational factors.

This study focuses on examining the relationship between a decision maker's personality and the way he outlines an information resource strategy. In Mintzberg's (1990) typology, many of the schools of thought in strategic management have a learning aspect to them, especially ‘cognitive’, ‘learning’ and ‘configurational’ schools. As the objective of this study is to link personality and strategy generation together, a ‘soft’ approach to strategy generation is relevant. The aim is to discover the cognitive maps of decision makers in their information resource strategy (IRS) process.

An IRS may be created using many different methods and through different ways of thinking. As the main problem is aligning business processes and IT, the planning process should be such that it promotes learning and action. Business people should understand more about the possibilities for which to utilise technology and information system (IS) professionals should understand more of how organizations operate. The IRS process is one potential way to increase mutual understanding and competence levels.

We have developed, and tested in many organizations, an Evolution Model for Information Systems Strategy Development (EMIS) (Reponen, 1987; 1994), which is an iterative learning based framework for strategy generation. We shall use the model as our hypothetical strategic schema to structure the laboratory experiment. EMIS was originally based on ideas about the strategic use of information technology (Porter, 1980, 1985; Ein-Dor & Seger, 1981; McFarlan & McKenney, 1982; Pyburn, 1983; Wiseman, 1985). Nowadays, a more extensive view of information systems is needed to improve the realisation of competitive advantage from IS solutions.

Since competitive advantage arises from a good understanding and good management of the whole information systems field (Reponen, 1994), the strategy should concentrate on a holistic view of information systems. To meet this objective the IRS generation has been defined in the following way:

Strategic planning of information resources is an interactive learning process for the creation of a strategy for business process development incorporating information technology. The strategy presents plans for information systems design, implementation and operation for this purpose.

During the last decade, IS planning has, in fact, evolved from special target planning into being an integral part of business planning. The competitive aspect has been strongly emphasised and many different planning methods have been used. EMIS has integrated principles of these previous models and focuses on aligning business and IS planning.

The key ideas in organizational information management planning are integration, interaction and learning. It is a process designed to increase the mutual understanding of information management in the minds of the participants. The process is based on the background knowledge of the different participants, and results in a strategy that can be considered as a way of thinking.

A broad viewpoint has to be adopted when examining a strategy. Planning is a learning process by which business people improve their knowledge of IT. The planning process itself should be intuitive but realistic. Managers may be able to think of new ways of using IT, but at the same time they should take care to maintain routine applications. This thinking process should also influence business processes, to enable business people to discover new ideas for their own sphere of responsibility.

The result of the strategy generation process is a strategy that is both a way of thinking and a written report. The strategy can be described as a profile of its components, as illustrated in Figure 1. The next step in a strategy process is its implementation. In this phase it is important to ensure that implementation really does follow the direction of the strategy. If the basic ideas and nature of the strategy have been internalised by the participants of the strategy generation process, implementation will be much easier. These combined steps have been referred to here as the EMIS. It supplies tools and advice on how to create the strategy but is flexible and offers each organization an opportunity to plan individually.

The model has been used in several case organizations during the last few years, and has proved to be a very useful framework for the information management strategy evolution process. This interactive, organized method of generating information management plans has proved to be both a stimulating and supportive tool. Learning was evident in all cases, but utilising the results of learning needed a clear effort. ## The research setting

The research setting is presented in Figure 2. The aim is to examine the impact of personality type on IR strategy formation and the resulting IR strategy profile. General managers form the object group of the study and they are classified into different personality types using the Briggs and Myer MBTI-test. As the test has four different dimensions with two alternatives to each, there is a total of sixteen different personality types. These are linked to cognitive maps of the creation of IR strategies. A repertory grid technique is then used to capture the strength and direction of relationships between the components of IR strategy.

Two groups, based on personality type, were given the same task: to form an IR strategy profile based on a case description using the EMIS model as a conceptual framework. It is assumed that the IR strategy profiles produced by these groups will differ from each other even if the task and method of analysis are given. As personality type has also some impact on the thinking and communication processes in problem solving situations, it is also assumed that the strategy formation process will differ. However, the cognitive maps of individual group members are expected to be quite similar within both groups.

The repertory grids are based on the EMIS framework for strategy generation. A tentative grid is first created by the authors which is then tested empirically. The EMIS framework has been designed based on action research in several case organizations. Its validity has been shown in those case examples (Reponen, 1994). We will therefore make an assumption that EMIS elements are generic to IRS generation.

The generic dimensions of the grids are strategic objectives and strategic means. By strategic objectives we mean the different targeted changes to improve business processes using IT. Examples of such objectives are: speeding up internal processes, optimising transportation systems, and collecting information on competitive position.

Strategic means are the different ways of realising the intended changes. In the EMIS model there are six areas of strategy realisation: improving competitiveness through technology, scheduling application development, designing a rough software/hardware architecture, setting target levels for expected benefits, presenting plans for investments, and organizing decision making on IS services. Strategic planning is a process to link means and objectives together.

A tentative grid for IRS generation is then a matrix presenting suggestions for strategic objectives and strategic means, but with space for a person's own objectives and means. These cognitive maps have been empirically tested in part. The objective has been to test the applicability of the proposed approach in a real world environment. In the next section this empirical experiment is described in greater detail.

## Empirical experimentation with IRS formulation

An empirical test was made on the whole population of managers in the Executive Education Programme at Turku School of Economics and Business Administration. The 20 participants represented different industries in both the service and manufacturing sectors. The sample is representative of upper middle management rather than random.

The experimentation was a closing part of the information management course and all the managers had been taught the structure of the EMIS model and its use in planning. To standardise the decision making environment they were all given a task based on the same written case: Aalsmeer Flower Auction (Heijns & Watson, 1986). Aalsmeer is the largest flower auction in the world, distributing large quantities of flowers to different destinations every day. Its operations are already heavily automated and the task was to find new potential targets for IT deployment to gain competitive advantage.

As the number of participants were limited, we were not able to test all sixteen possible personality types. We therefore decided to concentrate on the most interesting groups. According to an extensive survey (Heikkila, 1994) a visionary, intuitive-type manager is regarded as a typical future executive. His innovativeness and ability to construct cohesive vision from weak environmental signals supports future corporate success. They are, as a group, extroverts who are very interested in consulting with others to gain a fuller picture of the decision situation. Stimulus comes from without and energy is drawn from interaction.

Dominant, sensing-type managers currently serve in traditional organizations, where change has been predictable and the forecasting task fairly straightforward (Heikkila, 1994). Most of their managerial energy is drawn from inner sources: emotions, impressions and self-image. They rely on practical information and current situations, and the information they use in their decision making process is thus mostly factual and concrete. In this study we have used these two (intuitive and sensing) managerial archetypes to divide the respondents into observable groups. It was therefore possible to focus our attention on the behaviour patterns of current and future dominant managerial types and how they formulate strategic decisions.

The twenty managers were first tested with the MBTI-method and divided into two groups on the basis of their personality description. The intuitive group consisted of twelve managers and the sensing group the remaining eight.

The laboratory test was carried out in two phases. First, the intuitive and sensing groups worked in separate rooms to design a strategy for the case organization. Immediately afterwards, each participant filled in a cross-impact matrix of a cognitive map individually. The objective of the first phase was to observe how these two groups behaved in a team work situation, and to find out if their strategy suggestions would be different. After finishing the group work, each participant was given a tentative cognitive map of the strategy formulation process. They were asked to give an estimate of the weights for different relationships in the map. The objective of this second phase was to create a cognitive map of IRS generation. The first phase guided participants' thoughts on the IRS process and in the second phase an attempt was made to reveal this insight.

The final conclusions were drawn from both personal observations during strategy generation and from the participants' written material. A more detailed description of the tests now follows.

## Group work on IRS

The first phase of the laboratory experiment consisted of group work on the IRS. The assignment was to create an IRS for Aalsmeer and each group had an assistant (researcher), whose task was to help the group get started, and also to observe working methods and the contents of discussion. The researchers made notes of how the work progressed and how the final results were obtained. The groups had two hours in which to make their suggestions for the strategy.

The researchers expected major differences in the test results but the actual test situation also revealed many interesting differences in behaviour between the groups. The intuitive group was very enthusiastic and given to lively discussions. They did not pay much attention to instructions or to organizing their work. The first phase was to discover the core elements of the business. Many activities described in the case were questioned. Many new ideas were presented but forming a comprehensive plan for the case company was quite problematic. The coordination and control of the group's work was also quite difficult. They were very active and positive but did not pay much attention to the timetable or to the relevance of the ideas. The ideas were truly intuitive and other group members made many comments on the new ideas. The idea generation process was quite long, so the group found it difficult to finish the task in the time allowed. Despite coordination problems, the group was able to present several new areas of development for the case company, which reflected well on the group's creativity.

The sensing group read the instructions through carefully. They then elected a chairman and a secretary, whose job was to note down all new ideas. These preparatory actions took only a few minutes. The group systematically started to analyse different components of the case company's business activities. They had no problem in reaching a consensus concerning the nature of the business. They tried to visualise different activities and assessed potential development possibilities one after another in a logical sequence. If someone tried to deviate from the subject or present ideas considered too unrealistic, the group quickly brought him or her back to earth.

All opinions or ideas were reasoned out very explicitly; few arguments were intuitive. Discussion was therefore well coordinated and civilised. There were never two people speaking at the same time. The noise made by the intuitive group actually disturbed the sensing group (the rooms were separated but it was possible to hear loud discussion from one room to another). The group had no problem with the given schedule, they actually finished the task in 45 minutes. The sensing group was effective and fast, but not quite as creative as the intuitive group. They produced fewer new ideas and focused mainly on technical matters (eg robotics, global networks). Group members did not want to present any new ideas until they were sure they were relevant to the case. Other group members neither criticised nor commented very much on new ideas. They were simply either accepted or rejected. The group was quite satisfied with the final strategic plan.

These observations confirm the results of the MBTI test. Sensing-type individuals were expected to employ quite structured working methods and work efficiently, while the intuitive group were expected to be more creative.

## Creating cognitive maps

Cognitive maps were created using Eden's (1988, see also Kelly, 1955) repertory grid technique. In creating the maps there were several techniques to choose from: text analysis, interviewing, open-ended questioning or repertory grids. The drawbacks of the first three techniques relate to people's abilities to assess their own cognitive structures consciously. For example, open-ended interviews were rejected, since most people are not conscious of the way they cognitively organize and could not give valid and reliable answers to direct open-ended questions (see Reger, 1990).

The repertory grid technique is a set of innovative interview techniques, which elicit responses in a semi-structured manner; however the content and specific structure of responses may vary by respondent. Repertory grid data can be analysed using both qualitative and quantitative methods (Reger, 1990). The researcher can thus analyse the similarities and differences that constitute the constructs a respondent uses to differentiate among elements. Brown (1992) compared repertory grid technique and cognitive mapping. She suggests that cognitive mapping is more ‘user-friendly’, ie respondents like it more and it produces fewer spoiled datasets. However, repertory grids produce more comparable data. This feature is very important in our study, as we aim to compare groups. The three data collection decisions when designing a study using the repertory grid are methods for: selecting elements for the grid, eliciting constructs, and eliciting perceptions of elements in terms of constructs.

In selecting the elements, the researchers have basically two choices: either to provide the elements themselves (from some theoretical base) or to ask the respondents to provide them. In this research setting, we chose a mixed strategy and provide some elements based on the EMIS model, leaving the respondents to fill in any gaps. This strategy was selected to ensure that all relevant elements would be included in the research setting. In eliciting the constructs (strategic choices) based on these elements we chose the full context form, which means that all elements of interest are presented to the respondents at one time. We thus aimed at understanding the cognitive groupings of separate answers and the strategic choices the groups made. The perceptions of elements were drawn up by researchers after the data were collected. These assessments were made on the basis of observation reports and qualitative data analysis.

In analysing the repertory grids, the researchers were interested both in individual and aggregated group maps. It has been said that effective interaction between the members of an organization or problem solving team depends upon the extent to which they can each understand how the others interpret the situation in hand (Eden, 1988). In other words, the members of the group need to develop a common way of construing future events and making meaningful estimates of the whole problem-solving situation. The aggregated maps were thought to reveal this common way of thinking and provide more information on the impact of personality on the decision making process.

The individual cognitive maps were treated as the 'grounded' data for the quantitative construction of the aggregated maps. For further analysis, the means and deviations were calculated. In most representations of the causality relations between events and concepts, the simple notations of plus (+), signifying a positive relationship, minus (−), signifying a negative relationship, and zero (0), signifying no relationship, are sufficient (Weick & Bougon, 1986; Eden, 1988), since decision makers typically do not use a more refined set of gradations than this. However, these notations are usually limited to only simple logical relations and so weighted or fuzzy maps are also used to capture a richer picture of the decision making situation and bases for choice (Lee et al, 1992). These maps differ from simple maps in describing causal relations with numeric specifiers such as +2, +1, 0, -1, -2 or descriptive terms like 'little', 'somewhat', 'much' and 'very much'. The biggest problem with more sophisticated maps and relations is that their usage requires a more controlled test-bed and higher commitment from respondents. These methods otherwise fall short in their ability to capture the essence of the situation and the result remains fuzzy. On the other hand, when successful, the outcomes offer more precise information for researchers.

In this experiment the group members had to think of the strategic options as a means in the strategic planning process. They were then given four goals that the organization could aim at in order to develop business activities through IT usage. They were also asked to invent further goals. The goals given were: the improvement of the flower broker system, the improvement of inbound and outbound logistics, better market information usage and more effective internal logistics at the marketplace. The managers were then asked to estimate the relationship between goals and means, for example between internal logistics and distributed IS architecture, using the cause-effect matrices. If they considered the relationship strongly positive (eg distributed architecture enforces internal logistics) they gave it a value of two in the matrix. The scale was as follows:

2 strong positive relationship -2 strong negative

1 weak positive relationship -1 weak negative relationship

0 no relationship

The personal matrices were combined by calculating their mean values. The resulting matrices are presented in the Appendix.

## The revised tentative cognitive maps

Before the actual analysis of the collected material, the researchers designed a tentative cognitive map of the strategy generation process, in order to ensure that all relevant elements of the decision situation would be included in the analysis. The aggregated group maps were then generated and compared with each other. Data analysis will continue during the next few months and we therefore prefer to refer to the maps as tentative.

We have used certain basic rules in describing and evaluating group consensus in IRS decision making. As has been stated, business activity goals were related to IT usage and managers were asked to evaluate EMIS model options against these goals. In other words, they were asked to rank these strategic means and estimate their usability in achieving the determined goals.

The maps were created through a quantitative analysis of the aggregated group data. All those means that had at least a weak relationship to goals (value over $\pm0\cdot4$ ) were accepted as a meaningful part of the map. The number of the relations was thus reduced to a manageable amount, and evaluations were focused on the most interesting parts of the quantitative data. The tentative group maps are presented in Figures 3 and 4.

The work of the sensing group was very focused on key business areas and they went through the test situation relying heavily on logic and strict discipline. The selection of means is internally very cohesive and prioritisation in the implementation process is seen as a fairly easy task for these managers.

## Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to discover the relationship between a decision maker's personality and the way he outlines an information resource strategy. The objective was to confirm that personality has a clear influence on both the contents of the strategy and the way strategies are generated. In achieving this we used cognitive mapping techniques together with the EMIS model to reveal these relationships. We found that the mapping technique is a relevant method in addressing the topic.

In our empirical test of twenty managers we found evidence of our basic hypothesis. As a result of our test and data analysis we thus conclude that personality has significant effects on strategy formulation. The two groups (intuitive–extrovert and sensing–introvert) behaved differently and this resulted in different priorities for the development projects. The differences were consistent with the MBTI test results (ie groups behaved as expected on the basis of the MBTI test).

Since people use and perceive the frameworks of strategic planning differently, these models alone cannot solve problems of the planning process. Different perceptions cause many variations in the process itself in spite of the use of a detailed model. Therefore, if we want to support both innovativeness and efficiency in the strategy process, we should get various personalities involved in the planning task. It seems important to know the personality types in an organization and to try to have a balanced project organization for strategy generation.
