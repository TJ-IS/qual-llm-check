# End user computing sophistication and success in small firms

N Zinatelli $^{1}$ , PB Cragg $^{2}$ and ALM Cavaye $^{3}$

$^{1}$ Sierra Systems Consultants Inc, Vancouver, Canada; $^{2}$ AFIS, University of Canterbury, Private Bag 4800, Christchurch, New Zealand; and $^{3}$ Department of System Engineers, Delft University of Technology, Delft, The Netherlands

End user computing (EUC) is becoming an increasingly important activity in small firms. This paper explores factors influencing the sophistication and success of EUC in small businesses. Case studies of eight small manufacturing firms revealed both positive and negative influences of intra-organizational and extra-organizational factors on EUC sophistication and success. In addition, the case study results indicate a strong need for training and both internal and external EUC support for small firms.

## Introduction

Although many studies have focused on end user computing (EUC) in large organizations, there has been relatively little research concerning this topic in the small business environment (Raymond, 1990a). Furthermore, information systems (IS) success has been studied in small firms, but these studies have not provided empirical evidence of factors contributing to EUC success. As there is evidence that EUC is becoming an important activity in small businesses (Raymond & Bergeron, 1992), it is important to develop an understanding of the factors that affect EUC success: only once facilitators and inhibitors are properly understood, can small firms benefit from the full potential of computers.

In a paper on the foundations and directions for research on EUC, Raymond (1990b) proposed that different characteristics of small firms should be examined in order to determine their potential impact on EUC in small firms. The authors set out to do just that, ie, to develop a comprehensive framework for defining the key intra-organizational and extra-organizational factors contributing to EUC sophistication and success in small firms.

The terms ‘small firms’ and ‘end user computing’ both need clarification from the outset as there is little consensus regarding their definition. The term ‘small firm’ is nebulous since firm characteristics which identify it as ‘large’ or ‘small’ differ in different parts of the world. Many studies define small firms as having fewer than 250 employees (Raymond, 1985; DeLone, 1988; Kagan et al, 1990; Raymond & Bergeron, 1992) or fewer than 100 employees (Soh et al, 1992; Yap et al, 1992). However, the present study was carried out in New Zealand.

In the New Zealand context manufacturing firms are defined as ‘small’ when they have fewer than 50 employees; firms are ‘very small’ when they have fewer than 20 employees (Bollard, 1988).

A widely accepted classification of end users is provided by Rockart and Flannery (1983). They identify six types of end user ranging from professional programmers to non-programming computer users working in easy-to-use, menu-driven environments. Within small firms there are often no professional programmers or computer support personnel. For that reason EUC in small firms is broadly defined as control by users over their computing needs (Raymond & Bergeron, 1992). In the present study EUC is defined as the optional use of computers by professionals and managers (ie knowledge workers) for work-related purposes. This definition includes users of applications developed with a spreadsheet or database package; the definition excludes computer professionals and clerical/secretarial staff since their computer use is typically mandatory.

The paper begins with a brief review of relevant research. A research model is introduced which highlights and summarises previous empirical findings concerning EUC success factors. Next, the research method for this study is described. Case study results are then presented and discussed. As a result of case findings, a revised and extended research model is proposed.

## Literature Overview

The relevant literature includes research on the EUC phenomenon and studies discussing computing in the small firms context.

According to Cooley et al (1987), it is an oversimplification to assume that the computing environment of small firms is equivalent to that of large firms. Empirical evidence (DeLone, 1981; 1988) shows that firm size does make a difference when it comes to firms managing their computer resources; small firms face different computer-related opportunities and problems. Small firms have their own unique qualities in terms of their environment, structure, psycho-sociological climate, management, and IS usage (Raymond, 1985).

Various studies of small firms have identified factors that influence small firm computing. These factors include: top manager involvement (DeLone, 1988; Yap et al, 1992; Cragg & King, 1993), computer literacy (Malone, 1985; DeLone, 1988), internal and external support (Raymond, 1990a; Yap et al, 1992), experience (Amoroso, 1986; Montazemi, 1988), and training (Raymond, 1988; Raymond & Bergeron, 1992). In addition, several studies suggest that the role of the external environment has an important influence on computing in small firms (Kole, 1983; Martin, 1989; Raymond, 1990b).

A few studies have looked specifically at various aspects of EUC in small businesses. Raymond (1987) reported that firms in which EUC was present were larger in size and more decentralised. Montazemi (1987, 1988) found that the level of end user computer literacy was low in small firms and end users had difficulty developing complex applications. Raymond and Bergeron (1992) concluded that small business managers who developed their own analysis-type, spreadsheet-based applications experienced higher levels of decision-making satisfaction. Cragg and King (1993) found little EUC growth had occurred over time, with end users lacking training and support, as well as appropriate hardware and software.

Much of the previous research on EUC has been set in large firms and has focused on identifying factors influencing EUC success (Amoroso, 1986; Rivard & Huff, 1988; Igbaria et al, 1989; Igbaria, 1990; Amoroso & Cheney, 1991; Igbaria & Zviran, 1991; Abdul-Gader, 1992; Blili, 1992). More recently, some studies have focused on EUC sophistication (Blili, 1992; Marcolin et al, 1992; 1993). These numerous studies have identified both intra-organizational and extra-organizational factors. This literature is discussed more fully in the next section, including the variables: computer experience, computer training, and support.

## Research model

By combining the empirical findings from the various areas in the literature, it was possible to build a preliminary model showing factors influencing EUC success in small firms. Figure 1 presents the preliminary model which was used to guide data collection during the empirical component of this study.

The specific form of the model was derived from recommendations by Raymond (1990a,b), Blili (1992) and Marcolin et al (1992; 1993). Raymond encouraged the study of intra-organizational as well as extra-organizational factors influencing EUC success. Blili and Marcolin highlight the relationship between EUC sophistication and success. Internal and external factors could help build EUC sophistication and, in turn influence EUC success for the organization. Hence the model would suggest that:

\- internal factors affect EUC sophistication and success;

● external factors affect EUC sophistication and success;

\- EUC sophistication affects EUC success.

Computer experience has been found to increase end user information satisfaction and application utilisation (Amoroso, 1986), computer use (Igbaria et al, 1989) and EUC effectiveness (Igbaria, 1990). End user literacy among users in small firms generates higher user satisfaction (Montazemi, 1988).

Users (Igbaria et al, 1989) and small business managers (Raymond, 1988) who attend training courses tend to use more varied and sophisticated applications more often. Raymond and Bergeron (1992) found that microcomputer training had a significant effect on the decision-making satisfaction of small firm managers who developed their own applications.

EUC tools include hardware and software. End users need tools which are accessible, user-friendly, powerful, flexible and effectively supported by the vendor (Amoroso, 1986). End user information satisfaction increases when such tools are available (Rivard & Huff, 1988; Amoroso & Cheney, 1991).

Greater (perceived) internal EUC support leads to increased end user information satisfaction and application utilisation (Amoroso, 1986; Amoroso & Cheney, 1991). Igbaria (1990) reported a direct positive relationship between information centre support and EUC effectiveness.

Igbaria (1990) found that task structure, which is the extent to which tasks have clearly defined procedures, is positively related to computer usage, user satisfaction, and perceived changes in users' jobs.

Top management support, including encouragement and commitment to the allocation of resources, is positively related to EUC effectiveness (Igbaria, 1990) and success (Abdul-Gader, 1992). In small firms, successful use of computers is strongly linked to CEO knowledge of computers and active involvement with computerisation of the firm (DeLone, 1988; Yap et al, 1992).

Another source of support for EUC comes from outside the organization. External EUC support plays an important role in the computerisation of small firms (Kole, 1983). Yap et al (1992) reported that the quality of a consultant (in terms of experience, capability and effectiveness) and the level of vendor support are both positively associated with computing success in small firms.

Recent studies suggest that EUC sophistication may influence EUC success in organizations (Blili, 1992; Marcolin et al, 1992; 1993). EUC sophistication was therefore viewed as an intermediary factor, and defined as how capable and well-prepared an individual is for conducting EUC activities. Blili (1992) used utilisation and application sophistication to measure EUC sophistication levels. Marcolin et al (1992) identified breadth, depth, and finesse as three dimensions of EUC sophistication.

DeLone and McLean (1992) showed that IS researchers have used many variables to measure IS success and that IS is a multi-dimensional variable. Similarly, many approaches have been used to measure the success of EUC in large organizations. The two most widely used measures of EUC success are application utilisation and user information satisfaction (Amoroso, 1986; Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988; Rivard & Huff, 1988; Igbaria, 1990; Amoroso & Cheney, 1991; Igbaria & Zviran, 1991; Raymond & Bergeron, 1992). According to DeLone and McLean (1992), IS impact on the organization is probably the most difficult to define. This study explored ‘perceived impact’ as a relatively new and potentially important measure of EUC success (Blili, 1992).

The major purpose of the study was to identify factors that influence success, rather than study success itself. The literature had provided considerable guidance, yet much of this was untested in small firms. Therefore, at this stage, the primary research questions were formulated as:

1. What intra-organizational factors influence EUC sophistication and success in small firms?

2. What extra-organizational factors influence EUC sophistication and success in small firms?

## Research method

The qualitative method of case study research is particularly appropriate for studying phenomena that are not supported by a strong theoretical base (Yin, 1989b). In addition, Yin (1989b) suggests that case studies in IS are an appropriate research strategy when the objective is to study contemporary events in their natural settings and when it is not necessary to control behavioural events. Since all of these conditions are true for EUC in small firms, a case study research strategy was chosen for the data collection in this study.

The overall case study design included a single, exploratory, in-depth pilot case study followed by a more explanatory, cross-case analysis of eight firms (Yin, 1989a; Gable, 1992). The single pilot case study was used to refine data collection plans and to provide insight into the basic issues being studied. The investigation of the pilot case was much broader and less focused than the study of the multiple cases that followed.

The study was carried out in Christchurch, New Zealand. The case firms were selected according to the criteria listed in Table 1.

The entire sample was not selected at the beginning of the study (Marshall & Rossman, 1989). Rather, sampling was part of a continuous process of development of hypotheses and the search for negative cases that would disprove those hypotheses (Eisenhardt 1989; Pettigrew 1989). An attempt was made to investigate firms that reflected various sizes; firms that involved internal EUC support or external EUC support or both; firms with different levels of computer experience; and firms which were either well-established and more recently established.

Data was primarily collected from face-to-face interviews with one or more of the following individuals in each firm: a top manager, the individual responsible for computers, and all command level users and end user programmers (Rockart & Flannery, 1983). In some firms, one person fitted all three categories. In other firms, several different people were interviewed. In total, nineteen people from eight different companies were interviewed. Only thirteen of the people interviewed were either command level or end user programmers. Included in the sample were eight top managers, three of whom were not computer users.

The data collection was based on the approach advocated by Yin (1989a). An interview guide was constructed to investigate the constructs and relationships indicated in the preliminary research model (Figure 1). The interviews consisted primarily of open-ended questions. Responses were probed at the time to gather detail. Since it was important to make certain that the respondent's comments and perceptions were recorded accurately, all interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed. Questionnaires were not used in the interviews. Instead, the transcriptions for each firm were used by the researcher to code each variable, based on the literature. If clarification was required, then follow-up phone calls were made.

The interviews and transcriptions were also used to identify additional variables that could potentially influence EUC sophistication and success. A similar data collection and coding process was used for these new variables as for the variables in Figure 1. However, questions and coding were based on additional literature. The new factors were analysed for all cases (Miles & Huberman, 1984; Reich & Benbasat, 1990).

## Case study results

Table 2 contains descriptive information on the companies and end users included in the sample. The results are based on the data collected from eight case studies (referred to as firms A, B, etc). All firms had between twenty to fifty employees, with the exception of one firm which had twelve employees. Several other very small firms were contacted but could not be included in the sample because of their limited use of computers.

Very little EUC existed in the firms. As shown in Table 2, each firm had at most two end users. The case studies revealed that one individual in each firm was clearly the most knowledgeable user of computer resources. Typically this ‘EUC specialist’ was either a manager or accountant and he/she was responsible for managing computer resources and providing computer support for other users. None of the firms employed a full-time EUC specialist.

## EUC sophistication and EUC success

## EUC sophistication in the case firms

The case studies revealed the following four dimensions of EUC sophistication as being relevant in small firms. A combination of these four dimensions was identified as an appropriate EUC sophistication measure for small firms.

1. Breadth of end user skills and knowledge (Blili, 1992). Breadth reflects the range of software being used (see appendix), as well as the range of abilities with this software; similarly for EUC hardware and EUC concepts.

2. Finesse (Marcolin et al, 1992; 1993). Four users were highly self-sufficient and demonstrated an ability to learn to use new software. Three users were less self-sufficient, and one user was limited to items that had been covered in a course.

3. Application utilisation (Blili, 1992). All users, except C and H, used computers on a daily basis for at least 50% of their work hours.

4. End user type (Rockart & Flannery, 1983). Three end-user programmer/support, two command level/support, and three command level only.

Table 3 shows the level of EUC sophistication found for each of the specialists in the case firms. The EUC specialists (referred to as Users A, B, C, etc) are ranked in order of highest to lowest EUC sophistication. User A was the most sophisticated end user because this individual ranked highly on all four dimensions. User H was the least sophisticated end user and had low rankings on all four dimensions.

The influence of intra-organizational factors on EUC success

It was found that intra-organizational factors could be categorised into three main categories: individual, technical, and organizational factors. Results relating to factors within each of the three categories are discussed below.

Two of these variables were supported by the case study data: computer experience and computer training; the third one, task structure, was not supported. Other individual factors, which emerged from the case study data as having an influence on EUC sophistication and success, included: education, interest in computers, lack of time, and outside work time invested. Computer experience had a positive effect on EUC sophistication and success. With the exception of user H, all of the EUC specialists had at least five years of computer experience.

Lack of computer training had a negative influence on EUC sophistication and success. Although all users had received some computer training in their careers, most had no training on end user tools such as spreadsheets and databases. Many of the users complained about having great difficulties and expressed frustration at not being able to accomplish desired tasks on the computer. Users were frustrated with wasting time in their attempts to understand applications and stated that they would often give up on particular tasks.

The level to which tasks have clearly defined procedures had no influence on EUC sophistication and success. Since six of the EUC specialists held managerial positions, computers were used primarily to support unstructured managerial tasks. Raymond and Bergeron (1992) reported similar findings in their study of managerial EUC. Users D and G, in their role as accountants, performed structured tasks using accounting packages. However, user D was also involved with many unstructured tasks in that he developed applications for use by other end users in his firm.

Education had a positive influence on EUC sophistication and success for EUC specialists with university degrees (users A, D, E, F, G). Although these users had minimal exposure to computers and programming languages at university, all of them commented on the value of that exposure in giving them some background in computing and as an important influence on their computing ability. The users also identified their university education as providing them with the ability to 'learn how to learn' which led them to become self-taught computer users. Users B, C, and H had no exposure to computers prior to job-related computer use.

High interest in computers had a positive influence on EUC sophistication and success. All of the EUC specialists were highly enthusiastic and motivated computer users. Users described computers as being interesting, enjoyable, helpful, intriguing, fascinating, and challenging. Amoroso and Cheney (1991) reported similar results in that users who were highly motivated in developing applications had higher application utilisation rates.

Lack of time available for EUC had a negative influence on EUC sophistication and success. This influence is not surprising since the primary functional role of the EUC specialist was either as manager or accountant. Users complained about not having enough time during business hours to become more competent computer users. EUC specialists could see the potential use of computers to support many new tasks; however, they lacked the time required to learn new software tools and to develop applications to support more of their tasks.

Outside work time invested on computers had a positive influence on EUC sophistication and success. All of the EUC specialists invested considerable time outside normal work hours on developing their self-taught computer skills. Users A, B, C, D, and E described their computer learning experiences as being intensive self-study during evenings and on weekends over a period of several years. Users F, G, and H, who had lower levels of EUC sophistication and success, invested outside work time in computers but did so to a lesser extent.

## The influence of technical factors

Both of the technical factors included in the research model, EUC tools and internal EUC support, had an influence on EUC sophistication and success.

The availability of quality EUC tools had a positive influence on EUC sophistication and success. All but one user had sole use of a personal computer in their office. Appropriate tools were easily accessible, helped in part by the user's active involvement in the firm's computer-related purchase decisions. As a result, their current hardware and software needs were being met. The availability of these quality EUC tools made it possible for users to satisfactorily use a range of computing tools to assist with their work.

While quality EUC tools were available, very little internal EUC support was available. Only two users could turn to others within their firm for support. This support was not planned as EUC support. For one user, the company accountant could assist with spreadsheets. In addition, this user could consult full-time programmers who were employed to programme the company's product. Another firm had recently established a computer sales division with two full-time staff who provided their user with limited EUC support.

This lack of internal support had a negative influence on EUC sophistication and success. The two users who had internal support, viewed support as very helpful in sorting out queries and problems, as well as a sounding board for new ideas. There was strong evidence of other users being frustrated and wasting time due to a lack of support. For example, it took longer for users to become familiar with existing features of a package and to recognise they needed additional tools. Some were not aware of existing features, and potentially more efficient ways of operating.

## The influence of organizational factors

Top management support was found to have a positive influence on EUC sophistication and success in most firms. Financial constraints emerged as another new factor as having a negative impact on EUC sophistication and success.

Top management support, in terms of commitment to allocation of resources and top management involvement had a positive influence on EUC sophistication and success. Top managers in firms A, B, C, E, F, and G stressed the importance of computers and stated that although small firm computing requires a strong financial commitment, the overall benefits outweighed the costs. In contrast, top managers in firms D and H were very negative about computers and complained about the extremely high costs associated with computers. As a result, users in firms D and H had some difficulty in obtaining financial support for their EUC efforts.

High levels of top management involvement (ie the top manager was an EUC specialist or a direct, interested computer user) had a positive influence on EUC sophistication and success in five of the eight firms. Firms C, D, and H had top managers who knew little of computers; lack of interest and low involvement of top managers in these three firms resulted in a negative influence on the EUC sophistication and success of users C, D, and H.

Financial constraints had a negative influence on EUC sophistication and success. The following negative influences, which were apparent in most firms, were a direct result of small firm financial constraints:

1. Lack of internal EUC support: None of the firms employed a full-time computer professional since having an internal EUC specialist was seen as a cheaper alternative.

2. Lack of external EUC support: The main source of EUC support was informal help from friends.

3. Lack of new application development: In many firms, EUC specialists did not have time available for new application development; firms did not get outside help because they could not afford it.

4. Lack of EUC budget plans: Allocation of financial resources to computers was dependent on whether the firm had a strong financial year.

5. Used equipment purchases: Used hardware and software were purchased by two of the firms from friends or acquaintances.

## The influence of extra-organizational factors on EUC success

External EUC support was expected to influence EUC sophistication and success. The cases identified two dimensions of this variable: (1) quality of available external EUC support (which had a positive influence); and (2) lack of availability of (or access to) external EUC support (which had a negative influence). Each dimension is discussed below.

With the exception of user A, all users relied on friends and acquaintances as their main source of EUC support. Users obtained help from this source in order to save costs and because of the trust they had in the quality of support from people known to them on a personal basis.

A major source of external support was vendors. Most firms obtained useful knowledge from vendors and were very satisfied with the vendor support they had received. However, users B and C had negative experiences with their vendors and as a result were very sceptical of outside help. Another source of outside help, consultants, was not often used. Only three of the firms had ever engaged a consultant; two of these were very positive about the consultant support they had received, the third firm felt that a lot of time and money had been wasted and that the quality of the consultant support had been extremely poor. One user received support through a computer club; another had attended classes to learn about spreadsheets.

Lack of external EUC support had a negative influence on EUC sophistication and success. Three main reasons surfaced on why firms obtained very little external EUC support: (1) high costs of external support; (2)

lack of trust in external support and training; and (3) low awareness of external EUC support services. The perceptions of users were frequently influenced by earlier negative experiences with vendors regarding accounting packages. In general, support for standard packages, such as accounting and integrated manufacturing packages, was available from vendors. However, support for advanced features of EUC tools was reported as very limited, for example with spreadsheets, databases, fourth generation languages, and programming languages. As a result, the only form of support for these tools was informal help from friends and acquaintances.

## Discussion

Figure 1 was used as the preliminary research model to guide an investigation into factors that influence EUC sophistication and success in the small firm context.

EUC sophistication and success was found to be influenced by both intra-organizational and extra-organizational factors. Intra-organizational factors could be categorised into individual, technical and organizational factors. Extra-organizational factors related to the external support for EUC in small firms. The case results indicate that small firms experience a lack of external support, partly because of the relatively high cost of professional support and partly because of the low awareness of potential support services available to small firms. Since very few studies have investigated the influence of the impact of extra-organizational factors on EUC success, these findings are particularly important.

The study investigated to what extent the various factors had a positive or negative influence on EUC success. The case results indicate that high interest in EUC, high investment of outside work time, and top management support have a strong, positive influence on EUC sophistication and success. Factors which had a strong inhibiting effect included: lack of computer training, lack of both internal and external EUC support, and financial constraints. Some of the original factors were confirmed, others were not supported, and new factors emerged from the case study research. Figure 2 presents a revised research model based on the results. The variables that did not appear in the preliminary version are indicated with a (\*) in Figure 2.

Figure 2 presents a revised research model showing the major factors influencing EUC sophistication and success in small firms. The factors should be validated in settings other than manufacturing firms and in countries other than New Zealand. To test the generalisability of the revised research model, a quantitative survey research strategy is required. Once the generalisability of the model has been tested, such a model could be used to indicate the necessary conditions for EUC sophistication and success in small firms.

Researchers must decide what EUC sophistication and success variables to measure. As a step towards this, two relatively new constructs in the EUC literature were examined in this study: (1) EUC sophistication and (2) perceived impact as a measure of EUC success. Both were found to be relevant and worthy of further research to develop valid and reliable instruments for their measurement. In particular, the measures must be tested in both large and small firms to ensure the appropriateness of these measures to both organizational contexts.

According to the case data, many of the factors that influenced EUC sophistication and success were similar to the factors identified in large organizations, showing that studies in large firms can assist researchers of small firms. However, the inhibiting factors that emerged from the study data (including lack of time, financial constraints, and lack of internal and external EUC support) highlight the fact that small firms have special needs due to their unique organizational characteristics. Small firms do not often have computing expertise within their organization and have to draw on expertise from their external environment (Raymond, 1990b). And yet, small firms were found to avoid seeking professional, external expertise because of its high cost and undefined business value. This may indicate a need for new roles and tasks for computer consultants (Gable, 1991), vendors, small business centres, and tertiary institutions.

## Conclusions

This study is the first to research factors influencing EUC sophistication and success in small firms. Case studies of eight small manufacturing firms revealed both positive and negative influences of individual, technical, organizational, and external factors on EUC sophistication and success. In addition, the results suggest a positive correlation between EUC sophistication; and EUC success.

The studies revealed low levels of both internal and external EUC support in all eight firms. There is a strong need for support in training, planning, and application development in small firms. Financial constraints will most likely continue to prevent many small firms from increasing internal EUC expertise levels through additional staff, or to turn to external experts such as consultants and vendors. However, relationships with others could be developed. For example, the current reliance on informal contacts could become more formalised through developing training networks, possibly with the assistance of small business development centres or industry associations.
