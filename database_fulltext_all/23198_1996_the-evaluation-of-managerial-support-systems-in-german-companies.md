# The evaluation of managerial support systems in German companies

R Vetschera and H Walterscheid

Faculty of Economics and Statistics, University of Konstanz, PO Box 5560 D 130, D-78434 Konstanz, Germany

The development and evaluation processes of managerial support systems were studied in a survey of German companies. This paper concentrates on influences between variables within the evaluation process and external factors. Within the evaluation process, there was only weak evidence of the relationship between evaluation criteria and methods often postulated in the theoretical literature. In contrast, there were several strong influences from external factors. Interdependencies exist between the type of system (management information system vs decision support system) and the criteria used for evaluation. There is also a strong influence from the initiators of the system on criteria, especially when systems are initiated by functional departments rather than top management. Further connections were established between characteristics of the development process and evaluation methods.

## Introduction

More and more complex information systems have been proposed and developed over time to support management tasks. Concepts like decision support systems, executive information systems or expert systems have achieved broad attention in theory as well as in practice. In this paper, we will use the term managerial support systems (MSS) introduced by Scott Morton (1983) as a general term for all kinds of systems which support managers in solving complex problems.

Given the importance of such systems and the cost of their development, it is not surprising that the evaluation of MSS has received increasing attention, both in research and in practice. Up to now, however, most authors have discussed this topic from a theoretical point of view (Hamilton & Chervany, 1981b; Reimann & Waren, 1985; Adelman, 1989; Bidgoli, 1989; Evans & Riha, 1989; O'Keefe, 1989; Adams et al, 1990; Powell, 1992; Udo, 1992; Farbey et al, 1993; Marsden & Pingry, 1993; Farbey et al, 1994). There are relatively few empirical studies addressing this topic, (eg Kumar, 1990; Bacon, 1992; Szewczak, 1992; Bacon, 1994; Sutherland, 1994). These studies are mainly descriptive, providing an overview of the methods and criteria used for MSS evaluation.

Our present study extends these lines of research by putting the evaluation processes into a broader context, and by considering causal relationships among variables describing the evaluation process as well as external influences. Specifically, we consider external factors like the components of the system, the history of the project or the development process.

The remaining sections of the paper are structured as follows: in the following section we provide an overview of the underlying hypotheses and the design and analysis of our empirical study. The next section presents the results and the last section concludes the paper by summarising the most important results and providing a foundation for further research.

## Design of the study

## Variables and framework of analysis

Figure 1 gives an overview of the framework underlying this study. In the centre of the framework is the evaluation process of an MSS. We characterise this evaluation process along three dimensions. The first dimension is the set of goals to be accomplished by the evaluation. These goals are closely related to the phase of the development process at which the evaluation is performed. Before development of a system begins, evaluation is carried out to determine whether a project should be undertaken at all. During development, a system can be evaluated in order to provide feedback for the system's developers or as a basis for management decisions on the future of the project. Evaluations after completion of the project are performed in order to determine whether the project has achieved its goals and to identify possible unexpected costs or benefits (Serafeimidis & Smithson, 1995).

The second dimension is formed by the set of criteria used in the evaluation process. In the literature, many types of measures for evaluating MSS have been proposed. They can be broadly characterised into technical criteria, eg resources used by the system, monetary economic criteria like costs and 'soft' economic and organizational criteria like information quality (Hamilton & Chervany, 1981b; Meador & Mezger, 1984; Sussman, 1984; Reimann & Waren, 1985; Bidgoli, 1989; Le Blanc & Jelassi, 1989; Mahmood & Sniezek, 1989; Kumar, 1990; Bacon, 1992). While there is some empirical evidence about which criteria are used in practice and are considered important, not much information is available about the context in which these results hold.

The last dimension along which we describe the evaluation process is the set of methods used for evaluation. Many methods have been proposed in the literature (Keen & Scott Morton, 1978; Hamilton & Chervany, 1981a; Sprague & Carlson, 1982; Adelman, 1992; Powell, 1992: Farbey et al, 1994), but recent empirical work suggests that only a few of these methods are actually used (Szewczak, 1992; Tam, 1992; Willcocks & Lester, 1994). The conditions under which certain methods are applied are also a central question of this study.

Our framework indicates that the evaluation process, which is characterised by the three variables mentioned, is influenced by several factors related to the system itself and its organizational environment. A detailed justification of these influences will be given in the next subsection, here we just give a brief overview of the variables involved.

MSS can be classified into several subgroups which possess distinct characteristics (Alter, 1980; Huff et al, 1984; Casimir, 1988; Benbasat & Nault, 1990; Pearson & Shim, 1994). More specifically, systems can be characterised by the components they contain, eg for modelling, data retrieval or presentation.

The second group of influence factors is related to the organizational environment in which a system is developed. While earlier empirical studies have concentrated on general factors describing the organization like size or field of business, which have been related to IS activities in several other empirical studies (Dos Santos, 1989; Udo & Davis, 1992; Seibt et al, 1994), but failed to produce conclusive results, we consider the initiators of the project to be an important factor. Earlier empirical studies have shown that the initiative to develop an MSS can come from various actors like top management, functional management or the information systems department (Huff et al, 1984; Frackmann, 1990; Kemper, 1991; Watson & Rainer, 1991; Rainer et al, 1992). Different initiators formulate different requirements for the systems. Whether these different requirements are also reflected in the evaluation process is analysed in this study.

A third group of influences is related to the development process of the system. The development process can be characterised by several variables. The first variable is the development strategy used. The role and thus the structure of evaluation is different for systems developed following the software lifecycle model and systems developed through prototyping. Other variables by which we describe the development process are the formation and size of a development team, end-user participation in development, and the duration of the development process.

These factors will not affect all components of the evaluation process in the same way. While goals and criteria will probably be strongly influenced by external factors, the methods used for evaluation are, in our view, more dependent on the other internal variables of the evaluation process rather than on external factors. If external factors play a role at all in determining the evaluation method used, we assume that the development process is most likely to do so.

As a further step of analysis, it is not only of interest to determine which evaluation processes are used (and under what circumstances), but also whether the actors involved perceive these processes to be useful. The last stage of our framework therefore consists of the (perceived) quality of the evaluation process.

## Hypotheses

The general concepts identified above are linked by several hypotheses. We can distinguish hypotheses that concern only variables of the evaluation process and hypotheses linking the evaluation process to its environment.

## Dependencies within the evaluation process

The evaluation process is characterised by three variables: the goals of the evaluation process itself (ie why is the system evaluated at all), the criteria (ie along which dimension is the system evaluated) and the methods used (ie how is the system evaluated).

The possible influence of goals of an evaluation onto criteria has been widely discussed in the literature, both from a theoretical and an empirical point of view. Most of this discussion centres around the appropriateness of different criteria for evaluations at different stages of a project's lifecycle (and therefore serving different goals). For example, Serafeimidis and Smithson (1995) state that the 'nature and purpose of evaluation changes according to the stages of the lifecycle' (p 149). At the beginning of the lifecycle, evaluations are performed for choosing among possible projects, at the end, to verify the expected benefits and to discover unexpected benefits. These different goals should lead to the use of different criteria or at least a different weighting of criteria. Farbey et al (1993) argue that initial decisions to carry out a project should be based mainly on 'hard', monetary criteria. In an ex-post evaluation, where one wants to identify unexpected benefits of qualitative nature, 'soft' criteria are seen as more appropriate by these authors. A different point of view is taken by Adelman (1989), who argues that soft criteria are important both at the beginning and the end of the development lifecycle while, during development, more quantitative criteria are needed for effective management and control of the development process. Similarly, in the value analysis of Keen (1981), benefits are considered in the initial stage while, in later stages, costs are considered to be more important.

Empirical evidence of the connection between goals of evaluation and criteria used is available mainly for initial decisions about the start of a project. Both Bacon (1992; 1994) and Willcocks and Lester (1994) found that soft criteria were of great importance in the initial decision to start a project. The use of decision criteria over the entire lifecycle was studied by Tam (1992), who also found a high importance of what he called ‘strategic values’ in the initial decision phase, while decisions about terminating a project were based more on technical criteria and budget criteria were most important in ex-post evaluations. On the other hand, Kumar (1990) found soft criteria like accuracy or timeliness of information to be more important than hard criteria like costs or hardware performance in ex-post evaluations.

The relationship between criteria and evaluation methods has been widely discussed in the literature (Keen, 1981; Pieptea & Anderson, 1987; Money et al, 1988; Toraskar & Joglekar, 1990; Farbey et al, 1994). It has been argued that qualitative, non-monetary benefits (and thus criteria) can best be captured by value-based methods, while for evaluating hard monetary benefits, traditional methods of financial analysis should be used. This hypothesis has been confirmed by the empirical study of Szewczak (1992), who found that in evaluation processes in which hard criteria like effectiveness and utilisation were important, cost-benefit analysis was widely used. On the other hand, in evaluation processes where soft criteria like satisfaction, behavioural and organizational effects were important, informal methods and multi-attribute utility methods were more often used.

In addition to the indirect effect which goals might have on methods via criteria, they also have a direct effect. For example, evaluation methods which require the system to be in actual use can only be applied in ex-post evaluations. Theoretical work aimed at providing recommendations for the use of certain methods uses similar arguments. For example Willcocks and Lester (1994) recommend the ROM method specifically for ex-post evaluations because of its data requirements, and Serafeimidis and Smithson (1995) state that the selection of an evaluation method should depend on factors like inputs required. While no empirical studies deal directly with this question, case studies like Belcher and Watson (1993) confirm our hypothesis. In Belcher and Watson (1993), an executive information system (EIS) was evaluated ex-post, allowing for the use of highly quantifiable criteria like usage statistics which could not be used in ex-ante evaluations.

## The evaluation process and external variables

The framework presented in Figure 1 distinguishes between three groups of external factors: system type and components, initiators, and variables related to the development process.

The criteria used for evaluating MSS play a central role in the entire framework depicted in Figure 1. On one hand, they represent the wishes and demands of the organization towards the system under development. We can therefore expect that organizational characteristics and especially the initiators of the system will have a strong influence on the criteria used for evaluation. It is also plausible that different kinds of systems will be evaluated according to different criteria. In contrast, if criteria represent the wishes of the organization, the causality might also work in the opposite direction: demands from the organization, manifested in the importance of different criteria, lead to the development of different systems.

These influences have been discussed in the theoretical as well as the empirical literature. On the theoretical side, McBride and Fidler (1994) argue that in evaluating EIS, social and contextual criteria should play a dominant role. A more general view of the same aspect is taken by Willcocks and Lester (1994), who use the ladder model of Farbey et al (1993) to classify systems according to goals like increasing efficiency or gaining competitive advantage and recommend the use of different criteria and methods. Taking into account the influence of system type onto evaluation criteria might also help to explain some seemingly paradoxical results found in the empirical literature: Bacon (1994) found that while 88% of the organizations interviewed used ‘support of management decision making’ as a criterion, they used this criterion only in evaluating 29% of their systems. It might well be the case that this criterion is only of importance for certain systems, but the system type was not used as an explanatory variable in that study.

There are also many reasons why initiators of systems could be an important factor in determining the criteria used for evaluation. The importance of adapting management information systems (MIS) and decision support systems (DSS) to the individual users is widely recognised (Bidgoli, 1989). Hamilton and Chervany (1981b) identify different roles in the development process (eg users, developers, or management) and associate them with different criteria for evaluation. Case studies also provide examples of this relationship, for example in the evaluation performed by Matzke (1990), different criteria were used for top management and their supporting staff.

There is less evidence regarding the influence of the development process on criteria used for evaluation. Avison et al (1995) criticise that evaluations during the development process are usually focused narrowly on technical criteria, which in turn might be influenced by properties of the development process. Our statistical analysis confirmed that this relationship is indeed rather weak.

Similar arguments can be made for the goals of the evaluation process, although this issue has been rarely addressed in the literature. It is plausible to assume that the goal of an evaluation will be different for systems which are initiated by different parties. For example, the need for justification of the development effort will be greater when systems are proposed by the IS department than when they are directly requested by the top management. Different development processes might also necessitate different evaluations. For example, in a prototyping process, evaluations are more likely to be performed to decide upon continuation of the project than in a process structured according to the software lifecycle model. The type of system under development could also have an influence on the goals of evaluation. For example, if the type of system is highly innovative for the organization involved, as might be the case for DSS, evaluations to guide the system's developers might become more important.

Since there are convincing theoretical arguments that evaluation methods depend on the criteria used, and that criteria depend on external factors, there will be at least indirect influences of external factors on the choice of methods. However, there are also arguments for direct influences. Akoka (1981) classifies systems according to the different types of management activities supported and the structure of decision processes involved. For systems supporting structured decisions at the operational level, methods focusing on financial criteria like cost benefit analysis or cost effectiveness analysis are proposed, for systems supporting unstructured decisions at the strategic levels, tools like managerial assessment or anecdotal reports should be used. A similar analysis (but with some different conclusions) is carried out by Pieptea and Anderson (1987) and Toraskar and Joglekar (1990). The case study of Belcher and Watson (1993) also states that evaluation of EIS is particularly difficult and requires specific methods.

While the theoretical literature seems to indicate a strong influence of system type on evaluation methods, empirical results so far do not confirm this hypothesis. In the study of Willcocks and Lester (1994), less than 50% of the respondents adjusted the evaluation method used to the type of system being evaluated. Sutherland (1994) found that even 85% of the chief information officers interviewed used the same evaluation method for all kinds of systems. On the other hand, the figure for senior executives was only 18%, indicating that specific methods were used for systems with high strategic value.

Less evidence is available concerning the influence of initiators on methods. Since we are also considering the role of external initiators like parent companies or consultants, these might not only exercise influence on the development of an MSS, but also provide know-how for evaluation. Therefore, the hypothesis of an influence of initiators on methods was also tested.

Influences are more likely from the development process on methods. The development process provides the setting for the evaluation and can thus restrict the applicability of certain methods. For example, Keen's value analysis (Keen, 1981) is an iterative process, which fits better into a prototyping environment than into a development process based on the software lifecycle model.

This view is consistent with the argument of Serafeimidis and Smithson (1995), who state that the choice of evaluation methods should depend on the context of the evaluation, and the development process is part of that context.

## Perceived quality

This last step of our framework was motivated by the study of Szewczak (1992), who found satisfaction with the evaluation process to be higher when ‘advanced’ methods like multi-attribute utility analysis or value analysis were used. This result is consistent with the theoretical literature advocating such methods, especially in evaluation processes where soft criteria are considered important.

## Survey and analysis of results

## Measurement of variables

A written questionnaire was developed to measure the variables. It consisted of a mixture of open and (mostly) closed questions. Since the questionnaire was in German, it is not reproduced here, but can be obtained from the authors.

Variables of the evaluation process: The questions relating to the goals of evaluation contained a predefined catalogue of six possible goals like ‘verification of expectations’ or ‘preparation for future modification’. Although a seventh field was provided to name further goals, this possibility was not used by the respondents. The importance of each goal was ranked by the respondents on a five-point Likert scale, where the end points were coded as ‘very unimportant’ and ‘very important’.

Similarly, a list of eleven criteria was provided and subjects had to indicate whether each criterion was used in the evaluation and if so, how important it was. Again, the possibility to add criteria was not used by the respondents. Both lists of goals and criteria were the same as those used by Kumar (1990).

The set of methods which was provided to the respondents was based on Szewczak (1992). The questionnaire listed six methods ranging from ‘informal talks’ and ‘cost estimates’ to Keen’s value analysis (Keen, 1981). Respondents were asked to indicate whether these methods were used in the evaluation process and how satisfied they were with using these methods.

External variables: The external variables were measured in a similar way. System components were selected from a predefined list and systems were characterised as MIS or DSS according to their components. Systems containing database access and analytical methods were classified as DSS, the remaining systems as MIS. Initiators of the development were to be indicated from a prespecified list containing the possibilities 'IS department', 'functional department', 'top management', 'parent company' and 'external consultant'. This list was obtained by combining the variants used in the empirical studies of Kemper (1991); Rainer et al (1992); Spieß (1993); and Vogel and Wagner (1993). The possibility to indicate other initiators was not used by the respondents.

The development strategy was measured on a five-point scale ranging from ‘prototyping’ to ‘software development lifecycle’. This approach was adapted from Hogue and Watson (1994), who had used a seven-point scale with the same endpoints. Size and structure of the project team were directly asked for, the duration of the process was obtained by asking for the dates of specific milestones like the start of the project or formal completion.

## Survey

After a pretest with three known MSS users, the questionnaire was mailed in February 1994 to 300 large and medium-sized German companies, which were known to be users of MSS or where such use was suspected given the size of the company. A total of fifty-six usable replies was received by April 1994. At 19%, the response rate was similar to the comparable studies of Szewczak (1992) (10%) and Kumar (1990) (21%).

## Statistical analysis

The hypotheses were tested using different methods according to the level of measurement of dependent variables. For dependent variables measured on a cardinal scale, linear models (regression analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used. Probit analysis was used for dependent variables measured on an ordinal scale. All statistical calculations were performed using appropriate routines of the SAS statistical package.

In Table 2, we analyse the relationship of evaluation goals and methods used. The table provides the regression coefficients and significance levels of a probit analysis, in which the probability that a given method was used was regressed on the importance of the evaluation goals. Due to the structure of the model, negative coefficients represent a positive influence.

Only one relationship is significant at the 1% level, two more at the 5% level. Value analysis is more likely to be used when the goal is to initiate modifications of the system. Information evaluations are more often used in evaluations to verify expectations. On the other hand, informal evaluations are less likely to be used in decisions concerning the termination of the project. Since informal evaluation requires actual experience in using the system, this result is consistent with our initial hypothesis.

## Criteria

Table 3 provides the coefficients and significance levels of a probit analysis, in which the probability of using the various methods was regressed on the importance of criteria as indicated by the respondents. The reader should note that due to the large number of independent variables, in this table the dependent variables (ie the methods) form the columns of the table. Negative coefficients again indicate a positive relationship.

Most notable are the effects on monetary-oriented evaluation methods like cost estimates or cost-benefit analysis. When monetary criteria like cost savings or the costs of the system are important, there seems to be a tendency towards also evaluating the benefits which justify the costs. Therefore, cost-benefit analysis is used more often, pure cost estimation less. At first sight, the positive relationship between technical criteria and cost estimates seems to be inconsistent; one would not presume the likelihood of using cost estimates to increase with the importance of a non-monetary criterion. One possible explanation is that technical criteria are usually formulated as the minimum requirements a product has to fulfill to be considered at all, and then the remaining systems are subject to a cost-only evaluation.

These results clearly contradict the hypothesis from the theoretical literature, according to which utility-based methods and value analysis should be used more often when qualitative criteria are important. In fact, the statistical analysis failed to detect any significant influence at all on the use of these two methods.

## External influences on the evaluation process

## System type and components

Table 4 presents the significance levels of an ANOVA, in which the importance of goals and criteria was regressed on indicator variables for the type of system and the presence of certain components. For significant influences, the direction of the influence is indicated by plus or minus signs.

While the statistical analysis detected some significant influences between the components and the goals of evaluation, they mostly cannot be readily interpreted. On the other hand, some of the relationships between components and criteria are more plausible.

First of all, our distinction between MIS and DSS, which was not based on how the respondents called their systems but purely on its components, was clearly confirmed in the importance assigned to the criterion ‘quality of decision support’. For systems characterised as DSS, importance of that criterion was considerably higher than for systems characterised as MIS (on average, 3.45 vs 2.50 on a five-point scale). This evidence strongly supports our classification of systems.

Since the direction of causality between components and criteria is not clear, we will not comment on all significant effects of components on criteria. It should, however, be noted that the strong increase in the importance of the criterion 'security' for systems with access to operative databases (3.36 vs 1.10 on a five-point scale) is very plausible and reflects the necessity to avoid disruptions in the operative systems caused by the MSS. Other influences, while significant, work in the opposite direction than was expected from the theoretical literature. Components like analytical methods, model bases or knowledge-based components can be seen as attributes of advanced, decision-oriented systems of strategic importance. Yet, contrary to expectations, their presence tended to increase the importance of hard, monetary criteria like costs, savings and productivity.

As Figure 1 indicates, the criteria used for the evaluation of systems reflect the demands the organization formulates towards the system and therefore can be seen as factors determining the structure of the system and its components. Table 5 contains the results of a probit analysis in which the probability that a system contains the components listed in the columns of the table were regressed on the importance of the criteria listed in the rows.

At first sight, the significant relationship between timeliness of information and access to operative databases seems rather plausible. However, the direction of this relationship is just the opposite of what would be expected: systems for which timeliness of information is considered important are less likely to contain access to operative databases. Causality is not so clear with respect to the relationship between the importance of costs and the use of operative databases.

The connection between accuracy and the use of models is another example of a causal link from criteria to components: when it is important to obtain accurate information, the system is also more likely to contain some models. Models are also incorporated in the system if MSS are developed in situations in which improving productivity or decision support are important. In all three cases, the coefficients of the probit analysis indicate that the direction of the influence is as expected.

## Initiators

Table 6 presents the results of an ANOVA, in which the importance of evaluation goals and criteria was regressed on indicators of whether the different parties acted as initiators of the project.

Only a few influences could be identified linking the initiators of the project to the goals of evaluation. The most notable influence was an increase in the importance of evaluations for handing over the system to users when the system was initiated by the IS department, which was expected. It is also plausible that the importance of feedback to developers is less when a system is initiated by an external party like the parent company or a consultant, where the specifications of the system are probably also provided by that party.

On the other hand, the initiators had a notable influence on the importance of criteria. Most notable are the differences in the importance of criteria between systems initiated by functional departments and the top management, which are reflected in the high number of significant entries in the respective columns of Table 6 and are represented graphically in Figure 2.

Functional departments seem to have a very clear view of their needs. The quality of decision support is very important when evaluating systems commissioned by these departments. There is also a considerable focus on efficiency, as indicated by the relatively high weight of the savings criterion. On the other hand, these systems are tailored towards the special needs of the departments, so the variety of functions is not so important. Since these systems are probably used only within the departments that initiated their development, organizational consequences are not important.

In contrast to the systems initiated by functional departments, the functions of the system become more important when the system is initiated by the top management, as does the organizational impact. Security is also a more important issue in those systems. On the other hand, systems initiated by the top management are less oriented towards decision support.

As expected, no significant influences of the initiators on the evaluation methods used were found.

## Development process

Table 7 shows the regression coefficients and significance levels for the influence of various properties of the development process on the importance of evaluation goals and criteria.

## Conclusions and topics for further research

Our results can be summarised as follows: within the evaluation process, only a few influences were statistically significant. The goals of evaluation, which were assumed to have a considerable impact on the importance of criteria and the choice of methods, do not play such a central role. However, some specific goals like verification of expectations or termination of the development, do have a significant influence on the evaluation process. The relationship between criteria and methods also could not be observed to the extent it was expected from the theoretical literature.

On the other hand, strong relationships were identified between the evaluation process and external factors. There is a significant interdependency between the components of the system to be evaluated and the criteria used in the evaluation process, which can be attributed to causal relationships in both directions.

Probably the most striking result of this study is the strong influence the initiators of a system have on the evaluation process, especially on the importance of criteria. Not only are there significant influences in the importance of single criteria, but taken as a whole, they form a consistent and readily interpretable pattern.

From these results, several conclusions can be drawn for guiding future research. First of all, the relatively weak results for the connection between criteria and evaluation methods require further analysis. It could well be the case that some qualitative results obtained in this study would reach statistical significance in a larger survey. Another possible explanation is the concept of quality underlying this study. Here, respondents were asked to indicate their satisfaction with the use of certain methods in the evaluation. The suitability of different methods for different evaluation situations and criteria could possibly also be measured in other, more objective ways.

The results concerning the high importance of initiators also require further exploration. An important next step is to uncover the mechanisms by which this influence is exercised. This question is closely related to the role of evaluators (as opposed to developers) and other stakeholders in the evaluation process. Our results indicate that this line of research is an important topic for improving our understanding of the development and evaluation of MSS.
