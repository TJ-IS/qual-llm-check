# Supporting organizational relations through information technology in innovative organizational forms

C Ferioli $^{1}$ and P Migliarese $^{1,2}$

$^{1}$ Department of Economics and Production, Politecnico of Milan, Piazza Leonardo da Vinci, 32, 20133 Milano; and $^{2}$ The University of Calabria, Italy

The paper analyses some of the main organizational changes which have emerged in the last few years as an answer to the new economic environment calling for global, time-based competition: reduction of hierarchy levels, elimination of slack resources, enlargement of decisional autonomy of organizational units/offices and of lateral communication and coordination mechanisms. The paper affirms a new role of information technology (IT) connected to these organizational transformations. Three main areas for new IT support are recognised: (i) facilities for middle manager decisions; (ii) tools for improving the decisional autonomy of organizational units; and (iii) supports to lateral mechanisms of communication and coordination. For analysing and identifying these new areas of support, a new method of organizational analysis is proposed: the relational method of organizational analysis. This new multidimensional method and perspective overcomes difficulties deriving from the reductionism of the existing methods, looking only to the ‘economic’, ‘formal’ or ‘interpersonal’ components of the relation. Instead the proposed relational method is able to analyse simultaneously the several dimensions of a relation and to discover and to explain the new role of IT in innovative firms.

## Introduction: new roles of information technology and difficulties of change

New organizational forms require an increased capability to exchange and elaborate information (Hall, 1991; Davenport, 1993; Champy & Hammer 1993). Drucker (1988) argues that current trends are towards information-based organizations largely composed of semi-autonomous specialists, which are encouraged to act as entrepreneurs. In particular they can obtain the know-how and ability required for the assigned objectives through exchange of information and services with colleagues and/or other organizational units.

This paper analyses the emerging organizational features in order to identify the opportunities for information technology (IT). Three main areas are identified for IT-based support: (a) support for (middle) management decisions; (b) support for the decisional autonomy of organizational units; and (c) support for coordination and communication through lateral organizational mechanisms. In these three areas IT may provide effective and important facilities for new organizational models. In fact, most of the research and innovative applications in the IT field can be classified in these areas. For example management information systems (MIS) and executive information systems (EIS) provide specific support for management decisions, (Leidner & Elam, 1993; Drucker, 1995); decision support systems (DSS) give support to management decisions as well as to other kinds of decision (technical, etc), so they improve the decisional autonomy of organizational units, (Angehrn & Jelassi, 1994; Gorry & Scott-Morton, 1971); group decision support systems (GDSS), applications in the field of computer supported cooperative work (CSCW) and multimedia communication tools facilitate the coordination and communication among organizational units (lateral mechanisms), (Nunamaker et al, 1989; Gallupe et al, 1991; Poole & De Sanctis, 1992; Migliarese, 1992).

Nevertheless, the real use of these innovative tools is limited. In spite of their theoretically high support, organizations have not adopted these IT based applications except in some successful cases. One of the most important challenges for IT researchers and practitioners should be the explanation of the existing gap between technology and organizations (Ciborra, 1993). Different points of view are possible (Ferioli & Migliarese, 1996; Humphreys et al, 1996). It may be underlined that organizations and people have a natural inertia. The speed of technical development is greater than that of organizational and personnel changes. Innovative IT applications are useful tools for organizations, (Bariff & Ginzberg, 1982) but at present they are scarcely used because of psychological resistance. They will be completely adopted in the future, when new managers and employees will take the place of those who are now working in organizations.

A second perspective explains the gap through technical reasons (Radermacher, 1994; Bell, 1992). Innovative IT tools are not used because they do not provide real help to organizations. For example, according to some authors, current DSS provide only a low level support, based on data organization and on an enormous number of computations of an easy but tedious nature. Instead it is necessary to design more powerful DSS that are able to support complex and sophisticated decisional processes.

The third point of view considers mainly organizational problems (Mumford, 1991). The gap is due to a mismatch between IT and organizations: some innovative IT applications are implemented without considering social and organizational constraints (Whitaker, 1994). A socio-technical approach is then required: the design of innovative IT tools should comprise also the analysis of their impact on the organization (Poole & DeSanctis, 1990), and the introduction of new tools should be coupled with planned organizational changes (Keen, 1980).

This paper considers mainly the third point of view. It analyses the potential role of IT in new organizations from an organizational-based point of view. Moreover the paper proposes a new method for organizational analysis, based on the concept of organizational relation (defined as a complex and multi-dimensional link among organizational units). The proposed relational method describes organizations as a network of nodes (at large organizational units) linked through organizational relations. The application of the proposed relational method provides indications for designing IT support for emerging organizational features. In particular, two main design principles are indicated as important for the effective design of IT support for new organizational models: (i) IT tools should be coherent with other (existing and valid) supports to the organizational relation; and (ii) the technical supports to the relation should be coherent with the other 'dimensions' of the relation, as for example, the interpersonal-social dimension of the relation.

## The emerging company features

## The new requirements from the economic environment

Some important trends have emerged in the competitive environment in the last few years. These new emerging trends are forcing many companies to change several aspects of their organizational structures and organizational systems. Some of the most important changes in the economic environment are the following:

\- The evolution of markets: market demand of several products is orientated toward more customised products and less mass-produced of products (Levitt, 1991; Hamel & Prahalad, 1989).

\- The internationalisation of the economic competition: in most industrial countries local barriers against foreign competitors have disappeared and a number of other factors, (such as the diffusion of world wide communication networks, the reduction of cultural differences among different countries, etc), are fostering a global world wide competition (Ohmae, 1990; Aoki, 1990).

\- The diffusion of new advanced technologies. For example the diffusion of client-server technology has caused a revolution in the IT industry in only a few years: the industrial population has changed (thousands of new little hardware suppliers have entered the market, traditional large companies have changed their roles, etc); software design and implementation methods have been modified; and several organizations have substituted their hardware equipment (downsizing process) (McKenney & McFarlan, 1982; Strassman, 1990).

\- The speeding up of the economic process. Product lifecycles have shortened and companies are continuously redefining their business (Stalk & Hout, 1990).

These emerging environmental trends impose new requirements and features on organizations (Figure 1). Improvements in some key performances are needed for obtaining (or maintaining) successful economic results:

\- Companies must pursue effectiveness (making the right things, achieving planned goals) as well as efficiency (making things right, respecting standard costs and/or procedures). Traditional trade-offs (high product quality vs low production costs, augmenting long period company value vs increasing short period net income, high innovation vs high cash flows) are no longer accepted. Organizations must obtain high performance in all their activities (eg high product quality and low production costs) (Clark et al, 1986; Stalk & Hout, 1990).

\- The strategic relevance of time has increased dramatically. Companies must obtain efficiency and effectiveness while making things fast (time-based competition).

\- Organizational flexibility is required. Companies should be able to change more frequently than in the past, in order to adapt to new opportunities and/or ties (for example entering a new business, customising products etc) (Prahalad & Hamel, 1994).

## The new organizational models

These are but some of the new demands on organizations. They are compelling firms to adopt new organizational models and mechanisms that can deal with new problems. Some of the emerging solutions are based on drastic changes in organizational structures and mechanisms, whereas others are limited to improvements in managerial policies and techniques.

In general most organizations have adopted ‘mixed’ solutions composed of (some of) the following four changes: (a) the adoption of new managerial techniques based on eliminating resource wastage; (b) the reduction of the number of hierarchy levels coupled with an enlargement of organizational unit autonomy; (c) the reduced importance of short-term plans; and (d) the concentration on core activities coupled with the outsourcing of non value adding activities. These four organizational changes are tightly coupled: they are linked by complex reciprocal influences (Figure 2).

## New managerial techniques

Several companies have adopted modern managerial policies, eg just in time, pull production systems and total quality management (TQM) techniques. These new managerial systems look for efficiency improvements through the reduction (or even the elimination) of all resource wastage: eg non value-adding activities; inventories of raw materials, finished products and work in progress; surplus time; and excess labour. The reduction (elimination) of all resource buffers and organizational ‘slack resources’ enables direct money savings (for example, thanks to warehouse reduction) as well as efficiency improvements (because slack resources hide inefficiencies).

But one of the main effects of the buffer reduction is the increased need for integration mechanisms. Because of the reduction of slack resources, organizational units are becoming more tightly coupled and they need new integration mechanisms, that enable the exchange of information and coordination among organizational units (Migliarese & Paolucci, 1995). Buffers and slack resources have always been used for decoupling tasks and for reducing integration needs of organizations (Thompson, 1967; Galbraith, 1973). In the past, slack resources were considered expensive but they could potentially reduce integration and coordination costs and so they often proved profitable (ie the cost improvement due to buffers was smaller than the coordination cost saving) (Galbraith, 1977).

Nowadays the advantage of slack resources is drastically reduced and they are rarely recommended. Because of the disappearance of organizational buffers, organizational units are becoming more coupled and connected among themselves. For example it often happens that organizational units that seem to be independent when there were slack resources, become tightly coupled after these resources are removed. More coupled tasks require mechanisms for the exchange of information, in order to enable the synchronisation and the coordination of activities.

Two main solutions are adopted in organizations to provide the required integration mechanisms: (i) the use of lateral mechanisms among organizational units; and (ii) the increasing decisional autonomy of organizational units (Ferioli & Migliarese, 1995).

(i) The increased use of lateral mechanisms enables the exchange of information among organizational units, through channels different from classical hierarchy-based links. These mechanisms may link organizational units belonging to the same company as well as different companies. They are based on tools such as:

\- traditional lateral organizational mechanisms (teams, task-forces, project managers) (Galbraith, 1977);

\- IT-based channels of communication (Lee et al, 1988);

\- internal market mechanisms (organizational units can buy/sell services to/from other organizational units) (Miles & Snow, 1992);

\- personnel management techniques for improving interpersonal contacts (periodic meetings; engaging people from the same schools) (Hastings, 1993).

(ii) The enlargement of decisional autonomy of organizational units reduces the need for coordination because it creates (partially) self-contained organizational units, with the capability and the information required to perform their tasks. These organizational units are able to manage most integration problems on their own, with a reduced use of the hierarchy.

## The reduction of number of hierarchy levels

The enlargement of decisional autonomy is directly coupled not only with the integration needs but also with the reduction of the number of hierarchy levels. Semi-autonomous organizational units require a reduced hierarchy supervision, so that many middle-levels are no longer used. Organizational structures are becoming 'lean' (Womack et al, 1990) through a reduction of the 'vertical' dimensions and an enlargement of the 'horizontal' basis (Figure 3).

These structural changes are also due to the need for speeding up decisional processes. In traditional organizations (with many levels of hierarchy) the solution for a new problem was found only through the hierarchy. The question was solved by the first manager with the required autonomy and capability (who could be located two, three or even more levels above the starting point). The hierarchical solution required time and decision speed strongly decreased in large organizations. This decisional delay is no longer acceptable today. Organizations are changing in their structure in order ‘to solve problems where they grow’.

For example, the Italian car builder FIAT has drastically changed its production organization: FIAT Auto managers have modified their factories through a lean production organization based on the Elementary Technological Units (UTE). A UTE is composed of 20–30 workers and a team of different specialists, such as product engineer, process engineer and materials expert. A UTE boss directs the workers and the team of experts. Every UTE is assigned to a partially self-contained part of an entire working process. The UTE has the responsibility for the assigned phases of the global process, as regards timing, quality, costs, and quantity of the produced goods. Moreover every UTE is stimulated to continuously improve the quality of process and/or product. Beside the adoption of UTE as a model of job organization, a reduction of the number of hierarchy levels (from 7 to 5) in FIAT factories has occurred.

The remaining hierarchy managers have drastically changed their role (new role for middle managers). They have become less involved in everyday problems (supervision and control of subordinates) whereas they are now considered as ‘small entrepreneurs’. They are liable for economic results, strategy formulation, organizational competence improvement, and so on (Lawler, 1988; Evered & Selman, 1989). But the reduction in the number of hierarchy levels improves the need for integration mechanisms. Hierarchy is one of the pure mechanisms of coordination (Mintzberg, 1983). Its reduction needs the improvement of other mechanisms for communication and coordination.

## Reduced importance of detailed operational plans

Time constraints and time-competition also reduces the effectiveness of detailed operational plans. Plans provide a traditional mechanism for coordinating activities: a detailed short-term plan identifies the tasks each organizational unit has to perform and the timing. Coordination is obtained through the respect of plans: no exchange of information is required among organizational units. But detailed plans (eg a periodic master production scheduling coupled with a weekly production scheduling plan and a material requirement plan) often require modifications in order to couple with unpredictable events (a new important order, a machine break down).

Generally, formal plan modification is an expensive activity. It requires data collection (about the 'new' event), meetings, discussions, etc. (Migliarese & Paolucci, 1993). It needs time and it is not effective when fast decisions are required. To speed up plan variation, organizations frequently modify plans in an informal way: organizational units deviate from plan prescription without following the formal (time wasting) procedure for plan modification. They do not change the plan but (partly) they do not respect it. This informal solution improves effectiveness but: (a) it requires exchange of information among organizational units (whereas one of the aims of planning is the elimination of information exchange); and (b) it reduces the control over some variables (for example accounting value of stocks will be quite different from real value).

To reduce these plan problems, several organizations have adopted managerial techniques (such as just in time) based on the elimination of detailed operational planning (whereas strategic/long-term plans still remain valid), but this choice requires the enlargement of other mechanisms for coordinating organizational units such as lateral coordination and mutual adjustment.

## The concentration on core activities

Organizations have to deal also with an enlargement of coordination needs within external environments. In the past, organizations bought raw materials and sold finished products. Most of the tasks required for the production process were inside the firm; roughly speaking the organization's links with the external environment were limited to market exchange with suppliers and clients. Nowadays organizations tend to concentrate only on their core activities: they buy not only raw materials from other companies, but also intermediate activities and/or support services. Some phases of the production process can be managed outside the organization (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993).

This outsourcing trend causes problems of communication and integration among different companies (similar to the problem of integrating organizational units within the same company). Market contracts are no longer enough for managing increased integration needs: organizations are establishing new mechanisms for interfirm coordination, based on tools such as: inter-organizational information systems; long-term contracts; and sharing of market risk (Lamming, 1993).

## New opportunities for IT in emerging organizational models

The previous section has shown that new organizational models require an improvement in three main organizational solutions: lateral mechanisms of coordination and communication; decisional autonomy of organizational units; and new roles for middle managers (Figure 2).

The implementation of the three indicated improvements is the main difficulty for new organizational models. For example its quite easy to reduce slack resources: the real difficulty is to implement new coordination mechanisms that fill the lack of coordination due to buffer elimination.

For this reason, the main aid of IT to new organizations is through support for the three solutions indicated (Figure 4). Information technologies may facilitate emerging organizational models through providing tools in three main areas: (i) decisional support for middle management; (ii) instruments for improving decisional autonomy of organizational units; and (iii) tools for supporting lateral mechanisms for coordination and communication.

![](/api/attachments/7FGBT27R/fulltext/images/26e42a67c498113e5eaacd7348d5d731c16670e7199d606bb6b836fd3ad074ef.jpg)  
Figure 4 The new three areas for IT supports to new organizations.

(i) Decisional support for management should facilitate middle managers in playing the new role assigned to them (Leidner & Elam, 1993). Roughly speaking, these systems should provide two kinds of information:

\- data about organizational units subordinate to the manager, in order to facilitate control over a large number of employees (increased span of control) and the monitoring of the organizational unit performance (through economic and/or technical indicators);

\- data from outside the organizational unit, in order to facilitate the middle manager to act as an entrepreneur. This requires the capability to respond to new opportunities/ties from the environment (that can be fostered for example with information about the economic environment and the technical/scientific community) as well as behaviour in line with the strategy and the main decisions of the company (that can be facilitated through data about main company decisions and market/economic company results).

Various tools and software (under the label of MIS, EIS, and managerial DSS) have been proposed and are available to give exactly this type of support.

(ii) Decisional autonomy of organizational units requires the capability of autonomous problem solving and decision making. Organizational units require more information and know-how, for solving complex but well-known problems, and improved intelligence, expertise and creativity, for finding valid solutions for new problems. Partly, DSS provide solutions to this need for knowledge (Ferioli & Migliarese, 1994b).

Roughly speaking, knowledge-based DSS can provide organizational units with the technical capability for complex problem solving, through the collection of existing expertise and know-how. For example, a maintenance team whose activity is supported by a DSS has less need for interaction with other maintenance units for problem solving or sharing experience. All the know-how required is collected in the IT support, and the team is completely autonomous.

Tools based on artificial intelligence can improve the capability of discovering new solutions for emerging problems that were never faced before. For example, applications based on neural networks or genetic algorithms can find hidden dependencies among data, build new models, etc.

Research on DSS has been conducted for more than 15 years in an international scientific community (for a survey: Gorry & Morton, 1971; Angehrn & Jelassi, 1994) and it has obtained several important results, such as the development of decisional theories and software tools for individual preferences modeling, for finding single or multi-attribute functions, for decomposing such functions (Radermacher, 1994).

(iii) Lateral mechanisms of communication and coordination consist of the exchange of information among organizational units through different channels from hierarchy-based links. Lateral mechanisms are established by organizational units in order to pursue some specific organizational goal (for example: to buy services or to obtain information/solutions about a technical problem).

The goal influences the ‘pattern’ of the link. For example a production team (liable for a part of the production process, for example door assembling in a car production process) may establish links with:

\- the engineering office and the quality manager, for suggesting a door modification (this link may be based on a meeting);

\- the maintenance team, for planning plant servicing (this link may be based on a periodic planned meeting);

\- a similar production team, located in another plant, for solving a technical problem (this link may be based on interpersonal contact).

Two main problems may affect communication in these links: organizational units may be located in remote places and the identification of ‘suitable’ organizational units sometimes may be difficult.

The elimination of communication obstacles (first problem) is one of the main areas of IT-based application: IT communication tools solve the time and space constraints of communication among organizational units or even among different companies. Research in the field of GDSS (Nunamaker et al, 1989; Gallupe et al, 1991) and CSCW (Bannon, 1994) provide important tools in this area.

IT may also partially provide solutions for the problem of identifying the suitable organizational unit (second problem). In particular, organizational DSS (ODSS) (Lee et al, 1988) collect information about the organizational units and the personnel belonging to the organization, their expertise, and organizational rules and procedures. They provide a ‘map’ of the organization and facilitate finding a unit/person with particular characteristics.

An example of an ODSS is provided by British Petroleum (Hastings, 1993) (Figure 5). The BP high technology environmental department (called HSE technology team) provides knowledge and expertise for solving environmental problems and for preventing disasters. Those kinds of solutions are used by BP itself and are sold to BP's clients.

The HSE technology team is composed of three research laboratories, located in different places (two in the UK and one in the US); even though the HSE activities require great collaboration among all researchers.

To improve integration, they have connected the three laboratories through a distributed personnel database, accessible by every researcher. The database contains skills, specialisation, know-how and previous project experiences of all employees of the three laboratories, so that everybody in the department can access information about researchers as if they were working in a unique small group. For example, it is possible to identify who was previously involved in a specified problem, who has the required expertise, and so on.

## Proposal for a new method for the analysis of emerging organizational features

The previous sections have underlined the emerging trends in organizational models and the new areas for IT support. These new organizational features require a new method for the analysis and design of the organization because traditional organizational models have difficulty describing them. In most cases the description of present organizations through traditional methods misses some important information.

For example, traditional organization charts represent only hierarchy-based lines of control and communication: they are no longer adequate for describing organizations composed of semi-autonomous organizational units that can create several links with other organizational units (as well as with other companies) through lateral mechanisms of coordination and communication.

Figure 5 The ODSS of British Petroleum.

New client-supplier links provide a second example. In several cases the reduction of slack-resources has drastically changed this tie from a classical market exchange to a new mechanism. New client-supplier links are based on several aspects, such as: the definition of some common goals (partial economic-risk sharing, objectives of product quality improvements); long period contracts; strong interpersonal trust; organizational exchange (knowledge sharing, information exchange); and technical links (computer networks) (Lamming, 1993).

Traditionally, client–supplier links have been studied through the analysis of economic exchange based on the concept of economic transactions (Williamson, 1975; Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Alchian & Demsez, 1972). But the transaction-cost model provides only a partial description of these new links, because it considers only economic elements and disregards technical, social and organizational aspects.

This lack of adequate analysis methods penalises the effective introduction of IT support in organizations. Research on IT support for organizations has obtained important results, as indicated in the previous analysis. Nevertheless the number of innovative IT tools actually used in organizations is very limited. As discussed in the introduction, there is a gap between the potential support of IT and its real use in organizations. The absence of adequate methods for organizational analysis is certainly one of the reasons for this gap.

In the next section the paper proposes a new method of organizational analysis based on the concept of organizational relation. This concept is connected with the relational perspective of the organization, a development still in progress, as well as the proposed relational organizational analysis method. This proposed method overcomes some of the limits of traditional methods of organizational analysis in describing emerging organizational features.

## The relational perspective to organizational analysis

Organizations are considered as networks of nodes and relations linking the nodes. Nodes are composed of organizational actors. Several criteria may be defined for the location on nodes: formal borders of organizational units; similarity in technical activity; equality in hierarchical position; similarity in professional qualification etc. The choice of the best criterion may vary according to the purpose of the analysis.

For example, for the design of a DSS for supporting technical activities, organizational actors will be grouped in nodes according to their technical qualification. Instead, if the purpose of the analysis is the implementation of an IT support for lateral communication, the best criterion might be the relational homogeneity of nodes, that defines the nodes so that all the components of a node have the same set of relations with every other organizational actor (Baker, 1992).

Moreover the width of the nodes varies in relation to the size of the analysis (Ferioli & Migliarese, 1994a). For example if the analysis regards a whole organization with many divisions located in several countries, a local factory may be considered a node; whereas if the analysis is limited to a shop floor, the size of nodes is restricted to the individual employee.

Each node is linked with other nodes through a complex set of connections (see, for example, the previously described client–supplier link). that is described as an organizational relation.

The word ‘relation’ was first introduced into organization theory by Elton Mayo and his Human Relations school. However, the concept of a relation is used here with a limited meaning: only interpersonal relations are considered; and the analysis is centred on the organizational climate.

Other authors (Gabarro, 1990; Donati, 1991; Granovetter, 1992; Ferioli & Migliarese, 1994b) have proposed the concept of organizational relation as the basis for organizational analysis. The organizational relation is defined as a link between two organizational actors (individuals and/or organizational units and/or different companies) with the following characteristics: (a) it is orientated toward a (partially) common goal; (b) it is not trivial; (c) it is not an occasional link; and (d) it is supported by several tools.

(a) Two economic actors establish an organizational relation for pursuing a (partially) shared goal. For example the client–supplier link becomes an organizational relation if client and supplier identify a common objective (such as a product quality improvement or a reciprocal economic aid). If the shared goal disappears, the organizational relation dies or changes to another mechanism (for example to a classical market contract).

(b) The shared goals of the organizational relation are joined to the objectives of the organization (for example productivity objectives, market objectives). This means that the organizational relation is not trivial, but is created by the nodes in order to obtain their organizational objectives. This non-trivial feature distinguishes organizational relations from pure phenomenological, interpersonal interactions, that (roughly speaking) are not linked to any organizational objectives.

(c) A link requires a medium/long period to become an organizational relation.

(d) The organizational relation is supported by several tools, such as: formal contracts, interpersonal links, exchange of materials/services, or exchange of information. (Table 1).

## The proposed relational method for organizational analysis

The proposed method for organizational analysis provides a complete description of emerging organizational features, whereas other organizational perspectives (organizational economics, social analysis) miss some important aspects.

Nevertheless development of the relational perspective is still in progress and is not complete, mainly as regards its design capability. Actually the location of nodes is supported by a number of researchers and operational indications (eg Nohria & Eccles, 1992) but an operational method for designing organizational relations does not exist (unlike the design method from other perspectives, as the Williamson model of transaction costs, the Galbraith organizational model, the Thompson organizational model, etc.).

This paper proposes a method of analysis that describes organizational relations through four axes: the tools supporting the relation; the goal(s) common to the economic actors; and the norms regulating the behaviour of actors within the relation and the cultural background associated with the relation. They represent the four base elements common to every organizational relation, whereas they do not provide the real description of organizational relations. (For example a client–supplier relation has a different shape from the relation between two single employees. Nevertheless the four axes position the analysis and the design for both relations).

The axis of tools regards the necessary instruments for a relation to take place or the support to improve relation effectiveness. Different kinds of tools are used in organizations for supporting collaborative relations or for enabling the creation of relations:

-periodic meetings for people from different divisions;

## Goals

The axis of goals explains the purpose and objectives for which an organizational relation is established and maintained. The presence of a shared goal is the raison d'être of the organizational relation: no effective relation can survive without defined goals. For example the previously described client–supplier relations are different from classical client–supplier market contracts because they are based on (partially) common objectives (common design of new products, shared objectives of quality improvement, joint investments in new plants). If the goal disappears, the relation would change into another type of link (for example in a simple market exchange).

Moreover the goals influence the remaining characteristics of the relation: they determine the amount of information to exchange, the need for a negotiating mechanism and the required cultural background common to the actors involved in the relation (if the goals of the two persons/organizational units are different, the relation will require a negotiation and the creation of complex contractual norms and rules).

## Rules

The axis of rules and norms defines the set of implicit as well as explicit rules that govern relations. Relational norms define the accepted behaviour: in this way they allow/prevent the integration of (new) actors in the group created through the relation. Relations are regulated either by general organizational rules and values, and by specific norms defined among involved organizational actors.

For example, the organizational relations based on internal market mechanisms are regulated through: (i) specific contracts, negotiated by the organizational units that create the relation; and (ii) general rules, that the headquarters has decided (for example) in order to promote some company areas. In most cases, many organizational rules are implicit and based on unwritten social values. In some organizations for example, hierarchic relations are very formal and cold, while in others they take place in an informal and friendly way. (Figure 6).

## Cultural background

The cultural background is composed of the organizational culture common to the two economic/organizational actors involved in the relation, ie the values, traditions and customs the two actors share.

The cultural background forms the base knowledge shared by the actors of the relation, ie the common assumptions that do not require any negotiation or contractual definition (Schein, 1984). A larger shared cultural background improves the effectiveness of organizational relations, as it reduces the need for negotiation and information exchange.

## Advantages of the relational method

The proposed relational method for organization analysis seems to overcome some of the limitations of existing methods and to be better at capturing the characteristics of new emergent organizational models (like the network organization model).

With respect to the transaction costs approach (Williamson, 1979), the proposed relational approach substitutes the concept of transaction with the concept of relation. In fact the concept of transaction tends to be limiting and considers only economic exchange, even if within different possible contexts like market exchange or hierarchical exchange. In this way various types of connections among organizational actors, which are now considered very important, are not directly considered. The concept of organizational relation, within the four dimensions of goals, tools, norms, organizational culture is broader, and is able to capture the large variety of links among organizational actors.

The workflow analysis approach is limited to the so-called formal organization and looks to codified inputs, transformations, activities, and outputs which are unable to describe the entire meaning of the relation, which includes certainly a codified part but recognises also a non-codified part (De Terssac, 1992), linked to some common goals, to some codified norms, to a general organizational culture, and to tools that include also some unpredictable behaviour of the actors.

The relational approach also seems to be different from the classical socio-psychological approach to the organization. The socio-psychological approach has the merit of focusing on interpersonal relations and to the personal assumption of a role, but entirely omits the economic/formal organizational parts of the interaction.

Finally we can conclude that the proposed relational approach is able to combine several dimensions such as the economic, organizational, social and interpersonal dimensions. Our translation and adaptation of the AGIL parsonian model is not functionalistic, because the actors of the relation have a specific role, is not socio-psychological because other dimensions are contemporarily present, and is also not purely economic. In the concept of a network organization, the link among nodes has this multidimensional nature. Thus the proposed relational method is appropriate to these kinds of network organizational models.

## The new role of IT support as relational support

The proposed relational perspective for the analysis of organizations provides indications for the design of IT support and for its introduction into organizations. IT plays the new role of relational support in new organizational models because it facilitates the creation of a network of organizational relations.

Firstly, IT based tools foster the constitution of semi-autonomous nodes, through supports to technical and managerial decisions (DSS, MIS, EIS, etc) that improve the decisional autonomy of nodes. Moreover the location of nodes may be linked to the availability of IT supports.

On the one hand an IT-based decisional support can influence the choice of organizational actors grouped in the same node. For example electrical and mechanical maintenance workers, initially belonging to two separate nodes (electrical maintenance node and mechanical maintenance node), could be grouped in the same node if there were unique DSS for maintenance decisional support. On the other hand the design of a decisional support is clearly influenced by the competence (ie the organizational actors) presented in the node it has to support.

Secondly, ODSS facilitate the creation of an organizational relation between two nodes because they support the identification of the suitable node that possesses some required characteristics (technical competence, experience about some problems). This support is extremely important in large organizations, with hundreds of nodes located in remote places.

Thirdly, with the proposed relational method, IT can be considered as a tool and this confirms the capability of IT to foster organizational relations. In some cases the network of organizational relations would not be possible without IT-based tools.

An Italian example is provided by the network organization of Benetton. (Figure 7). This company is composed of a central node, situated in Treviso, Italy, about 500 small factories, and a lot of shops all over the world. The small factories and the shops are linked to the central node by different kinds of formal contracts (most of them are franchising contracts) and by a computer network. The computer network supports the collection of orders from the shops to the head office, so as to make the production planning easy. Through the information network, the central node informs the linked factories about the quantity and the quality of goods to be produced. Without IT tools, the network of organizational relation in Benetton would perhaps be very different: it would involve a limited number of nodes and the exchange of information would not be as easy as now.

But the design of IT tools is not enough for the implementation of effective organizational relations. The proposed method of analysis of organizational relations prescribes a coupled design of the four axes: tools, goals, norms, cultural background. In particular the design of IT for supporting existing organizational relations may fail when the technical design of the tools is not joined to the analysis of the remaining three axes of the relation. The relational method provides two main design principles for preventing IT failures.

(1) Coherence among tools. Effective relations use several tools at the same time. This means that IT design should look for the coherence of IT tools with the other ones. For example one of the most natural tools in group decision activities is interpersonal contact: the design and introduction of GDSS (IT tools) has to look for integration with the existing interpersonal contact tool, for example by planning perioding face-to-face meetings (Gallupe et al, 1991). The main causes of failure of most GDSS are that they do not respect this required coherence: often groups using GDSS are ‘co-located yet remote’ (Whitaker, 1994), participants in electronic meetings ‘report a feeling of dissociation from others, due to the lack of interaction’ (Bannon, 1994).

Figure 7 The Benetton network organization.

(2) IT tools (as well as the other tools) need to be coherent with the remaining axes of goals, norms, and cultural background. For example the organizational relations of franchising (among a central company and several dispersed shops) generally have the following characteristics: formal and easy to measure goals, standard norms (all the norms are clearly defined in the franchising contracts), and limited cultural background (the two actors must share a limited knowledge about the product and about some marketing techniques). This franchising organizational relation may be supported through a computer network that enables the exchange of a limited set of structured data (for example data about sales, and catalogues).

On the other hand the organizational relation between two remote R&D units is based on: non rigidly defined goals; formal as well as informal norms (they cannot negotiate a rigid contract); and a vast cultural background (that continuously changes with new information/discoveries). An IT support must enable a non structured exchange of information, data and hypertext document (E-mail is a proper support).

## Conclusion

This paper examines transformation in organizational forms: due to environmental changes calling for a new global and time-based competition, various transformations are occurring in organizational structure. Emerging trends in the new organizational forms seem to be towards lean organizations (hierarchical level reduction, process oriented re-design) as well as network organizations (Miles & Snow, 1992; Ferioli & Migliarese, 1994b).

In this scenario of organization transformation, organizational units or offices tend to be transformed into semi-autonomous nodes, with the capability of establishing interactions and relations with other internal/external nodes in order to pursue some organizational objectives. Even if no organization has a ‘pure’ lean or network organization, the metaphor of the network is able to describe the two main trends in the emerging organizational models: the enlargement of decisional autonomy of organizational units, and the improvements of lateral organizational mechanisms of communication and coordination (relations).

In this framework of organizational change, the paper affirms and describes a new role for IT. IT is considered one of the possible tools for activating organizational interactions and relations, thus supporting the enlarged decisional autonomy of nodes as well as the lateral communication and coordination mechanisms. IT is thus supporting the changes towards network organizations.

Existing organizational analysis methods (like workflow methods, transaction cost approach and others) are unable to examine those new areas of support of IT. This paper proposes a new organizational analysis method (the relational method) for a better identification of the areas of decisional autonomy and lateral communication enlargement in organizations.

The relational method is based on the concept of an organizational relation. This concept simultaneously involves several dimensions of interaction among organizational units, like economic, organizational, social and interpersonal dimensions, avoiding the reductionisms and simplifications of existing methods. Some of the dimensions considered assume a great and increasing importance in the emerging organizational forms (for example the client–supplier relation contains economic as well as organizational and social dimensions).

The organizational relation has to be analysed with reference to the four components: goals, tools, rules and norms, and organizational culture. The concept of organizational relations and the proposed relational method of organizational analysis are able to identify the new role of IT support and of the new information systems in the innovative firm.
