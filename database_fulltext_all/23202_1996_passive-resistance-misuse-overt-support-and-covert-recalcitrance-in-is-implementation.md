# Passive resistance misuse: overt support and covert recalcitrance in IS implementation

GM Marakas $^{1}$ and S Hornik $^{2}$

$^{1}$ Department of Information Systems, University of Maryland, College Park, MD 20742; and $^{2}$ School of Accounting, Florida International University, Miami, FL 33199, USA

A major postulate in many theoretical perspectives of information technology (IT) implementation is the rational assumption that resistance to change is an observable behaviour and that it is mutually exclusive of supportive behaviour. This perspective assumes that any resistance manifesting itself in a covert manner is generally dysfunctional and is motivated more by either criminal intent or opportunities for personal gain.

The purpose of this paper is to focus attention on a form of covert resistance to the IT implementation process that is neither couched in criminal intent nor motivated by personal gain. We define passive resistance misuse (PRM) to be a recalcitrant, covert behaviour that results from both fear and stress stemming from the intrusion of the technology into the previously stable world of the user. Such behaviour takes the form of overt cooperation and acceptance of the proposed system combined with covert resistance and likely sabotage of the implementation effort. We offer two related theoretical foundations to assist in explaining the phenomenon of PRM: (1) passive-aggressive behaviour theory and (2) action science's espoused theories vs theories in use. Through these two theoretical lenses we describe PRM, provide examples of PRM behaviour as well as its proposed causes and offer a framework for inclusion of this construct into an information system (IS) design and implementation research agenda.

## 1. Introduction

As each day passes, information technology (IT) becomes a more integral part of our daily lives. We are constantly faced with the need to accommodate and incorporate the technology into our work situations and to embrace its ‘intrusion’ into our long standing procedures and methods for performing our jobs (Keen, 1981). Despite recent evidence to suggest that new information systems may represent nothing more than the price that must be paid to stay in business (Clemons, 1991; Dos Santos et al, 1993) the seduction of the technology to enhance the competitiveness and productivity of the organization remains potent (Levine & Rossmore, 1993). Notwithstanding the ultimate question of whether value to the organization is realized from the IT, both academic and practitioner literatures abound with theories and frameworks intended to inform the designers and implementors of new information systems in an organization. The multitude of perspectives on the IT implementation process all seem to converge on at least one front: IT implementation must involve both the technical and social systems of the organization (Mumford, 1983). A focus only on the technological aspects of a new information system does not provide an avenue to successful implementation of the system (Bjorn-And ersen & Hedberg, 1977; Curtis et al, 1988). Accordingly, the majority of recent IT implementation literature includes guidance regarding methods of incorporating the user community into the implementation process as a way of increasing the likelihood of achieving both technological and social successes with the new system.

Despite the wide range of perspectives and approaches to IT design and implementation, the propensity is toward the assumption of a distinctly rational model of organizational decision making and IT implementation. This approach suggests that the implementation decisions made in an organization are the result of the collective individuals and organizational units evaluating and applying information in a rational manner for the purposes of making appropriate choices on behalf of the organization (Levine & Rossmore, 1993). Many of the early theories of implementation assumed this rational model of behaviour by all participants and further assumed that overt resistance to the system on the part of the user was nothing more than dysfunctional behaviour that should be eliminated (Hirschheim & Newman, 1991). Other theories allowed for overt resistance to be a manifestation of conflict resulting from the implementation process (Robey et al, 1989). Such resistance, however, is no longer necessarily viewed as dysfunctional and is addressed through methods including active participation early in the design stages of the project (Vanlommel & DeBrabander, 1975; Zmud. 1979), conflict resolution (Robey & Farrow, 1982; Robey et al, 1989; Robey et al, 1993), and increased communication efforts to all participants (DeBrabander & Thiers, 1984; Bostrom, 1989). Regardless of the source or manifestation of the user resistance, few theoretical foundations currently exist in the literature for explaining user resistance in an information system (IS) implementation context. Markus (1983) suggests that ‘better theories of resistance will lead to better implementation strategies’, (p 430).

A principal postulate in many theoretical perspectives of IT implementation is the rational assumption that resistance to change is an observable behaviour and that it is mutually exclusive of supportive behaviour. User resistance resulting from the implementation process will reveal itself in an overt manner and will therefore, be observable by other participants. This perspective assumes that any resistance manifesting itself in a covert manner is generally dysfunctional and is motivated by either criminal intent or opportunities for personal gain (Giacalone, 1990; Sacco & Zureik, 1990; DiBattista, 1991).

However, some have argued that user resistance to proposed changes to the status quo might be an indication or an ‘early warning’ that the system design is flawed or simply a ‘dumb idea’ (Keen, 1981). For example, Mumford et al, (1978) argue that lack of user participation combined with a strict adherence to cost-benefit analysis can lead to the active resistance to a new computer system. Without user participation, they argue, the proposed changes become threatening to the status quo, therefore causing the user group to resist the change. Viewing implementation as a political process, Keen (1981) suggests that user resistance can be a reflection of the user’s unwillingness to relinquish political power.

Another commonality found in the IT implementation literature is the assumption that acceptance and use of the system are reliable measures of the success of the implementation effort (Baroudi et al, 1986; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1987; DeLone, 1988). Given that both acceptance and use are relative constructs that can vary widely across situations (Sanders & Courtney, 1985; Srinivasan, 1985) it seems logical that a continuum of implementation success can be envisioned anchored by use and acceptance at one end (therefore establishing successful implementation of the system) and non-use and rejection at the other (thus demonstrating an unsuccessful implementation effort).

The purpose of this paper is to focus attention on a form of covert resistance to the IT implementation process that is neither couched in criminal intent nor motivated by personal gain. We argue that there exists a disjunction between the rational model employed in IT implementation and the world of practice. We define passive resistance misuse (PRM) to be a recalcitrant, covert behaviour resulting from both fear and stress stemming from the intrusion of the technology into the previously stable world of the user. Such behaviour takes the form of overt cooperation and acceptance of the proposed system combined with covert resistance and possibly sabotage of the implementation effort. PRM cannot be easily predicted or detected during the implementation process and can range from passive nonco-operation to physical destruction. Further, PRM can be subtly blended into the work routine in a manner that can sometimes unknowingly enlist the cooperation of other workers in reaching the goals of the passive resistant user. Following the suggestion of Markus (1983) we offer two related theoretical foundations to assist in explaining the phenomenon of PRM: (1) passive-aggressive (P–A) behaviour theory and (2) action science's espoused theories vs theories in-use. Through these two theoretical lenses we describe PRM, provide examples of PRM behaviour as well as its proposed causes, and propose a framework for inclusion of this construct into an IS design and implementation research agenda.

Section 2 of this paper contains a general overview of the concept of resistance to change, a concise review of the implementation literature focusing on success measured by system use and acceptance vs failure due to non-use and rejection, and a detailed review of the literature regarding both P-A behaviour theory and action theory. Section 3 contains examples of PRM in various IT implementation settings and raises several propositions regarding both the causes and effects of such behaviour. Section 4 proposes a framework for inclusion of PRM into the IT implementation research agenda and Section 5 closes the paper with some concluding remarks.

## 2. Review of the literature

## Resistance to change

The problem of resistance to change is well-known to both psychoanalytic and cognitive psychologists (Diamond, 1986). Both perspectives illustrate compulsive, security-oriented, error-inducing, and self-sealing human behaviours as defensive and adaptive dispositions usually intended to protect the status quo. Such behaviours often emerge during the course of an intervention effort, such as the implementation of a new IS or the restructuring of a department, as defensive techniques for avoiding anxiety and maintaining a perception of self-security.

Numerous researchers (Mumford et al, 1978; Keen, 1981; Land, 1992; Hirschheim & Klein, 1994) have moved beyond the traditional notion of resistance to change as dysfunctional behaviour to a more functional orientation which acknowledges that resistance may be the result of sincere doubts about the efficacy of a particular change. Such resistance may become manifest as a behavioural response where the doubter has no channel to communicate concern or where the fear of discipline or punishment for disagreement with superiors exists. In such cases, resistance may be the early warning that the design of the system is dysfunctional or that the design process is maladroit.

Much of the psychoanalytic research into resistance has focused on the bureaucratic organization as a common venue for the phenomenon. Such organizations are structured in a manner that easily fosters defensive behaviours and self-protectiveness (Diamond, 1984). Weber (1947) notes that bureaucracy is a ‘rationally-organized action’ where conscience is no longer the personal property of the bureaucrat but is perceived to belong to the more impersonal norms and hierarchical authority of the organization. Consequently, bureaucratic systems function as extensions of personal defences thus further reinforcing human tendencies to avoid the anxieties associated with conflict and change. Any intervention aimed at change in the status quo challenges the organizationally-embedded defensive structures that are particularly rigidified in bureaucratic settings and sets in motion the individual perception of a dangerous situation and the concomitant anxiety that leads to resistive behaviours.

## IS use and non-use

Much of the research regarding IS or IT implementation is concerned with the eventual success of the system. Typical measures of success are user satisfaction (Ives et al, 1983; Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Raymond, 1987), system use (Lucas, 1975), system quality (Morey, 1982), and system acceptance (Cerullo, 1980). In addition, more objective measures of system success have been proposed such as delivered cost/benefit or return on investment (ROI) measures (Strassmann, 1985), information economics (Parker & Benson, 1987), business value (Barua et al, 1995), IT and firm market value relationships (Dos Santos et al, 1993), and IT and firm performance (Weill, 1992).

DeLone and McLean (1992), in their survey of the literature on IS success measurement, identified six categories of system success based on the combined Shannon and Weaver (1949) and Mason (1978) taxonomies. Numerous studies exist as examples of research within these categories of system success including system quality (Franz & Robey, 1986), information quality (Rivard & Huff, 1985), information use (Baroudi et al, 1986; Benbasat et al, 1981), user satisfaction (Rivard & Huff, 1984; Rushinek & Rushinek, 1985), individual impact (Aldag & Power, 1986; Zmud, 1983), and organizational impact (Benbasat & Dexter, 1985; 1986).

It has also been widely prescribed in the literature that to improve the chances for system success there is a specific need for user involvement. In their classic review, Ives and Olson (1984) suggest that the necessity for user involvement in the IS implementation effort is almost axiomatic. Research which has focused on system use as a measure of success, however, is concerned with only one end of the use/non-use continuum. Implicit in these studies is the notion that by focusing attention on the factors and processes that have been found to be associated with successful systems we might gain insight into achieving future successes. We argue that this assumption essentially serves to define the non-use end of the continuum as well, suggesting that through the study of systems suffering non-use we might gain insight into the root causes of the failure.

In a paper that surveyed a vast amount of the literature concerning system failure Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) suggest that: (1) most studies use an ill-defined notion of IS failure; (2) most studies do not in fact even articulate a failure concept; and (3) there is no sensible framework to understand and analyse IS failure.

Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) define IS failure as:

... the inability of an IS to meet a specific stakeholder group's expectations where expectations are the beliefs and desires concerning how the IS will serve the group's interest. In most cases these expectations are vaguely expressed and are never rationalised or verbalised as real concerns (p 263).

In addition, the authors describe three other categories of failure that can be found in the literature: (1) correspondence failures – when the IS fails to fit with the pre-design objectives; (2) process failures – the IS is completed but only by exceeding cost and time budgets to such an extent the IS has lost its initial value; and (3) interaction failures – low levels of usage of the IS are used as a surrogate for IS failure. Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) point out that research focusing on high levels of usage has not necessarily concluded that such usage is an indicator of IS success. In fact, they suggest that focusing on user interaction with the system alone may not be appropriate for the study of implementation successes or failures. According to Lyytinen and Hirschheim these three IS failures are separate subclasses of the overall definition given above to expectation failure.

Accordingly, as we see it, the IS failure depicted as an extreme interaction failure (when system use is zero) fits well into our conceptualisation of the IS use/non-use continuum. To develop a better understanding of PRM behaviour we must centre our attention on the non-use end of the scale. System use as a measure of implementation success is not relevant to our understanding and therefore will not be discussed further. Our intention is for this focus on non-use as failure to provide a contrast to the concept of passive resistance misuse developed later in this paper.

One additional distinction regarding the IS use/non-use continuum must be made. The degree to which a system's use is mandatory or discretionary is of importance. Non-use of a mandatory system normally results in sanctions and, therefore, requires an act of heroism. Such systems would logically be more prone to resistance through covert techniques such as sabotage by circumvention or direct action (LaNuez & Jermier, 1994). In contrast, non-use of discretionary systems, such as executive information systems and many decision support systems, is much easier to disguise and is not often subject to sanctions by those in authority. Thus, for the purposes of our discussion, we focus only on those systems that have a predominately mandatory use.

An example at the non-use end of the continuum can be found in a study by Markus (1983) concerning 'Golden Triangle's' implementation of a financial information system (FIS). Through the chronicle of the implementation it was shown that although the FIS may have been present within a division it was not used by the division; instead the division continued to use its old accounting methods. While the staff within this division were required to submit accounting data through the FIS to the corporate accounting division, they did not make use of FIS for any other purpose. This pattern of non-use persisted for over two years until the old accounting methods (ledger books) were literally physically removed thereby forcing the division to adopt the new FIS.

A study by Walsham and Han (1993) describes three separate systems developed by a central government agency (CGA) in a Third World country that were classified as failures due to non-use. Similar to the findings of Markus (1983), agencies outside the central government may have provided data to the system, but the three systems nevertheless were ‘succinctly summarised as one of technical implementation, with no significant concern for organizational usefulness, particularly with agencies outside the CGA itself’ (p 205). As such, the intended users found no relevant application of the system to their work effort and did not attempt to make use of the system.

In a related example, the effect of threat of non-use can be found in a study by Newman and Robey (1992) that focused on the development of a comprehensive student IS. When the users were presented with a test system, they found the input screens to be significantly more complex than previously discussed and the time between screen changes to be unacceptably slow. When the user representatives approached the designers about these issues, they were told that the computer centre did not see these issues as a problem and that changes at this point were ‘non-negotiable’. The users’ response was a total rejection of the test system with a reiteration of their need for a single-screen approach to the system. In this situation, had the designers not acquiesced to the users’ demands the system would not have been used at all and the project would have ended in failure.

The above examples focus on systems that were designed and not used, or in the case of the study by Newman and Robey (1992), the mere threat of non-use forced a major redesign. In our review of the literature we find no evidence of any middle-ground to our conceptualised use/non-use continuum. In fact, we see the continuum as being more a scale of degree of use as a surrogate for relative degree of success with non-use being the point where total system rejection, and therefore system failure, resides. We argue that under this scaling, the common practice of ‘reverse engineering’ a system failure by retrospectively studying the events leading up to the failure may not provide adequate insight into its root causes. Many of the events associated with system failure may not be manifest in a tangible or traceable sense. We submit that certain user behaviours that can lead to system failure are either not easily observed or are, in some cases, seemingly unobservable. We offer PRM as one such behaviour. In the following sections we provide two theoretical foundations necessary to support our conceptualisation of PRM. P–A behaviour theory serves as the underlying theory for the nature of the PRM behaviour and action science (specifically, espoused theories vs theories-in-use) serves to explain how PRM can remain undetected in the implementation process thus potentially leading to negative implementation outcomes.

## P-A behaviour

In the context of an IS implementation setting, PRM behaviour can be partially explained as the consequence of P–A responses to real or perceived personal threats or stresses that the individual associates with a new information system.

The notion of P-A behaviour was first introduced by army psychiatrist Col William Menninger to describe the insubordination, refusal to follow orders, and resistance to orders of soldiers during World War II. More recently, P-A behaviour has been classified as a treatable personality disorder (Wetzler, 1992). To better understand how the P-A individual could employ a strategy of PRM it is important to review the psychological literature concerning P-A behaviours.

Much controversy exists in the psychological literature regarding the diagnostic validity (actual existence) of a P–A personality disorder (Fine et al, 1992). This controversy focuses on whether P–A is a disorder in its own right or simply part of a more broadly defined disorder. One of the dilemmas facing psychologists is the notion that P–A behaviour may often be observed in only one setting; it is a situational reaction to being a powerless subordinate (Fine et al, 1992). We argue that this situational nature of a P–A disorder contributes to an explanation of individual behaviour pertaining to PRM. The uncertainty associated with the introduction of a new IS into the work environment can create the conditions under which P–A behaviour, and thus, PRM can manifest itself in certain individuals.

Several sources can be found that describe the observable characteristics of P–A individuals. P–A behaviour has been described as a pattern of ‘active-ambivalence’ or ‘negativism’ with ‘sullenness’ as a central behavioural feature (Millon, 1981). The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders – III (1987) describes the essential feature of P–A disorder as a persistent pattern of passive resistance to demands for social and occupational performance. Passive resistance is often expressed through such actions as procrastination, stubbornness, intentional inefficiency and ‘forgetfulness’ (Fine et al, 1992).

Fine et al (1992) propose a model of P–A behaviour that we find useful in our discussion of PRM (Figure 1). In their model, P–A behaviour is shown as the continual interaction among five elements: rigidity, resentment, resistance, reactance and reversed reinforcement. Rigidity and resentment are likely to lead to resistance and reactance, which in turn lead to reversed reinforcement.

‘Rigidity’ is defined as inflexible and maladaptive behaviour. Rigidity makes adjustment to change difficult for individuals who believe no one can tell them what to do or not do. ‘Resentment’ involves a mixture of anger and shame. In P–A individuals this anger is not directed overtly but, rather, is inhibited by constraints imposed by reality (such as fear of demotion or being fired). As such, it is often repressed. ‘Resistance’ involves a stubbornness in fulfilling the expectations of others. ‘Reactance’ can be expressed by behaviours such as procrastination, verbal protests, ‘forgetting’ certain tasks, slow performance and resenting useful suggestions. This feeling is particularly aroused when an individual feels threatened. Finally, ‘Reversed Reinforcement’ often affords P–A individuals a sense of power and gratification from causing failure. If these feelings of power are reinforced through reduction in the stress stimulus, the P–A person may continue to cause problems to occur so that they can continue to feel powerful and in control of the situation.

Potential users of new IT may view such systems as intrusions to their established routines or modes of work. As such, they may be unwilling or even unable to adjust to the changes brought about as a consequence of the new system. The intrusion of the system exposes the individual's rigidity toward change and, when coupled with feelings of resentment, stress and fear, can lead directly to both resistant and reactive behaviours. We posit that certain individuals might resort to PRM as a means of reversed reinforcement that allows them to rationalise their covert activities as being in the best interests of the firm while simultaneously feeling a sense of relief from the threat of the new technology. In addition, the use of PRM behaviour allows the user to retain their status as a supporter of the implementation project while simultaneously effecting stress reduction through covert sabotage of the project effort.

While it would seem that the psychological literature provides us with a basis for arguing for the existence of PRM as a form of P–A behaviour we are still left with the need to account for how individual organizational members might come to embrace this type of behaviour over other, less covert, alternatives. To fill this need, we must further explore how individuals develop P–A traits. Two approaches to P–A trait development exist in the literature to help explain this issue: (1) the behavioural approach (Prout & Platt, 1983); and (2) the cognitive-behavioural approach (Burns & Epstein, 1983). Each is concerned with how an individual might learn to employ P–A behaviour when encountering stress or a perceived threat.

The behavioural approach to passive aggressiveness draws upon operant conditioning and social learning theory (Prout & Platt, 1983). In operant conditioning, behaviour is modified by the effects of positive or negative reinforcement, the extinction of a previously reinforced behaviour, or through punishment. Of these four conditions, negative reinforcement is most readily manifested in an IS implementation setting. A person experiencing negative reinforcement perceives some consequence or outcome as painful or threatening. That person then enacts a strategy of behaviour, such as PRM, that serves to diminish the pain or threat. The removal of pain or threat becomes a powerful positive reinforcement that can lead to repeated exhibition of the behaviour strategy until the perception of threat or stress sufficiently diminishes.

Social learning theory supports the behavioural approach by postulating that a person can observe another's behaviour in response to a stimulus or event and, from such observation, can learn to act in the same manner when placed in a similar situation. This potentiality is particularly possible in work environments where a dissatisfied or frightened employee finds respite in employing PRM behaviour. In organizational settings 'groups of employees working together may provide each other with reinforcement such that minor unethical acts eventually develop into more serious [PRM] offences. In this way, each employee may support the rationalisations offered by others in the performance of such acts to the extent that [PRM] comes to be defined as common and accepted practice,' (Parker, 1976, p 336).

In slight contrast, the cognitive-behavioural approach to passive-aggressiveness (Burns & Epstein, 1983) focuses on an individual's natural inhibition to the expression of assertive behaviour. Individuals who find difficulty in expressing their anger often face confusion regarding the validity and justification of their hostility. P-A individuals will often convince themselves that their anger is inappropriate and should therefore be modified. One possible alternative to expressed outward anger may be covert resistance via PRM behaviour.

It is here where P-A individuals will have an arena in which to enact this maladaptive behaviour in the form of PRM. ## Espoused theories vs theories-in-use

Within organizations the actions of individuals can be described both in tems of what they do and what they say they do. One approach to describing the actions of individuals can be found in the theory of action (Argyris & Schon, 1974; 1978; Argyris, 1976; 1980; 1982; Argyris et al, 1985). According to propositions derived from the theory ‘agents design action to achieve intended consequences, and monitor themselves to learn if their actions are effective’ (Argyris et al, 1985, pp 80–81). Specifically, there are two kinds of theory of action, espoused theories and theories-in-use.

Espoused theories are those that an individual claims to follow. Theories-in-use are those that can be observed from an individual's action (Argyris et al, 1985). For example, a person's espoused theory would be the description he or she gives for doing something (eg, supporting the implementation of an IS during group meetings) while the person's theory-in-use refers to how he or she acts in relation to what was originally said (eg do the actions of the individual appear congruent to their assertion of support for the new system). It is both possible, and likely, for one's espoused theories and theories-in-use to be inconsistent. Further, it is possible for these inconsistencies to be either conscious or unconscious to the individual. We posit, however, that the inconsistencies in the two theoretical positions associated with strategy, which is widely suggested in the literature, is assumed to lead to consequences of better system quality, system use, and user acceptance of an IS (Ives & Olson, 1984). If, however, one or more users employ PRM as their action strategy the outcome may, in fact, be detrimental to the IS implementation effort.

Differences in espoused theories vs theories-in-use can be seen in widely divergent areas, from managerial accounting (Argyris, 1990) to the economic reforms of the canned fruit and vegetable industry in Poland (Obloj & Davis, 1991) to IS implementation (Levine & Rossmoore, 1993).

We have argued that PRM behaviour differs from the more common overt resistance to change which has been described in the IS implementation literature. It is our assertion that PRM may be difficult to both predict and detect. As such, the rational model of behaviour assumed in IS implementation may serve to create a blindness to the potential for PRM behaviour to confound the success of an implementation effort. We therefore offer examples of how PRM behaviour may manifest itself in an IS implementation setting. We follow this with suggestions on how the research community might approach the study of this phenomenon with an eye toward better defining the characteristics of the behaviour and, thus, developing a means of predicting and/or detecting it.

## 3. Passive resistance misuse

Within an IS implementation setting PRM can manifest itself in many different situations. In this section, we take an anecdotal approach to providing examples of various PRM scenarios with the intention of furnishing the reader with a clearer understanding of the phenomenon. Our purpose here is not to create generalisable descriptions of PRM behaviour situations but rather to frame the existence of the construct within real-life situations.

The introduction of a new IS into a work environment that was previously managed without the aid of the technology may cause frustration and resentment on one or more of the principals involved in the design and implementation effort:

Henry's company has recently gone through a merger and, as a result, much of the work in Henry's department will be controlled by a new computer information system. The news of the new system was the ‘straw that broke the camel's back’ for Henry. Up to this point, Henry has been able to manage ‘quite nicely’ without computers and has prided himself on the efficiency and effectiveness of his department's work.

For Henry, this whole business of reorganization has not been a positive experience. In addition to the new computers, he has a new reporting relationship that changes his former sense of flexibility and autonomy. He complains to his peers, and sometimes his family, that he feels ‘overmanaged’. In addition, the merger came just as Henry was eligible for advancement and, as a result, he did not receive the promotion he had been expecting and felt he deserved. He feels that the merger was actually a step back for him and has begun to fear that the implementation of the new computer system is the next step toward eliminating him altogether.

With primary responsibility for facilitating the specification of system requirements as they relate to his department, Henry finds himself ‘knee-deep’ in the design of the project despite his fears and frustrations. At these meetings, he senses an attitude of acceptance for the new technology amongst his peers and realises he dare not express his inner thoughts for fear of reprisal. As such, Henry goes out of his way to posture himself as one of the system’s biggest supporters and is constantly urging his fellow department managers to ‘get everybody moving’ toward the completion of the project.

In the confines of his own department, however, Henry's actions are quite different. He often ‘forgets’ to assign an employee to the creation of a report containing information requested by the design team. He ‘misses’ established appointments with project designers and analysts citing numerous reasons why he was suddenly unable to meet. Even after an employee provides Henry with requested information he temporarily ‘loses’ it on his desk and then makes excuses as to why the information is delayed. In some cases, Henry has made certain modifications to data generated for requirements specification purposes. Finally, he often discusses the new system with his subordinates and has recommended that they ‘take their time’ in becoming used to the system when it finally arrives. He suggests to them that the success of this system may be negatively related to their collective job security. (Adapted from Connor, 1991)

Henry is displaying a clear case of PRM behaviour. The intrusion of the IS into his previously stable world has caused him to rationalise his covert sabotage of the implementation effort. Further, by projecting his own fears of extinction onto his subordinates he is tacitly enlisting their support for his covert resistance. From this scenario, it can be easily seen that Henry's espoused theory is one of total support while his theory-in-use is one of resistance and subversion.

PRM behaviour does not necessarily manifest itself in conjunction with the beginning of an IS implementation project. In some cases, PRM may become an action strategy that is employed to bring disrepute to a new IS that is about to be placed into full scale operation:

Miriam is employed as a clerk in a small-town hospital's admissions department. She is one of eight clerks who share the usual admissions duties. Despite the rural setting for the hospital, its patient base is quite large and its daily operations are often hectic. Miriam and her co-workers frequently work overtime and regularly seem to be caught between patient and medical staff demands and difficulties.

In an effort to reduce the labour-intensity of the admissions process, the hospital has approved the design and implementation of an automated Admission, Discharge, and Transfer (ADT) system. Miriam and her fellow clerks were told that the system would automate their currently manual methods without materially altering them. Instead of the index cards system, the clerks would perform their duties via a computer monitor and the system would allow for the automatic generation of many of the discharge forms and documents currently being prepared manually. All the clerks, including Miriam, expressed excitement and appreciation that some relief was soon to arrive.

The plan was for the vendor of the ADT to train the clerks during the implementation process. The system was to be tested in parallel with the manual system and was to be made operational upon software validation. Because of her seniority in the admissions department and her outward support of the new system, Miriam was appointed as the user representative to the vendor's design team for the admission's desk. This appointment was considered to be quite prestigious among the remaining clerks and they eagerly congratulated Miriam on her new found responsibility.

Due to a hiring freeze instituted simultaneous to system implementation, the admission staff was not able to use temporary workers to assist during the early stages of parallel processing. As a result, the workload was actually higher than ever. Additionally, the hospital had ordered only one CRT. This was insufficient to handle the peak processing load. Miriam's fellow employees began to complain to her to use her position as user representative to do something about the situation. Although she continually tried to console them with promises of improvement, she too found it frustrating to cope with the added workload. This frustration was exacerbated by her fear of losing her prestigious position as user representative if she complained too loudly. Thus, she chose to posture herself with the committee in a manner that suggested she was supportive of the new system and told the committee that her fellow clerks would soon come around as well. Miriam knew in her heart, however, that this was not going to be easy. She was faced with a need to return her worklife to a more reasonable level while still preserving her new-found status in the organization.

After 4 weeks, the manual index card system was completely discontinued and a hospital-wide audit assured that the ADT census and locator files were correct. After 2 weeks, however, the computerised census and locator files were hopelessly confused. To continue with the daily operations while the problem was being resolved, the manual index card system was reinstated. After 2 days of work the computer staff had reconstructed the ADT files and found no system problem. The system was put back on line and the manual system was, again, discontinued.

This same problem situation presented itself two more times during the following 2 months. Each time the hospital computer staff could find no problem with the system. As a last resort, the computer staff took over the data entry function for several random 24-hour periods during the next month. Never during these spot audits did the system indicate any sign of failure. Because of this, the hospital administrator concluded that an internal effort to bring the system into disrepute was underfoot and she, therefore, announced that an investigation would be conducted to identify the individual or individuals responsible for the problem if it did not discontinue immediately. The data problems never reoccurred. (Adapted from Dowling, 1980)

Miriam chose to reduce the frustration she and her fellow workers were experiencing through PRM behaviour. By covertly sabotaging the data, she believed she could cast doubt on the system's effectiveness and reliability. Her behaviour was reinforced by the reinstatement of the manual index card system following the first system failure. Her employment of PRM served to preserve her status on the committee as a system proponent while simultaneously allowing her to subvert the progress and long-term stability of the new system.

The previous two scenarios suggest that PRM may be one of the causes for the inability of IS implementations to achieve success. We do not imply that PRM is a major implementation problem, but rather that its potential existence must be accounted for in any model of user involvement in IS implementation efforts. We have argued that people may engage in resistive or destructive behaviour to regain a sense of mastery and control over their environment. We have also suggested that not all sabotage can be effected in a conventional aggressive or violent form. PRM may, in fact, be a symbolic method of reassuring oneself that they are not at the mercy of the new technology.

Using the Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) framework for classifying IS failures, we classify PRM across two domains: the user domain and the organizational domain (Figure 4). Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) define these two domains as follows:

(1) User domain – identifies the skills, competence, personality traits, and motivational factors of the user population that directly or indirectly are involved with the system.

(2) Organizational domain - captures the nature and content of organizational roles by which organizational activities and tasks are carried out (p 275).

## User domain (identification)

Using the characteristics of P-A behaviour as warning flags against possible PRM, we argue that researchers and practitioners can identify, deter and rectify possible PRM situations. Initially, identification will need to be made of users whose actions are manifested in procrastination, stubbornness, intentional inefficiency and 'forgetfulness' (Fine et al, 1992). Identification of PRM can follow two phases: (1) the necessary exploratory research to empirically support the PRM construct within IS; and (2) the construction of methods or measures for diagnosing the existence of PRM. For example, users associated with a design or implementation project could be screened for P-A traits (Sacco & Zureik, 1990).

## Organizational domain (deterrence and rectification)

Research at this level will need to focus on what aspects of project groups are conducive to either (1) the behavioural development of P-A behaviour (Prout & Platt, 1983); for example, feelings of negative consequences or threats due to changes from the IS project; or (2) the cognitive-behavioural development of P-A (Burns & Epstein, 1983), in which individuals are inhibited to express assertive behaviour or anger. The development of PRM from either of these two possible scenarios could be tempered by the proper functioning of the group leader (Hirokawa & Gouran, 1989; Zigurs & Kozar, 1994).

Deterrence and rectification of PRM will also necessitate the group leader to identify if project members are employing governing variables associated with theories-in-use. These include: (1) achieve purpose as defined by individual not by the group; (2) win, do not lose; (3) suppress negative feelings; and (4) emphasise rationality.

Identification of any of these behaviours could lead to the project leader intervening and stemming the PRM behaviour.

Finally, the framework suggests that the control approaches to PRM need to be studied along the dimensions of IS design and IS implementation separately. Unique characteristics can be associated with the design of a system in which PRM can lead to the failure of the IS. Examples of this include support for a particular information requirements determination strategy combined with procrastination in delivering completed report/screen designs or in making the necessary appointments with key personnel to complete the logical system design diagrams. The scenario about Henry describes some of the unique activities associated with the IS design dimension and PRM behaviour. Such behaviour at this stage can result in the creation of suboptimal or seriously flawed logical designs.

Similarly, there are unique aspects to IS implementation that allow for the potential of PRM to derail the implementation process and cause eventual IS failure. PRM at this stage of a project is primarily directed at casting doubt on the viability of a project. Research into PRM along this dimension must focus more on the possible sources of PRM and avenues of persistence that are available during the physical implementation of an IS. Miriam's scenario demonstrates the manifestation of PRM behaviour in a physical implementation setting. An additional consideration during the implementation phase not found during design is that since an authorised user of the system may be the source of the problem, most traditional access-restriction methods may need to be augmented by additional methods. One such example would be the comparison of input data distributions with expected probability distributions. Actual distributions which, over an appropriate time interval, do not match expected parameters could serve as a signal that the input data is faulty and possible PRM behaviour is taking place (Dowling, 1980).

## Conclusions

There is an extensive range and combination of choices of action available to employees to vent their frustrations and fears when faced with the implementation of new IT. Regardless of the manifestation of the conflict, however, all affect and influence the degree of effectiveness of organizations, industry, and the economy as a whole (Analoui, 1992). Argyris (1971) makes the point that the introduction of IT into the work setting is as much an emotional human problem that requires interpersonal competence as a technical one requiring specialised discipline-specific knowledge. We argue that the necessary interpersonal competence for successful IS implementation must allow for less rational displays of resistance, such as PRM, than have been previously acknowledged.

While we freely admit that it is difficult to assess the extent to which PRM behaviour may be directly responsible for an IS failure, we argue that acknowledgement of its potential for contributing to failure should lead to the design of recognition and deterrence programmes. We need to move beyond the rational model of implementation that assumes overt resistance and explore methods of resolving the conflicts that encourage PRM behaviour. Such exploration should result in the development of more appropriate and constructive channels for expressions of fear associated with the intrusion of information technology into our world. We must be mindful that people do not resist new technology, rather they resist the effect that the new technology may have on their own lives. By recognising the potential for PRM behaviour we may be more able to create an atmosphere in which people become less inhibited to voice these expressions of fear.
