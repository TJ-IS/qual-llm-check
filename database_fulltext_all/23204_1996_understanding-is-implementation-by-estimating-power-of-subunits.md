# Understanding IS implementation by estimating power of subunits

ALM Cavaye $^{1}$ and JK Christiansen $^{2}$

$^{1}$ Delft University of Technology, Systems Engineering and Policy Analysis, Jaffalaan 5, 2600 GA Delft, The Netherlands; and $^{2}$ Copenhagen Business School, Informatics and Management Accounting, Howitzvej 60, 2000 Copenhagen F, Denmark

Understanding distribution of intra-organization power can provide useful information about implementation processes and also explanations (or predictions) of events and outcomes. When the implementation of IS/IT is used for both automatisation and business process changes, implementation involves organizational politics. Politics involves the interplay of various groups pursuing different interests while exercising power. Previous research of IS in power is discussed and extended by presenting a framework to measure subunit power. This framework is derived from organizational literature. The value of the framework for IS settings is illustrated by a case study. This framework is found to be useful for mapping relative power distribution at different moments in time. Changes in power distribution help explain a dramatic change in an implementation process. The understanding of relative subunit power is found to provide valuable ex ante information on the likely outcome of an IS implementation project or the possible obstacles an organizational change project might face.

## 1. Introduction

Implementation of information systems (IS) remains an area of interest for IS researchers and practitioners alike. In spite of careful planning and good intent, successful implementation is by no means assured. Many reasons have been put forward for explaining why it is difficult to achieve easy, successful or quick implementation. There can be group related factors such as management of human resources, getting the right people on the project or getting the team to function (DeMarco & Lister, 1987), or there could be individual, technical or organization problems (Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987). These reasons include: the system specification might be inappropriate; the technical system (once designed) may be inadequate; the system as seen from the users view might be inadequate; the users might not understand how to use the system; or the system does not fit with the organizational culture and the way the organization handles its tasks (Markus & Pfeffer, 1983).

In the past, IS implementations often concerned the automation of existing processes to improve speed, accuracy and efficiency. Nowadays IS are seen not merely as ways to automate the existing status quo, but as a means and opportunity to support the redesign of business processes (Hammer, 1990). By changing the method of processing and changing the status quo, the political structure of the organization is affected (Bjørn-Anderson & Pedersen, 1978; Robey et al, 1989). Hence, IS implementation involving organizational change needs to take into account the politics and power distribution in the organization.

This paper focuses on the political context of systems and on the fact that IS are able to change the power distribution among units in organizations. Proposed systems, which may threaten to change the existing power distribution might be undermined and resisted by powerful units in the organization.

A substantial portion of the IS literature discusses IS in a political context, but no publication has specifically tried to operationalise the subunit power construct in an IS context. This paper argues that it is possible to (partly) explain the outcome of implementation by measuring relative power among IS interest groups, and being aware of the interests of those groups. A framework from organizational literature is used to identify and map power among subunits in an organization. A case study is used to demonstrate the use of this framework. The discussion focuses on the implementation of an IS in an organization after a 6-year long period of opposition in the organization. This critical phase and the turn-around in the organization is explained in terms of a shift in the power distribution among subunits in the organization.

This paper is structured as follows. Previous literature on power and political issues in IS implementation is briefly reviewed. Then the framework used to map subunit power is introduced. The next section applies the power framework and analyses the organizational adoption in the implementation process from a power perspective. Lastly, findings and limitations of the framework are discussed.

## 2. Previous literature on power

The concept of power has constantly been of interest to writers and researchers in the social sciences, and since the late 1970s power has become accepted as an important issue in discussing IS implementation.

Power can be discussed in many ways and, because of this, there are many different definitions (Lukes, 1974; Clegg, 1989). It is generally accepted that power characterises relationships among social actors; that power concerns the capability of one or a group of social actors to control resources; and that power is reflected in the ability to overcome resistance in achieving a desired objective or result (Pfeffer, 1981).

Two general concepts of power emerge from the literature (Clegg, 1989). Some authors discuss dispositional aspects of power. Their work addresses sources of power and discusses the basis of that which gives power to a social actor (or group). Sources of power indicate factors that enable actors to acquire or possess power. Other writers have a process oriented view of power; they are interested in political and strategic issues and in the use of power. Use of power describes ways in which actors influence events and direct outcomes by virtue of their power. Although an actor has to possess power in order to use it, these two aspects of power are only loosely related (Pfeffer, 1981). Power is not necessarily tangible; social actors can have power because others perceive them to have power. Also, powerful actors may not be aware of their (perceived) power, and even when aware of power, actors may choose not to exercise their power. In all, power is a complex notion that can be discussed in a variety of ways (March, 1994, pp 139–174).

Within the IS context, power has been discussed in terms of the political dimension to system implementation. Since the late 1970s it has become accepted that the rational, functionalist perspective describes but one aspect of IS implementation (eg Bariff & Galbraith, 1978; Markus, 1983; Franz & Robey, 1984; Newman & Noble, 1990; Kling, 1991; Borum & Christiansen, 1993; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). The physical development of a computer-based IS can indeed be viewed as a rational process consisting of a number of logical steps that can be broken down into sequential and detailed tasks. The outcome of this rational process is a technical system that is designed to improve the efficiency, and/or effectiveness, of organizational operations, or of decision-making in the organization. However, technical excellence is neither necessary nor sufficient for implementation success (DeLone & MacLean, 1992).

Viewing IS development as a social and political change process, adds a human dimension and enables a more realistic analysis of IS implementation in organizations. Organizations may be viewed as entities where political activity takes place: where an individual or a group may entertain conflicting views and therefore have different interests. IS can change the balance of power between groups in organizations, and as such, implementation of an IS can appropriately be discussed from a political perspective (Keen, 1981; Markus, 1983; Franz & Robey, 1984; Kling & Iacono, 1984; Newman & Noble, 1990).

The political dimension can explain why some individuals or groups resist and sabotage systems while other groups support development and adopt systems readily. Success in implementation is then a relative term. Some users can perceive a system as successful because it enhances their power position in an organization; other users may perceive the same system as undesirable because it represents a loss of autonomy and control (Newman & Sabherwal, 1989).

When the power distribution implied by a system is different from or incompatible with the existing power distribution in an organization, there is likely to be resistance to that system (Bariff & Galbraith, 1978; Markus, 1981; Markus & Pfeffer, 1983). IS can affect intra-organizational power by changing existing structures for access to, and control over information. IS can also enable and formalise structures for performance evaluation and action initiation. IS establish and participate in choosing the reality for the organization and its members (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1977; Christiansen & Mouritsen, 1994). In this way IS can threaten groups' autonomy, undermine present distribution of authority, challenge existing ownership of information, and altogether can be regarded a threat by some groups and an opportunity by others.

Previous research has provided sufficient empirical evidence to accept that an implementation pattern and its potential success may well be affected by existing power structures in an organization, and the effect of the planned IS on those structures. However, no research has described in any detail how to map the power of different IS interest groups in an organization or has demonstrated the usefulness of a contingency framework.

## 3. A framework to illustrate the identification of relative subunit power

Operationalising power, and power distribution, is potentially difficult: power itself is a nebulous concept and as such is not easy to measure. Crozier (1964) was among the first to point out the importance of differences in power among subunits in an organization. In the 1970s organizational researchers developed a framework to operationalise interdepartmental power in organizations (Hickson et al, 1971), and subsequently tested the framework empirically (Hinings et al, 1974). The essence of their framework is embedded in several variables, which in combination, provide contingencies for power in organizational subunits. This framework has been mentioned in IS literature (Lucas, 1984): Lucas tried to determine the relative power of IS departments in three companies versus other departments in the companies. He used the Hickson et al framework, but did not describe the measurement instrument or his findings about its usefulness in detail.

Hickson et al (1971) identified a number of variables which may be used to indicate the degree of power of an organizational subunit. These variables are a subunit's ability to cope with uncertainty and the substitutability and centrality of activities. The variables are briefly described below and are summarised in Table 1.

## Uncertainty and coping with uncertainty

All organizations and units are faced with uncertainties; some groups have to face more uncertainty than others. What is important in the context of power, is units' abilities to cope with this uncertainty. The power of a unit is related to its ability to handle uncertainty and also to its ability to help other units cope with uncertainty, thus becoming an important 'mediator' between the other unit and its environment. A unit can cope with its own uncertainty by prevention (ie forestalling uncertainty), by good use of information (ie forecasting when and how uncertainty will affect the unit), and by absorption (ie by taking appropriate action when uncertainty hits the unit). A unit coping with uncertainty for other units also becomes the one which defines the input to those units, and thus to a large extent defines the area of discretion for the other unit.

## Substitutability

The power of a unit is related to the extent to which, and the ease with which its activities can be substituted and performed by other units, inside or outside the organization. For example, if it is possible to outsource the work of a unit, that unit is not considered to be a highly powerful unit, since its services or production can easily be replaced by another technically competent unit.

## Centrality

Activities of an organizational unit are central in an organization if the activities are related to many others in the organization (pervasiveness), and if the activities are vital to the workflow in the organization (immediacy). Power of an organizational unit is related to both the pervasiveness and the immediacy of the workflows of that unit.

Each of these three factors is necessary but not sufficient to explain the power of subunits. The combination of these variables explains to what extent a unit's activities affect the activities of another subunit in the organization. These dependences are termed strategic contingencies. Hickson et al (1971) argue that it is the combination of the three main variables that determines control of strategic contingencies, which in turn determine the power distribution among individual groups in an organization (Figure 1).

The framework is a comprehensive one as it includes aspects from both resource dependency as well as rational models of organization (Astley & Zajac, 1991). The variable ‘coping with uncertainty’ concerns the level of inderdependency with other units; the greater this dependency, the lower the degree of power of the subunit. The variables ‘substitutability’ and ‘centrality’ relate to the functional importance of the subunit; power is increased when the subunit performs tasks that are instrumental to achieving organizational goals (Astley & Zajac, 1991).

Empirical testing of the strategic contingency theory (Hinings et al, 1974) suggested that dominant power in an organization requires high values on all three of the variables. High scoring on only one of the dimensions is not sufficient for a department to exercise power. In terms of importance, Hinings et al (1974) suggest a probable ordinal ranking of (sub)variables as follows: (1) coping; (2) immediacy; (3) non-substitutability, and (4) pervasiveness.

The division of labour becomes the ultimate source of intra-organizational power in this framework. Power is explained by variables that are elements of each subunit's task, its functioning, and its links with activities of other subunits. The organizational perspective behind the framework is one viewing organizations as open systems consisting of interdependent functional groups, which have a power distribution ascribed due to the division of labour among the units. Groups rather than individual actors control power, making power a property of social relationships and not of individuals.

## 4. Explaining organizational implementation of IS using the framework – a case study

Strategic contingency theory provides the basis for apportioning power among various groups in an organization. Once aware of the relative power distribution and of the interests of subunits in organizations, it is possible to explain the general process of implementation. Although other variables might also affect the implementation process, the proposed framework is useful for getting an understanding of the power perspective. The framework presented in Figure 1 is applied here to a case study. IS implementation in this case study was not straightforward; it took 6 years of development and implementation efforts before organizational adoption of a formalized project management system was achieved.

Information concerning the case is derived from a longitudinal case study. One of the authors undertook all the fieldwork which included two intensive periods of data collection at the beginning and at the end of a 6-year timespan; contact with the organization is continuing. The researcher had a desk in the company during the intensive data collection periods making field observation possible. Data sources were multiple and information was derived from on-site interviews and observation. Information was updated, checked and double checked through many discussions with the informants. The approach can be characterised as an explorative, single case study inspired by ethnography (Yin, 1989).

A total of 49 interviews was carried out. Interview data represent the core empirical data. Each interview lasted between one and four hours – most lasted one-and-a-half to two hours. Several central informants were interviewed more than once during the implementation process. In addition, the researcher observed meetings and had access to many documents including internal papers, plans, annual reports, agendas and minutes. In all, the researcher was in the position to gain a good understanding of the organization, its structure and its processes and to describe the changes over time.

The organization, Bank Data, is a company owned by banks and offers computer services to Danish banks. Bank Data has approximately 550 employees. More than 200 employees are directly involved in systems development projects; the remainder are involved in activities such as operations, services, administration and finance, consultancy to the banks, and methods development. Senior management at Bank Data initiated the development of a project management system (PMS). The PMS was meant to be used for planning, monitoring and control of system development projects. Full implementation of the PMS was resisted for a long time; it took 6 years before the organization adopted the system as an integral part of its work procedures.

What were the reasons why the PMS in the end became adopted and used in the organization? Can subunit power explain how and why organizational implementation of the PMS took place 6 years after initialisation of the process? Furthermore, can the explanations we find be related to issues raised by the framework for analysing subunit power, ie is the .framework useful for understanding the process?

## 4.1 Subunits within the organization

The company consists of five major, clearly identifiable groups of employees each with their own function and value system. These five groups constitute the organizational subunits in this case. The five subunits and their involvement with projects will be described briefly.

## Systems Development

This large department (consisting of around 40% of all employees) upgrades existing systems, adds functionality to existing systems and develops new systems and applications. This department does ad hoc and creative work. Its employees tend to be young computer science graduates or are other well-educated employees interested in working on exciting projects with modern computer tools. The more experienced employees become project managers. The programmers and analysts work in a loosely structured project environment; 'chaotic' is what one programmer called it. The System Development employees enjoy the degree of freedom they have 'most of the time we can do whatever we want to do'. They realise that there is a downside to this freedom and that people outside their department hardly know what is going on at any one time.

When asked about the role of the Project Managers, one of them mentioned:

It was a very curious situation, really. No one knew what we did with our resources. Our main role was to manage projects. We needed resources to run the projects. One had to have a sense of the politics of the company and to be able to understand the workings of the organization. That way we would get the resources we asked for.

In all, projects in the System Development department were not organized in a formal way; a high degree of freedom characterised all project work.

## Operations

This department is responsible for the day-to-day running of the computer systems. Tasks in this area of Bank Data are repetitive and maintenance oriented. Because of this Operations is a department with a relatively low status in the company; those in Systems Development do not think particularly highly of their colleagues in Operations.

Operations has a team of network specialists who run and maintain the banking network connecting more than 1000 branches of the affiliated banks. Operations also employs programmers to work on internal projects. Although these employees have similar skills to the employees of Systems Development, their work is considered to be less interesting. The projects of Operations are of a much smaller scale than those in Systems Development. In addition, the Operations projects are for internal use within their own department; they concern maintenance of equipment and procedures for the handling, storing and processing of data. Lastly, Operations employs a large number of people to take care of physical handling of data-storage, printing and mailing. These employees are not as highly skilled as the Systems Development employees.

## Finance

The Finance department is concerned with financial monitoring and control; it tracks cash flows and budget transgressions. It is a bureaucratic, administrative department that is not very visible in the company. Bank Data suffered unexpected cash shortages a few years previously and the head of Finance had been asked to explain the incident to the Chief Executive Officer (CEO). Since then the main issue for the head of Finance is the management of liquid assets. Finance does not interfere in the daily activities of the company; Finance employees merely ask departments for explanations when budgets overrun and those explanations are forwarded to the CEO. Financial reporting is confined to aggregate reports about departments; no detailed information is maintained about individual projects.

Finance is aware of the loose structure of many Systems Development projects: most projects are started without any estimated costs having been produced; other projects overrun their budgets. However, Finance focuses on other areas and does not feel called (or able) to improve the financial management of projects.

Other departments did not seem to regard Finance very highly. This was the response of the head of Finance when asked about the attitude of other departments towards his department:

The vice-president of the company and the managers of Systems Development have mentioned that the company could operate without a Finance department. The Systems Development people would prefer to have the Finance department closed. It has been suggested that we could have an account in a bank for money transfers in and out of the company. They say that we would need perhaps one person only to operate the account.

## Methods department

This department is concerned with the concepts of system development. The Methods department works out new plans and methods and aim to provide smarter, safer, more effective ways of developing systems within Bank Data. Not surprisingly, the Methods people favour highly structured approaches to system development and they lament the free-and-easy attitude to projects in the Systems Development area.

Methods has a difficult time getting methods and standards accepted and used by those involved in projects and system development work. Often the project managers succeed in arguing for use of methods and standards other than the official Bank Data ones. Project managers use the argument that their specific project is a special case where the official methods and standards do not fit well or do not apply.

In general those employed in Methods are not highly respected by the system developers and project managers. On one occasion an informant said:

All those who could not develop systems were put in that thing called the Methods department. There they could think about methods and standards. That way they could not do any harm.

## The consultants

The consultants provide a liaison between Bank Data and the customer banks. The consultants visit banks to market Bank Data products and services; they provide Bank Data with ideas and requests for new computer applications; they also play an important part during implementation of newly developed systems, especially those involving a large number of end-users.

Although the consultants are the official link between the clients outside the organization and the Systems Development department, they regularly find that Systems Development takes on that role directly. The managers in Systems Development prefer to have their own direct contact with end-users (the clients) something which is also valued by the clients themselves. Hence, the consultants usually lose control over projects during system development. Once a system is built and implemented, control and responsibility lies with the consultants again. This responsibility is not always pleasant: on one occasion a major system had been released with software bugs and this caused over 2000 telephone calls with queries and complaints to the consultants.

## 4.2 Power distribution among subunits

The relative power of the various subunits of Bank Data was measured according to the strategic contingency framework described in Section 3. Using descriptive information, interview data and data gathered by observation, the groups were assessed on all three variables affecting subunit power.

## Uncertainty and coping with uncertainty

Uncertainty is high for Systems Development who do not know about future projects, and is medium for the consultants who do not know what new queries and requests await them when visiting customers. Uncertainty is low for the Operations department (running the computer systems) and for Finance (who work according to well-defined guidelines and frameworks).

The ability to cope with uncertainty is high for both IS groups (Systems Development and Operations) who are prepared for uncertainty, ready to define and handle new projects and ready to cope with unexpected system breakdowns. The Consultants on the other hand, cannot easily solve customer problems or fulfill requests since they are dependent on other groups in Bank Data to do so. Similarly, Finance does not have a high score since they do not face great uncertainty: their books, plans and systems are highly structured and so are their operating practices in the organization. They stick to their book-keeping.

## Non-substitutability of activities and staff

Substitutability concerns the ability of Bank Data to replace personnel in a subunit and to obtain the output of a subunit from elsewhere. One could consider how easy and painless it would be to outsource the activities of a certain subunit.

High non-substitutability indicates that it is not easy to find a replacement for staff and expertise; low substitutability indicates that it would be relatively easy to carry out the activities of a subunit in an alternative way. The consultants and Finance groups rate low on non-substitutability. It would be relatively easy to find new clerical staff for the Finance department and replace their services; similarly, it would not be hard to recruit new consultants among (ex)bank employees. It would be less easy to find personnel for the Systems Development, Operations and Methods groups; in each of these groups specific skills are required.

## Centrality of workflows

Centrality of a subunit's activities is related to the immediacy and pervasiveness of the group's workflows. Immediacy particularly refers to the speed with which a subunit's workflow affects the output of the organization.

The Operations department clearly has a very immediate role in Bank Data: the moment that Operations break down, services to customers is stopped. The work of Systems Development and the Consultants is less immediate: delays in their workflows affect output, but only in the medium and long-term. Immediacy of the activities in Methods and Finance is low: their workflows do not have a direct effect on Bank Data's service provision.

Pervasiveness is the degree to which a unit's workflows are linked to the workflows of other units. The Finance group has links with all other departments, collecting information on all groups and sending reports to all others. The Operations group on the other hand, has few links with others. Methods would like to have links – especially with the projects – but has few.

## Relative power of subunits

In order to determine relative power among the groups at Bank Data, the findings concerning coping, centrality and non-substitutability must be combined for each subunit. If the relative power of a group is greater, the better it is able to cope, the less substitutable, and the more central are its activities.

Table 2 shows how power is distributed among the interest groups at Bank Data. The rows of Table 2 relate to the various interest groups; the columns show the contingency variables. The variable ‘centrality’ has been considered in two separate columns, ‘immediacy’ and ‘pervasiveness’. This has been done to concur with empirical findings of Hinings et al (1974) which suggested that those two sub-variables should be rated separately when trying to measure power of subunits.

Ratings for ability to cope, immediacy, non-substitutability, and pervasiveness were derived from descriptions of the data obtained about each subunit. The authors gave values of high, medium, or low to the variables for each subunit. Independently of each other the same values were arrived at.

The groups were then ranked according to their power rating. There were two subunits scoring ‘medium’ and two scoring ‘low’ in relative power. Analysis of the power distribution at Bank Data, presented in Table 2, shows Finance to be in the weakest position. This is so, because the Finance group cannot deal with uncertainty and so does it carry out functions of importance to the other groups? The Finance group depends heavily on routine and, although their role is pervasive, they do not take independent action and do not influence any of the other groups. The Hinings et al (1974) study also found accounting departments to be the weakest in terms of power when compared to other units in an organization.

The Systems Development group turns out to be the most powerful group at Bank Data. Previous use of the framework in an IS setting resulted in very different findings about the relative power of the main IS group in organizations. Lucas (1984) found that the IS departments are relatively weak in terms of power. The difference between Lucas and the current findings probably stem from the fact that the case presented here concerns a company providing computer services, whereas Lucas was studying three manufacturing companies. In the computer services company presented here Systems Development controlled critical activities, while major resources and processes in a manufacturing company tend to be controlled by production and marketing groups.

Systems Development at Bank Data derive their power levels from their high scores on all but the least important of the variables. In particular, their readiness for meeting and, hence, their ability to cope with changes and challenges presented in their day-to-day activities contribute to the power of Systems Development. Also, the Systems Development group (by developing and implementing systems) have the other units dependent on their skill and activities. At the same time its staff is not easy to substitute, which enhances the power of the group.

The power distribution at Bank Data is not easily changed because the different levels of power are derived from structural conditions within the organization. However, full implementation of the proposed project management system could potentially bring about changes: the PMS would make groups more accountable and hence could enhance the power position of the Finance group and could decrease the power position of Systems Development.

## 4.3 Using the power bases to explain resistance to implementation

During a meeting of the Board various owners of the company voiced concern about the use and allocation of resources, and asked for an improvement in the management of Bank Data projects. The CEO of Bank Data decided that such improvement could be achieved by designing and implementing a computerised project management system for internal use. Hence the CEO officially initiated the development of such a system.

When announcing the plans to the organization at large, the Finance and Methods departments were extremely interested in the proposed project management system (PMS). Finance was concerned about project overruns and budget transgressions; they wanted improved methods for the monitoring and control of the project. Methods was concerned about the causal manner towards system development; they wanted better planning and management of projects.

Development of the PMS was entrusted to the System Development group. The group readily took on the responsibility for design and building of the system: this type of development was exactly in their area of expertise. However, the group that had been given the task to develop the system within Bank Data was also the group that had most to lose from implementation.

Implementation of the PMS was not in the interests of the Systems Development group. The PMS was meant to streamline and formalise system development activities but such formalisation of operations did not fit in with the existing structure and culture of the Systems Development unit. Increased formalisation and accountability for project development would undermine the mystique and entrepreneurial atmosphere in Systems Development and would increase visibility of their activities.

Systems Development offered quiet and covert resistance to PMS implementation using excuses, delays and stalling tactics. Once PMS development was completed the Systems Development department ignored the system and quietly refused to enter data into it. The CEO wrote two official memos stating that everyone should use the system, that all new projects should be entered into the system, and that project calculations and estimates should be based upon data from the system. When it became obvious that Systems Development would have to comply and use the PMS, the department argued that the system was difficult to use. This was a strange excuse from Systems Development since they had built the system themselves! Systems Development was given the opportunity to build a new user-interface and a whole year went by before the interface was redesigned. Only then, when there was no other way around it, did Systems Development start to use the PMS.

In spite of efforts from Finance and Methods to insist on PMS introduction, Systems Development was able to resist its actual implementation for several years. The Finance and Methods subunits had little power and lacked the political weight to have their policies acted upon by others. Systems Development, on the other hand, was in a powerful position within Bank Data. They represented the largest group of employees; their unit was prepared for and could cope well with uncertainty; and their people and expertise were not easy to substitute.

## 4.4 Using the power bases to explain organizational adoption

Organizational implementation of the PMS started 6 years after the issue of ‘better project management’ had come to the fore and the CEO had initiated development. During these 6 years several projects had been initiated to design, develop and implement the formal project management system in the organization. The reasons for the long-awaited, successful outcome of organizational implementation are multiple, but in combination they brought about a noticeable shift in the power distribution among the subunits at Bank Data:

\- Change in important actors in the organization. The

CEO and several top managers left Bank Data. New managers put a greater emphasis on management and on value for money. New management thus created a shift in culture and values within Bank Data.

\- Changes in the success criteria for the systems development projects. The newly appointed senior management team was not particularly interested in technically sophisticated projects. Instead, management demanded projects that were completed on time, within budget and technically sound, in that order of importance.

\- Change of focus from a purely technical and quality-oriented Systems Development group towards Systems Development concentrating on resources and time required for completion of projects within economic budgets and schedules. Within the new value system the PMS became very important. The PMS actually enabled management to implement the new values.

\- Change in scope and control of Finance. As the PMS was used and became an integral part of the organization, Finance had access to information on budgets for existing and proposed projects. Finance was therefore, with the use of PMS, able to monitor projects and report use of resources.

\- Change in that which was considered to make a ‘good’ project leader. It was no longer technical competence alone that made a successful project manager. The new CEO and his associates appointed project managers with technical and economic insight and with an obligation to have an eye on both in their supervision of projects.

All these factors facilitated a general change in the power distribution within Bank Data. The nature of the Systems Development group changed; formal project management methods became accepted. Finance got a more central role to play in the work process and began to cope with the uncertainty in the work flows of the organization. Control of the PMS was moved from Systems Development to Finance. In this way the power of Finance was increased and the power position of Systems Development compared to Finance decreased. Table 3 shows the Bank Data 'powermap' on the basis of the Hickson et al (1971) framework at the time of adoption of the PMS.

Changes in the power distribution in the organization were subtle, but sufficient to enable organizational implementation of the project management system. Finance became a more powerful group backed by changes in the way of thinking of the organization, which in turn were caused by changes in the top management team and by external pressure on the organization. The Systems Development group, which previously had resisted PMS implementation, adapted to changes in the organization and accepted use of the PMS as an integral part of their work. The PMS fitted into the new view that the organization had of itself. By accepting full implementation of the PMS the Systems Development group contributed to change in the organization and affirmed shifts in the power distribution among Bank Data's subunits.

## 5. Discussion

The Bank Data implementation process – and the shift in the process – can be explained by analysing the power distribution among the subunits over time. Original variation in support for the PMS could be explained firstly, by the extent to which the system facilitated the work performed by the subunits, and secondly, the fit between the system and expressed interests of each group. By tracking the interest of the subunits and mapping power distribution among the subunits, the course of events concerning the implementation process could be explained. Differences among the subunits in their handling of uncertainty, centrality and non-substitutability appear to describe important dimensions of an organization when we want to understand implementation processes. It was found that the use of the framework helped us to understand why organizational adoption of a project management system finally took place.

Shifts in power distribution do not usually happen spontaneously. In the Bank Data case the relative power distribution over time was affected by change among the important actors of the organization and a change in the perception of the organization about itself. The vision of that which the company ought to be, and ought to stand for, changed from ‘an organization which is innovative, technically focused, but with little economic orientation’ to ‘a company which carries out projects on time, inexpensively and effectively’. This change in vision came at a time of change among senior management and led actors in subunits to regard finance and economic considerations as important issues. Once the financial and efficiency issues became more prominent in Bank Data, power among the subunits shifted subtly and the project management system was finally adopted fully.

Apart from its explanatory qualities ex post we tentatively suggest that the framework could be used to help foresee potential difficulties of a proposed implementation. Using the framework it is possible to map relative power among units that exists before, and that is likely to exist after, a proposed system implementation. Shifts in power of the individual units could indicate potential bottlenecks. In this way it may be possible to map out ex ante whether a proposed implementation is likely to be easily achieved and where the bottlenecks are likely to be. If problems in implementation are foreseen, measures may be taken to counter resistance or to overcome difficulties before the problems become unsurpassable.

The intra-organizational power framework based on strategic contingencies has its limitations. Hickson et al (1971) already mention that their framework does not include all types of power. Other sources of power include: tradition; control over someone in top management (or them being dependent on subordinates); or individual differences of personnel in the various subunits. Individual differences could be differences with regard to intelligence, age, sex or personality factors as well as dominance and risk taking behaviour. The culture within an organization or the culture of a specific country might also influence the role of individual subunits, departments and individuals. For instance, power distances between employees and their bosses differ greatly from one country to another according to the findings of Hofstede (1984). In Denmark, the country setting of the case study described earlier, power distance is small when compared to that in countries in southern Europe.

Another issue not explicitly addressed in the Hickson et al (1971) framework is the effect of the external environment on the power position of a subunit. The variable of intra-organizational ‘coping with uncertainty’ might have little relevance if that uncertainty is not seen as part of the organization’s handling of its whole environment. As detailed by Salancik and Pfeffer (1977) the environment includes, on the one hand, the internal environment with its shifting situational contexts in which particular decisions get made; and on the other hand, the external environment that the organization can hope to influence but is unlikely to control. Coping with external uncertainty may be critical for the organization’s survival. Hence, the subunits which handle uncertainty facing the whole company deserve a higher score than the units facing local and internal uncertainty.

The framework does not deal with the issue concerning the power of individuals within and above subunits. Individual actors differ in their abilities and skills to exercise power and to use their opportunities. Other studies have shown how individual players who are very skilled in controlling the flow of information, and controlling access to important decision arenas, get more influence than those less skilled in these areas (Pettigrew, 1973). Therefore one question is how an actor gets into the position of being able to control information flows? Is that due to personal skills or is it a result of the power the person has due to the contingency factors described, and has the actor achieved the control of many strategic contingency factors due to the personal skill possessed?

The framework and its use as discussed in this paper focuses on the measurement and mapping of power which is by all accounts an ephemeral organizational variable. It is hardly possible to measure the power concept in a scientific manner, and rating power according to the framework will always be a subjective matter. Implementation and organizational action is carried out by, and among, people – each of whom have their own interpretation of the world surrounding them. A social constructionist view (Berger & Luckmann, 1966) on power issues might raise questions like: why does someone or some group come to be perceived as powerful? What social processes led to the relative weakness of the Finance and the Methods departments in the case? Why did those two departments accept their position? In order to answer such question reference could be made to Lukes (1974). His three-dimensional view of power is concerned with the issue of the ‘socially structured and culturally patterned behaviour of groups and practices of institutions’ and with the subjective and real interests of individuals versus the official political agenda. Such a perspective could expand the understanding of power in the case described here. However, an analysis of this kind was not the objective of this paper and these questions remain to be addressed at another time with another framework.

## 6. Concluding comments

This paper set out to demonstrate the use of the strategic contingency framework in an IS setting. This framework maps out power distribution among subunits in an organization. Rating and ranking power among subunits can be helpful in many implementation settings since politics and power distribution in organizations often affect the implementation process.

The framework was applied to the case of Bank Data. Technically the system described in the case was implemented after 3 years of development, but organizational implementation was not accomplished until the power balance shifted after 6 years. Although March (1994, pp 145–146) emphasises the difficulty of measuring power he also notes that significant differences in power might help us understand organizational processes. Since the differences between the power of the subunits in the Bank Data case were significant we feel they provided us with insight into the organizational processes. Furthermore, the case organization seems to fit nicely into the type of organizational framework that the strategic contingency framework is suited for: an organization with several subunits closely connected and linked in the work flow.

In spite of its limitations the framework can be usefully applied in empirical research and in practical settings. The framework can be adapted easily by including the external environment when rating ‘uncertainty’ and ‘coping’ sub-variables; the framework can be added to by including main individuals as well as subunits. It is a framework to help take a shot of power distribution; it does not claim to explain why or how power distribution at any one time has come to be the way it is.

The Hickson et al (1971) framework has been previously used by Lucas (1984). However, the current paper provides substantially more detail about operationalisation of the framework's variables. There is also an additional reason for rekindling interest in the use of the framework in IS settings. In the current climate of organizational transformation and process changes supported by information technology (IT), there is an increased need to be aware of forces within the organization over time, and explicitly taking into account power distribution of subunits could help those responsible for IS implementation processes.
