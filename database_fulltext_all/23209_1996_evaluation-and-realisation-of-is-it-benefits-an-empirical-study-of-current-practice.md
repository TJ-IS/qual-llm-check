# Evaluation and realisation of IS/IT benefits: an empirical study of current practice

J. WARD, P. TAYLOR and P. BOND

The Information Systems Research Centre, Cranfield School of Management, Cranfield, Bedford MK43 0AL, UK

This paper presents the main findings of a 1994 survey of UK industry practices in the evaluation and realisation of IS/IT benefits ('benefits management'). The survey addresses the issues which affect the ability of organizations to realise the full benefits of IS/IT investments, i.e. not only the pre-investment appraisal and post-investment evaluation processes, but also how organizations do or do not ensure that benefits claimed are actively managed through to realisation. To do this a new benefits management process model was used to structure a questionnaire to elicit details of how effective organizations are in addressing benefits management throughout the investment lifecycle. Sixty organizations responded to the survey, thus providing a wealth of data for analysis. This paper presents some of the key results of that analysis. From the survey, it is clear that many organizations believe that current methods are far from satisfactory in ensuring that the benefits are properly identified and realised. Very few have a comprehensive process for managing the delivery of benefits from IS/IT. This paper offers new insight into the reasons for the current unsatisfactory situation and points the way to how the situation could be significantly improved.

## Introduction

This study presents the main findings of a 1994 survey of UK industry practices in the evaluation and realisation of information systems/information technology (IS/IT) benefits ('benefits management'). The paper takes a lifecycle viewpoint on the evaluation of IS/IT investments, by considering the overall process by which the benefits from a potential investment in IS/IT are identified and appraised, realised in practice during a project, and evaluated post-project.

This work considers evaluation of the outcome of investment in IS/IT, rather than appraisal of the prospect of investment in IS/IT. The central theme is to understand how benefits are (or not) realised in practice and to understand what has to take place in order to ensure that the potential benefits are actually realised. The overall process of evaluation and realisation of IS/IT benefits has been termed benefits management and may be defined as:

'The process of organizing and managing such that potential benefits arising from the use of IT are actually realised'.

This process-based focus of IS/IT investments has been developed within a research project into benefits management at the Cranfield School of Management Information Systems Research Centre (ISRC). This research project has been sponsored by ten major private and public sector organizations, with the aim of developing best practice guidance in benefits management. This guidance is initially restricted to the sponsoring organizations, but will be published at a later date. The survey described in this paper was undertaken as part of this research project, in order to understand the current industry situation with respect to a lifecycle viewpoint of IS/IT investments.

## Background

Evaluation of investments in IS/IT has been the subject of much academic debate and literature in recent years. Two main themes from within the literature which are of relevance to this current paper are:

\- strategic information systems planning (SISP)

\- investment appraisal techniques for evaluating IS/IT investments.

SISP seeks to align IS/IT investment with business strategies, in order to maximise the opportunity for overall competitive advantage through IS/IT. Ward et al. (1990) and Earl (1989) exemplify the SISP approach, and Galliers (1991) provides insight into successful implementation of such an approach. However, while the investment priorities for IS/IT may then be determined, it is typically through individual IS/IT investment projects that value to the organization is realised in practice, i.e. investment appraisal techniques for evaluating IS/IT are still required in addition to SISP (Peters, 1990). While the survey reinforced the importance of SISP (see Figure 3), the emphasis for this particular paper is on the lifecycle evaluation of an individual investment in IS/IT. The lifecycle emphasis within this paper takes into account not only the pre-investment appraisal and post-investment evaluation processes, but also how organizations do or do not ensure that the benefits claimed are actively managed through to realisation.

## Evaluation of IS/IT benefits

Evaluation of IS/IT benefits is, in most cases within the current literature, concerned mainly with either the classification of types of benefits or with the pre-project assessment and justification techniques for identifying and evaluating potential benefits which an IS/IT project may deliver. However, it seems that there is general recognition that these evaluation techniques are frequently inadequate, even in assessing the value of IS/IT investments.

There is a great deal of literature in this area, and only the most recent relevant work is discussed here. Willcocks (1992) provides an overview of the major evaluation methods, and concludes that the area of IT evaluation is not well developed but is increasingly important. Farbey et al. (1994) outline a taxonomy of techniques, and suggest criteria for choosing a particular method. They suggest that ‘a broader view of existing methods is required, one which explicitly addresses the questions of what is being evaluated, for what purpose, by whom, and for what audience’. Brown (1994) argues for a more appropriate treatment of intangibles, and notes that ‘The soft benefits of IT projects are difficult to estimate or value, but are of increasing significance’. Powell (1992) reviews existing methods and searches for the underlying motives behind using or not using such techniques. His concluding remarks are that ‘Currie (1989) terms IT justification as a ritual of legitimacy, not a process of really assessing benefits’ and most importantly for this paper ‘But then, as was pointed out a quarter of a century ago, the important thing in business is not to make good forecasts but to make them come true’. This is the essence of benefits realisation which we discuss later, and which formed an important part of the issues which the survey addressed.

Recent work in the field has started to focus on the actual use of such evaluation techniques. Willcocks and Lester (1991) surveyed 50 organizations and found that most still use traditional cost/benefit analysis, possibly with some additional criteria. Balantine et al. (1994) surveyed 98 organizations and also found that simple cost/benefit analysis was most widely used. A survey by Farbey et al. (1992) discovered that only 50% of projects were subject to formal pre-investment appraisal, and in less than half of the cases was a recognised financial analysis technique used. One approach to the apparent inadequacy of investment appraisal techniques has been to develop new techniques such as information economics (Parker et al., 1988) and return on management (Strassmann, 1990). However, Farbey et al. (1993) and Hares and Royle (1994) have criticised these methods as difficult to apply, and there is little evidence that they are at all widely used in practice.

From the literature we can conclude that existing evaluation methods are considered to be unsatisfactory and are not applied widely or consistently. More sophisticated evaluation methods do not appear to be a satisfactory answer. Against this background, the authors have taken an alternative approach, that of revisiting what constitutes a benefit, and how such a benefit could be realised in practice. To do this requires a consideration of the wider organizational context within which benefits are being sought, and a wider notion of what needs to be evaluated, other than just the proposed investment in IT itself.

## Realisation of IS/IT benefits

IS/IT implementation is usually brought about by a desire to improve business performance, i.e. in order to realise business benefits. Equally, it is recognised that IS/IT on its own does not deliver benefits. What IS/IT can do is to enable benefit opportunities. To take advantage of these benefit opportunities, changes must take place in the way that business activities are performed or in the way that information is used. Benefits may therefore be considered as the effect of the changes, i.e. the differences between the current and proposed way that work is done.

Earl (1992) provides support for this view that benefits are associated with business change and not the technology itself. Commenting on the 1980s view of IT as a new and strategic weapon, he claims: 'The shrewder and more experienced observers suggested that investment in IT alone usually yields very little return. If substantial business benefit was to be earned, other changes were also required'. He then goes further by suggesting that 'Firms should identify business change needs or opportunities first. They should then agree what capabilities and actions, including IT, are needed to achieve the change and be managed as a whole if full business benefits are to be delivered'.

This generic view of benefits through business change can be compared to existing classifications of types of benefits. Remenyi et al. (1991), adopting a similar view to Parker et al. (1988) and using the terminology of Zuboff (1988), describe three phases of IS/IT adoption and the associated types of benefits as:

'Automate (efficiency), Informate (effectiveness), Transformate (new business)'. Only in the final transformate stage is any explicit reference made to business change. However, the previous stages also depend on business changes in order to realise benefits, e.g. work changes enabling headcount reduction benefits in the automate phase. During the informate phase, benefits will only be realised if the information is used to change the way that business is done; e.g. customer sales information can enable effectiveness benefits based on a different sales incentive scheme concentrating on the most profitable customers.

If benefits are derived from IS/IT through business changes, then it is reasonable to assume that the implications of these changes must be assessed pre-project in order to quantify the potential benefits. Also, it is the effects of these changes which must be measured and evaluated post-project in order to determine if the desired benefits have been achieved in practice. If no measurable effects can be identified post-project, other than the implementation of the technology itself, then one must conclude that no benefits have actually been realised. Furthermore, it is reasonable to propose that in order to realise the potential benefits in practice, the business changes must be undertaken in a planned manner, with well-defined responsibilities for making such changes.

## A benefits management process model

A process model of benefits management has been developed within the Cranfield research programme (see Figure 1), derived from an analysis of the context within which benefits management is required. It was developed from other models of planned business improvement such as total quality management, and existing work on benefits management within the NHS (1993). This model has been tested and refined in reviews of actual IS/IT projects within the research project sponsors' organizations. The model now forms the basis of best practice guidelines on benefits management, and the project sponsors are already gaining benefits from implementing tailored versions of this benefits management process within their organizations. Having developed and validated the process model within the research project, it was proposed as the basis for this wider survey of current industry practices.

The elements of the process model are described briefly in the following sections, since it formed the basis of the structure for the survey questionnaire and the issues which it addressed.

## Identifying and structuring benefits

The proposed benefits are identified and, for each proposed benefit, suitable business measures are developed (both financial and non-financial). The benefits are structured in order to understand the linkages between technology effects, business changes and overall business effects, e.g. business objectives. At this stage, it becomes possible to assess the overall business benefits. It is also possible to assess the current situation with the measures that have been developed.

## Planning benefits realisation

For each benefit, specific responsibility for realising the benefit is allocated within the business. The required business changes are also assessed and planned for, and a benefits realisation plan is produced. At this stage, the full costs of both IT development and business changes can be assessed, in order to make a fully informed judgement as to the viability of the proposed project. Any intended benefit for which responsibility cannot be allocated and for which a delivery plan cannot be developed should be rejected at this stage.

## Executing the benefits realisation plan

Alongside the implementation of the proposed IT application, the necessary business changes as detailed in the benefits realisation plan are carried out.

## Evaluating and reviewing results

Following the full implementation of IT and business changes, the previously developed business measures are used to evaluate the effects of the project. Review of ‘before and after’ measures provides an explicit mechanism for evaluating whether the proposed business benefits have actually been delivered.

## Potential for further benefits

As a result of the post-project review, it may become apparent that further benefits are now achievable, which were not envisaged at the outset. This stage provides the opportunity to plan for, and realise, these further benefits. In a wider context, this stage also provides the opportunity to review and learn from the overall project process, thus facilitating the transfer of lessons learned to future projects.

## The benefits management survey

## Methodology

The research presented in this paper is part of a larger study concerned with benefits management, motivated by a belief that the scope of current approaches, methodologies and processes usually fail to address the management of business change adequately, with a consequent significant loss of potential benefit to the organization. The purpose of this larger study to date has been to identify the scope of benefits management, and develop models and processes to serve as a basis for understanding and communicating the concept and its implications.

The larger study has incorporated the results from ten case studies, of organizations wishing to evaluate and further incorporate benefits management into their own practices. In this way the research seeks to ensure that the research will have demonstrable relevance to any organization seeking to improve the benefits it derives from IS/IT investments. A combination of research techniques was employed for the larger study, including a literature search, and case study-based organizational interviews and workshop sessions involving a number of companies associated with the project.

Having developed a process model of benefits management, and having validated this model through its application in case studies within the sponsoring organizations, it was necessary to evaluate the relevance of the model in a wider range of organizations. The overall purpose was to establish a ‘benchmark’ of current industry norms and practices, and also to find out the relative proportion of organizations currently using a comprehensive approach to managing IS/IT benefits. A postal survey comprising a series of structured questions was selected as an appropriate research method, in order to obtain a wide spectrum of data from a variety of organizations. The survey was undertaken during the fourth quarter of 1994.

The principal objectives of the benefits management survey were therefore:

\- to establish current industry practices and norms in managing IS/IT benefits

\- to investigate whether organizations currently have a methodological approach to managing IS/IT benefits - to evaluate further the relevance of a process model of benefits management.

The questionnaire was developed from the set of issues which were known from the larger study to be vital to benefits management. However, the questions were presented in such a way that they could easily be understood even where no formal benefits management procedures existed in the respondent's organization. No attempt was made to define benefits or benefits management formally, so that respondents were free to interpret benefits in their normal manner.

An initial survey was piloted with ten respondents, selected from member organizations of the Cranfield ISRC as being likely to comment freely on its structure, and the results used to revise the wording and format of the questionnaire (these respondents were not used in the main survey).

The target audience for the survey was senior IS/IT and business management within the ‘Times Top 100’ companies, plus a further 150 large organizations (selected from an existing Cranfield database) screened to be a representative cross-section of UK industry. A few public sector organizations were also included in the survey, in order to establish initially whether any major differences exist between public and private sectors in terms of managing IS/IT benefits (although this issue is not addressed in this paper). Further surveys are currently underway in the public sector and for the NHS, in order to explore their differing situation in more detail.

In all cases the survey target was a single named point of contact within the organization, in order to maximise the potential response rate, and to avoid duplication of replies from an organization. The responses were optionally anonymous in order to maximise the chances of obtaining meaningful answers to potentially sensitive questions. Though the majority of the target audience was senior IS/IT management, in several cases a business manager actually responded. The mailing consisted of an introductory letter, the questionnaire, and a supplementary sheet optionally identifying the respondent (in practice only 10% of respondents (6) opted for anonymity). No attempt has been made to derive statistics-based deductions from the data, owing to the small sample size. The data analysis is mostly presented by indicating the percentage (and actual number in brackets) for any given response.

## Survey structure and issues addressed

The questionnaire was structured according to the benefits management process model, with additional sections for background information and general questions. The questionnaire contained 59 questions, with a mixture of yes/no, multiple choice and free text response possibilities.

Background questions provided general information on the respondent, such as job title and whether they considered themselves to be IS/IT or business oriented, and also general information on the organization, such as industry type and a categorisation of the strategic importance of IS/IT. General questions were concerned with assessing the types of benefits that the organizations' senior management perceived as being provided by IS/IT, and also the respondent's level of confidence in the delivery of these benefits. This section also asked questions about methodologies for:

\- systems development

\- project management

• IS/IT investment appraisal

\- benefits management.

In each case the respondent was asked if their organization had such a methodology, how widespread its usage was, and its degree of effectiveness in ensuring successful information systems. A final general question was asked at the very end of the questionnaire, to determine the respondent's views on the scope for improvement in their current approach to managing IS/IT benefits.

The remainder of the questionnaire was structured according to the benefits management process model, and addressed the key issues discussed below.

## Identifying and structuring benefits

Key issues here are how the organization currently identifies potential benefits for a proposed IS/IT project, and how a justification for the project is prepared. Questions were asked on the appropriateness of the current justification process, and any problems this causes. Also, whether the current process identifies and quantifies all available benefits, and crucially, whether the process overstates benefits in order to get approval. A further key issue from the case study research work was the use of pilot studies as a means of understanding the available benefits, and how they may be realised.

## Planning benefits realisation

Key issues are how the roles and responsibilities are allocated for realising business benefits and planning business changes. In particular, the role of a business project manager was explored. Questions were asked as to whether the organization prepares benefits delivery plans, and the extent to which process and organizational changes are addressed.

## Executing the benefits realisation plan

Key issues are concerned with actions and responsibilities for ensuring that the benefits are actually delivered during an IS/IT implementation.

## Evaluating and reviewing results

Key issues are how the organization currently concludes whether they are successful in realising the proposed benefits. The types of success criteria were sought, and also the time at which any success measures are developed, i.e. before, during, or after implementation. Questions were asked about whether formal post-implementation reviews are held, and the objectives of these reviews were sought in terms of technical conformance, project management effectiveness, benefits delivery, or other objectives.

## Potential for further benefits

Key issues are whether the respondent believed that it is possible to identify all benefits at the project approval stage, and whether their organization has any formal process to identify and realise any further benefits after implementation. Also, whether there is any mechanism for transferring ‘lessons learned’ from successful (or unsuccessful) implementations to future projects.

## Survey results

## Results overview

The survey shows that around 50% of the organizations in the survey have methodologies for IS/IT systems development, project management and investment appraisal, though only 20% have all three. Only a minority of organizations consider these methodologies to be effective in ensuring successful information systems. Very few organizations have a methodological approach to benefits management, and the majority of respondents agree that there is much room for improvement in their current benefits management practices.

Though most respondents express confidence that IS/IT is delivering benefits to their organizations, further analysis of their responses reveals little grounds for this confidence. Though post-project reviews are common, they do not usually specifically address benefits delivery (or non-delivery). Success criteria in terms of benefits delivered are the exception rather than the rule, and in many cases measures of project success are defined after project implementation or not at all. In terms of realising benefits, few organizations allocate specific responsibility to managers for realising the benefits claimed in a project justification, and very few produce a benefits delivery plan. Finally, many respondents openly admitted that their current project justification processes overstate the benefits in order to get approval, hence benefits are claimed which are unlikely to be achieved in practice.

## Background information, general questions

Sixty replies were received (almost a quarter of the 250 that were sent questionnaires). Most of the respondents (Figure 2) were either IS/IT specialists (24), or a combination of IS/IT specialist and business manager (31). Nearly three-quarters (44) indicated an IS/IT background.

A wide range of industry sectors was represented by those that responded, including construction, financial services, food and drink, light and heavy manufacturing, local government, marketing and selling, pharmaceuticals, research, retailing, service provision (including IS/IT), telecommunications, transport and shipping. In terms of overall investment in IT over the previous year, the 60 survey responses represented an estimated total of at least £300m. Broadly similar figures were projected for the next year's investment in IT.

In terms of the top three issues which the survey respondents currently face, benefits management ranked as one of the major issues, but not as highly as alignment of IS/IT with business strategy and objectives (Figure 3). Strategy was the single most important issue in the majority of cases, thus supporting a continued need for SISP. However, as noted earlier, while SISP will provide the overall investment priorities, it will not in itself guarantee the delivery of benefits. Cost and budgets were also seen as a major issue, reflecting the continued drive for value for money from IT. Against this continued pressure to reduce IT costs, perhaps it is time to address seriously the benefits side of the value for money equation.

## Current perceived IS/IT benefits and investment drivers

The current benefits which are perceived to be provided by IS/IT were heavily biased towards cost savings (Figure 4). Improved management information was perceived to be a further major benefit, with improved process efficiency, and the ability to support business change and growth, also ranking highly. Notably, several respondents perceived little current benefit from IS/IT.

By contrast, the underlying issues which drive the organizations' investment in IS/IT showed a different pattern (Figure 4). Cost reduction and profit improvement was still the most important driver, followed by improved process efficiency. However, major drivers which were not perceived as current benefits were quality and customer service, and business necessity. Management information is seen as being a major current benefit, but not a major driver.

Confidence in IS/IT benefits delivery

Most respondents showed a high level of confidence that IS/IT was actually delivering benefits within their organization. While this initially sounded promising, responses to the more detailed questions discussed later revealed little grounds for such confidence. Key issues included a lack of post-implementation measurement of benefits, and confusion regarding success criteria and delivery of benefits. In many cases the success criteria of project delivery ‘on time, to budget, user satisfaction’ was quoted, rather than measured benefits as a result of changes within the business.

## Use of methodologies

Respondents were asked about their current use of methodologies in the areas of system development, project management and investment appraisal. Their use was found to be commonplace but by no means universal. Use of the three methodologies was split evenly, with 52% (31), 52% (31) and 60% (36) proponents respectively stating that they have such a methodology. Nine indicated that they did not have any of these methodologies, while only 13 had all three (see Figure 6).

Of those that had methodologies, respondents were satisfied with their systems development and project management methodologies in 45% (14/31) and 52% (16/31) of cases, respectively. However, only 36% (13/36) of those who had an investment appraisal process expressed satisfaction with it. It was also found that the more a system development methodology was used, the more likely that it was perceived to be effective. This was also seen, to a lesser extent, for project management methodologies.

The respondents were also asked whether they had a benefits management methodology. Out of the seven that said yes, all seven also practised an investment appraisal process, while use of systems and project methodologies was mixed (see Figure 7).

## Identifying and structuring benefits

## Project appraisal techniques

Cost/benefit analysis and return on investment (ROI) were the most commonly mentioned appraisal techniques for deciding upon IS/IT investments (Figure 8). Less than 50% of respondents mentioned formally recognised techniques, such as payback, ROI, internal rate of return, net present value, or discounted cash flow. In terms of appropriateness, the appraisal techniques were generally rated as midway between very appropriate and inappropriate. The majority, 82% (49), rated their methods as less than very appropriate.

Common problems with the methods were that they were unable to take account of the full range of potential benefits, especially intangible benefits. Some respondents went as far as stating that the ‘wrong’ projects were approved as a result. Projects often have benefits that are not tangible in nature (e.g. morale, goodwill). However, over one-quarter (16) of the sample did not consider intangible benefits in their IS/IT project appraisal process. Of the rest that did consider intangible benefits, only 25% (15) 'often or always' took steps to review these benefits at a later stage. These results on project appraisal techniques confirm the findings of previous researchers in this area, such as Willcocks and Lester (1991), Farbey et al. (1992) and Balantine et al. (1994).

## Effectiveness of techniques

Nearly three-quarters (42/58) of respondents believed that their current project justification process fails to identify all available benefits; 70% (40/57) believed that their current process fails to quantify adequately the relevant benefits (Figure 9). Perhaps the most significant finding of the survey was that in 47% (27/57) of cases the respondents openly admit that their current process actually overstated the benefits in order to get approval. This implies that not only are benefits claimed that are unlikely to be realised in practice, but also that the process itself places greater emphasis on getting project approval than on delivering any proposed benefits.

Of the 27 that felt benefits were overstated, 89% (24/27) conducted post-implementation reviews (PIRs), and 56% (15/27) often or always targeted benefits delivery as part of the PIR process. In contrast, of those that did not feel benefits were overstated to get approval, only 60% (18/30) conducted PIRs, and of these 43% (13/30) often or always targeted benefits delivery as part of the PIR process.

While initially this may seem a slightly anomalous finding, in that those who ‘overstate’ are more likely to review the outcome, the explanation may be simple. Possibly those who carry out PIRs systematically to review benefits can, from experience, determine that benefits are generally overstated during the approval process. Perhaps the others just do not know, or do not want to know. Whatever the reasons for overstating benefits, from a business user perspective the practice is likely to lead ultimately to a lack of confidence in the ability of IT to deliver what is promised.

## Pilot studies as a means of determining benefits

Some 87% (52/60) of respondents conducted pilot studies when implementing IS/IT. Of these, 48% (25/52) stated that one of the objectives of these studies was the evaluation of technology. Having an objective of understanding the benefits available was slightly less popular (38%; 20/52), as was demonstrating how benefits might be realised (40%; 21/52). An overall implication is that the purpose in carrying out ‘pilots’ is not always clear, and in the majority of cases the primary purpose does not appear to be to obtain a better understanding of potential benefits or how to realise them.

Planning benefits realisation and delivering the benefits In over 80% (50) of cases the organization appoints a business project manager. However, the responses indicate that the role is most often concerned with managing the interface between the IS/IT group and the business, rather that actively managing a business project in order to deliver actual business benefits (see Figure 10). Over two-thirds of the survey respondents indicated that specific responsibilities for realising business benefits (identified in the project justification) were not allocated to managers. Similarly, over two-thirds of respondents did not produce a plan for delivering benefits.

The allocation of specific responsibility to individual managers for realising business benefits (claimed in the project justification) occurred in just 32% of cases. However, responses to further questions identified that line/department managers and users were primarily responsible for ensuring that the benefits are delivered. Thus while there appears to be a clear understanding of who is responsible, in most cases there is no specific responsibility for taking the necessary action. Only 27% (16) of respondents claimed that their organization prepares a benefits delivery plan. Without such a plan, it is difficult to envisage how an organization might effectively realise business benefits.

The realisation of business benefits usually requires changes to business processes or practices in order to achieve maximum effect. Such changes associated with an IT project were stated as (often or always) being planned after system implementation or not at all in 16% (9/57; process changes) and 28% (15/54; organizational changes) of cases (Figures 11 and 12). Given that the central concept of this paper is that benefits are derived through business changes, one can conclude that in these cases benefits are unlikely to be realised in practice. Some 24% (14/58) indicated that they often or always plan for process changes during implementation, making them difficult to realise in practice.

## Evaluating and reviewing results

In very few cases was there an explicit statement that an IS/IT project is considered to be successful if the proposed benefits are delivered. In many cases the replies were the traditional project management success criteria of ‘working, on time, to budget’. Many of the replies indicated some subjective assessment of user satisfaction as success criteria. However, neither delivered functionality nor user satisfaction is any guarantee of benefits delivery.

## Post-implementation reviews

Some 72% (43) of respondents indicated that formal post-implementation reviews are conducted. Of these, technical conformance often or always featured in 55% (22/40) of the cases, and project management effectiveness in 66% (27/41). In 76% (31/41) of responses, benefits delivery is (often or always) an objective of these reviews, which might be reassuring if there were stronger evidence that methods and techniques were being used to make this objective realisable. Thus, taking the sample as a whole, only 52% of respondents (often or always) assessed benefits delivery as part of their post-implementation review process. In terms of those who always assessed benefits delivery, this figure fell to just 26%. The implication of these findings is that the objectives of post-implementation reviews are by no means clear, and that the objective in many cases is not the review of actual benefits delivery. A possible explanation is provided in the literature by Kumar (1990), who found that in the majority of cases the primary objective of a post-implementation review was just to close out formally the IT development project.

## Potential for further benefits

The majority of respondents, 86% (51/59), believed that it is not possible to anticipate all potential benefits at the project approval stage. However, only 19% (11/59) of respondents claimed to have a process for taking advantage of this fact in order to identify further benefits after implementation, and take action to realise these benefits. The implication is that there are often more benefits to be gained after implementation, but that current practices mitigate against exploring these potential further benefits.

In terms of the generic issue of transferring lessons learned from successful (or unsuccessful) implementations to future projects, only 29% (16/56) of respondents claimed to have a formal process. How can those that do not, ever improve their implementation processes? This view is supported by Kumar (1990) who concludes that current practices ‘may not provide the more important long term feedback improvement benefits of the evaluation process’.

## Scope for improvement

In their responses to the final question of the questionnaire, most respondents strongly indicated that they thought there was significant scope for improvement in their current approach to managing IS/IT benefits (Figure 14).

There appears to be a potential paradox between the current confidence that IS/IT is delivering benefits to the organization and the view that there is significant scope for improvement in how benefits are being realised. This may be explained by the nature of the benefits that respondents perceived were actually delivered, and a view that much greater potential exists to deliver other types of benefits; or that only a proportion of the benefits that were readily realisable from current investments were actually delivered and that more could be delivered with a more effective process. Since this was the final question of the questionnaire, it might also be that by completing the questionnaire the respondents' views may have been changed.

## Conclusions

The survey was carried out as an integral part of a research programme devised to improve approaches to the realisation of business benefits from IS/IT investments. It elicited 60 responses, mainly from IT managers in large private sector organizations, to an extensive list of detailed questions about processes used to identify, plan for and realise benefits. The results may therefore have limitations in terms of drawing general conclusions. However, based on the information gathered the following conclusions can be put forward:

(1) Very few organizations have a complete or comprehensive management process to ensure the proposed benefits from IS/IT investments are actually realised. However, the majority realise that they could significantly improve their current approach to managing IS/IT benefits.

(2) Only just over 50% of organizations have formal methodologies for:

\- systems development and/or

\- project management and/or

\- investment appraisal.

With respect to investment appraisal, these findings are in accordance with other surveys quoted earlier. However, only 20% have implemented all three of these. This is surprising, given that these methodologies are now well-established approaches. In many cases these methodologies are not applied consistently and, for each type of methodology, only a minority of organizations believe that they are effective in ensuring successful information systems.

(3) The results of this survey confirm the findings of previous researchers that existing methods, including investment appraisal, are deemed generally unsatisfactory in identifying all available benefits or adequately quantifying the benefits. This survey indicates that they can even lead to benefits being overstated in order to gain investment approval. Most respondents also believe that it is impossible to identify all potential benefits before approval is given. This suggests that there is a need for further work to produce more appropriate appraisal methods and also to reappraise projects during development to ensure that the right benefits are derived. Given the volume of literature on the subject of IT investment justification, it is surprising that the methods are still deemed unsatisfactory in practice.

(4) While most organizations appoint business project managers for IT projects, in only a minority of instances is specific responsibility for benefits realisation allocated. This poses questions about the role of such project managers, since it would seem that a key reason for such a role is to achieve what an IT project manager cannot, i.e. managing the necessary business changes in order to deliver the business benefits.

(5) The need for post-implementation reviews is well understood, but the focus is on assessing technical and time/cost conformance for the IT development, rather than on evaluating success or failure in achieving the desired results. However, it is interesting to note that those who conduct benefit PIRs are more likely to conclude that benefits are overstated in the approval process. Very few organizations actively seek further benefits after implementation, or transfer experience of success or failure in benefits delivery to future projects, i.e. they actually learn very little from post-implementation evaluation.

(6) Although improved investment appraisal techniques are likely to improve the decision-making process for IS/IT investments, on their own they are unlikely to ensure that the benefits claimed for those investments are realised. Post-implementation evaluations, if focused on benefits delivery issues, could enable better judgement of the potential benefits which are actually achievable within an organization, and the feasibility of achieving them. But both to increase the realisable benefits which are used to justify investments and to actually deliver those benefits requires a more comprehensive process. This process runs throughout the system lifecycle and complements existing systems, project and appraisal techniques. Perhaps in the longer term, a benefits management process should be the core process around which the other methodologies have to fit successfully. The research programme has already identified a few pioneering organizations which are successfully using such a benefits management orientated approach to IS/IT investment, and further organizations who intend to do so.

As part of the next stage of the research programme, further surveys are underway in the public sector to complement existing work within the private sector. The data collected from the surveys is being further analysed to identify possible cause and effect relationships in more detail, based on the benefits management process that has been developed. In parallel a number of case studies have been carried out to examine the issues affecting success or failure in realising the benefits of IS/IT investments.

However, this is merely one research programme addressing a complex and difficult subject. There is considerable scope for further research and new thinking in this area which would clearly be of great benefit to the majority of organizations who are struggling to realise the potential benefits of their IS/IT investments.
