# Evaluating maintenance management information systems

I Hipkin

School of Management Studies, University of Cape Town, Rondebosch 7700, South Africa

The evaluation of maintenance management information systems (MMIS) in five case organizations is investigated by considering a taxonomy taken from the literature. The objectives in implementing a MMIS are assessed against factors in the taxonomy which classifies the systems in eight possible ways. These factors provide an indication of the appropriate evaluation for the IS. The study finds broad congruence between the perspectives of managers in the case organizations and the taxonomy, but emphasises that other key factors should be considered to ensure successful implementation and evaluation.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) have impacted on all management disciplines, profoundly affecting the ways in which organizations function. The reasons for the implementation of IS and their effectiveness are the subject of wide debate, and research on their impact on managerial capability has proved inconclusive. Changing expectations in organizations have demanded action, and IS have frequently formed part of the solution. One area where significant changes are sought is the maintenance of plant and equipment. The reasons for this are numerous: stringent safety and environmental regulations, the unremitting insistence on operating cost reduction, increased automation and mechanisation, a changing understanding of the functionality of equipment and the nature of its failure. In response to these demands, a variety of maintenance interventions has been adopted, such as reliability-centred maintenance, total productive maintenance, hazard and operability studies, and condition monitoring. These and other initiatives are usefully accompanied by the implementation of maintenance management information systems (MMIS).

The reasons for embarking on an MMIS and the expectations from such systems are varied. The general research problem considered in this paper concerns the motivation for implementing MMIS and the evaluation process thereof. This is done by using the ‘benefits evaluation ladder’ presented by Farbey et al, (1995) to analyse the experiences of five case organizations in introducing an MMIS as a new technology (the term technology is used in the sense described by Zhao and Reisman (1992) as ‘differentiated knowledge about a specific application’). Using ‘analytical generalisation’ (Yin, 1989), technical and environmental factors (McCutcheon & Meredith, 1987) appropriate to the analysis of MMIS are developed and compared with those in the taxonomy of Farbey et al, (1995).

Following a brief description of some managerial issues relating to MMIS, the benefits' evaluation ladder is summarised. This is followed by an outline of the five case organizations. The paper explores the reasons for choosing to introduce MMIS in the case organizations, and evaluates what the MMIS achieved. This was done using a questionnaire (shown in Appendix 1) where production and maintenance managers were asked to score on a Likert-type scale the extent to which each level in the taxonomy reflected their motivation for introducing an MMIS. A discussion follows of the relationship between the experiences of the organizations and the taxonomy presented by Farbey et al, (1995). Congruence with these taxonomy levels is discussed and implications for managers are considered.

## Maintenance management information systems (MMIS)

This section considers the role and importance of maintenance management related information, the purpose of which is to improve the effectiveness of the maintenance function. Earlier ‘computerised’ maintenance systems were for planning and scheduling, and were intended to support preventive maintenance programmes and shutdown planning. The output of such systems was essentially the generation of maintenance schedules at appropriate frequencies. Records of work undertaken, items repaired or replaced, and other relevant information permitted extraction of data, but such data were not readily useful as information which could be applied in refining maintenance intervention.

The requirements of an MMIS have gone beyond the more traditional modules outlined by Pintelon and van Wassenhove (1990): 'bills of materials, spare parts inventory control, personnel management and work order management.' The increasingly widespread use of maintenance philosophies such as reliability-centred maintenance, with their rigorous data requirements, demands greater availability and processing of accurate information. Safety and environmental regulatory bodies insist on the collection and analysis of an increasing quantity of data. An IS must now provide all facilities previously encompassed in a computerised maintenance system, as well as downloading to a variety of applications, such as costing, financial and inventory systems, and spreadsheets. Extracting data for performing mathematical calculations is now a prerequisite. This paper does not discuss technical details of the IS installed in the case organizations, but concentrates on the managerial considerations behind the implementation and the evaluation of the IS.

The literature suggests that there is an optimum point for maintenance intervention. In practice this is difficult to determine. A survey of the quantitative literature from 1976 to 1990 (Cho & Parlar, 1991) presents an overview of optimal maintenance and replacement models. These quantitative studies, covering theoretical and mathematical aspects of maintenance, are seldom of much use to practitioners, as they are ‘frequently chosen to correspond with current modelling practice or are selected for computational convenience’ (Flynn et al., 1990: p 251). A second source of information on optimal maintenance policies is the equipment vendor whose guarantees of performance and equipment life depend on specified maintenance being carried out at set intervals. But the technical data given to customers relating to reliability, service life and life-cycle costs are not extensive (Bennett & Jenney, 1980). The third possibility is to use an MMIS and utilise the data which will be captured therein.

If maintenance intervention and systems are to contribute to organizational improvement, a high level of production competence (Cleveland et al, 1989) and knowledge of the plant and equipment are essential so that relevant maintenance requirements can be determined (Jaikumar, 1986; Ferdows & De Meyer, 1990; Moubray, 1991). The discipline and rigour imposed by an IS plays a key part in acquiring and applying this knowledge.

Several writers contend that the most important benefits to be derived from new technology are technical and that improved performance is the main justification (Bennett & Jenney, 1980; Banerjee & Flynn, 1987; Meredith, 1987). The benefits derived from an IS will depend on the degree to which data are transformed into usable and accessible information (Bohn, 1994). With an MMIS, a necessary condition for technical benefits is the continuous entry of appropriate data, analysis and use thereof. Benefits may take the form of greater asset availability, a reduction in labour costs, improved reliability and increased life of equipment (Meredith, 1987). Figures for availability and reliability are seldom available with any great degree of accuracy. This is partly because of a lack of machine performance data (Turban, 1967; Christer, 1984; Currie & Sneddon, 1992), and also because the complexity of modern systems frequently renders rigorous calculation difficult, if not impossible (Banerjee & Flynn, 1987).

## The benefits' evaluation ladder

Farbey et al (1995) have developed a benefits' evaluation ladder for IS applications which provides a 'framework for classifying applications in the form of an eight-rung ladder' and which maintains that different applications should be managed and their impact evaluated in different ways. The ladder is shown in Table 1.

This section gives a brief description of the levels of the ladder and describes how MMIS can be classified onto a number of the rungs. The benefits accruing from the MMIS will vary according to the type of system implemented, and the method of evaluation will depend on the rung on which a system is classified. Evaluation of the MMIS in the case organizations is considered later in the paper, and compared to the evaluation techniques suggested by Farbey et al (1995).

## 1. Mandatory changes

These refer to changes which must be undertaken in order to survive. In a maintenance context, excessive machine downtime, high maintenance costs, increased competition and poor adherence to performance and quality standards may render change imperative for organizational survival. Policies to reduce stocks of raw materials and finished goods, and increased pressure to improve service by delivering on time, have placed a greater burden on the maintenance function in that unscheduled machine downtime can no longer be tolerated (Moubray, 1991).

Mandatory changes may also result from competitive and regulatory pressures outside the organization. Safety considerations and environmental concerns frequently require mandatory changes in maintenance practice. One way of partially addressing these demands is the introduction of an MMIS, particularly where regulations dictate that records of all functional testing and plant conditions are retained and analysed (Nakajima, 1989). In such cases, organizations may implement an MMIS despite not being able to justify it on financial grounds.

Evaluation methods at this level typically use cost accounting and work measurement techniques to consider the costs of alternative options (Farbey et al, 1995). In the maintenance situation, evaluation at this level is essentially twofold. The first is a comparison between different systems, with a financial assessment of the extent to which each can convincingly contribute to the survival of the organization. The second aspect relates to safety or environmental issues. Regulatory authorities do not accept a financial trade-off between the cost of preventing failures and the consequences of the failures themselves, so an MMIS will be evaluated on the extent to which it assists in providing the facilities necessary for appropriate risk assessment.

## 2. Automation

This rung of the benefits' evaluation ladder is appropriate for technologies which replace or improve existing systems, typically by processing large volumes of repetitive work. The desired effects are enhanced competitive advantage through a reduction in costs and/or an increase in productivity. In maintenance applications this may be interpreted as improving the effectiveness of maintenance and achieving greater functionality of existing installations through the provision of more and better information on equipment performance, the issuing and monitoring of schedules (of which there can be tens of thousands per month in large facilities) and the easy retrieval of data. Maintenance managers and planners consider automation of this function essential (Nakajima, 1989; Monplaisir & Arumugadasan, 1994).

Farbey et al (1995) suggest evaluation at this level by traditional work measurement and operations research techniques. The effectiveness of an MMIS can be assessed by the ability to plan and schedule maintenance work, tasks which are labour intensive and tedious, if not impossible, if done manually. The existence of reliable plant history and data manipulation techniques is a further indication of the usefulness of an MMIS.

## 3. Direct value added systems

Information technology (IT) at this level adds value, increases effectiveness and improves the manner in which businesses are run, frequently by introducing activities which were previously not done. The maintenance function adds value directly by ensuring greater machine availability; indirectly, maintenance adds value by improving product quality and service. Proponents of an MMIS believe it can enhance this value adding process.

Estimating the precise value added impact of new technology is often difficult, since measurement techniques, attributes and outcomes are seldom specified and systems must be created for the purposes of measurement (Willcocks & Margetts, 1994). There are frequently unexpected yet favourable outcomes from new maintenance technology implementation (Safayeni & Purdy, 1991) such as the benefits accruing from knowledge of the functionality of plant and process together with an understanding of the nature of equipment failure, and its design capability in relation to desired performance (Moubray, 1991). The discipline which an MMIS imposes in terms of data gathering and entry enhances the assiduity of maintenance intervention (Watson, 1991).

At this level evaluation takes the form of assessing the value added by the implementation of a system. Measurable outcomes such as greater output, improved quality and lower maintenance costs attributable to an MMIS would point to its effectiveness.

## 4. MIS and decision support system (DSS)

The essential feature of this rung is the provision of information to improve managerial decision making, although Farbey et al (1995) acknowledge that there is little hard evidence that managerial capability has been enhanced by the use of IT. Value will only be added to decision making if managers are able to avail themselves of the necessary facilities. If the information that can be generated through a comprehensive database is available, then technical staff are in a position to take decisions based on substantive data (Bohn, 1994; Nakajima, 1989).

In the maintenance context decisions may have a profound effect on safety and environmental integrity, and go beyond the quantitative value added dimension of the previous rung. The information derived from an MMIS can have a beneficial effect on levels of plant and process knowledge. An MMIS can provide previously unknown information concerning availability and reliability of plant and equipment, factors which are essential for long term planning and decision making (Vanneste & van Wassenhove, 1994). Fundamental changes in design and operating philosophies may also be made on the basis of new or better data obtained from an MMIS.

Evaluation at this level is not easily quantifiable, and may best be assessed by the quality and nature of decisions that may be made as a result of improved information.

## 5. Infrastructure

This level provides a general capability and the foundation for increased added value, although direct benefits themselves may not be evident (Farbey et al, 1995). A new technology may highlight areas of incomplete understanding, and assist in providing greater insight into the potential changes that may emanate therefrom (Crawford et al, 1988; Currie & Sneddon, 1992;

Geisler & Rubenstein, 1987). An important feature of this level is the extent to which other systems can be built on the foundations laid by the initial system. In the maintenance context these further developments may be in a narrower sense maintenance management systems, such as reliability-centred maintenance and total productive maintenance, or they may extend to other functions such as inventory and spares management, costing and purchasing.

An MMIS may provide the stimulus for infrastructural changes which are in fact prerequisites for the proper implementation of a maintenance management system. Successful implementation of new technologies generates further management commitment such as integration of activities, training (Klein, 1984; McLoughlin et al, 1985), action to reduce resistance to change (Leonard-Barton, 1987; Crawford et al, 1988), and setting up appropriate measuring systems (Meredith, 1987). While new systems may create an infrastructure, management commitment is in turn crucial to the success of that new technology (Huber, 1984; Meredith, 1986, Crawford et al, 1988; Hitomoto, 1988; Doll & Vonderembse, 1991; Currie & Sneddon, 1992; Harris & Yit, 1994).

Evaluation at this level refers to the ability to demonstrate a link between the investment and subsequent initiatives. In the case of an MMIS, this may take the form of creating a maintenance management system or an all-encompassing production system of which the MMIS is an integral part. Such systems would require formal reporting, and the co-ordination of maintenance and associated activities, where these were previously informal and unstructured. An MMIS would have even further infrastructural benefits if other systems were developed directly as a result of its implementation.

## 6. Inter-organizational systems

This rung refers to the sharing of systems across organizational boundaries (typically between trading partners), possibly resulting in some loss of flexibility and independence by each party. These would provide benefits through partnerships, profoundly affecting business relationships (Holland, 1995). Conceptually, there may be some scope for this facility in maintenance management in the form of data banks or shared technical databases, although this would not be between partners and is frequently between competitors. Vanneste and van Wassenhove (1995) see this as a type of benchmarking. Commercially available data banks provide technical information such as failure data, the useful life (where applicable) of items and other features for a range of machines and components. The extensive use of such data is limited as process and equipment operating in different contexts can have profoundly different performance and failure characteristics (Nowlan & Heap, 1978; Smith, 1993; Moubray, 1991). In practice, there is little scope for an MMIS at this level and the literature makes no reference to maintenance at the inter-organizational systems level.

Farbey et al (1995) suggest that evaluation at this level should involve all collaborators considering the balance between loss of freedom and flexibility against 'the potential benefits from shared systems and the value of partnerships underpinned by the systems'. This approach to evaluation is not applicable to an MMIS.

## 7. Strategic systems

In this rung senior management sees IT as a strategic resource, although Farbey et al (1995) contend there is scant confirmation of exceptional performance in those companies which claim the strategic use of IT. The literature makes no mention of the maintenance function or an MMIS as a strategic resource: both contribute to manufacturing effectiveness at an operational level.

Evaluation of this level is difficult, but should be 'based on a broad appreciation of the business situation, and in particular a focus on the competitive situation' (Farbey et al, 1995). An MMIS could, at best, possibly show an operational benefit.

## 8. Business transformation

IT at this level seeks to transform an organization. There is little evidence that maintenance management has achieved this, or indeed could contribute to the transformation of a business. While developments in production and operations may transform a business, maintenance is a service function which can only assist in supporting a strategy led by the production and operations functions.

This section has described the rungs of the benefits' evaluation ladder and indicated those which are potentially relevant in analysing the implementation and evaluation of an MMIS. Details of the organizations whose MMIS implementations will be analysed in terms of the ladder are given in the following section.

## Case outlines and methodology

The experiences of five organizations in the UK have been used in this study. Brief details of each and their objectives in implementing an MMIS are summarised in Table 2. The companies have all introduced an MMIS within the last 12–18 months. Maintenance and production managers were interviewed on the basis of the questionnaire summarised in Appendix 1 and asked to state to what extent each level of the ladder was significant in motivating the introduction of an MMIS (Part 1 of the questionnaire). They were also questioned about the evaluation of the MMIS (Part 2 of the questionnaire). Further valuable insight into the decision making and evaluation processes was gained by discussing points beyond those detailed in the questionnaire.

The objectives summarised in Table 2 are increased output and improved plant performance, fewer breakdowns and shorter periods of downtime, higher levels of product quality, and enhanced communication and coordination between functions. ## Relationship between MMIS and taxonomy

In order to relate the MMIS to the taxonomy, maintenance and production managers were asked to state their motivations for implementing an MMIS. For each level in the taxonomy, managers scored (on a 5-point scale, as detailed in Appendix 1) the extent to which each level corresponded to their reasons for introducing their MMIS. The scores and overall averages are shown in Table 3.

The scores in Table 3 show that an MMIS may best be classified in terms of rungs 3 (direct value added systems) and 4 (management information systems and decision support systems) of the benefits' ladder, with some attributes of rungs 1 (mandatory changes, from the perspective of maintenance managers), 2 (automation, more strongly advocated by maintenance managers), and 5 (infrastructure, greater support from production managers). Table 4 summarises the managers' perceptions of how an MMIS relates to each level.

The following paragraphs illustrate some of the contrasting opinions of maintenance and production managers. At the mandatory level, some maintenance managers considered an MMIS necessary for survival, whereas production managers saw this as a rather dramatic call for special treatment, particularly as some felt than an MMIS could not be justified financially. Company A's maintenance manager was adamant that the manufacturing excellence programme made an MMIS essential as this was the only way in which the maintenance function could guarantee the performance of machines demanded by the production schedule. Company A's production manager considered the poor service received from maintenance as an organizational issue which an MMIS would not solve. This difference of opinion remained intractable. Production managers generally did not concur with the maintenance managers' line of thinking regarding the first level, except for Company E where the production manager agreed that regulatory requirements for critical plant called for a powerful MMIS.

At the automation level, maintenance managers viewed the replacement of their existing systems as essential, whereas production managers rated this of lesser importance. The maintenance manager of company B claimed that it was extremely difficult to manage his plant, consisting of many thousands of maintainable items, with the existing maintenance system, particularly as this could not accommodate the required number of items. The production manager viewed the ‘maintainable units’ as being determined at too low a level, thereby unnecessarily complicating the maintenance system. He did however, see an MMIS as a way of reducing overall costs by eliminating unnecessary maintenance.

Maintenance and production managers in the case organizations agreed that a new system must add value, and provide information for subsequent decision making, resulting in measurably improved effectiveness. Maintenance managers saw better information as a key benefit from an MMIS, which could enable them to improve designs and maintenance activities. Production managers recognised the potential for enhanced operating methods. All managers gave examples of how they felt an MMIS could lead to improved decision making. Two typical examples are given for illustration. In company D identical production lines had markedly different production outputs. The production manager believed that detailed analysis of breakdown and condition monitoring data could give a clue to this discrepancy; this was subsequently found to be the case. The maintenance manager in company C believed that analysis of performance figures and costs gathered through the MMIS could assist him in justifying the modification of the method of operating duty and standby equipment, while maintaining his utility's statutory performance requirements.

The belief that an MMIS should provide a general capability (infrastructure) was supported particularly by production managers who saw benefits of an MMIS extending beyond the narrow view that an MMIS is no more than a sophisticated record keeping system. The production manager of D believed that benefits would result from integrating the MMIS with the production planning and control system (infrastructural enhancement). The operations manager of C saw the MMIS as part of an overall management system over which she had control. For B and D which were implementing the reliability-centred maintenance (RCM) system, the MMIS and RCM complemented each other. With the maintenance function generally less influential than its production counterpart, maintenance managers did not see themselves as key contributors to overall infrastructural improvement, although an MMIS clearly provided possibilities for interfacing between maintenance, production and materials management.

The inter-organizational systems level was not mentioned as a possible application of an MMIS: no inter-organizational communication of the type described by Farbey et al (1995) could be foreseen. Opinions regarding the strategic systems level generally reflected the belief that neither the maintenance function nor an MMIS could directly contribute to strategy formulation and achievement. An MMIS was not expected to transform the organizations studied.

## Evaluation of applications

This section compares the evaluation methods proposed in the taxonomy and the procedures adopted in the case organizations in assessing the benefits of MMIS implementation. A limitation of this study is that the investigation of the outcome of the MMIS implementation was generally only 12–18 months after installation: the functionality of some equipment is such that changes in maintenance may only become evident after years or even decades and so it may well be that the benefits of some changes in maintenance practice had not yet manifested themselves. Nevertheless, the analysis has yielded some interesting findings. Managers were asked to evaluate the MMIS under the taxonomy factors headings. Their responses are summarised in Table 5.

In the absence of quantifiable performance and machine availability figures, and with few cost and productivity indicators, it proved more difficult to evaluate the lower levels in the taxonomy where more certainty and quantification are required. The less quantifiable the benefit, the more the objectives were perceived to have been achieved.

1. For mandatory changes Farbey et al (1995) suggest that cost accounting and work measurement techniques are appropriate for the evaluation. Mandatory changes imply that without an MMIS, organizational performance would be critically impaired. No evidence of the use of these techniques was found. Case organizations had in some instances evaluated various systems prior to introduction (that is, compared what different systems offered, whether they met the company's requirements, and the cost of such systems), but no evaluations of the results of implementation were encountered. From discussions with managers it could not be established that company survival would be seriously jeopardised without an MMIS. This is not surprising since no MMIS was exclusively installed for reasons of organizational survival, despite some maintenance managers' claims that an MMIS was essential. Those organizations which had claimed that statutory regulations required an MMIS felt that these had been satisfied.

2. At the automation level Farbey et al (1995) suggest evaluation by work measurement, and organization and method studies. Where an MMIS is installed to replace an existing systems with the intention of facilitating data processing in order to reduce costs and improve productivity, the facilities must be in place to measure these. This factor was not judged a prime motivator for MMIS and the formal work measurement techniques proposed by Farbey et al (1995) were not deemed necessary by managers in the case organizations, none of whom had installed appropriate measurement procedures. However, the fact that the organizations were able to complete their planning, issue all maintenance schedules at the required time (and this had not been possible in the past), and build up plant history records, meant that the MMIS was achieving at least some of the goals which had been set. Certain maintenance managers felt that this was sufficient justification for having introduced the MMIS.

3. Evaluation techniques suggested by Farbey et al (1995) for direct value added systems include 'experimental techniques' such as gauging responses to the availability of a system. Adding value was a key objective, but no organization could demonstrate any specific steps that had been taken to measure added value. All maintenance managers claimed that the MMIS had enhanced the maintenance function, but more important were the opinions of the production managers who were the ultimate beneficiaries of an improved maintenance service. They generally felt that maintenance was being planned and performed more systematically. They further believed that the discipline imposed by the MMIS ensured that plant condition was being monitored and improved, and that machine performance figures were higher than prior to implementation. These were subjective assessments.

4. Farbey et al (1995) suggest that evaluation of management information and DSS should look at potential added value or should assess the value of better information. Management information and decision support systems were an important motivating factor in implementing an MMIS. Organizations gave examples which illustrated their satisfaction with the MMIS in improving decision making, such as different ways of operating equipment (company C), and changes in design philosophy for plant modifications (company D). More comprehensive and reliable information gave technical staff a sound basis on which to formulate maintenance policies. However, there were no formal attempts to measure the value of the information which was now available.

5. The infrastructure level may be evaluated by a system's ability to support a range of future activities (Farbey et al, 1995). Although the factor was less often cited as an objective, and acknowledged as less tangible, both companies B and D found the MMIS invaluable in their implementation of their reliability-centred maintenance systems. Other companies claimed that the MMIS would be used to support future planning, control and purchasing systems.

6. Evaluation methods for the inter-organizational, strategic and business transformation levels were not relevant in the maintenance context.

We have shown that an MMIS may be introduced for a number of reasons and that these may be associated with various levels in the ladder, each of which has an appropriate evaluation method. The approaches to evaluation suggested by Farbey et al (1995) were not implemented in the case organizations as formally as the taxonomy suggests. It is interesting to note that for levels 1 and 2 managers' opinions varied as to whether their MMIS had been successful. This is not surprising as the MMIS were generally not introduced solely for mandatory and automation reasons, but as management formation and DSS. At levels 3, 4 and 5 (which were the levels at which implementations were most commonly directed), managers may not have had formal evaluation systems in place, but they were able to point to specific issues which constituted plausible benefits derived from MMIS implementation.

## Implications for managers

This study illustrates that the motivation for introducing an MMIS depends on organizational requirements as well as the opinions and needs of managers. According to the benefits' evaluation ladder, the way in which evaluation is carried out should depend on the reasons for implementing the MMIS in the first place. The benefits of the systems introduced in the case organizations were not quantified, and some disappointment was expressed by managers (particularly production managers) that this either had not been done, or that it was not possible. The taxonomy is useful in assisting managers in setting up appropriate evaluation methods. For example, for organizations who see an infrastructural component as the greatest benefit of an MMIS, quantification in terms of reduced maintenance costs, or even improved plant performance, is inappropriate: they should be looking at the extent to which other systems can be or have been built on the back of the MMIS.

An important extension to the relationship between level and evaluation is the realisation that systems may be used to greater purpose than the original classification may suggest, thus rendering inappropriate some initial approaches to evaluation. The initial motivation for introducing an MMIS may be at levels 1 or 2 (as some maintenance managers claimed), but the real benefit occurs at level 4 (the decision support level, which is where some production managers saw the true benefit). At level 4 the system unexpectedly provides management information on which broader decisions can be taken. In such instances, the evaluation process would be seeking quantifiable results at levels 1 and 2 (and if these are lacking the implementation may be deemed a failure), whereas the true benefit should be evaluated in terms of the criteria for level 4 (where, according to Farbey et al (1995), evaluation takes the form of assessing the value of ‘better’ information).

The link between objective setting (initial level assessment in the taxonomy) and evaluation is affected by a number of individual factors and interaction between them, as shown in Figure 1.

Figure 1 suggests that the level of attainment on the ladder may be influenced by external factors such as functional knowledge and management commitment. Where these have a significant impact the MMIS can produce results at a higher level. In order to establish whether an MMIS is likely to achieve, or has achieved the level of impact implied by each rung of the ladder, measurement systems must be in place. The literature emphasises that knowledge of plant and process, and management commitment contribute to achieving objectives, in this case as represented by the ladder rungs. While functional knowledge and management commitment were on occasion lacking, the main factor preventing effective evaluation of the MMIS discussed in the cases was the lack of appropriate systems for implementation and evaluation. This in turn can mean that the MMIS achieves a lower level on the ladder than would have been the case had systems been in place.

## Conclusion

This study has discussed the classification of MMIS in the context of the benefits' evaluation ladder postulated by Farbey et al (1995). The taxonomy shows that the choice of appropriate classification is a useful step in prescribing meaningful evaluation. The paper demonstrates how the levels in the taxonomy may be used to describe the objectives of maintenance and production managers in deciding to introduce an MMIS, and to determine appropriate evaluation methods. The findings indicate that MMIS are introduced mainly at the direct value added level (level 3), and MIS and DSS level (level 4), and to a lesser extent automation (level 2) and infrastructure (level 5). Evaluation at levels 3, 4 and 5 is not quantifiable and effectiveness is measurable indirectly by looking at attributes arising from MMIS implementation. Although evaluations of the MMIS were not formally executed, managers were able to point to improved operating circumstances, thereby permitting them subjectively to claim successful implementation. The paper also contends that other factors influence the effectiveness of implementation, and hence the level in the taxonomy actually achieved.

Comparison of the taxonomy attributes with the case findings shows broad congruence. Moreover, the comments in Table 5 suggest that the benefits of the MMIS implementations were not only in quantifiable and value added terms, as had been envisaged, but as MIS/DSS and infrastructure, something was not obvious to the maintenance managers at first, but required the input and vision of the production managers to make it happen. The research has illustrated an interesting application of the benefits' evaluation ladder, but further investigation is needed to appraise the extent to which the classification taxonomy can 'transcend' (McCutcheon & Meredith, 1987) the particular technology being introduced and how far can it be used in a wider classification and evaluation context.
