# Assessing determinants of desirable ES impact on end-user jobs

Y Yoon $^{1}$ , T Guimaraes $^{2}$ and AB Clevenson $^{3}$

$^{1}$ Department of Information Systems, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, VA 23284-4000; $^{2}$ Tennessee Technological University, Cookeville, TN 38505; and $^{3}$ Artificial Intelligence Task Force, E. I. DuPont de Nemours & Company, Kingwood, TX 77339, USA

To increase the likelihood of experts systems' success it is useful to identify the more important determinants to focus managerial attention and resources. Seven major determinants of expert system success, here defined as desirable impact of the system on users' jobs, have been identified from the literature. DuPont's collection of expert systems (ES) provided the sample of 114 applications used in this study. The results corroborate much of what has been proposed in the literature, except for user characteristics which have been found to have no significant relationship to the measure of success. Based on the results, some recommendations are made to expert system development managers, including: (1) besides the recommendations already proposed in the literature, managers should deliberately seek to deal with important, large, labour intensive and complex problems which are important to the organization; (2) the selection of a development shell with the characteristics described in this study, and which matches the problem at hand is an important determinant of success; and (3) even though the primary source of knowledge is one or more domain experts, user involvement in the development process remains an important factor for ES to have a desirable impact on the latter's jobs.

## Introduction

Many organizations have been investing heavily in expert systems (ES) technology due to its ability to capture invaluable organizational expertise and distribute it to various areas within the organization (Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994). ES has demonstrated its potential for enhancing the quality of decision making by reducing information overload and by augmenting the cognitive limitations and rationality bounds of its end-users (El-Najdawi & Stylianou, 1993). ES have received credit for many benefits including improved quality of product and services, improved customer services, cost saving on operations, crisis management, and stimulation of innovation. ES deployed in the field have also made significant impacts on end-users jobs: significant increases in speed of complex-task accomplishment, increased accuracy, increased quantity of works accomplished, reduced personnel requirement, and reduced training time (Report, 1994). As the implementation of new technologies in organizations has frequently caused the rearrangement of personnel tasks and responsibility (Igabaria & Guimaraes, 1993), ES has resulted in major changes in the way a company does its business. In many cases, this in turn has enabled companies to gain a competitive advantage (Feigenbaum et al, 1988; Braden et al, 1989; Sipior & Garrity, 1992).

However, ES projects have not always been successful. Some projects have failed to be operationalised, while others in operation have failed to provide expected benefits. Coleman (1993) pointed out that business and managerial issues need to be carefully addressed for ES to reach its full potential. Benders and Manders (1993) also emphasised the importance of ES organizational issues for maximising the ES advantages. Although ES technology was introduced over two decades ago, the main stream of research in ES has mainly focused on technical aspects. A few studies have been conducted to study managerial aspects of ES (Barsanti, 1990; O'Neal, 1990; Prerau, 1990; Turban, 1992b; Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994). However, previous studies in ES managerial issues have been based predominantly on the opinion and personal experience of individuals and the assertions have not been empirically tested. A few empirical studies have been conducted to identify the factors influencing the ES implementation success (Byrd, 1992, 1993; Tyran & George, 1993; Will et al, 1994; Yoon et al, 1995). Although these studies have provided a general understanding of factors, additional study is needed to synthesise personal opinion and previous findings, as well as to formulate and empirically test the factors affecting ES implementation success.

The objective of this study is to identify the factors influencing ES success in terms of the desirable impact on end-users' jobs. Users often have great fears about the ES affecting their job security, thus developing strong negative attitudes and challenging the system implementation (Lu & Guimaraes, 1988). This problem is more apparent with ES that by their very nature are more likely to significantly change the nature and requirements of the job and replace human tasks with artificial systems, ie, the effect of XCON (Sviokla, 1990). Because of the unique nature of ES described earlier, knowledge engineers have recognised ES impact on the end-users' jobs as an important factor to successful ES implementation (Byrd, 1992). Changes in a work environment frequently increase end-users' fears about their jobs and, in turn, may generate resistance against a new ES (Sloane, 1991).

The psychological impact and the organizational changes brought on by ES technology can be far more marked than those created by any other type of system. Sviokla (1990) analysed the reason for the successful implementation and use of an ES (XCON at DEC) by examining its impact on end-users' jobs. Previous studies show that the end-users suffered from stress and loss of control when artificial intelligence (AI) technologies were introduced to their working environments (Argote et al, 1983; Argote & Goodman, 1986). The fear of job loss can result in end-users resisting the ES implementation and making its success very difficult.

The independent variables in this study, the factors related to ES success reported in the literature, were categorised into seven groups: problem importance; problem difficulty; developer(s) skill; end-user(s) characteristics; shell characteristics; user involvement; and management support. Some of these success factors are not unique to ES implementations. Indeed, the literature has an abundance of studies assessing computer-based systems success factors. Unfortunately, the vast majority of the studies deal with transaction processing systems (TPS), decision support systems (DSS), executive information systems (EIS), or other computer-based information systems besides ES. The basis for an ES is the capture and use of knowledge from high-level experts to assist the less proficient ES end-users. The other systems deal with the flow of data and information through the organization unit, and their development requires considerable end-user input to define the nature, functions and features of the system. Therefore, for TPS, EIS and DSS, the end-users are the 'domain experts'. ES are also dramatically different from other types of systems in other ways. TPS perform routine data processing and focus on business procedures. DSS support decisions and focus on computational and statistical models. However, while ES also make or support decisions, they focus on knowledge/expertise rather than on transaction processing or computational techniques (Duda & Shortliffe,

1983). ES are heuristics-based and are particularly viable for problems that cannot be solved algorithmically (Writzel & Kerschberg, 1989).

Even a summary discussion of the many differences between ES and other systems would require several pages, but three references stand out and should be mentioned. Jih (1990) found differences between ES and TPS in seven areas: basic mission; knowledge structure definition; requirements for input certainty and completeness; system shareability; system human interfaces; data abstraction; and validation. Turban and Watkins (1986) discussed the differences between DSS and ES along 11 attributes: objectives; who makes the decisions; major orientations; major query direction; nature of support; data manipulation method; characteristics of problem areas; repetitiveness of problems addressed; content of databases; reasoning capability; and explanation capability. Subsequently, Turban (1990) addressed the differences among TPS, DSS, EIS and ES in terms of application types, support focus, nature of the databases, decision-making/support capabilities, numeric vs symbolic manipulation, types of information produced, highest organizational level served, and primary impetus for the system.

Given the dramatically different nature of ES, results obtained from previous studies are likely to provide at best a partial picture of the major issues surrounding ES development and implementation in practice. There are several reasons why studies dealing specifically with ES are important and must be undertaken: (1) despite widespread use and increasing importance of ES technology, little effort has been made to systematically identify and empirically test the determinants of ES success; (2) managers responsible for the development and implementation of ES cannot assume that the set of determinants for success with other system types are equally important for ES. Even if one believes that to be so, this assumption must be tested empirically; (3) many important factors are unique to ES, ie, their ‘expert mimicking’ nature, the domain-oriented problems addressed, the characteristics of ES shells, the required activities and characteristics of domain experts and knowledge engineers, and their unique relationship with end-users; (4) the reports about ES (Smith, 1988; Keyes, 1989b; Barsanti, 1990; O’Neal, 1990; Prerau, 1990; Ignizio, 1991) are mostly based on the opinion and personal experience of one individual and have not been empirically tested. Empirical studies conducted by Yoon et al (1995), Byrd (1992) and Tyran and George (1993) represent just the beginning of an effort to understand the factors surrounding ES implementation. Much more research is needed to build a theoretical foundation in this important area.

## Conceptual framework and hypotheses

Prior studies have identified various factors related to the ES success. The factors can be classified into 10 broad categories: problem importance; problem characteristics; developer(s) characteristics; domain expert(s) characteristics; end-user(s) characteristics; expert system characteristics; characteristics of ES building tools; user involvement; management support; and system usage. The ES impact on end-user(s) jobs as a surrogtae measure of ES success is discussed next. That is followed by a discussion of each of the independent variables.

## ES impact on end-user(s) jobs

Prior research has employed various measures of system success, including user satisfaction (Ives et al, 1983; Kendall et al, 1987; Galletta & Lederer, 1989; Mahmood & Sniezek, 1989), level of system usage (Fuerst and Cheney, 1982; Mykytyn, 1988), perceived benefits of systems (Money et al, 1988; David, 1989), improved decision quality and performance (Aldag & Power, 1986; Cats-Baril & Huber, 1987; Kottemann & Remus, 1989), and business profitability (Benbasat & Dexter, 1982; Eckel, 1983; Sharda et al, 1988). The choice of 'best' measure for system success depends on the study objectives. All the measures mentioned above, including user satisfaction, have advantages and disadvantages (DeLone & McLean, 1992).

ES are thought to have significant impact on end-user(s) jobs by providing high-level expertise in performing specific tasks. Such systems have often altered the nature of end-users jobs, rearranging tasks and shifting authority and responsibility for completing them, to and from individuals. Due to the possible change in end-user(s) jobs by ES, job stress and loss of control have been observed (Argote et al, 1983; Argote & Goodman, 1986). The fear of job loss can cause end-users to resist ES implementation and make the ES success extremely difficult. Although ES applications can result in negative impact on end-users jobs, their positive impact can be far more significant. ES have provided flexibility to free end-users from performing repetitive tasks and enabled them to do more creative work, increasing job satisfaction (Byrd, 1992). ES have also enabled end-users to accomplish a larger number of complicated business tasks within a shorter period of time (Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994). The increases in end-user productivity can motivate a positive end-user attitude about ES in general, as well as the specific ES at hand. Due to ES potential for profound impact on end-users jobs, such impact has been frequently used to measure ES success. For example, when analysing a successful ES, XCON at DEC (Sviokla, 1990) examined the positive impact of ES on end-user(s) jobs and their job satisfaction. Agarwal et al (1994) also assessed the impact of ES to measure its success. This study uses the degree to which an

ES is thought to have a desirable impact on end-user(s) jobs as the dependent variable and measures ES success.

## Problem importance

Prior studies have stressed the ES should address a needed and useful task so that ES solution has a high payoff (Slagle & Wick, 1988; Casey, 1989; Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994; Medsker & Liebowitz, 1994). A useful task must be non-trivial and important to an organization. In many cases they are used to solve problems that are core to the entire business organization (Barsanti, 1990). For example, the Authorizer's Assistant at American Express performs a function critical to the firm by assisting the credit approvers in approving or denying credit in a timely manner. Another well-known case is DEC's XCON which quickly configures computer system components according to customer request (Bobrow et al, 1986). These ES and other systems perform functions which are essential for their user organizations to obtain competitive advantage. As an ES effectively addresses a problem or opportunity important to a firm and ties in with its strategic objectives, it becomes important in the eyes of top management. That, in turn, may become a crucial factor in acquiring the necessary resources and political support essential for ES success. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Problem importance is positively related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

## Problem difficulty

Many studies have emphasised the importance of selecting an appropriate domain to ensure ES successful implementation (Waterman, 1986; Slage & Wick, 1988; Keyes, 1989b; Liebowitz, 1989; Barsanti, 1990; Beckman, 1991; Turban, 1992a; Medsker & Liebowitz, 1994; Will et al, 1994). Prerau (1990) stressed that the selection of an appropriate ES domain is more closely related to the ultimate success of the project than any other factor. Medsker and Liebowitz (1994) also sterssed that the selection of a proper problem before ES development is the most critical step. Other authors have also emphasised the need for special attention in the selection of ES applications and proposed specific evaluation methods for choosing appropriate applications (Lu & Guimaraes, 1989; Slagle & Wick, 1988). Yoon et al (1995) found that problem difficulty provides an opportunity for ES to help end-users with their tasks, thus increasing user satisfaction with the system. On the basis of the above discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: Problem difficulty is positively related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

## Developer(s) characteristics

The importance of skilful ES developers for successful implementation has been emphasised by several authors (Couger & McIntyre, 1987–1988; Fellers, 1987; Payne & Awad, 1990; Shacklett, 1990; Turban, 1992a; Liebowitz, 1993; Mykytyn et al, 1994; Will et al, 1994). The construction of an ES requires developers to elicit the decision rules employed by domain experts. In order to elicit the decision rules, developers must ask relevant questions and quickly comprehend the decision procedures reasoned by domain experts in problem solving. The knowledge elicitation procedure – the lengthy process of interviews – is widely known to be a bottleneck in ES development. It is obviously desirable for a developer to possess excellent communication skills in order to alleviate the difficult knowledge elicitation process. A developer with poor interpersonal skills may not be able to properly perform the critical knowledge acquisition task, causing project implementation failure (Mykytyn et al, 1994).

Additionally, prior to the knowledge elicitation process, a developer frequently conducts a self-study to obtain introductory knowledge in a chosen domain in order to communicate with domain experts. Strong knowledge of various functional areas in an organization improves developers communication with end-users as well as domain experts, and also helps save everyone's time and effort. Thus, in general, developers should have prior knowledge of a chosen domain (Medsker & Liebowitz, 1994). Once knowledge is elicited from domain experts, it is represented and stored in a knowledge base using a programming language or an ES shell. For this purpose, a developer should be familiar with various knowledge representation paradigms and ES building tools, which are important requirements for technical competence. Despite many unique characteristics, ES development has much in common with the development of information systems. Their system development life cycles contain several similar phases: user requirement analysis, design, and implementation phases; thus, general system development skills are all likely to be useful. Also, a developer should be able to sell an ES potential business benefits to management as well as to users in order to foster their support and positive attitude toward it. Last, a developer should be well aware of the social and political atmosphere in an organization when planning to introduce a new system. Based on the basis of the above discussion, the following hypothesis is formulated:

## H3: Developer(s) skill is positively related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

## End-user(s) characteristics

Prior studies have stressed the importance of end-users characteristics to ES success (Slagle & Wick, 1988; Smith, 1988; Liebowitz, 1991; Hayes-Roth, 1994). The dominant end-user characteristics affecting ES success include user attitude, user expectation and user knowledge of computer and ES technology (Smith, 1988), user confidence with their system (Will et al, 1994), and user commitment to learn how to use the system (Slagle & Wick, 1988; Liebowitz, 1991; Hayes-Roth, 1994). User attitude has been considered an important factor to ES success since end-users with a negative attitude toward ES will not utilise the system, completely wasting development costs. Users often have fears about the ES affecting their job security, thus they develop negative attitudes and challenge the system implementation (Lu & Guimaraes, 1988; Byrd, 1992). Compared with other types of systems (ie, transaction processings, and office systems), the problem of user negative attitude and resistance is more apparent with ES since the latter may significantly change the nature and requirements of a job and mimic human expert behaviour with artificial systems, ie, the effect of XCON (Sviokla, 1990). Another end-user characteristic mentioned in previous studies is the user knowledge of computer and ES techniques. Unlike ES developers who are expected to know a great deal about ES development techniques and tools, end-users do not need to have much knowledge about AI and ES. However, they should be committed to learn how to use the system and make suggestions for continuous improvement (Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994). End-users should also be trained to comprehend how to interpret the results of an ES and how to incorporate them into their job performance (Prerau, 1990). The above discussion strongly indicates end-users importance to ES success, thus we propose the following hypothesis:

## H4: User characteristics are positively related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

## Shell characteristics

The characteristics of the ES shells used to develop an ES can be an important determinant to its quality (Barsanti, 1990); therefore, employing an appropriate shell is vitally important to ES success. Other studies have also stressed the importance of these building tools to ES success (Harmon et al, 1988; Vedder, 1989; Vedder et al, 1989; Kim & Yoon, 1992; Turban, 1992a; Tyran & George, 1993; Will et al, 1994). The desirable shell features will vary significantly depending on the tasks to be performed by the ES. The ES shell should match a chosen problem so that the ES reasoning process is natural to end-users, thus encouraging ES use. For many applications, building tools must enable the ES to be easily integrated with existing database and other computer-based systems (Keyes, 1989a; Liebowitz, 1991). However, many ES are capable of only limited interface with existing systems due to the ES shell used. Similarly, a shell providing a friendly user interface enables ES developers to produce ES with user-friendly interfaces. The execution time of a building tool is also very important to ES success since it determines the response time of ES (Plant & Salinas, 1994). Since ES shell characteristics affect the quality of an ES and in turn affects ES success, the following hypothesis is formulated:

H5: Shell quality is positively related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

## User involvement

While for ES development domain experts are many times the only source of knowledge and inference about the problem overall, high levels of user involvement are considered important to its implementation success (Smith, 1988; Will et al, 1994). ES development is thought to require a higher level of user involvement during the verification and validation phases due to ES applications dealing primarily with ill-defined business problems. Turban (1990) claimed that in the verification and validation phases, user involvement becomes more important. Hayes-Roth and Jacobstein (1994) stated that one of critical institutional success factors for ES implementation is user involvement. Medsker and Liebowitz (1994) warned that insufficient user involvement is likely to result in ES failure and the importance of having a project leader with a good track record dealing with the user community. Users who initiated an ES project and were involved in establishing its goals/objectives are more likely to be satisfied with the system upon completion. Keyes (1989b) argued that if the end-users were excluded up front, they would exclude themselves at the end and not use the ES. Therefore, using the above studies as support, we propose the following hypothesis:

H6: User involvement is positively related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

## Management support

Management commitment to ES development, utilisation and maintenance has been recognised as a vital success factor for ES success by several authors (Leonard-Barton, 1987; DePree, 1988; Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988; Smith, 1988; Keyes, 1989b; Liebowitz, 1991, 1992; Turban, 1992a; Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994). Keyes (1989b) reported that lack of management support was a critical barrier to ES success, and Barsanti (1990) said that a key predictor of ES success in an organization is the existence of top management support. The surveys conducted by Byrd (1992) and Tyran & George (1993) also revealed management support as an important factor to ES success. The essence of management support for ES success has been emphasised for several reasons. First, Medsker and Liebowitz (1994) have emphasised the importance of management willingness to commit the necessary human and material resources. Second, the adoption of a new technology by an organization always results in some change in the manner in which decisions are made, business tasks are performed, and power is allocated. Changes in a work environment frequently increase end-user fears about their jobs and in turn may generate resistance against a new ES (Sloane, 1991). The psychological impact and the organizational changes brought on by ES technology can be far more marked than those created by any other type of system. The major factor in determining the success of ES implementation is the willingness of an organization to accept the substantial change brought by the ES technology (Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994; Will et al, 1994). In such cases, management support is crucial to mitigate end-users negative attitudes toward ES, and to overcome the user resistance. Third, a desirable application area for an ES project is where expertise is scarce or expensive or where the experts are overworked (Lu & Guimaraes, 1989). In such cases, the direct supervisors of domain experts may find it difficult to share their time for ES development. However, higher-level management may take a longer view and be more amenable to investing substantial amounts of time over the period necessary for effective ES development (Prerau, 1990). Management can also play a vital role in dealing with uncooperative domain experts by assuring them about their jobs and help convince them of the potential ES benefits to the organization. Thus, this final hypothesis is formulated:

H7: Management support is positively related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

## Study methodology

This section discusses the study setting, the development of the data collection instrument, the sampling procedure and the measurement of variables used.

## The study setting

Due to the large number of company specific variables potentially important to ES implementation success, the limited literature available on the topic and the serious lack of empirical evidence about the determinants of ES success in practice, instead of a multi-company survey a case study approach to data collection has been used. This approach is expected to reduce the possibility of confounding results due to inter-organization differences such as ES development, budget, methodologies used, policies, conflicts between user departments and the company's ES development group, etc. This approach, of course, calls for careful consideration on whether or not the results from this study are generalizable to specific organizations. Further multi-company studies will be necessary to explore these factors' potential effects on ES implementation success.

The company in this study is EI Dupont de Nemours & Company. In 1986, Dupont management made the decision to pursue the use of expert systems to improve company-wide decision making. The Artificial Intelligence Task Force was created and charged with the broad implementation of expert systems technology throughout the company. This AI Task Force is still functioning today with the same vision that was identified in 1986:

ALL critical decisions will be made with our cumulative best knowledge and relevant information at the point of decision making.

Since 1986, Dupont has implemented over 1200 ES throughout the company. These systems are used by all functions and almost every location in the company is using at least one ES. Dupont's approach to the implementation of ES is very different from the traditional systems implementation approach. The plan is to provide low cost ES development tools (shells) that are easy to use for system development. The domain experts are trained to use the tools to develop their own systems without the need from the company support groups such as the Information Systems department or consultants. Corporate licences were established for software and training courses were developed. About 700 employees have been trained.

For most ES development projects at Dupont the project leaders are usually also the developers. ES development teams are usually very small. The most common team size for a development team is one (70–80%). For the rest of the applications, the development team is typically two. Only for a small number of ES were the development teams greater than two. Most ES are written by the experts to make their expertise more widely available to users who are less knowledgeable in the particular domain. Some ES (perhaps 10%) are written by the domain experts as a tool for their own personal use (or for other experts) to enhance their knowledge retention and ease of access.

## Development of the data collection instrument

As it will be described in more detail later, each major variable contains one or more sub-items extracted from the literature. These variables and their measuring scales make up the questionnaire, which was pre-tested for readability through personal interviews with several practitioners and academics (Yoon et al, 1995). The questionnaire asked respondents to indicate their agreement or disagreement with statements regarding each sub-item using a seven-point rating scales from (7) completely agree to (1) completely disagree. For example, one question on the ES impact on end-users job states: on the average, the ES has increased the job appeal followed by the scale described above. The questionnaire was divided into two parts. The first part is for project leaders and/or ES developers, consisting of questions regarding characteristics of domain experts, ES building tools, management support, and ES developer's skills. The second part was designed for end-user(s), consisting of questions on problem importance and difficulty, end-users characteristics, ES impact on end-users jobs, and user involvement in ES development. One of the more frequent end-users of each ES was chosen by one of the authors, a team leader of the Dupont's AI Task Force, to anonymously provide their responses directly to the author via e-mail or hard copy.

## Validity of the measures

Despite the exploratory nature of this study, several precautions were taken to ensure the validity of the measures used. Many of the recommendations by Carmines and Zeller (1979) were followed. To ensure content validity, a thorough survey of the relevant literature was undertaken to understand the important aspects of each major variable and its components, and so that any important dimension of any variable would not be neglected (Yoon et al, 1995).

As discussed earlier, the theoretical underpinnings of this study are relatively well established, and most of the constructs and the proposed relationships have been previously addressed by several authors. To further reduce the possibility of any non-random error, the main source of invalidity, a group of four academics and three practitioners, experts in the area of ES development management, reviewed the questionnaire for validity (measuring the phenomena intended), completeness (including all relevant items), and readability (making it unlikely that subjects will misinterpret a particular question). A few questions were reworded to improve readability; otherwise, the items composing each major variable remained as derived from the literature (Yoon et al, 1995). Before collecting data within Dupont, the same validation process was repeated with only cosmetic changes to the questionnaire being required.

## Reliability of the measures

Many of the measures used were chosen because they had been used previously, and their psychometric properties were relatively well known. Factor analysis showed that the items for each scale loaded unambiguously ( $\geqslant0.50$ into one factor and $\leqslant0.30$ into the others), thus indicating construct unidimensionality, a requirement for computing the Cronbach's Alpha. As discussed in the next section, the internal consistency reliability coefficients (alpha) for the constructs in this study are all well above the level of 0.50 acceptable for exploratory studies (Nunally, 1978).

## Measurements of variables

Although the validation process for measures of the study variables is in its infancy, the sub-items for each of the major study variables are well established in the literature. Every sub-item, except for the demographic information about leaders/developers and end-users, was followed by the same scale described earlier.

## ES impact on end-users jobs

When measuring the success of an ES project, the ES impact on organizations and end-users job has been used frequently. The impact on jobs has been measured in a variety of ways: time reduced to accomplish the tasks, accuracy increased, and change in the nature of a task (Sviokla, 1990; Rochette, 1992; Agarwal et al, 1994). Byrd (1992) measured ES impact on end-user(s) jobs based on two items: fear of loss of control and fear of loss of jobs. Sviokla (1990) analysed the impact of XCON on end-user(s) job in terms of the changes in input and output, the increase in the task accuracy, the amount of work completed, the shifts in the end-user(s) role and responsibilities, and job satisfaction. Based on Byrd (1992) and Sviokla (1990), this study employed 11 variables to measure the desirable impact of an ES on end-users' jobs: increase in the importance of the users' job, decrease in the amount of work required on a job, decrease in the accuracy demanded on the job, increasing skills needed to do the job, increase in job appeal, increase in feedback on the job performance, increase in freedom in how to do the job, increase in opportunity for advancement, increase in job security, increase in relationship with fellow employees, and increase in job satisfaction. The average score for the 11 items is computed to assess the overall ES's impact on end-user(s) jobs. The internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach's Alpha) for this scale was 0.73 which is considered satisfactory for these types of empirical field tests.

## Problem importance

As done earlier (Yoon et al, 1995), a single item is used to measure this variable. Respondents were asked to rate the importance of the task which the ES deals with.

## Problem difficulty

Prior studies have listed many desirable attributes of an ES domain. Waterman (1986) described several attributes of appropriate ES problems: not requiring common sense, not too difficult, symbolic structure, manageable size, and stability. Smith (1988) emphasised simplicity, ease of understanding, and manageable size. Other studies listed stability of task knowledge (Beckman, 1991; Medsker & Liebowitz, 1994) and a narrow/well-defined focus (Casey, 1989; Will et al, 1994). The following eight variables are operationalised to measure an ES task difficulty: problem size; complexity; interdependence among variables; the level of expertise required; uncertainty; instability; labour intensity; and problem unstructuredness. Problem size refers to the number of variables and the depth/width of the problem addressed by the ES. Problem complexity refers to the number and variety of ways in which the variables in the problem relate to another, and how well these relationships are understood by ES developers and end-users. The interdependence among variables is defined to the degree which a variable is correlated with each other. The level of expertise required refers to the level of education and experience domain experts are required to have to effectively solve problems in a specific domain. Input uncertainty is defined as the degree to which the ES input is unknown. Domain instability refers to the frequency which domain knowledge changes over time. The degree of labour intensity deals with the amount of people's time and effort necessary to solve the problem. Degree of problem structure refers to the extent to which the problem is programmable. The internal consistency (Cronbach's Alpha) of the eight-item scale was 0.79. For each subject, the average response for the eight items is the measure of problem difficulty.

## Developer's characteristics

Debenham (1990) listed four essential skills of knowledge engineers: the ability to extract accurate and complete knowledge from human experts; the ability to represent and implement knowledge; the ability to design an ES for maintenance; the ability to design an ES that exploits existing investments in information processing. A comprehensive list of knowledge and skills necessary for ES developers has been developed by Payne and Awad (1990). It includes knowledge of computer technology, knowledge of general fact-finding techniques, knowledge of prototype methods, knowledge of human factors, knowledge of functional areas, communication skills, project planning skills, human relations skills, organizational skills, and personal attributes. Behavioural and interpersonal skills of knowledge engineers has been emphasised by Mykytyn et al (1994). Although the range of developers skills and abilities varies slightly between studies, they can be classified into six categories, according to Nunamaker et al (1982): people, models, systems, computers, organizations, and society. People skills refer to communication and interpersonal skills. Models skills are defined as the ability to formulate and solve models of the operation research type. Systems skills is referred to the ability to view and define a situation as a system-specifying components, scopes, and functions. Computer skills refer to knowledge of hardware/software, programming languages, and ES techniques. Organizational skills are defined as knowledge of the functional areas of an organization and organizational conditions. Last, society skills generally refer to the ability to articulate and defend a personal position on important issues of the information technology impact on society. More specifically, it refers to the ability to perceive and describe the impact of an ES on society. This study used these six items to examine the relationship between developer(s) skills and ES success. The average response to the six items represent the measure of developer(s) skills. The internal consistency reliabilities (Cronbach's Alpha) of the six-item scale was 0.76.

## End-user characteristics

The dominant end-user characteristics affecting ES success include user attitude, user expectation and user knowledge of computer and ES technology (Smith, 1988), user confidence with the system (Will et al, 1994), and user commitment to learn how to use the system (Slagle & Wick, 1988; Liebowitz, 1991; Hayes-Roth & Jacobstein, 1994). Another end-user characteristic mentioned in previous studies is the user knowledge of computer and ES techniques (Prerau, 1990). Five items were used to assess end-user(s) characteristics: education, experience, positive attitudes toward the ES, expectations on the ES, and computer and AI knowledge. The internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach's Alpha) for this scale was 0.64. The average response to these five items is the measure of end-user(s) characteristics.

## Shell characteristics

The desirable features of an ES shell listed in literature include flexibility of knowledge representation, flexibility of the inference engine, ability of monitoring session activities, knowledge-based editing facility, knowledge acquisition facility, debugging aids, uncertainty management, end-user interface, explanation facility, integration with external databases and other systems, integration with other programming languages, easy to use, easy to learn, portability, response time, real time support, documentation, and vendor support (Harmon et al., 1988; Brody, 1989; Vedder, 1989; Vedder et al., 1989; Kim & Yoon, 1992; Plant & Salinas, 1994). Obviously, some features are applicable only to specific expert systems; ie, the capability of real-time support is applied exclusively to a real-time ES application. Based on the above studies, 10 general features deemed applicable to a wide range of ES applications were used in this study: flexibility for knowledge representation and inference engine, developer interface, end-user interface, system interface, portability among different platforms, easy to use, easy to learn, training and vendor support, response time, and a shell's appropriateness to the problem. The average score for the ten items was computed to measure ES shell quality. The internal consistency coefficient for the scale was 0.86.

## User involvement

The measure for end-user's involvement has been validated through a series of continuous studies in use involvement (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1989, 1990, 1991;

Torkzadeh & Doll, 1994). This study has adopted the measure and included nine items suitable to assessing user involvement in ES development and implementation: initiating the project, establishing the objective of the project, determining user requirements, accessing ways to meet user requirements, identifying the sources of data/information, outlining the information flow, developing the input forms/screens, developing the output forms/screen, and determining the system availability/access. The average score for the nine items is the measure of user involvement. The internal reliability coefficient for this scale was 0.95.

## Management support

Management support was measured by four items: management understanding of the ES potential benefits; encouragement by management to use ES in their job; providing the necessary help and resources for effective use of ES; and management interest in having employees satisfied with ES technology. The items were averaged to compute a measure of management support. The internal consistency reliability coefficient for the scale was 0.89.

## Sampling procedure and sample description

The project leaders and end-users of 150 expert systems which were operational for at least 2 years throughout Dupont were invited to participate in this study. This should be viewed as a convenience sample since many ES were excluded because their developer was not known or no longer worked for the company, or their operation has been discontinued, or their developers or project leaders could not be reached or were too busy at the time to participate in this study. Of the 150 questionnaires which were mailed out, 114 were returned in time to be processed for this report (a response rate of 76%). The 114 ES applications reported on by each project leader can be categorised according to problem type: control procedure (26), planning (8), education (4), configuration (4), selection (24), diagnostics (25), forecast (4) and others (19). According to their functional areas, these 114 ES are classified into: service (10), manufacturing (59), finance (5), management (9), personnel (3), marketing (3), IS (22), and others (3). Demographic information on respondents are shown in Table 1.

## Results from an integrated model

While the intercorrelation analysis provides evidence for the relationships of each independent variable with the dependent variable, it does not take into account the possible interrelations among the independent variables.

To examine that, a preliminary integrated model for ES job impact determinant is tested via multivariate analysis, with results shown in Table 3.

Using the stepwise method for first entering the independent variables which contribute the most to statistically explaining the variance in the dependent variable, the results indicate that, in total, close to 43% of the variance can be explained with this integrated model. To test the possibility of a multicollinearity problem among the independent variable, the well established ‘condition number diagnostic’ approach was used (Jobson, 1991). If the condition number is less than 100, multicollinearity is not to be considered a problem. Table 3 indicates the highest condition number is 39.45, well below the safe limit.

Based on this multivariant regression model, there are three statistically significant (at the 0.05 level or better) determinants for ES desirable impact on users' jobs: problem importance explains 22.41% of the variance in ES job impact, followed by shell characteristics (14.26%), and developer skill (4.18%). The contribution from the other determinants in this model, given the order in which they have entered the regression equation, are not statistically significant.

## Discussion and managerial recommendations

Several insights can be gleaned from the study's results which have important implications to ES technology management in organizations. We can conclude that ES can have substantial positive impact on users' jobs, thus providing organizations with a major opportunity to enjoy many of the promised benefits. Six of the seven major variables addressed in this study show significant influence in determining a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

Considered individually, the most important major variables determining a desirable impact of ES on users' jobs are shell characteristics, developers skills, user involvement in the ES development process, management support for the use of ES technology in the organization, and the nature of the problem being addressed by the ES (its importance and difficulty).

Considered together, according to the integrated model developed in this study, the selection of business problems which are important in the eyes of end-users, and the availability of high quality shells and the assignment/recruitment of high-quality ES developers to address such problems, are important factors determining favourable ES impact on users' jobs. Based on the results, the following recommendations can be advanced.

The selection of suitable applications is indeed worthy of careful management attention. Many useful general recommendations have already been made (ie, Lu & Guimaraes, 1989) for the systematic selection of applications with the objective of deriving long-term leverage of ES development resources. The results from this study provide additional and more specific criteria for ES application selection. Under the reasonable assumption that favourable ES impact on users' jobs represent a valuable contribution to the organization, ES project managers should deliberately seek applications which deal with important, large, labour intensive, and/or complex problems which are important to the organization.

The selection of an appropriate shell which matches the business problem, as well as user and developer requirements is an important event. Yoon and Guimaraes (1994) have provided guidelines for matching specific problem characteristics with shells supporting four main ES development techniques (knowledge-based, neural network, model-based, or case-based). Yoon et al (1994) have shown how these ES development techniques can be combined effectively. Unfortunately most organizations today are woefully unprepared in this area, with few organizations providing the main alternatives. The results from this study regarding shell quality may not be applicable only to the more widely used knowledge-based ES which comprise our sample. Nevertheless, developers and project managers should carefully select shells along the important features studied here, i.e., flexibility in knowledge representation and construction of the inference engine, interface with other systems, ease of learning and of using, training and vendor support, response time, and appropriateness to the problem. As the cost of ES shells continue to decrease, managers are quickly running out of excuses for not making available to developers and encouraging the use, ie (providing developer training) of appropriate shells for the development of new ES applications, particularly those dealing with problems of major importance to the organization.

Despite the fact that the knowledge for an ES will come from a domain expert who expectedly has more knowledge about the problem than most users, user involvement in the ES development process seems to significantly affect the degree to which the ES will positively affect the users' jobs. Unfortunately, as pointed out by Wells and Guimaraes (1992), organizations are severely weak in enlisting user involvement in ES development. This needs to be remedied urgently. It is interesting to note that management support is not related to user involvement. Even though management support is having a significant impact on user characteristics, apparently project managers are not using available top management influence to recruit users for ES development. The difficulty of the problem, its importance, and characteristics of the user seem to be doing more to attract user involvement to the development process. Project managers should ensure appropriate level of user participation by discussing the need with department and top managers, and by following many of the recommendations to ensure management support previously proposed in the context of more traditional systems such as DSS and transaction processing systems.

Last, the importance of skilful developers and ‘quality shells’ suggest that for companies starting to use ES technology, outsourcing the development of the first ES applications may be a worthwhile consideration. However, given the strategic potential of ES technology, this should be viewed primarily as a mechanism to move the organization faster through the learning curve, avoiding long term dependence on any vendors.

## Research limitations and opportunities

There are several aspects of this study which represent opportunities for further research in a technology area which has captured people's imagination, already produced great benefits to business organizations in practice, and continues to expand its promise and potential. As said earlier, due to the lack of general information and empirical evidence about the ES-related factors affecting users' jobs in practice, ES from one company were used. This approach reduces the possibility of confounding results due to a wide variety of reasons such as inter-organization differences as to ES development sophistication, budget, methodologies used, development resources available, policies, and conflicts between user departments and the company's ES development group. On the other hand, this approach raises the question of generalizability. The results from this study provide researchers with more focus for defining hypotheses which can be better tested with a multi-company survey. This is a much needed extension of this work.

The general application of this study's results can also be questioned in terms of the industry sector and the sophistication of the ES development environment. We have no reason to suspect that the results and conclusions are applicable only to manufacturing organizations; however, that needs to be formally tested. On the other hand, one should assume that the organization studied has an extremely sophisticated ES development environment, with a wide variety of development tools and methods, and hundreds of ES developers and users with many years of experience using ES technology. It may be vital to take that into consideration while attempting to apply some of the results to other organizations. Last, as with all field tests based on people's opinion, it would be very important to re-test the relationships among the variables studied here with more objective measures, and to explore other potentially important success factors and construct relationships.

Aaron B Clevenson is a consultant with the Artificial Intelligence Task Force at EI DuPont de Nemours & Co, Inc. He received a BS in Physics in 1977 and an MS in Measurement and Control Sciences in 1978 from Carnegie-Mellon University. He has been with DuPont since 1978, and has been a part of the AI Task Force since 1986. In 1989 and 1990 he led DuPont's efforts to integrate Expert Systems into their manufacturing operations in Europe. He has been the team leader for the AI Task Force since 1993. He is the author of thirty-four expert systems implemented throughout DuPont which include production advisors, maintenance troubleshooters, operations schedulers, and various support systems. He has developed the curricula that is used in DuPont's Expert Systems Workshops, has taught dozens of workshops and trained hundreds of employees in the use of tools for the development of expert systems.
