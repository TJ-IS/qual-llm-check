# Outsourcing in the private and public sectors: an unpredictable IT strategy

W.L. CURRIE

Senior Lecturer, Department of Management and Organisation, School of Management, University of Stirling, Stirling FK9 4LA, UK

IT outsourcing has grown considerably in the private sector, with IT software and service suppliers reporting increased revenues. Similarly in the public sector, the introduction of market testing and compulsory competitive tendering (CCT) will inevitably lead to the contracting-out of significant levels of IT work. This may lead to the demise of some public sector IT departments where external bids are favoured over those offered by the in-house team. This paper is divided into two sections. First, it considers some of the relevant literature on IT outsourcing in both private and public sector British and American organizations. It draws together important themes which explain the attraction of IT outsourcing to senior executives. Second, it introduces the results from a questionnaire survey of nearly 200 UK private and public sector organizations on IT outsourcing. In particular it looks at the proportion of organizations that claims to use outsourcing, how contracts are negotiated and the type of IT solutions preferred by IT managers. The central argument is that IT outsourcing poses significant challenges to both private and public sector organizations and is not simply a quick-fix panacea. Moreover, public sector IT managers would do well to analyse some of the less favourable reports from private sector outsourcing as they gear themselves up for CCT in the months ahead.

## Introduction

The subject of IT outsourcing has received much attention in recent years as a result of the widespread publicity surrounding some of the largest contracts which amount to many millions of pounds (Buck-Lew, 1992; Currie, 1994a; Evans, 1994). In many ways, outsourcing is a new strategy for managing IT (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994). For although numerous companies have sought strategic alliances with IT vendors over many years, the spectre of total outsourcing (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993a, 1993b) suggests that an external provider of IT services is a more optimal strategy than retaining an in-house IT function (Huber, 1993). Clearly this is a contentious debate and many authors now question the arguments in favour of IT outsourcing in the context of economies of scale, cost control, efficiency, labour reduction and service quality (Willcocks & Fitzgerald, 1993; Lacity et al., 1994). In this paper, we attempt to contribute to this growing body of literature by exploring the subject of IT outsourcing in private and public sector contexts with a view to making some comparisons.

The paper is divided into two main sections. First, it explores some of the current debates in the literature surrounding the phenomenon of IT outsourcing in private and public sector organizations. Delineating the two sectors is important since they are both affected by legal and political influences which possibly serve to converge organizational processes and managerial decision-making practices (Currie & Bryson, 1995a). Indeed, recent empirical evidence points to some convergence between the two sectors. For example, the legislation of CCT which advocates the market testing of IT services in the public sector (Fenwick & Shaw, 1994) is indicative of the current political ideology which supports a free market approach to the provision and purchase of IT services (Carnaghan & Bracewell-Milnes, 1993). As such, the provider–purchaser split which is currently being forged within the public sector supports moves towards contracting-out its IT services to an external IT vendor, a practice which, in turn, will further blur the boundaries between private and public sector management control of IT services. This is because the market testing of IT services will mean that cost and efficiency imperatives are arguably more inclined to take precedence over other considerations (e.g. providing a service to the community), thus ensuring that the public sector increasingly emulates many of the management practices of its private sector counterpart. Against this background, we consider some of the recent literature on private and public sector IT outsourcing, with particular emphasis on how the latter is adopting some of the organizational and managerial practices more commonly associated with the former sector, e.g. cost control, rationalisation and restructuring, performance measurement and benchmarking (internal and external) and new management and staff employment contracts.

Second, we consider empirical evidence from survey and case study research carried out by the author on managing IT in private and public sector organizations. Here a questionnaire survey was sent to IT managers in the financial services, manufacturing industry and public sector to elicit information on their perceptions of outsourcing as a viable strategy for IT (among other lines of enquiry which are outside the scope of the present paper). Some 184 usable questionnaires were returned, giving a response rate of 25 per cent. The questionnaire survey focused upon the following three lines of enquiry relating to IT outsourcing: the proportion of organizations in the representative sectors that claims to outsource part of their IT facility (only one company claimed to adopt a total outsourcing approach); the type of contract used for contracting-out IT work (e.g. time and materials or fixed term, fixed price); and finally the preferences for IT solutions (e.g. to purchase off-the-shelf software or develop their own IT system). The questionnaire survey was also followed up with in-depth interviews with respondents from the two sectors. Since there are already many valuable academic contributions focusing largely on private sector IT outsourcing, this section discusses some of the important legal and political changes currently affecting the UK public sector and offers some comparisons with the former sector.

## The growing phenomenon of IT outsourcing in private and public sector organizations

## The private sector

In recent years the phenomenon of IT outsourcing has gained momentum in private and public sectors in the

UK and the USA (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993a; Quinn & Hilmer, 1994; Takac, 1994; Cross, 1995). In the private sector, the overriding rationale for outsourcing is encouraged by the drive to improve competitive performance by reducing costs in conjunction with improving operating efficiency (Owen & Aitchison, 1988; Kass & Caldwell, 1990; Krass, 1990; McMullen, 1990). In short, those in favour of IT outsourcing contend that managers should concentrate on improving their core competencies and contract out all support services to outside agencies for cost and operating efficiencies (Eckerson, 1990; Datamation, 1994; Deutsch, 1994).

Table 1 gives an indication of some of the largest UK IT outsourcing contracts to be signed in recent years, and the cases of the Inland Revenue and the DVLA demonstrate how public sector IT work has been transferred to the private sector. Each year witnesses the signing of ever larger IT outsourcing contracts, with some running as high as £100 million over ten years.

In the UK, in 1994, the software and IT services industry grew by 14 per cent to £7.75 billion, which is the fastest rate of growth so far recorded. The UK revenues from software and services of the three largest companies grew by 10 per cent, 45 per cent and 24 per cent respectively (Computer Weekly, 1995). The report concludes that the significant reason for such impressive growth is the switch from in-house user spend to outsourcing.

In the literature, case study evidence on IT outsourcing suggests the overriding reasons for opting for this route are to reduce costs and gain access to new resources (from the IT supplier). The following US and UK case examples confirm this finding. In the case of a US bank, senior managers elected to hire an external IT vendor on a long-term contract to take control of the IT facility. The rationale underpinning such a move was to reduce costs which would be achieved, in part, by releasing senior management time to concentrate on developing new products and services for key customers. [This would enable senior managers to focus] on business customers and wealthy individuals, the type of clientele that demanded increasingly personalised banking services.

For the purpose of managing the IT outsourcing contract, the bank would retain an appropriate number of ‘technically literate business people’ to manage the relationship between the bank and the IT vendor. An estimated \$10 million a year would be saved through outsourcing, although the author concedes it is not possible to ‘predict the long term savings accurately’ (p. 129). Such a rationale in favour of IT outsourcing is based upon the view that bankers should concentrate on banking and leave IT specialists to enhance the bank’s technical capabilities.

In a more recent example of outsourcing at British Petroleum, Cross (1995) argues for an IT outsourcing strategy to purchase IT services from multiple suppliers, yet 'have pieces delivered as if they came from a single supplier'. This company was against the strategy of selective outsourcing adopted by many companies, which is to purchase IT services from a large single supplier, because 'we believed such an approach could make us vulnerable to escalating fees and inflexible services' (p. 94). Indeed, evidence for such a scenario is now well documented in the literature on single supplier IT outsourcing contracts (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993a, 1993b). Instead the company hired three IT suppliers with the proviso that each work together to 'deliver a single seamless service to our 42 businesses around the globe'. The arrangement of multiple IT suppliers which act as one was thought by British Petroleum to be most appropriate for obtaining high-quality IT services at low cost. This is achieved by the three IT suppliers also contracting out their clients' work to other suppliers while, at the same time, seeking to benchmark their portfolio of services against the IT outsourcing market. The company claims success from its selected IT outsourcing strategy with a reduction in IT staff of 80 per cent and reduced operating costs down from \$360 million in 1989 to \$132 million in 1994 (Cross, 1995).

While it is outside the scope of the present paper to review a wide selection of the literature on private sector IT outsourcing, the managerial impetus behind this strategy appears to be twofold. First, it is based upon the preconception that companies should continually market-test their IT services to seek out ways of reducing costs and improving efficiency. This has led some companies to adopt the position ipso facto that an external IT supplier will be able to supply IT services more cheaply and efficiently than an in-house IT department. Second, and related to the first point, senior managers commonly believe that external IT suppliers possess a wide portfolio of technical expertise which is unequalled in their own organizations. By outsourcing the IT facility, the client will be able to take advantage of the expertise contained within the supplier organization and thus concentrate on its own core business. Access to new resources also includes the deals which the IT supplier may have with other small suppliers of IT software and services, thus further reducing costs in what may be described as a complex web of client–supplier relationships.

While the above arguments in favour of IT outsourcing are based upon a cost/efficiency imperative and access to new resources, they are seen as contentious in the literature. Convincing arguments have been voiced which question three common precepts of IT outsourcing. They are the extent to which an external IT supplier can offer IT software and services more cheaply than the in-house IT department, the degree to which an external IT supplier possesses new resources in the form of technical expertise, technology (hardware/software) and business acumen, and the notion that the client and supplier can form a partnership that is politically and financially neutral (Currie, 1994; Lacity et al., 1995). Against this backdrop, the following section explores the growing phenomenon of IT outsourcing in the public sector. In particular, the discussion focuses upon some of the legal and political decisions which render IT outsourcing not a voluntary option but a compulsory prospect for UK public sector IT managers.

## The public sector

In the public sector, the term IT outsourcing is now being used interchangeably with the following government initiated programmes: market testing, compulsory competitive tendering (CCT) and contracting out. These terms are politically emotive within UK public sector organizations (local authorities, government departments, NHS trusts, etc.) and each has a separate meaning which is defined (HMSO, 1993) as follows:

In market testing, an activity currently performed in-house is subjected to competition . . . Competitive tendering covers the stages in the market testing process from developing the specification up to, and including, awarding the contract or service level agreement . . . Contracting-out, which follows competitive tendering, is the purchase of a service from an outside organisation, and can be as a result of a market test involving an in-house bid or a strategic decision to obtain the service from the private sector.

The Local Government Act of 1988, in relation to CCT, extended the provisions of the Planning and

Land Act of 1980 by including a range of white-collar services such as legal services, computing, financial, personnel, corporate administration and construction-related activities (engineering, architectural and property management). These services are now subject to the new CCT arrangements covering the period from 1994 to 1997. However, timescales vary according to type of service, geographical location and internal management issues, and also because of the different political flavour of local authorities (Fenwick & Shaw, 1994).

Writing on the subject of CCT in the UK, Moules (1995) contends that public sector IT managers are expected to commence the tendering process by 1997, with as much as 70 per cent of IT work being contracted out to a third party organization. Contracts should be for a maximum of five years' duration. While it is recognised that local authorities vary in size and scope in regard to IT departments and projects, it is recommended that a minimum of £300,000 worth of business should remain in-house (see Table 2).

Some UK public sector managers prefer a voluntary approach to competitive tendering (known as VCT) and they have already invited tenders from interested parties. The key advantage of VCT is that IT managers can select their own timescales for the tendering process, as opposed to adhering to the government's compulsory tendering alternative. They can also select the IT work for which they wish to invite tenders, rather than selecting an obligatory 70 per cent under the CCT rules.

In recent months, however, the government's deadlines have been put back one year (to 1997) to enable public sector IT departments to make the necessary management and administrative changes. But a more cynical view suggests the shift in timescales may be due to unforeseen problems in managing the transition to

CCT, particularly in those public sector IT departments where managers are politically against such enforced changes (Currie & Bryson, 1995a)

Table 2 gives a brief history of the political changes which have led to the adoption of CCT in the UK public sector. Here it can be observed that CCT for IT and other white-collar departments is based upon similar arrangements for blue-collar forms of work.

While CCT in the white-collar area is still in its infancy, the implementation of the policy seemingly invites more questions than it is currently able to answer. For instance, the ad hoc figure that some 70 per cent of IT business should be exposed to CCT, together with the recommendation that some £300,000 of IT work should remain in-house, are likely to pose complex problems for public sector IT managers who must select what to retain and what to outsource. This is because they must now manage the IT resource according to a relatively rigid framework which emphasises market, cost and timescale priorities. Indeed, the UK Department of the Environment's claim that it has 'allowed enough flexibility to accommodate all shapes and sizes of authority by negotiating with suppliers and consultants as well as council offices' (Moules, 1995, p. 17) is likely to be interpreted as naive by some public sector IT managers. This is particularly likely where IT activities are not easily delineated into neat categories, each of which is easily specified for the tendering process.

One scenario is that public sector IT managers in smaller departments may contract out work to only one IT supplier, a strategy which has already been criticised by some writers on IT outsourcing (Lacity et al., 1994; Cross, 1995). Notwithstanding these potential pitfalls, the move towards CCT has gained considerable momentum in public sector IT departments and, while not being politically supported in all of them, is likely to continue in the foreseeable future. Against this brief overview of some of the relevant issues relating to private and public sector IT outsourcing, the next section discusses survey and case study research by the author and attempts to compare and contrast outsourcing strategies adopted by the two sectors.

## Survey and case studies on IT outsourcing in the UK

## Methodology

## Questionnaire survey

During 1994 the author conducted a questionnaire survey on managing IT in private and public sector organizations. The questions focused on IT strategy formulation, evaluation, implementation and human resource issues. For the present purposes, we shall concentrate on the findings from the questions relating to IT outsourcing. The questionnaire survey was mailed to some 900 organizations which subscribed to the Computer Users' Handbook (1994). Selection for the sample was on the basis of organizations possessing at least 150 PCs and being in one of the following sectors: public (UK SICC codes 91–95), financial/services (UK SICC codes 81–83, 96) or manufacturing (UK SICC codes 21–26, 30–49). The addressee for the questionnaire was the contact name provided by the database. This was usually the most senior manager of IT within the organization. In about 20 per cent of responses, the questionnaire was completed by someone other than the addressee, sometimes the deputy IT manager or a non-IT manager, such as the managing director. This meant that the responses obtained could vary according to the experience, background and position of the individual completing the questionnaire.

The questionnaire survey achieved a response rate of some 25 per cent. This was considered favourable in the light of the frequent requests these managers receive to answer similar surveys (both academic and commercial). Some 184 usable questionnaires were returned.

## Semi-structured interviews

The questionnaire survey proved a useful instrument to gain access to respondents who agreed to be interviewed by the researchers. Using the questionnaire survey as background information, the researchers sought interviews with 15 organizations from the financial services, manufacturing industry and public sectors (totalling 45 interviews). Since this paper considers IT outsourcing in the private and public sectors, examples will be given from two in-depth case studies from the financial services and public sectors, as space limitations do not allow for the inclusion of further interview data. These organizations include a bank, an insurance company, an NHS trust and a government agency (see Table 3). Interviews with respondents lasted between two to five hours, depending upon time restrictions imposed by the interviewee. In each case, the most senior IT manager/director was interviewed about IT outsourcing and this person had previously completed the questionnaire survey. In all cases, respondents refused the use of tape recorders as some of the information was highly contentious, e.g. political decisions underpinning the IT outsourcing decision. Interviewers thus used the questionnaire survey as a basis for asking further questions, and these answers were reported in a notebook. Other information received from the organizations included IT strategy documents, financial reports and service level agreements.

The case study organizations were selected to draw distinctions between managing IT and outsourcing in the private and public sectors. Clearly this represents only a snapshot, although some interesting observations can be drawn from analysing the political and market positions in which these organizations operate. Having outlined the methodological approach adopted by the researchers, the following section discusses some of the key findings in relation to IT outsourcing strategies in the UK, drawing from both the questionnaire survey and case study data.

## Management decision making and outsourcing

The 184 questionnaire survey respondents from the private and public sectors were asked to give some indication of the extent to which they used outsourcing as a viable option for IT. From the responses obtained, the majority of respondents interpreted IT outsourcing in a broader sense than simply hiring-in IT contractors to carry out software development work (among other activities). Lacity and Hirschheim (1993a, 1993b) give three categories of IT outsourcing. The first category, body shop outsourcing, is where management uses outsourcing as a way to meet short-term demand; the most common type being the hiring of contract IT personnel (analyst/programmers etc.). Project management outsourcing, the second category, is where management outsources a specific project or portion of IS work to an external IT supplier. Examples of this are applications development work, disaster recovery and mainframe management. The third category, total outsourcing, involves an external IT supplier taking charge of a significant piece or even all of a client's IS work (e.g. managing a data centre and telecommunications).

From the findings of the questionnaire survey, some 71 per cent of organizations said they did not outsource their IT facility (see Figure 1). This was against 29 per cent who claimed they did employ the services of an IT outsourcing vendor. In the context of the three categories outlined by Lacity and Hirschheim (1993b), it is likely that more organizations in our sample were engaged in some form of body shop outsourcing than our findings would suggest. Evidence for this was where respondents divided labour into permanent/contractor categories, since the latter was likely to be engaged in short-term IT work, possible handled by an external supplier.

However, of the organizations who claimed not to outsource IT work, as many as three-quarters were considering this option for the future. In particular, all public sector IT managers were gearing themselves up for CCT, and over half were concerned that in-house IT departments would be unable to compete with external IT suppliers.

A cross-sectoral analysis of the questionnaire survey data found that companies in the financial services were only marginally more likely to outsource parts of their IT work than their manufacturing or public sector counterparts. Yet this was likely to change if and when the policy of CCT was fully implemented post-1997.

The prospect of IT outsourcing was of great concern to three-quarters of IT managers, as large-scale outsourcing could threaten the survival of the in-house IT department. Banks and insurance companies tended to have the largest IT departments, often with over 800 employees. Fears of outsourcing were expressed more fully by IT managers in the financial services and public sectors than in manufacturing firms.

Follow-up semi-structured interviews with IT managers from the financial services and public sectors suggested they were ‘constantly inundated’ by external IT suppliers who claimed they could provide ‘a cheaper and more efficient IT service’. A senior IT manager at a UK bank said that:

Consultants target senior executives who have little understanding of IT. They come up with attractive proposals that will save money so it is not surprising they are listened to. We [the in-house IT department] are then asked if we can compete on price and quality. The problem for us is trying to convince senior executives that many of the proposed cost savings from external companies are pie-in-the-sky and will never be achieved in practice. Indeed, we argue that if an external company can achieve savings, then so can we!

In public sector organizations, IT managers said they were ‘trying to make sense’ of the CCT rules they were being forced to enact by 1997. One IT manager with 25 years’ experience of local government said that he was uncertain whether his IT division would be around in the next five years. He said that from 1 April 1996, the council would no longer exist since it would be merged with another council. This would affect all central services including IT. He saw three possible scenarios for the future of his own IT division. First, it could receive residual backing from the new council structure and provide IT services in a similar way to its current method of operating (e.g. developing, implementing and maintaining IT throughout the region). Second, it could become absorbed by the leading council where its activities would be merged with other existing IT service providers. This would no doubt lead to job cuts in spite of the present council policy of no compulsory redundancies. Third, the IT division could become a separate independent entity which operated as a business in its own right. This could emerge from a management buy-out where the new company could provide services to clients within the new council structure as well as others in the private sector. Should this scenario be followed, the new business would adopt commercial rates with the objective of profit maximisation rather than the present situation where any financial surplus was redistributed to the council departments.

At the time of interview, the IT manager said that so much uncertainty surrounded the future provision of IT services that he was unable to plan ahead more than a year. This situation was not helping staff morale, as many people were fearful of losing their jobs. It was also pointed out that cross-communication with managers from other district councils was ‘totally forbidden'. Such a situation, he asserted, was akin to 'managing in the dark'.

On the subject of outsourcing the IT manager said that it was now official policy that some 70 per cent of the total IT spend would be subject to CCT from October 1997. When asked exactly what he meant by total IT spend, he was unable to clarify this point. He said that because of the ‘total confusion’ about the future, the 70 per cent could refer to software applications development, implementation and maintenance services and other support services. While he saw this as a possible threat, he felt that his current team of 80 people in development, together with a further 100 in operations, communications and client consultancy (comprising the IT division), were more than able to provide cost-effective and efficient IT services to a range of clients.

Though he felt his team could compete with most IT vendors, he was concerned that senior managers engaged in market testing activities would be impressed by the slick approach adopted by external management consultants. He saw this as a problem because the existing IT division could not compete with external management consultants on image and presentation, although they had experience and informal knowledge of the IT needs of most of their internal markets and customers. He saw this tacit knowledge as crucial, yet was aware that it could not be written into formal proposals in support of the in-house IT facility. Thus many of the qualitative advantages of the in-house team were likely to be overlooked by senior managers, who would instead concentrate on the financial forecasts presented to them in the form of competitive tenders from external interested third parties.

To support his belief in retaining a strong in-house IT facility, the IT manager said that two important points should be noted. The first concerned loyalty of service where internal IT staff were keen to do a good job even if it meant working at home, often unpaid. The prospect of IT outsourcing, according to the IT manager, would not necessarily equate with better service or reduced costs. The second was about providing IT services across the board. He pointed out that:

In my opinion, senior managers forget that, in the current regime, we are unable to turn down work even if we want to. Where CCT operates, and more so if we became an independent company, we would be very selective about the work we do and who we do it for. A lot of my staff offer help and advice to their colleagues, even in their own time. If I was a director of an independent IT company, I would not allow my staff to do work unpaid. Outsourcing could therefore be more expensive for the client as each hour would be recorded and invoiced.

The IT manager argued that, in his opinion, IT outsourcing was a symptom of the 1980s fixation on competition, performance measurement, value for money and market testing. But the notion that outsiders always provide a better service simply because the client can select the supplier was not an adequate reason in itself for outsourcing IT services or any other service for that matter. This person felt that senior managers were more inclined to favour the external bid simply because the in-house IT department was 'caught-up in the internal politics of the organization'. He also dismissed the value for money arguments put forward by the external IT supplier, as they were 'purely theoretical and could only be proven or disproven once a contract has been signed and sealed (if not delivered!)'. Clearly then, the type of outsourcing contract adopted by client and supplier organizations was an important factor underpinning any cost and service improvements. It is to this issue that we now turn.

## IT outsourcing contracts

Exploring some of the contractual issues of IT outsourcing from the questionnaire survey and follow-up case studies tended to reinforce the concerns raised in the academic and trade literature (Deutsch, 1994; Evans, 1994; Yazel, 1994). A significant problem was defining the legal contract between client and supplier. While this relationship is euphemistically described by some as a partnership, others would see it more cynically as two separate businesses, each trying to make a profit from the other. Recent examples show that many companies with IT outsourcing contracts have paid insufficient attention to the small print of the contract, usually at the expense of the client organization (Currie, 1994b). This has often resulted in the situation where the client realises that many peripheral IT services are not included in the original standard contract and are therefore subject to additional costs at full commercial rates (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993a, 1993b). The complexities of IT outsourcing contracts lead Deutsch (1994) to argue for a more protracted negotiation period between client and supplier. He also stresses that no outsourcing service or activity should be undertaken without the contract being signed – nor even under an initial client–supplier agreement or letter of intent. He also advises that IT outsourcing contracts should build in adequate terms which ensure that the business is not dislocated in the event of the contract being terminated on either side.

The questionnaire survey examined the type of IT outsourcing contracts between client and supplier and found that all sectors preferred the fixed-term, fixed-price contract over the more open-ended (time and materials) contract (see Figure 2). Public sector organizations were marginally more likely to negotiate fixed-term, fixed-price contracts than their manufacturing or financial services counterparts. In fact, 80 per cent of public sector IT managers said that high-profile IT disasters coupled with more stringent rules of public accounting and auditing meant that this type of contract was becoming the norm. Similarly, in the financial services and manufacturing industry, IT managers were becoming more concerned to develop more rigid and measurable contracts for outsourcing IT work.

Follow-up interviews with IT managers confirmed that time and materials contracts where suppliers were given an open-ended financial contract and were expected to invoice the client at the end of the job were becoming fewer in number. This was fuelled by the situation where resources were harder to obtain, forcing IT managers and their clients to take more difficult IT decisions over choice and priority. In the public sector, market testing, competitive tendering and greater accountability were making IT managers closely scrutinise supplier contracts and request a more detailed breakdown of costs for a whole range of IT services.

Even in the private sector, IT managers said it was now policy to avoid open-ended contracts for IT services, as past experience had shown that loose contracts simply 'run and run'. A salient example of this problem was believed to be the recent collapse of the city-based TAURUS system, where a complex web of clients, suppliers and staff worked to transform a complicated idea into a fully workable information system. The project's collapse was ultimately blamed on poor managerial practice over controlling suppliers and staff, and not one which could easily be written off as a 'technical malfunction' (Currie, 1994a, 1994b).

But against the view that managers should seek to control suppliers, an IT manager at an insurance company said that if a large-scale systems development project was seen as strategic by senior executives, then 'cost can almost become irrelevant', at least in the short term. As such, he said that it becomes important to track costs over time. This is because financial controls are likely to vary according to the strategic interpretation held by senior executives over a specific IT project or service. This company had recently initiated two strategic information systems as a response to business development strategies in the insurance industry. At this company, IT outsourcing was not perceived to increase, because ‘we believe we have the expertise in-house to develop IT at a reasonable cost’. This IT manager was very critical of the whole IT outsourcing bandwagon, as he felt that it was simply ‘a fashion’.

Interviewing an IT manager working for an NHS trust similarly found that time and materials contracts were discouraged in favour of the more stringent fixed-term, fixed-price arrangement. This IT manager said that:

If the outside company fails to deliver the system by the due date, it is their problem not ours. Whilst late delivery may pose some problems for us, the cost is met by the supplier. Penalty clauses may also be built into the contract. Fortunately, our suppliers have so far delivered on time since they want to ensure that we use them in the future.

The selection criteria for IT outsourcing suppliers also elicited some interesting comments from respondents. Many potential clients in all three sectors were reassessing their selection criteria with many developing service level agreements (SLAs) for the first time. One government agency had set up a framework agreement for consultancy services, to ensure that value for money was achieved in conjunction with good-quality service. The framework agreement comprised five areas for assessing consultants. They included:

\- Expertise relating to three areas – Scoping/strategy work Implementation work Operations

\- History

\- Cost

• Management organization

\- Control

The IT manager said it was now corporate policy to advertise for firms to tender for work throughout the European Union. A consequence of this was that the small consultancy would be unable to meet the above criteria. For example, a small software house with only five people in business for only two years might not be able to demonstrate a track record of successful projects, nor a well-resourced management structure. This meant that only large IT suppliers were likely to meet the tendering criteria for government contracts. This was amplified in the comments of one public sector IT manager at a government agency who said that:

If you select a small supplier and it goes wrong, you might get the sack. But if you select company X [a major IT management consultancy] and that contract goes wrong, you can say, 'well I hired the best so it's their fault'. In my experience, nobody got the sack for hiring company X – whatever the cost or outcome.

At this organization, external IT consultants were used for three types of work: scoping/strategy work, activities where IT needs were assessed in conjunction with the overall business strategy; implementation work, which comprised designing and developing information systems; and operations work, where consultants were brought in to handle mainframe operations.

Like other public sector organizations, this manager realised that his own IT division would also be subject to market testing like other service providers (catering, cleaning, recruitment, etc.). His strategy was therefore to retain some 50 ‘expert IT professionals’ from a total of some 230 staff and ‘lose the remaining people around the organization’. He recognised that some IT services could eventually be outsourced, but it was likely that a smaller, leaner IT division would be the outcome, provided it could compete effectively with other IT providers. He contended that market testing and CCT had brought about ‘chaos and confusion’ and most managers of service departments were responding by ‘manipulating the situation to suit their own interests and the interests of their [most valuable] staff’.

In the public sector, it was evident from interviewing IT managers (at the government agency and NHS trust) that pay levels for IT staff (managers, analyst/programmers, database administrators, etc.) were lower than those in the private sector (particularly in financial services). According to the IT manager at the government agency, skills shortages would pose a problem for managers attempting to put in an in-house bid to run IT services. For example, even though pay levels may be lower in the public sector, poor skill levels would mean a failure to meet stringent service levels imposed by the client (in this case public sector management). As such, an external bid with access to highly skilled technical experts working for the IT supplier may be more attractive than an in-house tender.

The questionnaire survey gathered data on managers' perceptions of preferred IT solutions in the three sectors. Figure 3 gives a breakdown of five options available to organizations. These include: the acquisition of off-the-shelf IT products from external IT suppliers, the modification of commercial IT products (customisation), an undertaking to do in-house IT development work, the adoption of parent company solutions, or a mixture of the aforementioned options.

The questionnaire survey data showed that buying off-the-shelf IT solutions was the preferred choice, though one which was not always feasible for organizations. A breakdown of these options by sector showed that buying off-the-shelf was favoured mostly by public sector organizations, followed by manufacturing and financial services companies. The manufacturing and financial services sectors, on the other hand, were more likely to develop their own IT solutions compared with the public sector; and the former was more likely to modify a commercial product than the other two sectors.

Respondents to the questionnaire survey stressed there was more choice in commercial software than ever before and that buying IT solutions off-the-shelf would increase. This was perceived as the most cost-effective solution for private and public sector organizations, as opposed to developing bespoke IT systems which were high risk, expensive and not easily maintained. In summing up this discussion, it is apparent that many opportunities and threats face both private and public sector IT managers in negotiating IT outsourcing deals. Perhaps the most difficult element of IT outsourcing is defining a lucrative contract, especially on the part of the client. The following section synthesises some of the common themes arising from the above discussion.

## Discussion and conclusion

The paper has given an overview of some of the relevant literature on IT outsourcing in conjunction with examining data from our questionnaire survey and case study research. It is apparent that the legal, political and organizational issues of IT outsourcing are more complex than a simple managerial choice between make or buy options: that is, whether it is more cost-effective to purchase IT services from an external IT supplier or retain an in-house IT department. In the public sector, significant political and market changes are currently underway in the form of market testing and CCT. Successful bids by external IT suppliers will therefore lead to contracting out as much as 70 per cent of public sector IT work, although political changes over the next two years may halt the process altogether. Consequently, the future of public sector IT departments is somewhat uncertain. Public sector IT managers responding to the questionnaire survey stressed that the new CCT arrangements were highly complex and not easy to implement. Indeed, blanket rules applying to all public sector IT departments were difficult to enact because the scope and nature of IT work varied across the spectrum. Public sector IT managers were equally concerned that pressures imposed upon them to emulate the behaviour of their private sector counterparts were at odds with the traditional public sector ethos of service to the community. As such, not all public sector IT managers were politically in favour of IT outsourcing.

Follow-up interviews with private and public sector IT managers suggested that greater uncertainty tended to confront the latter group. For, whereas private sector IT managers perceived client–supplier relationships as inevitable, public sector IT managers were confused by market testing and CCT and were concerned that their own IT department might not survive over the medium term (three years).

In both private and public sectors, evidence suggested that IT managers were increasingly looking to negotiate tighter supplier contracts in the form of the fixed-term, fixed-price contract as opposed to the more flexible and high-risk time and materials contracts. While the strategy of using several IT suppliers was an option and one which is supported in the literature (Cross, 1995; Willcocks & Choi, 1995), public sector IT managers were aware that leading IT suppliers were only interested in profitable (high-expenditure) IT outsourcing contracts as opposed to undertaking small-scale IT work. This suggested that, even in cases where 70 per cent of IT work could be contracted out, the large IT supplier would only be interested if the client's annual IT budget was correspondingly large. Thus, small public sector IT departments with budgets of less than £1 million would probably have to request bids from smaller IT suppliers. This scenario would obviously pose questions about the degree to which cost and quality targets could be met from IT outsourcing contracts, as the small public sector IT department would have less bargaining power than its larger counterparts.

One way to ensure service quality from IT outsourcing was by developing service level agreements (SLAs). Our research gave an example where one government agency was attempting to devise a list of preferred suppliers, who were generally large IT suppliers with a track record. But such a move tended to disadvantage the smaller IT supplier who may be unable to compete with its larger rival. Thus an IT software supplier industry ‘shakeout’ was inevitable and something which could benefit the large supplier.

In conclusion, it seems that while IT outsourcing is likely to increase over the next five years (Computer Weekly, 1995), a significant factor underpinning its success for private and public sector organizations will be the skill by which the contract is managed. To the extent that IT outsourcing decisions are simplified by examining only cost and resource imperatives, it seems that the significant challenges will be political and managerial. IT outsourcing for many organizations confronts new territories and the recent high-profile failures of TAURUS, the Wessex Health Authority computer system and the London Ambulance computer system are all examples where IT outsourcing has played a part (Currie, 1995). Against this background, IT managers in the process of evaluating outsourcing as a viable option would do well to look beyond the rhetoric of IT suppliers and instead analyse some of the common threads underpinning IT outsourcing failures. In turn, senior managers who perceive IT outsourcing as a quick-fix panacea should instead treat market testing and CCT as useful exercises and not an end in themselves.
