# Convergence and divergence in information systems and knowledge based systems development methodologies: a case for integrated strategic planning

N.N. MITEV

Information Technology Institute, Salford University, Salford M5 4WT, UK

There is a need to integrate knowledge based systems (KBS) with information systems (IS) technical solutions, which implies that KBS and IS development methodologies should be less isolated from each other. KBS and IS development methodologies are generally examined in terms of their similarities and differences. There is divergence at the feasibility and analysis stages, convergence at the design and coding stages, divergence during testing, convergence at the implementation stage, and divergence during maintenance. There are more similarities than there are differences between IS and KBS methodologies, particularly during the strategic planning stage. It is argued that linking KBS strategic planning to the planning element of an IS methodology will go further towards ensuring that the whole of the business is considered, leading to better integrated IS/KBS solutions. Application selection activities of two well-known KBS methodologies are briefly analysed. We show that these activities, which precede the feasibility study, do not consider the strategic aspects of the use of KBS (and IS in general) in business organizations. It is argued that a KBS strategy should be formulated in relation to the IS strategy and the business strategy, therefore increasing the convergence between IS and KBS methodologies.

## Introduction

Knowledge based systems (KBS) and KBS development methodologies have evolved from small standalone expert systems developed using prototyping methods to larger KBS developed using structured methodologies (Edwards, 1991; Tansley & Hayball, 1993). These improvements were motivated by the notion that the initial failure of expert systems was due to bad engineering and inadequate development methods, and operational KBS have been built in more recent years, many using these structured KBS development methodologies (Andrews, 1989; Department of Trade and Industry, 1992; Land, 1995). However, business and organizational failures have been neglected and it is symptomatic that KBS methodologies do not appear to have any strategic planning component. The lack of business emphasis of KBS development methodologies may be one of the factors responsible for the commercial failure of knowledge based systems, as well as their isolation from other computer-based information systems (IS) developments and methodologies.

## Product-driven KBS and marketing hype

The success of knowledge based systems ultimately comes when the business has real benefit. However, the underlying motive for developing KBS has often been to acquire this new technology, and only then to try to find a problem for it to solve. The implication in much of the literature is that 'first you decide you want to attempt a KBS approach, then you look around for a problem to address' (Edwards, 1991, p. 31), hence putting the cart before the horse. The failure of expert knowledge based systems can be attributed to a series of factors and has been analysed elsewhere (Stevenson, 1989; Coats, 1991; Millett & Powell, 1993; Mitev, 1994). Some of the reasons often invoked are: the immaturity of the technology and the theoretical difficulties in solving complex real-world applications (Gupta & Biegel, 1990); the related exploratory nature of KBS projects which were often academic or R&D initiatives (Edwards, 1991, p. 36); and the technology-push perspective and associated search for projects which serve to promote the technology and KBS specialists' careers (Montgomery, 1991). KBS researchers have addressed some of these causes but have mainly concentrated on solving fundamental technical design issues such as knowledge representation and knowledge engineering techniques.

In parallel, early commercial KBS development was characterised by inflated claims of business benefits (Stevenson, 1989; Melymuka, 1994), a proliferation of commercial expert system shells with a limited number of knowledge representation techniques, the use of prototyping techniques, and the predominance of small standalone systems. The prototyping approach does not offer clear guidelines on how to produce a well-engineered commercial product (Jamieson & Szeto, 1989) and many of the marketing claims were not fulfilled. One reaction to this situation was to argue that the lack of effective methodological approaches was a major contributor to the problems of low output yield, non-use and obsolescence of expert systems (Hilal & Soltan, 1991). In order to develop well-engineered products and increase the efficiency and reliability of expert systems, work on KBS design and development methodologies became a priority in the mid 1980s.

## Improved knowledge engineering and KBS methodologies

Knowledge elicitation, acquisition and modelling were identified as the major ‘bottlenecks’ in expert system development; i.e. the problems around the identification and limited availability of experts, the difficulties in expressing knowledge, the conflicts between multiple experts, etc. Techniques to support the acquisition and modelling of human expertise during the analysis phase, in which a detailed analysis of knowledge requirements is conducted, formed the basis of KADS, one of the earliest KBS development methodologies (Hickman et al., 1989; Schreiber et al., 1993; Tansley & Hayball, 1993). KADS is also a structured methodology similar to conventional information systems development methodologies in that development is separated into the phases of analysis, design, coding and maintenance. The aim of KADS is to ‘offer a set of methods and techniques, encapsulated within a theoretical framework, that will enable competent knowledge engineers and systems analysts to build quality KBS with a degree of confidence that matches conventional systems development’ (KBSC, 1990a).

Existing KBS methodologies may have various origins and orientations, for instance a primary focus on large projects, on users, or on knowledge elicitation; they may use a variety of knowledge acquisition and representation techniques; they may have computer-aided knowledge engineering support tools; and they may have a particular development philosophy, e.g. rapid prototyping, incremental development, evolutionary, one-shot approach, or different life cycle models. Montgomery (1991) compared various KBS methodologies in terms of their origin and suppliers, which fall broadly into two categories:

\- methodologies such as KADS, Knacq and RIME developed by KBS technologists;

\- methodologies such as STAGES, Model/1 and

SUMMIT-Dk [see endnote 1] developed by conventional information systems practitioners.

Whatever their differences, KBS methodologies have noticeably evolved over the last few years and are now fully structured methodologies and cover issues such as project control, project management and logical design (Edwards, 1991; Schreiber et al., 1993; Tansley & Hayball, 1993; Guida & Tasso, 1994), whereas an expert systems methodology was widely regarded as being synonymous with knowledge acquisition techniques in the past (Thomas, 1991a). Many KBS methodologies use notations and techniques similar to those of structured methods such as SSADM (Structured Systems Analysis and Design Method), JSD (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 56) or Information Engineering. More recently it seems that improvements in techniques and methods are being made in order to test and maintain KBS better and to improve their quality. For instance, Klahr (1991) argues that:

Emphasis [in KBS methodologies] should be placed not only on the upfront analysis and design phases, but also on developing quality test plans, on defining verifiable acceptance criteria, on developing easily maintainable systems ... activities that are rarely given the attention they need.

These issues are oriented towards maintenance (Bench-Capon & Coenn, 1992) and engineering problems (Gonzalez & Dankell, 1993), and the same emphasis is present in conventional information systems development. For example, the use of CASE tools in KBS methodologies (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 56) is expected to improve the maintainability and testability of KBS. Other current technical issues addressed by both conventional software engineering and KBS development are life cycles, object-oriented methods, neural network components, quality assurance and standards (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, pp. 470–471).

## Towards an integration of KBS and IS solutions and methodologies

As well as considering the technical issues described above, IS and KBS methodologies also need to address how to integrate KBS and conventional information systems. There is a move away from standalone systems and KBS are being integrated with different types of information systems: 'most expert systems are sold as part of a complete solution, instead of as standalone products' (Melymuka, 1994). There has been work on integrating expert systems and decision support systems for some time (Turban & Watkins, 1986; Doukikis et al., 1989, pp. 18–19), as well as integrating them with other conventional systems (BANKAI Workshop, 1991), and 'AI techniques are now embedded in many unexpected places' (Hedberg, 1995). For example, manufacturing companies use KBS as part of their integrated plant systems. McQuillan and Moore (1995) describe one of Texaco's integrated solutions which includes KBS modules, a database environment, a hypertext environment and a CAD environment. The traditional technical criteria to select a KBS application were initially a problem which requires expert knowledge, judgement and experience, or a repetitive, frequent decision-making task. Additional criteria can also be the existence of online and accessible data necessary to make a decision, the need for management or user support, problems with complex logic, or the need to access corporate databases in a distributed environment. Operational KBS satisfying those criteria have been and are being designed in the context of conventional information systems in many businesses (Land, 1995).

A significant implication of this trend is the necessity of linking KBS methodologies and conventional IS methodologies. This integration of methodologies reflects the integration of systems and should facilitate it. Efforts have already been made to compare IS and KBS methodologies, for instance Edwards (1991, pp. 96, 106–207, 231) who explores how conventional structured methods and life cycle models can be linked to KBS development and proposes a methodology which can serve what he calls 'hybrid' knowledge based/conventional systems. Using conventional methods alongside KBS methods provides 'a single tool able to capture the whole of a complex mixed KBS and non-KBS system' (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 207). Also, learning and using different formal methodologies for systems development is costly and difficult. Klahr (1991) argues that it is easier for organizations to adopt KBS development when KBS and IS methodologies are more compatible.

Compatibility and ‘openness’ of development methodologies can be related to the notion of ‘meta-methodologies’. Flexibility of methodologies is being called for by various IS and KBS authors. There is a myriad of IS development methodologies, techniques and tools which compete for attention and adoption, offering overlapping sets of features. There is no real consensus on the applicability of these methods and there are proponents and critics of each method. Attempts have been made to produce a ‘framework’ that does to IS methodologies what methodologies do to projects, i.e. provide a generalised description of several of them, together with a theory of what makes them more or less successful (Olle et al., 1988). Palvia and Nosek (1993) analysed the actual use of major existing IS techniques and methodologies. They found that half the methods extracted from the literature were used by less than 10 per cent of their respondents, and that a methodology ‘may not be uniformly applicable during the life cycle stages’ (p. 78). The authors formulate a contingency analysis of the applicability of the methods which can support method selection. For Montgomery (1991), a KBS developer, an 'open' philosophy implies that it is possible to add new techniques, and import other methods and tools into a methodology. Thomas (1991a) defines a meta-methodology as one which helps developers modify the methodology to suit their own requirements. Indeed, the trend in IS support tools is also to build in more and more flexibility, and tool vendors are working towards 'meta-tools', or CASE tools programmable to support 'any' method (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 207).

## KBS and IS methodologies and strategy

The priorities in KBS methodologies have therefore evolved from fundamental analysis and design to issues related to testability, maintainability and modifiability, and the use of support tools. The need to integrate technical solutions is also driving a move towards linking IS and KBS methodologies themselves. According to Thomas (1991a) this integration will happen at five levels:

\- the systems themselves – this is already happening, as mentioned above;

\- the teams building the systems – from the existence of hybrid IS/KBS systems, one can presume that this is also happening;

\- the underlying technology which is being used – for instance, the development of more flexible support tools, as referred to above;

\- the methodologies they are using;

\- the strategy which they are implementing.

Having summarised the general context of KBS methodologies corresponding to the first three levels above, this paper now concentrates on the last two levels, the methodologies in use and the strategic aspects. It first generally compares IS and KBS methodologies, paying particular attention to the strategic planning phase. It examines in more detail the application selection of two well-known KBS methodologies, KADS and STAGES [see endnote 2], in order to assess their strategic planning aspects. These particular KBS methodologies were chosen since they represent two extremes in the range of existing KBS methodologies. KADS originates from the KBS tradition and STAGES comes from the IS development tradition. This illustrates different approaches in terms of compatibility with IS strategic planning.

The particular emphasis on strategic planning and KBS is motivated by the view that expert system technology as a whole has not been very successful and that this often related to business planning failures. KBS systems often fail to meet business and organizational needs (e.g. Montgomery, 1991; Thomas, 1991a; Wainwright, 1991; Little, 1993; Millett & Powell, 1993, p. 53). By strategic planning we mean here the business planning phase, prior to the selection of applications which takes place at the beginning of the development life cycle. In general terms, the business planning activities include the examination of the organization's commercial needs, which are then translated (the difficulty of this translation should not be underestimated) into a set of objectives for the outcome of the projects. It is symptomatic that many KBS methodologies do not appear to have a strategic planning component. Even GEMINI (GEMINI, 1991), the CCTA/MOD/DTI expert system development methodology standard – the equivalent of SSADM for information systems – does not have a strategic planning stage or component, as envisaged in this paper. Mockler (1989) contends that there is a need to link the development of KBS systems to the business objectives and Edwards argues that most versions of KBS methodologies are at their weakest in the first (feasibility) phase, and 'provide little or no support for deciding whether it is worth detailed consideration of building a system at all' (Edwards, 1991, p. 107). Often the strategic framework of priorities for further developments and enhancements of business systems does not include any specific knowledge based initiatives (Little, 1993). It is suggested here that this lack of business planning for KBS can be alleviated through an improved linking of IS and KBS methodologies in general, and in particular for their strategic planning activities, which it is argued, converge.

## Convergence and divergence in IS and KBS development

Knowledge engineering can be considered a subset of software engineering: they have similarities and differences, and structured approaches to the two disciplines differ. KBS development may be accommodated in the conventional software development cycle, although it requires more iteration between stages than conventional systems. Killin et al. (1991) and Taylor et al. (1991) argue that a spiral life cycle model is more appropriate since risk assessment and iteration are critical to KBS development and are not well catered for in conventional waterfall life cycle models. However, Tansley & Hayball (1993, p. 190) state that KADS's analysis and design phases are 'readily mappable onto the generic life cycle PRINCE [see endnote 3] offers'. Other similarities and differences can be examined at each stage of the development life cycle and, according to Killin et al. (1991), they follow a pattern of convergence and divergence between IS and KBS stages, as follows. There is:

• divergence at the feasibility stage;

• divergence at the analysis stage;

• convergence at the design stage;

• convergence at the coding stage;

\- divergence during testing and debugging;

• convergence at the implementation stage;

\- divergence during maintenance.

## Feasibility

Killin et al. (1991) assert that KBS feasibility is specific (therefore divergent from IS feasibility) in that identifying technically feasible potential KBS applications necessitates the characterisation of the 'generic task type' that underlies any expertise involved. This may be true if one considers knowledge expertise as the only criterion for identifying a KBS application. However, as mentioned above, additional criteria such as problems with complex logic, particularly in the context of integrated IS/KBS solutions, are now being considered. Also, technical feasibility is accompanied by business and organizational feasibility, which are extremely similar activities in IS and KBS development. They all record information which addresses the business activities, the objectives, the environmental, technical and policy constraints, and the solution details and consequences. It is argued here that, consequently, the feasibility stages in IS and KBS development are more similar and convergent than accounted for by Killin et al. (1991).

## Analysis

Divergence during analysis is due to knowledge engineering techniques being used for KBS analysis, which is very different from the logical analysis carried out in IS methodologies. Knowledge modelling and knowledge acquisition are the most significant differences (Killin et al., 1991). Despite these differences, some KBS specialists are building bridges between the KBS and IS analysis phases. For instance, STAGES experts have added explicit links between systems analysis and KBS analysis (Thomas et al., 1991). KBS developers and methodologists Tansley and Hayball (1993, p. 403) present a 'process analysis' which provides a basis for using KADS expertise analysis methods while conventional methods, such as SSADM, Yourdon, etc. can be used to explore the more conventional system components. KBS developers Davenport et al. (1991) added a logical data model to KADS to be able to relate parts of the KBS 'domain layer' to the surrounding traditional database, in their efforts to integrate KADS with a conventional method, Information Engineering. More theoretical work is being done on relating knowledge models and conventional models, in particular where non-knowledge-based functionality is complex and/or substantial (KADS II, 1990), and on integrating KADS to conventional system development methods such as SSADM (Saward et al., 1993). A major thrust in the field is in methods integration and the aim of Tansley and Hayball's book (1993, see pp. 460–471) is to address how KBS-specific methods can be made to work in conjunction with conventional structured methods. The process of requirements analysis in a KBS development follows the same lines as in an IS development and uses similar techniques. KADS methodologists Tansley and Hayball (1993, p. 403) use data flow diagrams which can be used to describe both the current view of the organization and also the way in which this will change. For all these reasons, it is argued here that the analysis is more convergent than divergent, and certainly more convergent with IS analysis than Killin et al. (1991) contended.

## Design, coding, testing and maintenance

According to Killin et al. (1991), the gap between KBS and IS development narrows during the design stage. In some cases traditional design methods are sufficient. KBS design differs from IS design mainly in the system architecture of a KBS, such as the use of production systems, semantic networks, and frame-based or rule-based systems. For instance, KADS only fully supports the design of the KBS components of the system. To design non-knowledge-based components, conventional design techniques should be used (Tansley & Hayball, 1993). These differences influence the later phases of testing, debugging and maintenance. Generating code for a KBS is almost identical to generating code for IS. Standard testing rules for integrity are carried out as for IS. One major difference is the case of KBS data-driven systems where the virtually infinite combinations of data cannot be tested exhaustively. For the same reason, the effects of changing code are more unpredictable in KBS than IS, hence debugging activities become divergent in KBS and IS development. There is very little difference, and much convergence, in the implementation aspects of IS and KBS. The divergence in maintenance is again due to differences in knowledge modelling and design.

Killin et al. (1991) conclude that there are more, or at least as many, similarities between IS and KBS developments as there are differences. They comment that KBS methodologies have benefited from a transfer of the best practices of IS development into KBS development. This reasoning is pursued further with respect to the strategic planning phase of IS and KBS methodologies in the next section.

## Strategic planning and KBS

In their comparison of KBS and IS methodological phases, Killin et al. (1991) did not include the strategic planning phase and their comparison starts with the feasibility study. This demonstrates that KBS development methodologies are only beginning to consider business and strategic issues as KBS developers gradually adapt to the needs of business organizations. Many authors (Jamieson & Szeto, 1989; Klahr, 1991;

Thomas, 1991a) state that KBS methodologies assume that an application exists and that the justification for its development will be carried out at the feasibility stage:

Very few [KBS] initiatives have really considered the strategic issues facing them, and as a result, a large number of expert systems groups are poorly regarded within their organizations both by potential users in the business and by the practitioners of conventional IS. All of this is entirely avoidable but requires expert systems practitioners to focus on strategic business issues before project issues. The ideal methodology would provide them with the tools they need in order to do this (Thomas, 1991a).

Obviously, mentioning KBS in the IT (information technology) and IS strategy neither guarantees success nor prevents failure. However, experience suggests that users who take the time to determine whether a KBS is appropriate or advantageous in solving a business problem will make better decisions about potential KBS applications at an earlier point (Laufmann et al., 1990). Millett and Powell (1993) also found that more paperwork was carried out in successful organizations, reflecting the greater effort involved in selecting and analysing a real business problem before KBS development started.

In order to develop a KBS application useful to the business, Klahr (1991) recommends a business analysis which covers the understanding of the organization's business units, and the identification and ranking of business problems and opportunities. Examining business operations involves investigating the strategic direction of the business units, business requirements, business processes, information flows, people's responsibilities and activities, the decisions being made, the data used, the planned changes, and the information systems in use. The candidate projects are then ranked according to criteria such as business payoff, corporate importance and strategic value, scope and complexity of the knowledge base, and integration difficulties. Business and functional requirements are then derived.

The way in which business and strategic analysis is carried out, prior to the selection of a KBS application, has a great deal of commonality (or convergence) with any IS strategic planning exercise. Divergence occurs further on, when applications are ranked and requirements established, leading to more divergent feasibility activities.

The remainder of this paper very briefly summarises the activities of application selection of two KBS methodologies. We show that these activities, which precede the feasibility study, do not consider the strategic aspects of the use of KBS (and IS in general) in business organizations. A KBS strategy should be formulated in relation to the IS strategy and the business strategy. As Millett and Powell (1993) propound, a corporate IT/IS strategy should include the use of KBS and be linked to a business strategy. It is argued here that the KBS strategy should be linked to the IS strategy. The reasons put forward are:

\- most KBS development methodologies do not yet possess a strategic planning component;

\- there is much convergence already between KBS and IS methodologies as illustrated in the previous section;

\- bridges are being built across the divergent stages, particularly analysis, as discussed above;

\- linking and integrating IS and KBS systems and consequently development methodologies is becoming a necessity.

## Application selection in KADS and STAGES

KADS and STAGES are typical examples of two KBS methodologies originating respectively from the artificial intelligence development tradition and from the IS development tradition. KADS is a well-tested methodology with both academic and commercial strengths. It has a unique approach to implementation-independent knowledge models and has a sophisticated analysis stage which entails the understanding of difficult concepts. STAGES, although less well-tested and less widely used than KADS, is easier to understand and is more readily compatible with traditional information systems methodologies. It is based on extending the principles of conventional software engineering methodologies, rather than inventing a strictly KBS-oriented approach. It has very strong links with Ernst & Young's IS methodology Navigator Systems Series and its support tools, which are closely related to the Information Engineering application development workbench. Navigator's latest release also covers the full life cycle of business process re-engineering (Caldwell, 1995), therefore emphasising a global business approach to systems development. Both methodologies support the whole KBS project, from selection of an appropriate application through to delivery and maintenance of an operational system [see endnote 4].

In both KADS and STAGES the application selection phase precedes a full technical and financial feasibility study, which in KADS, for instance, provides an overall view of the cost and time necessary for the prospective development. The results of the feasibility study are used in the subsequent phase, the analysis, which includes the analysis of system requirements, organizational requirements and project requirements.

In both cases, some suitable business areas are presumed to exist. Identifying an application proceeds through defining a problem, describing a domain knowledge, identifying the expert/skill base, establishing which function(s) the potential KBS could perform, and appreciating what is the environment and who the users will be. Application identification is followed by an assessment which leads to a ranking of the candidates. Technical and organizational criteria such as the background and terms of reference, the personnel and operational constraints, the technicalities of the knowledge domain and expert task are used.

STAGES is more concerned with identifying possible areas of benefit to the business where expert systems might be applied and whether they would be the right solution. In contrast, the approach in KADS is to ensure that all possible applications are examined in the light of their KBS potential; the ones which provide cost-effective knowledge based solutions are selected. Concentrating on STAGES, the identification and selection process is divided into the following steps.

## (1) Identification

This consists of gathering background information in order to understand the organization and identify potential expert system applications:

(a) In order to understand the organization, it first uses business planning techniques such as interviews with senior management to analyse

• the business mission;

• the organizational strategy;

• the organizational structure;

● the business goals and objectives;

• the critical success factors.

IS planning techniques are then used

\- organizational and functional decomposition diagrams;

\- analysing key decisions and information requirements;

• analysing key data flows;

\- analysing existing IT/IS strategy and infrastructure;

\- strategic value and impact analyses, which are matrices showing the contribution of various processes to the business objectives.

(b) Having picked key business areas and decisions, it uses techniques such as noting expertise contribution and knowledge intensity to generate candidate expert systems applications. To evaluate the knowledge intensity in business areas, questions are asked, for instance, about the difficulties in retaining skilled staff, their training, and the existence of complex written guidelines. Each function is rated on its contribution to business goals and its knowledge intensity. Classification grids are used to classify business decisions for expert system identification.

## (2) Apply criteria

The potential candidate applications are then screened using ten KBS criteria. Some of these criteria are: whether the expertise related to the domain is volatile;

whether the solution of problems in the domain is based on domain-specific expertise rather than common sense knowledge; and whether the problems of a domain are recurrent business problems.

## (3) Scaled-down feasibility study

Having ranked likely KBS applications, a scaled-down feasibility study is carried out which covers:

\- development costs;

\- risk analysis;

\- tangible and intangible benefits;

• corporate benefits;

• time and organizational constraints.

Finally a business case (costs, benefits, risks) is prepared for each application selected.

In STAGES, expert systems are seen as being part of an overall IS strategy. Background information on the business mission and strategy, critical success factors, etc. is taken into account. IS planning techniques (adapted from Navigator) such as an impact analysis are used to identify opportunities. A strategic value analysis is carried out to study and extend the functional and organizational decomposition diagrams to focus efforts on parts of the organization that have the greatest use of knowledge. Classification grids are used to appraise the degree of benefit to the organization of building an expert system to support that area or function. More so than KADS, STAGES attempts to build a global high-level view to determine which areas offer the greatest benefit to the business; its use of IS strategic planning techniques reflects the convergence of IS and KBS strategic planning. In both KADS and STAGES, the KBS planning element runs on its own, however. An awareness of the specificity of KBS is obviously important. It is clear that the types of business problems solved by expert systems are different from conventional systems, and that the nature of the business contribution they make is also different.

Nevertheless, it is argued here that linking KBS planning to business planning and IS planning will go further to ensure that the whole of the business is considered and that a broad information model is constructed in which KBS will be included. This will support the detection of problem areas which may not have seemed KBS-related initially, leading to the realisation of integrated IS/KBS solutions.

## Discussion

## Business planning methodologies

Some KBS developers (Edwards, 1991; Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 456) recommend that a focus on business issues could be gained from applying 'soft' planning methods such as operational research or Checkland's soft systems methodology. Other in-house or published methodologies which enable business objectives to be clarified and made explicit can also be applied to pre-analysis activities in KBS and IS systems development. The diagnosis of the commercial needs and of the types of systems called for is not straightforward and the pre-analysis activities vary greatly between organizations. 'Soft' planning methodologies help consider both the organization's commercial needs and the project needs, and help concentrate on the links between those two levels. It is possible to use an in-house business and IS planning methodology and link KBS components to their strategic planning elements (Deloitte & Touche, 1991; Mitev, 1991) to ensure KBS and IS fulfil business needs. A wider approach, i.e. a complete redesign of the organization's information systems to include KBS components, is suggested by Beerel (1987). Whichever business planning methodology is chosen and in whatever way it is used, adapted, borrowed or integrated, it is clear that:

we should not regard KBSs as a field in isolation but one which must be integrated with other problem solving approaches such as conventional software engineering, operational research and 'soft' systems analysis ... KBS must be considered as another competing solution technology which implies being very clear about the business objectives which the solution systems are supposed to address (Edwards, 1991, pp. 232–233)

Although a strategy does not guarantee success, an organization is unlikely to achieve it without one. Millett and Powell (1993, pp. 52, 54) found that successful organizations put a greater effort in selecting and analysing a real business problem before development starts and that if there is a corporate IT/IS strategy, which includes the use of KBS, linked to a business strategy, then many barriers may be removed. Relative business priorities to a range of systems development opportunities must be assigned (Little, 1993, p. 445).

However, business and IS planning methodologies are not a panacea. Business Systems Planning, Information Engineering and other IS planning methodologies are problematic in terms of resources, process and output. Furthermore, they only align IS with business goals (which is difficult enough) and do not include the impact of IT on organizational strategies (Bergeron et al., 1991). For some writers, strategic planning should also examine the possible use of IT to gain a competitive advantage by, for instance, gathering data on the organization's strategic aims, its product-market mix, suppliers, customers, market strengths and weaknesses, opportunities, and environmental and competitors' threats. Mockler (1989, pp. 14–15) designed an expert system to support this data gathering and help planners and information managers explore areas where IT might help a company gain a competitive advantage, although the author acknowledges that not all companies are faced with industry situations where IT can give them a competitive advantage (Mockler, 1989, p. 254).

Not much is known about the process by which opportunities for the use of strategic information systems (SIS) are identified. While various SIS planning (or SISP) methodologies have been proposed, Bergeron et al. (1991, p. 89) state there is a paucity of information on empirical results obtained from applying them. These authors applied two SISP methodologies (Porter's and Wiseman's) empirically and found that both were effective in generating a significant number of ideas for IS worthy of implementation (Bergeron et al., 1991). However, Lederer and Sethi (1992) argue that SISP practices are badly structured and too demanding and that there are many SISP pitfalls, e.g. organizational, contractual, technical and implementation problems, and issues of commitment, expertise, expectations and outcomes.

Planning is a social activity, which therefore exhibits limited rationality. Caught between incrementalism and rational comprehensive planning, planners have tried to improve the inflexibility-generating aspects of planning methodologies (Rosenhead, 1980). If a methodology is to be more than a hypothesis it should be tested by experience on real projects. A good example is Tansley and Hayball's book (1993) which is based on their real-world experience of developing KBS to industrial and commercial standards using the KADS methodology in practice. However, many projects use a hybrid merger of two or more methodologies so it is difficult to test and compare different methodologies. In this context, evaluating the effectiveness of IS and KBS strategic planning is undoubtedly a very ambitious research aim. As companies re-engineer their business processes, many are finding that artificial intelligence and KBS can successfully help streamline business practices (Hedberg, 1995). It is therefore even more important to understand the uncertainty and complexity of organizational contexts (Little, 1991) before designing systems.

## KBS and IS integration

The comparison of KBS solutions with those produced by other types of approach is an under-developed area. More experience of the long-term operation of KBS is needed before any general lessons can be drawn. Although there is a shortage of comparative studies, there is nevertheless a large body of work on the suitability of KBS looked at in isolation from other systems (Edwards, 1991, p. 235). Deciding which parts of the system should be built as KBS components and which as conventional components is an important area for further research. This seems very relevant in the context of re-engineered streamlined business processes, which need to be supported by integrated systems solutions.

Since knowledge systems are a special type of information systems, and knowledge engineering a subset of software engineering, it could be argued that IS methodologies should form the 'master plan' of integrated IS/KBS methodologies, and KBS-specific 'branches' should be attached later onto the IS 'tree', for instance during analysis, testing and maintenance, as they are the stages which are the most divergent. Several approaches are possible and work has been done on the integration of KADS II and SSADM (Saward et al., 1993). Ways of interworking with conventional software methods, including formal techniques and modelling languages, need to be defined to facilitate the production of industrial-quality KBS software tools (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 476). The GEMINI standard framework for expert systems development (Scarff, 1991) is intended to be compatible with SSADM and ideally PRINCE. Some of its features are a risk-driven spiral life cycle model, project control guidelines and detailed definitions of deliverables (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 477).

Another topical research question touched upon in this paper is to make methodologies in general more open, flexible, capable of catering for various alternatives according to the needs, experiences and attitudes of the business and technical experts involved. It was hoped to show in this paper that bridges can be built across IS and KBS methodologies during the business planning stages. KADS has been made more flexible for systems with small or large KBS elements; it has 'hooks' for allowing the application of non-KBS development methods in certain areas (Tansley & Hayball, 1993, p. 469). Further comparisons at various stages of different methodologies, such as the comparison suggested by Bergeron et al. (1991) between traditional alignment planning methodologies such as Business Systems Planning and SISP methodologies, should lead to a contingency approach in which a methodology would be chosen on the basis of its congruence with factors such as the level of uncertainty in the environment, the strategy and the management of information resources.

## Conclusion

Since the initial hype about expert systems in the early 1980s, KBS systems and KBS methodologies have evolved from standalone product-driven expert systems using prototyping development methods, to systems developed using fully structured methodologies. Although considered a dead market because of the failure of early designers to provide promised results, KBS are used in a variety of roles and as parts of integrated solutions which include, for example, KBS modules, databases, hypertext and CAD environments. KBS specialists are addressing technical issues such as object-oriented methods, neural networks and quality assurance, which are also IS design issues. In this context of integration of KBS and IS solutions, it is important to compare KBS and IS methodologies and identify their similarities and differences. Each stage of the development life cycle was examined in terms of divergence and convergence between IS and KBS analysis and design. Despite some fundamental differences during the analysis, testing and maintenance stages, we conclude that there are more similarities than differences between IS and KBS developments. It is argued that development methods should be better linked and integrated, and one area which could benefit from such an integration is strategic planning. This activity is of particular importance since it has been found that many KBS failures are essentially due to business planning failures.

The pre-analysis activities of two KBS methodologies, KADS (a methodology created by KBS specialists) and STAGES (a methodology which comes from an IS development tradition), were assessed. It was found that in both cases some suitable business areas are presumed to exist. There is a small difference between the two methodologies in that KADS is chiefly concerned with ensuring that all business areas are examined in the light of their KBS potential, whereas STAGES is more concerned with areas of benefit to the business.

This is because STAGES comes from the IS tradition where the strategic planning phase of systems development is more developed. IS methodologies precede KBS methodologies which have often borrowed and adapted IS development methods and techniques. Similarly, IS methodologies have borrowed pre-existing business planning techniques from the fields of business policy and strategic management. Application selection in STAGES uses business planning and IS planning techniques, displaying a stronger focus on business issues.

When compared with IS methodologies, KBS methodologies are only beginning to consider business and strategic issues. The way in which business and strategic analysis should be carried out, prior to the selection of a KBS application and its feasibility analysis, has a great deal of commonality with an IS business planning exercise. A corporate IT/IS strategy should include the use of KBS and be linked to a business strategy, therefore increasing the convergence between IS and KBS design and development methodologies.
