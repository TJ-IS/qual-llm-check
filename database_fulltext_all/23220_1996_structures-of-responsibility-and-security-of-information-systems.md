# Structures of responsibility and security of information systems

J Backhouse $^{1}$ and G Dhillon $^{2}$

$^{1}$ London School of Economics and Political Science, CSRC Houghton Street, London, WC2A 2AE; and $^{2}$ Cranfield School of Management, ISRC Cranfield, Bedford, MK43 OAL, UK

Our research suggests an improved theoretical and conceptual foundation for analysing information systems security. We argue that an analysis of structures of responsibility in organizations leads to the development of secure information systems. The paper surveys the existing approaches to security and proposes an alternative perspective for viewing organizational and security issues.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) security has become an issue commanding ever more attention from the various professions attending to the needs of computer using organizations, be these accountants, auditors, lawyers, computer managers or business managers. Widespread dependence upon computer systems has brought about this shift in interest for obvious practical reasons. The quest for principles which can be systematically applied to problems in this area has opened the subject to the attentions of the academic researcher. Increasing numbers of higher education institutions in the UK and abroad are including the subject in their agendas for education, training and research.

Our research into IS security suggests a distinctive theoretical and conceptual stance, in contrast to that found in much of the work on the subject. The general approach, in our perspective, is to view organizations as embodiments of structures of responsibility, characterised by groups of responsible agents charged with specific responsibilities over the substantive actions and tasks of the business. The approach taken in this paper is an interpretive one and introduces a framework for security evaluation based on ontological interpretation. The discussion is divided into five sections. After the introduction, the second section critically surveys the current approaches to IS security: checklists, risk analysis and security evaluation. The third section develops an alternative conceptual framework for understanding information security issues based upon the technique of modelling the underlying patterns of behaviour which characterise the domain of action. The modelling highlights the actions that must be accomplished and draws attention to the agents who are responsible. A further benefit is in how the approach then draws the analyst through an examination of the norms that the responsible agents follow. The fourth section sketches how the alternative approach can be used. Finally the concluding section reaffirms the research emphasis.

## The current scenario

Information security research can broadly be classified into three categories. First is the category characterised by extensive checklists, such as the Checklist for Computer Centre Self-Audits (AFIPS, 1979) and IBM's 88 point security assessment questionnaire. The checklists monitor the establishment of controls which are identified for every conceivable threat. Second is the group of approaches which use risk analysis to identify the necessary controls. Third is a set of approaches which use security evaluation methods. The discussion in this section focuses on the latter two categories only. The checklist approaches, which are excluded, although still widely used carry less conviction when searching for theoretical foundations in security. They indicate where exclusive attention has been given just to the observable events without considering the social nature of the problems. Checklists inevitably draw concern onto the detail of procedure without addressing the key task of understanding what the substantive questions are. Procedures are constantly changing and for this reason offer little in the way of analytical stability.

Risk analysis has become the flagship of modern security management. On this basis it has been possible to cost-justify new IS security. It has also been possible to avoid the implementation of unnecessary and expensive controls. Risk analysis techniques provide a means of forecasting critically the financial benefits vis-à-vis the initial investments. Such management science principles laid the foundation for techniques that were proposed by researchers such as Courtney (1977) and Wong (1977). The utility of risk analysis approaches has prompted many researchers to identify the necessary controls that are needed to build a secure IS. Birch and McEvoy (1992) for example developed the structured risk analysis method (SRA). They were able to demonstrate how SRA can be used to perform security and safety oriented risk analysis in specific environments. Recent years have also seen the emergence of risk analysis methodologies, such as CRAMM (CCTA Risk Analysis and Management Methodology). It is used to conduct risk analysis and other related management reviews.

The opportunities offered by risk analysis have also been a subject of interest to researchers. Merten (1982) looks at the technique from a managerial perspective while Boockholdt (1987) cites its importance in establishing security and integrity controls. Saltmarsh and Browne (1983) and Gallegos et al (1987) differentiate between risk analysis and risk assessment. The former being the process of identification while the latter determines the degree of exposure. Based on this differentiation, they comment on the usefulness of risk analysis in establishing monetary value of the risks. Baskerville (1991), however, takes a different view. He appreciates the utility of the technique in establishing the feasibility of IS controls but he feels that its predictiveness is of less value and its real usefulness lies in it being an effective communication tool, especially between security and management professionals.

Risk analysis has had an influence on a number of other approaches. Notable among the earlier work is Parker's program (Parker, 1981) and Fisher's methodology (Fisher, 1984). Both approaches use risk analysis as a means to design controls. However, Parker introduces the use of a different kind of an analysis, the 'exposure analysis', which he feels eliminates the elements of guesswork and consensus determination. He also proposes an alternative threat model. Loch et al (1992) have gone further to develop a four-dimensional model of IS security which focuses on threat identification. Warman (1993) also considers the various threat categories but gives primacy to the social aspects in establishing security.

Risk analysis prompted yet another stream of thought: that behaviour of people is a major and central factor in security and should be the first factor to receive attention (Lane, 1985). Lane proposes that in an organization staff with special responsibility should be designated. This he feels is an effective way of reducing risks in computer based systems. He also proposes the division of responsibility and the division of knowledge about the system amongst many personnel. Lane's concepts show a slight departure from the underlying principles of other approaches but he has been unable to show how his ‘psychological model of human action’ can perceive ‘social causality’.

Baskerville (1988) adopts a somewhat different approach. He attempts to minimise the importance attributed to risk analysis. This he does by embedding controls in the logical model of an IS. Baskerville feels that the 'best approach to the development of security design and development methodology would be to nest it with an existing, established, successful overall information systems analysis and design methodology'. He suggests that a structured security analysis and design can be carried out in much the same way as a structured systems analysis. He chooses De Marco's (1978) structured systems analysis and specification approach and implements controls in its logical design phase. Control identification is carried out by developing formal heuristics. While starting from a different set of assumptions, at a functional level Baskerville's approach is not very dissimilar to the others.

Criticism of the use of risk analysis as a basis for developing secure systems has always been strong. Clements (1977) regarded classical probability theory to be inappropriate in assessing the security risks because threats are invariably random in nature. He proposed a methodology, based on the theory of fuzzy sets, for evaluation of data processing installations. More recently researchers have critically analysed the technique of risk analysis (Willcocks & Margetts, 1994; Beck, 1992; Baskerville, 1991). They believe that over reliance on risk analysis as a technique in the design of secure IS has negative consequences and benefits should not be found in using the technique for predictive modelling.

## Security evaluation methods

Another category of research in computer security is in security evaluation methods. This stems from the need to measure security (Longley, 1991). Though it is often difficult to place a value on the level of security, a number of techniques exist which help in grading the security of systems. Early work on establishing such levels of assurance was sponsored by the US Department of Defence. The emphasis then was to prevent ‘unauthorised disclosure of information’. Among the various models of secure systems, the Bell La Padula Model (Bell & Padula, 1976) was the most prominent. The model deals with mandatory and discretionary access control with the primary objective of preventing illegal disclosure of information.

In 1983 the National Computer Security Centre in the USA published the Trusted Computer Systems Evaluation Criteria, targeted at Automatic Data Processing systems. These provided computer vendors with an evaluation procedure to develop trusted computer systems. Today these criteria form an integral part of the US Department of Defence security procedures. Recently research has been carried out to improve and supplement these evaluation criteria. Chokhani (1992), for example, expands upon these criteria and proposes an information security (INFOSEC) approach to such an evaluation.

Hoffman et al (1978) adopted a different basis for security evaluation. They proposed an automated tool SECURATE, which exists in the form of a design and a selection process. The system automates the security analysis process and provides detailed ratings of a system security profile. SECURATE calculates the security ratings on the basis of fuzzy set theory and ultimately outlines the strengths and weaknesses in system design. Critics have however argued the statistical validity of fuzzy metrics.

Besides the US, evaluation criteria have been established in other countries as well. In the UK, for example, the Department of Trade and Industry and the government communications headquarters produced a series of 'Green Books'. These were specifically intended for the commercial computer security centre. Germany and France have also been quite active in this area. The German Information Security Agency (GISA) have produced a 'Criteria for the Evaluation of the Trustworthiness of Information Technology Systems' while the French Service Central de la Securité des Systemes d'Information issued a 'Catalogue de Critères Destinés a evaluer le Degré de Confiance des Systèmes d'Information'.

In an attempt to harmonise the work on information security standards in Europe, France, Germany, the Netherlands and the UK decided to combine the best features of each of the national initiatives. As a consequence, in May 1990, the first draft of the Information Technology Security Evaluation Criteria was issued. The text is being referred to as the 'White Book'. Evaluation criteria, while having found public approval, still fail to provide answers to many important questions and are unacceptable to a body of researchers in the area (eg McLeen, 1990).

The research carried out in the past decade or so has indeed enriched the field of IS security. It has been possible now to implement legislative measures, especially in relation to a variety of security and privacy related issues (Turn, 1982; Bequai, 1987). These have also helped to some extent in implementing operational security so it is now possible to establish management control by setting objectives and guidelines for accountability, surveillance, and authority (Hsiao et al, 1979; Weber, 1982; Norman, 1983). Threats and risks can also be identified with a reasonable amount of precision. Users have greater access to computer based IS than previously and, as a consequence, identification and authentication methods have been well researched. However, the focus of attention has shifted and in particular database access control has received much attention (Highland, 1985). Database access control mechanisms and means often have a direct bearing on legislation.

This has led to relating access control issues to those of privacy (Adam & Wortmann, 1989).

New research directions consider security as an organizational issue which can be understood in terms of roles (of people), actions, goals and policies (Dobson, 1990). Furthermore security issues are also being considered as problems of communication breakdown (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1995). These research directions stem from the understanding that organizations are communication systems. Increasingly researchers have started considering organizations as social forms with patterned, ritualised and conventionalised interactions (Manning, 1992).

## A conceptual framework for security

As researchers, we are faced with a major question: what sort of conceptual approach do we need in security research? The answer is indeed not an easy one. Growing interest in security demands a broader perspective but concrete alternatives have not been provided. Walsham (1991) uses Morgan's (1986) approach in indicating that problems are to be viewed in a multidimensional manner. What is needed is a theory which can support the multiple images of a problem and at the same time brings in consistency.

It is likely that any criticism of the existing techniques will itself be subject to severe counter-criticism, but it is not our intention here to enter into such debates and discussions. What is proposed is an alternative means for evaluating security within the context of an organization: a framework which views organizations in terms of their stable underlying patterns of behaviour. By doing this, it is possible to model the interactions that are necessary to achieve co-ordinated collective action. Because security problems often arise where the communication necessary for co-ordinated action breaks down, our ability to model allows us to understand the communication aspects of security. Furthermore, we can place the essentially technical problems into their social and organizational context. Research into security is often concerned with technical issues such as systems architecture, hardware performance, databases and software design. Instead this approach allows us to integrate these issues with considerations of norms, purpose, and interpretation of information as regards the security of organizations.

In analysing organizations as patterns of behaviour, one very important aspect of social causality becomes evident: that is responsibility. In the literature, it has been mentioned several times in relation to IS, computer security and organizations in general (eg Lane, 1985; Buchanan & Linowes, 1980; Withington, 1980; Shaver, 1985; Liebenau & Backhouse, 1990). It is also the endeavour of the present framework to analyse the various aspects of the eliciting and assigning of structures of responsibility. These structures provide a means to understand the manner in which responsible agents are identified; the formal and informal environments in which they exist; the influences they are subjected to; the range of conduct open to them; the manner in which they signify the occurrence of events; the communications they enter into and above all the underlying patterns of behaviour.

The ontological-epistemological approach we use is based on understanding social norms and individual affordances, that is patterns of behaviour at social and individual levels (Stamper et al, 1988). The framework assumes reality to be the outcome of human interactions which generate shared norms and experiences. Through the responsible agents identified we can relate the norms, patterns of behaviour, and experiences to their referents, which are actions effected in the real world. The framework can, in part, be shown as a semantic schema.

In preparing a schema, it is important to identify the agents who determine what takes place, and what behaviour is realised. Every agent in the organization under consideration has a determinate range of possible conduct, which aggregates to the behaviours that are afforded by that environment. Taking an example from the domain of secure computing, the manner in which the schema is prepared can be demonstrated. It is important to identify the agents, those who can take responsibility for their actions and would generally be associated with purposeful behaviour, for example providing access to a PC and subsequently access to the network. They are thought of as realising the affordances and capable of taking responsibility of their actions.

Agents are associated with communication acts (Searle, 1987) which serve to change the social world, which in turn constitutes the world of interrelated obligations. These communication acts can take the form of 'performatives'. The traditional view of such performatives was linked with their truth-functionality. Austin (1962), however views them in terms of performance of a certain act. In the information security domain the authorising of access to a PC by a particular individual and of access to a storage device by a PC are examples of such 'performatives'. Such an exercise helps in identifying those parts of communication which govern the world of interrelated obligations. This presents the domain as a social and physical world affording certain mechanisms for behaviour (Gibson, 1977). Having defined the enterprise in terms of patterns, of actions, of behaviours and of responsible agents, a semantic schema is prepared by arranging them in a sequence of existence dependency $^{1}$ (Backhouse, 1991). Such dependency forms the fundamental principle in developing a semantic schema showing ontological dependencies and its representation takes the form of an ontology chart.

An ontology chart represents the invariants in any domain as patterns of behaviour to be realised by agents acting therein. Those invariants on the right of the chart can only be realised when those on their left have been realised, hence the name ontology, from the principle of ontology or existence dependency. The chart is a way of modelling what behaviour can be realised in any domain, but where the restrictions are only existential and not given by rules or conventions. Each invariant pattern is shown as a node in the chart and the analysis task is to elicit for each node the responsible agents and the norms used by the organization in practice when the patterns of actions represented are actually instantiated. For example the ‘position’ of a hospital registrar is a node and one of the actions is the treatment rendered to the ‘patient’, another node on the chart. The act of treatment takes the form of a ‘performative’.

Where a node has two antecedents, then both these must be realised if the invariant is to be realised: both the person and the PC must exist if access from the first to the second is to exist. The chart is used to provide a very stable model for analysing the domain, since it contains little that will change over time. It is a useful platform from which we can study the norms and structure of an organisation.

## Using the conceptual framework

In illustrating the usefulness of the ideas sketched above, we will apply them to a case study that was done for the British National Health Service Hospital Trust. The Hospital Trust in question specialises in providing services to people with learning disabilities. Its administrative functions are supported by an integrated computer-based IS. The system holds information of varying kinds: patient case histories, medication reports, pharmacy details, etc. This information is highly sensitive and personal. It is important that a high level of security be maintained. It would be very embarrassing to the organization if personal information about patients gets into the wrong hands $^{2}$ . However, the task of maintaining a secure environment is not straightforward. There are problems with various power groups in the Hospital Trust. The most obvious conflicts are between the doctors, nurses and the managers. The uncertainty created by governmental regulations imposes another dimension. In such a turbulent environment it is extremely difficult to negotiate access to relevant data, establish accountabilities and identify structures of responsibility. The following paragraphs explain this in greater detail by using the example that appears in the appendix.

## Preparing a semantic schema for the Hospital Trust domain

In order to identify the structures of responsibility, we ought to recognise the patterns of behaviour that might be associated with the general area of data security. Our representation should not refer to any specific rules or procedures employed by an organization but rather sketches the generic affordances that constrain any agent in this domain. Every organization that addresses the question of security of data will be engaged, in part at any rate, in realising these patterns of behaviour. A providential organization will have careful policies designed to deal actively with these matters in a way that contributes to the overall confidentiality, integrity and availability of equipment, programs and data. Others will be less formal undertakings where these matters are not driven by rules and policies, but where instead decisions are taken by individuals in an informal manner. There may be norms governing the domain; these could be strong or weak. In a context where the norms are strong, the conduct of an agent will be constrained informally yet effectively. In such situations the norms may coincide with any rules that apply.

In our example the agents capable of taking responsibility are: Hospital Trust and Person. These agents realise affordances through their roles. For instance, the affordance of treating a patient can be realised through the role of a ‘Registrar’ (a junior doctor). In mapping the patterns of behaviour in our example, we are required to place the agents and their affordances in an ontological existence-dependence manner (Figure 1).

At this stage we must identify the norms associated with the agents. The ‘Hospital Trust’ which realises its behaviour through the roles of a ‘Registrar’ and the ‘Consultant’ doctor must take responsibility for part of these positions. In our example, though the Consultant is responsible for the patient Joanne Lee (see Appendix), it is a norm that the Registrar will be looking after the day-to-day affairs. This is a typical example of a ‘cognitive norm’ characterised by standardised beliefs and knowledge within a group. Identifying such norms is extremely important since they play an important role in negotiating access into personal files (of Joanne in this case). It must be noted that though the ‘Registrar’ may not have formal clearance to access certain files, nevertheless the prevalent norm system in the organization determines the privilege level. This has severe implications for security since a rigid rule based structure of the formal system (the computer-based IS) is imposed onto a predominantly informal norm based environment. This is even more significant in an organization which is characterised by distinct groupings each with a very strong sub-culture (the doctors, managers and the nurses in this case). In our example the prevalence of cognitive norms, in the consultant–registrar relationship, deters the growth of any rational knowledge thereby confining our thinking in an orthodoxy. This makes the group even more cohesive and it becomes difficult to subject such norms to any criticism.

Thus the identification of agents, their roles and the associated communication acts help us to understand and evaluate the environment better. Furthermore, we are able to identify precisely the structures of responsibility and authority. The technique also implicitly creates a place for the agents, or the responsible people at each node who decide: who has access to a PC, which PCs have access to what data, which PCs are sited in which rooms, and so on (see Figure 1). In doing so we are able to elucidate the ‘performatives’ as well, thereby allowing us to comment on the interrelated obligations of the agents (eg performative in our example is ‘accepts’).

The responsible agents are necessary elements in the conception of a node and form the answer to the question: 'who is responsible in the domain for determining the existence of any instance of actual behaviour?' This breaks down into two sub concerns: who decides the start of any realisation and who the finish? Agents can be individuals or groups, such as committees or teams which have the collective responsibility. At this point we can envisage a structure where the same underlying frame as in Figure 1 is used, but at the nodes are the names of the agents who are responsible for the substantive actions depicted. Such a structure of responsibility is arrived at by starting with the patterns of substantive behaviour associated with organizational computer systems, and not with the associated procedures. It is therefore much more robust.

In most cases the responsible agents will make their decisions in the line with prevailing norms, rather than arbitrarily. The greater part of the security task is to ensure that these norms reflect the practices espoused by the careful organization; and in turn that its practices conform to those of various over arching jurisdictions – statutory, professional (codes of practice) and standards, such as British Standards and those of the International Standards Organisation.

## Interpreting IS security

The framework introduced above treats security issues in terms of a structure of patterns of action, and sketches the secure information domain schematically. These patterns determine the various repertoires of behaviour, which are grouped in an ontological order. The whole ontological structure is governed by the agents following norms.

The pattern so developed rests explicitly on the notion of persons responsible for a set of actions. The manner in which these patterns can be realised or implemented signifies the changes in job allocation and the prevalence of different norms. It is useful to compare this responsible structure against the explicit security management structure of an enterprise. This comparison is often between the formal and the informal systems. The understanding gained from the approach outlined in this paper is gained from looking at the substantive actions required of members of the organization in the course of using the computer systems in place, bottom up if you will, whereas the security management structure looks from the top down.

The security functions of most organizations have formal mechanisms for designing and maintaining secure systems. Such approaches may suffice in cases where the norms are very strong, and it is relatively easy to identify responsible agents in a conventional manner. However, if the norms are not strong and the environment is informal, it can be quite difficult to attribute responsibility and identify key decision makers.

In a given organization a rule may exist that when a person takes on a position in an executive grade, he is given access to a PC which in turn has access to the network. Implicit in this situation, though at a very basic level, two security procedures are revealed by the analysis as instrumental: first, the 'start' and 'finish' of an incumbency, that is the identification of a responsible person who decides when a person fills the position and when the appointment to be terminated; second, the 'start' and 'finish' of access to a PC, that is who decides to authorise access and who actually gives access, a situation of shared responsibility. This situation clearly prevails in the Hospital Trust where the ‘Registrars’ are not core members of the organization since they are attached to a hospital as part of their job-training or rotation. Yet they are members of a particular group in the organization (the doctors) because of a common belief and a norm system. In such situations it is even more important to determine the ‘starts’ and ‘finishes’ of an incumbency and that of access.

Thus in identifying the responsible agents and capturing the norms associated with each action, we are in a position to understand the underlying repertoires of behaviour. By looking at the informal environment, the semantic schema is able to capture the structures in their cultural context. This enables the analyst to understand the object system better. In managing and developing IS security in an organization, such an approach can aid in illuminating concepts such as attribution of blame, responsibility, accountability and authority. The current approach also helps in the interpretation of sign functions which play an important role in the decision making process. Such an interpretive approach is useful in establishing base-line standards for security products and practices. It also helps in establishing the trigger points, knowledge of which is so essential during a security lapse. The most important use however is in developing an understanding of an organization including its informal, underlying cultural infrastructure. This is critical in an organization where security policies remain marooned in the paper medium in which they are articulated, gathering dust on the shelf, because secure behaviour has not entered into the norm and patterns of behaviour which characterise de facto practice, as opposed to the de jure rules and procedures.

## Conclusion

Responsibility is therefore a central research theme of this work. Responsibility analysis is a technique which has been developed as a tool for use in checking the structures of responsibility that exist in an organization and in deepening management understanding of how it functions on the ground, and not merely on paper. The analysis reveals the essential patterns of behaviour that characterise the organization and represents these as a schema. From unearthing the patterns, the focus moves to identifying the persons responsible for instantiation, and examines the norms they use when, for example giving approval to requests. These norms can be cross-checked against company policies and statutory requirements. The interdependency of the decision making by one responsible agent on the decisions of others is highlighted so as to be able to specify improvements to the IS.
