# Drivers and barriers to adopting EDI: a sector analysis of UK industry

B Cox and S Ghoneim

The Management School, Imperial College of Science, Technology & Medicine, 53 Prince's Gate, Exhibition Road, London SW7 2PG

This paper presents the results of research comparing the effects of Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) on seven UK industry sectors. The sectors investigated were retail, manufacturing, distribution, utilities, pharmaceutical, government and services. The analysis focuses in particular on similarities and differences in the implementation process and examines the difficulties experienced and benefits achieved by the various industry sectors. Further, the level of EDI integration with internal business processes is investigated. A framework is described which is based on a survey, a case study of successful EDI implementation and interviews with both service providers and users. It provides a potential tool for practitioners covering important aspects of EDI implementation and integration.

## Introduction

The Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) market continues to grow steadily. In the UK about 10000 companies are estimated to be using EDI and the number is growing at a rate of between 20 and 30% per year. The European market is set to increase from about 196 million ecu in 1992 to about 590 million ecu in 1997 (figures include customer software and support), a compound annual growth rate of 24% (Taylor, 1993). There has been a great deal of academic research in the area of EDI. To date the literature has focused on the strategic role of EDI (Cash & Konsynski, 1985; Benjamin et al (1990) and its ability to facilitate the restructuring of industry as a whole as well as individual partnerships (Johnson & Carrico, 1988). In addition, the benefits achieved from the adoption of EDI, in particular those associated with reduced costs, improved communication with trading partners (Reekers & Smithson, 1993, 1994) internal streamlining of business processes and business scope redefinition have been highlighted (Venkatraman, 1991). What has not been so widely investigated is the impact of EDI on specific industry sectors (Clarke, 1992).

This paper is based on the hypothesis that different industrial sectors will have different experiences of EDI and that it is necessary to improve our understanding of the problems and opportunities particular to each type of business sector. Motor manufacturers, for example, will want to know what the experience has been of other motor manufacturers in the implementation of EDI. The experience of the banking industry or the retail sector may be interesting but is not necessarily relevant to them.

Seven sectors have been investigated to test this proposition and an EDI implementation framework based on these investigations is described. The sectors of interest were retail, manufacturing, distribution, utilities, pharmaceutical, government and services. Within them research is presented into three aspects of EDI implementation. First, the extent to which sectoral differences influence the benefits of, and barriers to, the implementation of EDI. Secondly, the degree to which EDI has been integrated into the internal business processes of each sector. And, finally, whether in each of these industrial sectors EDI delivers its maximum benefit when integrated with internal business processes or whether for maximum benefit to be obtained these processes must be representative of the organisation's core business activities. The results of the research suggest that industry differences do apply and that for maximum benefit to be derived from EDI technology it is essential that EDI is both integrated into internal business processes and that these processes are representative of core business activities. Results of the survey findings, follow up interviews and a detailed case study of the EDI experience of Her Majesty's Stationary Office (HMSO) are analysed and presented in the EDI project implementation planning (PIP) framework, a potential tool for EDI practitioners.

## Research methodology

The methods selected for this research were a postal questionnaire, interviews and an in-depth case study. The structured questions in the questionnaire provided a broad perspective across a large number of organizations in the various industry sectors, while the interviews and the case study provided the depth and contextual details needed to focus on particular aspects of the research. The postal questionnaire was sent to 300 British organizations representative of a broad range of industries. To identify barriers to implementation and assess the perceived potential and achieved benefits of EDI, both EDI users and non users were included. The targeted organizations were randomly selected from the EDI Association, International Network Services and Top 1000 Times Organisations Community lists.

Selection of the factors included in the questionnaire was based on previous research in this area to ensure broad consistency with existing literature, and provide a comparative framework with previous work (Banerjee & Golhar, 1991; Reekers & Smithson 1993, 1994). To assess industry differences, industry specific questions were included.

Factual and background information was solicited by direct questions asking for precise information such as annual turnover, number of employees or number of trading partners. Where subjective opinions were sought, such as factors influencing the adoption of EDI, perceived implementation barriers, actual implementation difficulties and benefits achieved from EDI, a five point Likert scale was adopted. Open questions were also included so that respondents could provide further details concerning critical issues in EDI implementation and operation, recommendations to improve the use of EDI or any additional information. In addition respondents were provided with the opportunity of free-format response so that some of the richness of the phenomena addressed in the questionnaire could be investigated.

A response rate of 28% comprising a total of 85 (75 users and 10 non users) was achieved. A further fifty responses were received indicating interest in results but an inability to complete the questionnaire on the basis of organizational strategy or confidentiality. The majority of respondents were typically large organizations, both in terms of turnover and employment. The average turnover was £1877 million (maximum £30 billion, minimum £3 million) and average employment figures 12951. Only 7.8% of respondents reported an annual turnover of £10 million or less and only 9% employed fewer than 250 staff. The retail sector represented the largest user of EDI.

Data were analysed using a one-tailed t-test to identify factors of high influence and to allow for comparison of results with those of previous research. It is acknowledged here that certain assumptions inherent in the t-test are not fully matched by the survey procedure and results. In order to take this into account, only factors with a P-value of less than 1% were considered statistically significant. The results were analysed both on an aggregate level to highlight national industry trends and characteristics and within a comparative framework to demonstrate the similarities and differences between the various industry sectors.

## Implementation drivers

The survey responses supported the hypothesis that drivers to implementing EDI vary across industry sectors. A detailed analysis of these results is outside the scope of this paper, but is reported elsewhere (Cox & Ghoneim, 1995). However, some of the salient findings are briefly described below.

While operational gain, remaining competitive and improvement to inter-organizational relationships are the primary drivers to adopting EDI across sectors, their significance varies depending on the state of EDI adoption within the sector and on individual organizational requirements. For example, drivers for the government sector, which is at present following a number of total quality management programmes are primarily those of improved customer service and increased effectiveness where the pharmaceutical industry, which has been slow to adopt EDI, is mainly driven by the trading requirements of the retail industry. Retail, where EDI adoption is mature and most of the operational benefits have already been achieved, is now seeking additional benefits in the form of facilitating the streamlining of business processes. The manufacturing and distribution sectors, where once again the state of EDI is relatively mature, primarily aim to enhance their inter-organizational relations as well as improve efficiency.

## Sector analysis of the benefits of EDI

The research intended to identify whether the benefits perceived to have been achieved through the introduction of EDI would vary between the various industry sectors. The results support this view (Table 1).

Somewhat predictably, benefit was most significant for the retail, manufacturing and distribution industries. This reflects the fact that the supply chain in these industries mandates a high level of efficiency in the transfer of invoices and orders for which EDI supplies an ideal tool. On the other hand the analysis did not show significant benefit for the pharmaceutical sector. This finding is discussed further in the next section of this paper.

Cross-sector analysis supports previous findings where the primary benefits of EDI are operational, in particular those of increased speed of communications and reduced clerical error. However, contrary to widely held views, the reduction of inventory costs was not found to be significant. This result is congruent with previous research results in the US, Germany and UK (Banerjee & Golhar, 1991; Reekers & Smithson, 1993). However, previous research findings suggesting that a major benefit of EDI is that of ‘improved customer service’ were not supported (Reekers & Smithson, 1993). This result may be interpreted in the light of the state of maturity of EDI trading which is tending towards the norm and is therefore no longer a differentiating factor.

The suggestion that EDI has the potential to transform relationships between trading partners by bringing them closer (Holland et al, 1992) is supported by the findings, where improved trading partner relationships is significant in the retail, distribution and utilities sectors. With respect to benefit gained from the ‘streamlining of business processes’ the results of this survey suggest that this is only achieved in the manufacturing sector.

## Benefit associated with integration of EDI with internal business processes

It is widely asserted that maximum benefit is obtained where EDI is integrated with internal business processes (Swatman et al, 1994). However, compared to the large body of theory and speculation there is little empirical research into the level of EDI integration in internal business processes (Bergeron & Raymond, 1992). An objective of this research was therefore to investigate this question. The degree of integration of EDI applications with in-house applications, that is, the extent of automated processing and electronic transfer without human intervention was used as an indication of the level of integration of EDI with internal business processes. Respondents were asked to select the statement most appropriate to their EDI practice.

\- EDI data is processed separately and rekeying is the norm.

\- Incoming data is usually adjusted by employees before further electronic processing takes place.

\- Outgoing data is processed on a different machine, the data generated requires adjustment before transmission.

\- Incoming data is transferred electronically without human intervention.

\- Outgoing data is transferred electronically without human intervention.

Responses to these statements, classified by industry sector, are detailed in Table 2.

Support for the view that maximum benefit is achieved through the integration of EDI with internal business processes is provided by the retail, manufacturing and distribution sectors which show correlation between benefit achieved and the level of EDI integration. However, of interest, is the pharmaceutical industry where despite the fairly high level of integration into internal processes, business benefits were not significant. Whilst acknowledging that this is based on a small number of respondents, it would tend to challenge the assertion that directly attributes benefit achieved to the level of EDI integration into internal business processes irrespective of the nature of the EDI application. It is hypothesised here that not only is benefit correlated with integration with internal processes but is also dependent on the extent to which such processes are representative of the organization's core business activities.

## Relationship between EDI applications and core business activities

Data were collected on the type of applications used in the various industry sectors and the extent to which these were integrated with applications which might be considered core to the organization's activities.

The EDI applications adopted by the retail, manufacturing and distribution sectors, in which benefit was most significant, are primarily invoices and orders which are representative of their core business activities in terms of procurement and sales.

The government sector where only operational benefits were significant does use applications which are representative of its core activities. For example, educational boards use EDI to provide information for teachers, exam entries and results. However, while these applications are representative of core business activity, there is limited integration of EDI with internal business processes, which may account for their limited success with EDI. Where government organizations have successfully integrated EDI into core business activities the results have been very satisfactory as is highlighted in the case study of HMSO presented below.

The pharmaceutical sector where benefit was not significant in contrast with the government sector, has integrated EDI into business processes but its use is primarily in the traditional context of EDI, that is, exchanging procurement documents. In this case, EDI is integrated with internal processes, but these are not representative of core business activity such as research and development.

These findings would tend to suggest that, for maximum benefit to be achieved, EDI must both be integrated into internal business processes and these must be representative of the organization's core business activities.

## Barriers and implementation difficulties

An interesting outcome of this research was the lack of correlation between what respondents initially perceived as potential barriers to the introduction of EDI and the actual difficulties subsequently experienced with the implementation process itself. In general, organizations did not perceive factors such as the adaptation of existing processes, cost, or the introduction of trading partners as barriers prior to EDI implementation, yet subsequently experienced these factors as difficulties during the implementation process as detailed in Table 3. This highlights the need for early comprehensive planning.

Those industries with the highest EDI usage (retail, manufacturing and distribution) experienced the least difficulty with its implementation. Where implementation difficulties were significant in high usage sectors, such as manufacturing and distribution, these were primarily confined to the introduction of new trading partners. Of interest is that there were no apparent implementation difficulties for the government sector. This may reflect the relatively limited use of EDI by the government within a controlled environment.

Technical requirements in terms of selection of communication technology, value added network services (VANS) and standards did not present problems. ## The introduction of new trading partners

In line with the previous research the introduction of new trading partners was perceived as a major barrier to EDI implementation (Taylor, 1993). However, this did not apply in the case of the retail, government and utilities sectors. This may be attributable to their trading power. Difficulties experienced by the manufacturing and distribution sectors where EDI is well established and its usage widespread is somewhat surprising. A possible explanation of this result is that this is not a reflection of reluctance by trading partners to embrace EDI but rather reflects their caution in commitment to inter-organizational systems. The results are more easily explained in other sectors such as the pharmaceutical and services industries where the usage of EDI has not yet reached a critical mass. The need to address the problem of how best to introduce trading partners was highlighted by the respondents throughout the free format survey responses. It is interesting to note that despite the commonly held view that awareness of EDI is widespread, 45% of respondents emphasised the need to increase community awareness of EDI business applications and benefits and thus facilitate the introduction of new trading partners. 40% of respondents recommended that the business community be better educated with regard to EDI and its benefits promoted. In addition to establishing new trading partners, difficulty was also experienced in meeting the requirements of existing trading partners in the service sector. This may be attributed to the wide range of EDI applications used in this sector.

## Adapting existing business processes and EDI software integration

Emphasis has been placed in the literature on EDI as a vehicle for business process re-engineering (Swatman et al, 1994). In order to further investigate the relationship between business process re-engineering and the role of EDI, respondents were asked whether prior to implementing EDI, they had considered the adaptation of existing business processes to meet EDI requirements as a barrier to implementation. The responses were not statistically significant which implies that having to adapt existing business processes was not perceived as a barrier prior to EDI implementation. However, in reporting post implementation difficulties experienced, the adaptation of existing business processes was statistically significant as was the process of integrating EDI software with in-house applications. This contradiction between pre-planning expectations and post-implementation outcomes highlights the need for early awareness of the impact of EDI on internal applications and processes as well as the incorporation of mechanisms to identify and implement potential subsequent changes.

## Cost justification

As in the wider area of information systems, justification for investment in EDI is problematic and this was a statistically significant difficulty for the manufacturing, pharmaceutical, utilities and services industries. In-depth EDI-specific cost justification techniques need to be developed, highlighting benefit and validated through empirical research. What is required is evidence not only of operational benefits but also support for the claim for the wider benefits claimed from EDI implementation, reflecting industry-specific gains.

## Case study

The results of the research highlight the need for early comprehensive planning for EDI reflecting the industry characteristics, corporate requirements and state of EDI maturity. They further focus on the need for integration of EDI with internal business processes representative of core business activity. In order to illustrate these points, a case study of HMSO is described to capture the richness of the results of the survey and to illustrate how successful integration of EDI has been achieved. It focuses on the critical factors highlighted in the research, in particular those to be taken into consideration early in the planning stages.

HMSO is a British government organization with a turnover approaching £400 million per annum. It supplies stationery products, office supplies and equipment, printing and publishing facilities to the public sector as well as publishing services to the private sector.

In 1987 a review was undertaken at HMSO to improve performance, control costs and increase competitiveness. The review particularly highlighted the need to increase efficiency and the quality of customer service. As a result, HMSO embarked on a total quality management (TQM) programme aimed at improving and differentiating customer service. In order to achieve their objectives, senior management adopted a business process redesign strategy and endorsed the implementation of EDI technology. The focus was therefore on streamlining core processes associated with customer ordering. EDI provided the enabling technology to improve the ordering process, enhance communications with customers and improve delivery. This strategy provided uniqueness to the products and services and increased the customer's cost of switching to alternative suppliers (Bakos & Treacy, 1986).

HMSO integrated its EDI strategy within its corporate planning strategy in order to ensure that the organization's strategic as well as operational and efficiency requirements were targeted. HMSO's primary objective focused on enhancing trading partners co-operation and establishing closer relationships with their community. HMSO also aimed to adopt EDI as an enabling technology to facilitate business process re-engineering.

Sawhney and Williams (1994) detail the EDI implementation history at HMSO as follows. The first implementation of an on-line order receipt system was developed to reflect the need of the Publication Division users and the implementation was restricted to this community. The successful outcome of this project indicated that an electronic capture facility could provide significant benefit to HMSO and had a clear application to the Business Supplies Division where the goods supplied could be identified by discrete coded numbers and the exchange document information structured. HMSO were aware of the fact that the majority of their customers were not EDI capable, and, therefore, an on-line order entry system was implemented. This system, HMSO Enquiry Access and Rapid Trading (HEART) was developed based on customer requirements and provided on-line access to an electronic catalogue with screen based order creation and authorization facilitation. In parallel, HMSO's EDI capable customers communicated their requirements through VANS providers.

The next stage in the implementation was the extension of the service into logistics and supply activities. This operation was controlled centrally within HMSO through the implementation of an ‘EDI corporate gateway’. The corporate gateway automated the creation and dispatch of HMSO’s orders to suppliers originating either from customer’s orders or to replace warehouse stock.

The EDI corporate gateway facilitated the transfer of structured data internally within the organization as well as externally amongst HMSO's suppliers and customers. It provided an integration enabler for in-house applications (integrating various organizational applications, whether ordering, accounting or other) as well as systems (connecting diverse information technology (IT) platforms).

HMSO reported organization-wide technical, operational and strategic benefits. The operational and efficiency benefits reported focused on ‘reduced clerical error’ and ‘improved customer service’ as well as ‘improved cash flow’. However, the strategic benefits reported are the most important as HMSO reports that it has gained competitive advantage through the use of EDI. ‘In an extremely competitive field, HMSO has been able to retain and expand its customer base’ (Currie, 1994).

The HMSO implementation scenario suggests a further potential use of EDI as a ‘change agent’ and ‘integrator’. The centrally situated EDI corporate gateway accommodates potential future change requirements with minimum impact on existing IT platforms and applications.

The critical success factors to the HMSO case study may be best summarised as follows:

\- A corporate wide, coherent and integrated, both upstream and downstream, electronic trading strategy, reflecting the industry structure and vigorously supported by senior management.

\- An EDI strategy focused on customer requirements and organizational business processes, aimed at adding value to the core business (buying–distribution–logistics) and trading communities.

\- Comprehensive review and assessment of processes to provide for further exploitation of EDI both as an integration enabler and an agent for change within the business context of increasing customer satisfaction and hence, profitability.

## The PIP framework

The survey responses and HMSO case study highlight the need for early planning in the EDI implementation process. There is no single framework which will provide for the diversity of organizational structures and requirements (Holland et al, 1992), for example, suggest a strategic implementation model which incorporates the nature of the markets and distribution channels, organizational structure and processes and buyer power influence. The PIP (processes, infrastructure and partners requirements) framework described below attempts to cover some of the broader crucial planning issues involved in the implementation, planning and control of EDI (Figure 1). It calls for the early assessment of benefits, potential impact on business processes as well as core business activity, understanding of trading partner requirements and the potential impact on infrastructure. It also focuses on the need for dynamic assessment of the implementation process to reflect changing partner and process requirements. Strategic, implementation and control planning stages are covered.

## Strategic planning

Planning for EDI implementation should be integrated with both corporate strategy and information systems strategy. EDI must be seen as a business solution to corporate requirements rather than a technical solution. As in other areas of information systems planning, the importance of incorporating the EDI strategy with corporate strategy is emphasised (Holland et al, 1992; Currie, 1994; Cox & Ghoneim, 1995). Comprehensive evaluation of costs and benefits is required with particular emphasis being placed on the industry dynamics. An organization-wide strategy to identify potential areas which would benefit from implementing EDI may be carried out (Sawhney & Williams, 1994). Further, implementation of EDI requires appropriate governance. Resource requirements planning must be integrated with information systems strategy where the allocation of resources is determined. A total of 63% of survey respondents indicated the need for permanent dedicated resources for EDI implementation. The EDI strategy itself should identify EDI applications relevant not only to operational improvement but also aligned to core business activity. Long and short term objectives should be detailed with a dynamic tracking component built in at review stages to ensure the EDI strategy responds to changing market and, corporate and trading partner requirements. As in the wider area of information systems, successful projects require leadership, commitment, focus and management. A project leader should be nominated to undertake the responsibility of implementing EDI. 'The position of the EDI champion within the organization is crucial to the implementation of success' (Currie, 1994). The champion must be able to control the implementation, input to the strategy and have access to and support from top management.

## Project implementation planning

The EDI project implementation planning stage is crucial (Holland et al, 1992). Based on the research results three critical areas for attention are: business processes, infrastructure and trading partner requirements.

## Business processes

This research found that improvement to internal business processes is a major driver to implementing EDI. Yet, adapting existing business processes and integrating EDI software within an organization's in-house systems were reported as major implementation difficulties. A lesson to be drawn from this is that identifying the EDI application, investigating its potential impact on traditional processes and specifying the EDI software integration requirements should be incorporated early in the planning stages.

## Infrastructure

The selection of communications technology is not problematic. However, difficulties are experienced in integrating the EDI software with internal applications and the implementation of standards. This highlights the need for infrastructure planning. These findings are further supported by Holland et al (1992) who assert that companies without an effective information systems infrastructure will be unable to develop EDI on a company wide scale. The building of the EDI infrastructure should not, therefore, determine the EDI platform in terms of software, hardware, VANS and standards in isolation but should closely investigate the business processes to be affected as well as trading partner requirements. An organization needs to accommodate a trading partner's requirement for implementing a particular standard which will subsequently need to be reflected in its internal business processes and integrated in internal systems. Security and legal issues should also be investigated at this stage to allow for any commercial adjustments required.

## Trading partner requirements

Better communication with trading partners and better trading partner cooperation are significant drivers to EDI implementation. Yet the introduction of trading partners is difficult. This reflects the need to understand the trading partner's environment, commercial set-up, requirements and drivers to implementing EDI in the early stages of planning to enable the implementation of a mutually beneficial project.

## Implementation and validation

As in the wider field of information systems management the project plan should include detailed objectives, milestones, timescales, cost details and contingency plans. In particular, close attention should be paid to the impact of the introduction of EDI on internal systems, information flows and business processes. Procedures concerned with EDI implementation such as transmission schedules, commercial obligations, handling errors and regulatory requirements must be tested and validated. Contingency studies should be undertaken to address future changes.

A pilot phase is recommended to test the EDI system with trading partners in order to validate the implementation and ensure that the commercial aspects are complete.

## Reviews I and II

The interviews conducted highlighted the importance of building in a dynamic tracking component to monitor and review the development and implementation of the EDI project. This is represented in the PIP framework by the ‘review components’. Review I monitors and assesses the pilot results. At this stage any changes to commercial arrangements need to be made and reassessment of the project feasibility and costs carried out.

Building the second dynamic Review II tracking component is aimed at the identification of future requirements for change, exploration and adaptation to new EDI applications and the development of the electronic trading community. The requirements identified should be incorporated into the EDI strategy for future EDI implementations.

## Conclusion

The research reported in this paper investigated the impact of EDI on various sectors of British industry. The data presented suggests that the drivers, benefits, barriers and implementation difficulties to adopting EDI do vary between sectors. Such differences need to be taken into account early in the EDI implementation planning process.

The research explored the hypothesis that maximum benefit is achieved in industries where EDI is integrated into those processes which are representative of core business activity. Support was found for this assumption as well as for the hypothesis that difficulties in the EDI implementation process are more likely to be significant in those industries which have failed to integrate EDI into core business activity.

The HMSO case study described the strategic use of EDI and suggests that the role of EDI may be extended to both that of change agent and an integrator. It supports the view that although the initial effects of EDI are at the interfaces of the organization, normally the purchasing and sales departments, change permeates further throughout the whole organization (Holland et al, 1992). Finally, the results have been integrated in the EDI PIP framework which provides a practical tool for EDI practitioners.
