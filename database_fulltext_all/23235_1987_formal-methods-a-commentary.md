# Formal Methods: A Commentary

Tom Maibaum and Martin Sadler\*

What is formal methods? The first comment to make is that we feel this is the right question to ask rather than the related: what are formal methods? That is, 'formal methods' refers to, or should refer to, a discipline rather than a collection of tools or methodologies. Although as a first approximation formal methods is concerned with providing a formal model of, and tools for supporting, the programming process, it is actually a lot more. This review, in the first place, attempts to convey something of what we see that 'more' as being, representing some of the views and work of the 'formal methods group' at Imperial College. Having outlined our view we comment on what we see as the strengths and weaknesses of the positions of the other contributors to this volume.

Conceptually, formal methods is focused on the idea of (a) specification (i.e. the process of construction and also its result). The usual, but not exclusive, way of formalizing the idea of a specification is as a theory over some suitable logic. Naively, a logic is just a system for carrying out reasoning, and a theory is a body of facts or knowledge or requirements. In contrast to other uses of theories, in for example logic, linguistics or the philosophy of science, which emphasize model theoretic interpretations, the view that a specification is a contract between, for example, client and software developer adds both dialogical and deontic elements that are, unfortunately, often ignored. Dialogical elements are relevant in the sense that a specification is arrived at by some process of (hopefully constructive) interaction, or dialogue, between client and software developer. Deontic elements are apposite in the sense that both client and software developer gain, and of course may subsequently lose or gain again, during this dialogue, both rights and obligations.

Whereas for the most part theories remain essentially ‘static’ entities for logic, linguistics and the philosophy of science, for computing science it is the ‘information preserving’ maps between specifications, implementations say, that do the ‘work’. If our underlying logic is something like classical predicate logic, then the notion of an interpretation between theories is a suitable way of modelling such maps. Program development is then modelled by a finite chain of specifications, the first specification in the chain corresponding to our initial requirements specification (the object we arrived at by a process of dialogue), the last to a program, or at least to a theory in a program logic. The implementation steps correspond to the design decisions we make in refining our initial specification, turning it into a program.

There is not only an interest in notions of mappings between theories where both source and target theories live over the same logic. Being able to cope with different logical bases is vitally important. It is unfortunate that many logicians and linguists coming into computing replace the absolutism of: 'everything can be coded into classical predicate logic', by the absolutism of: 'everything can be coded into PROLOG'. The key word is of course 'coded'. There is no reason why we should expect, or require, that the logic we use for 'natural' knowledge representation be the same as the program logic of the programming language in which such knowledge is ultimately represented and manipulated. Thus we need to be able to talk about coding one logic as a theory over another. It is reasonable to assume that the target logic is conservative with respect to the source logic, 'it adds no new pieces of reasoning'.

Another concern of computing science is modularity. In the same way that presenting programs in a monolithic fashion as thousands of lines of code but broken up into suitably packaged chunks should be avoided, so too should specifications. Much of the research work in specification has been focused on the design and implementation of specification languages, mechanisms for putting small specifications together to form larger ones. A familiar logician's structuring mechanism would be the idea of a definitional extension, or the more general idea of a conservative extension.

So, a reasonable question to ask is when can we guarantee that our structuring mechanisms are preserved under our notion of implementation. In terms of the formal objects suggested so far, when are conservative extensions preserved under interpretations between theories, and maybe additionally, conservative maps between logics? The answer, which need not concern us here, is a technical one. Having set up various pieces of formal machinery to model aspects of the software development process, what is important is knowing that the pieces mesh together without causing any problems. And this is where the 'formal methods community' splits. On the one hand are those that feel that such theoretical endeavours are not required: we can either live without them, or our formal framework should mysteriously guard us just because it is predicate logic or set theory or whatever. On the other hand (which is the camp to which we belong) are those who feel that without such formal guarantees we do not have anything of value anyway.

In practice, specifications themselves do not live over a single logic. For example, a requirements specification may involve a temporal component for reasoning about timing constraints; a different temporal system for reasoning about the sequencing of various actions; a deontic component for reasoning about the 'rights' of various users; and maybe some non-monotonic component for reasoning about states of some databases; without mentioning the process of dialogue required to create such a specification. We can identify properties of logics that guarantee that it can all be put together properly. Of course, the properties that are easy to find are invariably limiting, imposing too severe restrictions on what we can do.

However formal methods is not just interested in such theoretical results, although identifying properties of logics that guarantee that the logics support particular methodologies is clearly important. We can ask what constraints are imposed on the presentation of a logic in order that such properties can be easily verified, preferably by a machine. The traditional attention paid to restrictions on sentences (e.g. prenex forms, Horn clauses) turns out not to be as important as restrictions on rules (e.g. all introduction rule presentations and cut-elimination results).

The more tangible side of such investigations are 'logic environments'. One wants an environment where one can enter a presentation of a logic (in a straightforward way) and be told whether or not it is suitable for supporting the ideas of specification and stepwise refinement. One aspect of our work has focused on the verification of certain meta-properties (like the one needed above), and techniques for reflecting back to the user gaps in an attempted proof (of such properties) either as rules that the user should delete from his presentation or as additional rules that he can consider adding to his presentation. Such modifications 'buy' the proof of the required meta-property. A refusal might cause the environment to search for a more complex proof, and so on. Perhaps surprisingly, the results are not as bizarre or useless as one might expect.

As a final claim, in a rather crude form: whereas AI often suggests that one adopt a new logic (e.g. a non-monotonic system) and logic programming leaves one's logic alone, formal methods extends one's view of logic by saying 'if you just modified it a little bit here you'd buy this nice property and...'.

Regarding the practice of formal methods in industry. There is a natural conservatism in all organizations against the use of new techniques and methods. Jobs depend on performance and tried, if not proved, methods are more dependable than new wonder cures. Old ways of working are familiar and dependable, if not so effective. The job will be done, even if it is troublesome and exceeds the budget.

How can these obstacles be overcome? Certainly overselling is not the way to do it, as the many false dawns of the past lead to scepticism about outlandish claims. It takes courage on the part of a company manager or individual to take up the cudgels of formal methods and risk all.

Let us examine what actually has been achieved. The bottom line for formal methods would seem to be the establishment of a 'mathematics culture'. In other words, a little bit of mathematics goes a long way. The clean room experiments at the Federal Systems Division of IBM demonstrate that conventional approaches to system development when augmented by a reasonable facility in discrete mathematics (as taught, for example, in the first year of the undergraduate programme at Imperial College) provides a significant increase in quality and productivity. The systems were constructed more quickly and with fewer errors. The experience seems to be repeated wherever a little bit of mathematics is adopted: the experiments with the specification language Z at IMB UK, with VDM (the Vienna Development Method) in the development of an ADA compiler at the Danish Datamatics Centre, etc. The style of notation or the underlying formal system is, to a first approximation, irrelevant. It is the style of analysis and the approach to problem solving engendered by the ‘mathematics culture’ which delivers the goods.

In his talk, John Nichols of IBM UK indicated that the end Z formalism offered just the right vehicle for their efforts. However, the main advantage of Z, he claimed, was that it used a very familiar part of mathematics as its basis, namely set theory. This is in contrast to what must be judged the very intricate and baroque notation and conventions used in Z. Further, as used at IBM, Z had no refinement concept to support program development from Z specifications. It must have been the simple mathematics which won through! In the clean room experiments, no particular specification formalism was used – just familiar mathematical concepts based on functions, relations and sets.

Having achieved the bottom line, what more can be done? J. McDermid indicated in his talk\* how a framework based on a detailed analysis of the software life cycle and the identification of important activities, within the framework, which utilize the 'mathematics culture' in a rational way, will lead to even greater productivity and quality. Moreover, the framework provides the basis of a better management tool for controlling the process. This is bringing us back to the observation made earlier about formal systems (mathematical languages) requiring frameworks to guide us in their use to make this use effective.

Having got this far, we might expect efforts at further improving the system development process. One focus for this might be the observation that one formalism alone cannot hope to support very well, if at all, the need to say different kinds of things about our intended system. It is a trivial, but significant, observation that set theory, used to specify some systems, is not the same as a programming (or Hoare) logic, which is used to reason about programs. Both Robin Milner and Michael Jackson advocated the merits of a pluralistic approach to formal methods. The former argued strongly for support environments which admitted numbers of suitable logics, possibly constructed by the engineer as and when necessary. He then seemed to contradict himself by advocating a universal metalogic in which to define these different formal systems. Why is what is good for the 'logic engineer' bad for the 'software engineer' and vice versa?

Michael Jackson considered at length, and justifiably so, the pragmatic necessity of using different views/aspects/perspectives in the process of describing and building a system. A 'good' engineer is able to manoeuvre between these different aspects as necessary and appropriate. This kind of complicated formalism would be very difficult to use just because of its complexity. Conversely, such a logic could be very simple and all the conceptual distinctions required would have to be 'coded'. Such coding would be tedious and certainly against the spirit of the specification enterprise. The idea of using views would seem to be a sound methodological compromise, but there is the caveat that we do not as yet have any sound theory underpinning it.

Much of the discussion after each talk and the general comments at the end illustrated the morass of ill-defined terminology and lack of agreed foundations which characterize current formal methods work. This situation is common to all young disciplines, but it is discouraging how little progress seems to have been made in recent years in agreeing this terminology, at least among academics, and formulating the appropriate culture in a form that industry will take up and put to extensive and productive use.

Biographical notes

Dr T.S.E Maibaum was born in Hungary and grew up in Toronto, Canada where he obtained his first degree in Mathematics from the University of Toronto. He obtained his PhD in Computer Science from the University of London (Queen Mary College) in 1973 with a dissertation on generalized formal language theory. He then worked as an Assistant Professor in the Department of Computer Science, University of Waterloo until 1981. He is presently a Reader in the Department of Computing, Imperial College of Science and Technology and Head of the Computing Theory and Systems Technology Section.

Dr Maibaum's interests have included algebraic semantics, distributed computing and the theory of abstract data types. His present interests include theories of specification and implementation, logic engineering (the design and construction of logics for specific needs), inferential databases, and logics for concurrency.

Martin Sadler obtained his first degree in pure mathematics from Adelaide University, South Australia in 1978. The next three years were spent as a graduate student in the philosophy of mathematics at the London School of Economics. In October 1982 he joined the Department of Computing at Imperial College of Science and Technology as a Research Assistant on a SERC project working on the theory of specification, becoming a lecturer in the department at the beginning of 1987.

His main area of research is formal methods and the application of logic to computing science.
