# Information Technology and People: The Challenge of Change

Eric Landles, Scottish College of Textiles

Change in an organization's environment can have a great influence on its internal functioning. The current revolution in methods of information processing is presenting organizations with the problem of managing technological change. It seems that the introduction of computers is often accompanied by unforeseen circumstances (1).

Problems that arise are either of a technical or non-technical nature. It is the latter i.e. problems with the people who will operate the new technology which may be the most difficult and expensive to solve. Current experiences within the newspaper industry provide dramatic evidence of this.

This encourages us to consider what we know about the management of people during organizational change. Most of this knowledge was developed after attempts to introduce forms of technological change other than computerization but the lessons learnt can give us guidance with current problems.

One of the best documented accounts of technological change was written about the mining industry in North Durham (2, 3). Before the change, miners, using picks, shovels and man-pulled trucks, worked in small, tight-knit groups. Each group performed all the tasks necessary to get coal to the surface. Technological advances meant that mechanical cutters and conveyor belts were introduced.

Management devised a new production system which involved one shift cutting the coal, a second moving the coal to the surface and a third moving machinery and preparing the faces for the return of the cutters. The technological improvements did not result in output at the expected levels and there was a high level of absenteeism and labour turnover. The new production system was technically well organized but it failed to meet the needs of those who were using it. The good relations in the small groups had been disrupted and the variety in work had disappeared. Trist and his co-workers redesigned the system in an attempt to meet both human and technical needs. They allowed the re-formation of largely autonomous, self-selected work groups with all three tasks being performed on each shift. This allowed the miners the benefits of both good relationships and varied, responsible work. Using this method Trist apparently managed to cut absenteeism by 11.8 per cent and increase output by over 50 per cent.

The general principle to come from this work can be applied to any kind of work. That is that an organization is a socio-technical system and must be managed as such during times of organizational change. This means that an organization has two inter-dependent parts i.e. a social part (people and relationships) and a technical part. The management is therefore required to design a system that meets both human and technical requirements.

If employees think that the new technology is not going to meet their needs then they are likely to resist the change. Management ignore employee resistance to change at their peril. The outcome of resistance can be obvious (e.g. strikes and confrontation in the newspaper industry in the 1980s) or less apparent e.g. low morale, restriction of output, sabotage, absenteeism and labour turnover.

What are these employee needs? We must not make the mistake of assuming that the needs of the miners in North Durham in the 1940s are necessarily the needs of all workers in all situations. There are certain needs that may be important at work and these are described below (4). It must be remembered that the relative importance of these can vary from individual to individual.

First is the need to make a living — to have a job — and fear of redundancy can induce high levels of resistance. Introducing computers may or may not require redundancies. A recent report from the Fraser of Allander Institute (5) suggests that the latter is more likely. If this is the case then management can save themselves a lot of trouble by informing employees in the strongest possible written terms that their jobs and salaries are not at risk. If jobs are to go or wages are to be reduced then resistance of some kind is almost inevitable and the delicate process of consultation, negotiation and compensation must be undertaken with the purpose of damage limitation.

The second need is for security. People need to feel safe and times of change can cause insecurity. They worry not only about losing their jobs but also about whether they can cope with the new technology. Employees must be clear on what is expected of them both during and after the change. A good training programme is essential. Visits to other companies where the 'evil' new technology can be seen working tamely should reduce anxiety. Overall, management must be as supportive as possible throughout.

Third is the social need or the need for good relationships. The possible consequences of disrupting good relationships were seen in the Trist study. Management must beware of disrupting good, productive relationships just because the new technology at first appears to demand this. Thought must be given to ways of maintaining good working relationships while still using the new technology.

A fourth need is the need for esteem or respect. A new technology can lead to damaged self respect or a reduction in the amount of respect others have for an individual. By altering a person's job we may be altering their status within the organization. A new technology can also drastically change promotion prospects. An employee who has worked hard for 20 years mastering the old system suddenly finds himself at the bottom of a pile of whizz kids.

Finally, many people demand job satisfaction — they need to feel that they are doing useful, meaningful, challenging work. A technological change like computerization can greatly simplify a person's job and rob them of much of its satisfaction.

Thus when management are designing the jobs, which existing employees will do once the change is introduced, they should consider employee esteem and the need for achievement. Thus jobs may have to be enriched to include planning and organizing tasks, decision making and responsibility. Promotion paths will also have to be considered. Consultation, discussion, interviews and questionnaires can all be used to assess what needs are important with a particular workforce.

There are other reasons for resistance to computerization than the non-satisfaction of employee needs.

Many people have negative attitudes towards computers. Changing a person's attitude is not easy but should management find it necessary to attempt to do so they can attack the three components of attitude — belief, emotion and behaviour. An individual's attitude to computers consist of what he believes about them, what he feels about them, what he has done about them and what he intends to do about them. Management must first assess employee attitudes, probably within these categories. Then communications in the form of information and discussion can provide the basis of appropriate beliefs. Positive emotions can be encouraged by stress reduction. Any fears that still exist can be used to good effect if management can provide the anxious person with a useful means of escape e.g. a training programme. Finally, by allowing people to work with computers in a relaxed atmosphere where success is guaranteed by careful planning, a positive attitude can be encouraged via the behavioural component.

Another form of resistance comes from the informal organization of a business. This is a set of practices and relationships that have not been explicitly established by management but that arise through time and human interaction. This can cause resistance in a variety of ways. If people have developed certain norms or shared standards and find them useful they resist them being changed. Also, because these standards are shared people work in unison to avoid them being dismantled. Thus management may find themselves being stonewalled by a group rather than having to deal with the relatively easy problem of individual resistance. Another parallel problem is that a management information system is usually designed to meet the needs of the formal organization i.e. that officially established by top management. However in reality the organisation might function quite differently. In these circumstances the management information system should have perhaps been designed to suit the informal organization. The possible negative consequences of destroying the informal organization in order to introduce a new technology were demonstrated in the Trist study (2, 3). Management and the management information system designers must acquaint themselves with what is really happening in an organization rather than relying entirely on what top management thinks is happening.

Trade unions may resist the change or help management by selling the innovation to their members. The major influence on their policy will be the extent to which the new pay and conditions will benefit their members. Any problems that do arise can be confounded if there are two or more unions involved who disagree about their members' role in the new operation.

Certain organizational structures seem more resistant to change than others. Highly organized concerns with clearly defined chains of authority, standardized procedures and numerous specialists may find their organization very inflexible. The less bureaucratic structure can be more successful in adapting to changing circumstances (6). The process of debureaucratization that may be necessary can be difficult. Authority relationships and well established procedures are often resistant to change. Often people who are specialists in their area are not equipped or think they are not equipped to attempt new work. Such people are likely to resist the change. Also, they may be able to produce a job contract which clearly specifies their job content and it is unlikely to include any mention of operating the new technology.

Thus the management of change can be a complicated operation. A system must be designed that meets social and technical needs and has the social and technical sub-systems in equilibrium. Consultation and discussion are nearly always essential even if it is only to find out where the resistance is likely to come from. Help, support and training will be needed. Organizational structures may have to be expertly modified. Past research must be studied. Other companies that have adopted similar technologies can be consulted. By using the knowledge available and gathering all necessary information in advance, management should be able to construct a plan that allows them to successfully meet the challenge of change.

Eric Landles is a psychology graduate from Edinburgh University and is a trained teacher. His first research work was done with the Donaldson's Research Unit in Edinburgh and his most recent, dealing with industrial leadership, was awarded an M.Litt. degree from the Department of Business Organization, Heriot-Watt University. He has worked in consultancy and management development in industry and commerce and is currently employed as Lecturer in Industrial Psychology at the Scottish College of Textiles.
