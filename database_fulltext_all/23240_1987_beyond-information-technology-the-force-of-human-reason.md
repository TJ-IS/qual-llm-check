# Beyond Information Technology: The Force of Human Reason

Igor Aleksander, Imperial College\*

My primary aim for the next hour or so, is to venture into a discourse that is by no means comfortable. It is conventional for an inaugural lecturer to refer to his own experience. It is now the 11th anniversary of my first inaugural lecture, where I looked at the brighter side of the way in which electronics and computing can lead to a greater understanding of natural phenomena. Today I wish to look at those factors that, during my work as a researcher both in the design of machines that emulate the brain and the management of IT, have caused me considerable discomfort.

In a nutshell, I am worried by a sense of unbridled omnipotence that overtakes some who research into the advancement of information technology and their underestimation of the competence of the human being. There appears to be an unwillingness to show interest in the real limitations of what has been invented and a ready willingness to exaggerate and extrapolate from the sometimes meagre performance that can be achieved with computers, particularly when the human being is taken as a standard. There is also an unwillingness to recognize that the power of human reason should not be made subordinate to the demands of information technology.

The complexity of computer science is such as to make this an exceedingly dangerous situation. The pronouncements of experts are taken as sacrosanct simply as they cannot easily be repudiated. Repudiation is not popular: it is taken as a sign of a lack of vigour and does not lead to further funding. But lack of repudiation misleads the decision makers in our society. It gives them magnifying factors with which they can pursue their own aims, the justification for which may be hidden under a mantle of technological ignorance.

My targets are:

● the myth of automation

## The myth of automation

As with my other targets, this is not a denial of the great strides that have been made, in this case, with the automation of many manufacturing processes. I simply wish to examine the right and the wrong reasons for automating and the human potential for being unable to distinguish between the two.

Henry Ford's notion that automation would put a car at everybody's doorstep has an obvious appeal. Indeed the net gain to people is obvious and machinery is seen as enhancing the limited production capacity of available workforces. The obvious counter to this is Taylorism which, in aiming to partition work down to sub-human levels reduces the workman to a disposable cost item. In this environment automation is a net loss to people as it impoverishes them and makes them unable to benefit from the goods that they themselves produce. Luckily, due to the obvious success of alternatives in Japan, Taylorism is probably dead.

Here, I stress the myopia that can befall a decision maker within an organization and the tunnel vision that can be developed when viewing technological advances as a goal in their own right. A hypothetical example based on real experience may be of some use.

A farming company had automated the washing and preparation of pears for grading and inspection. Twelve persons (women) were employed in the grading and quality control function. They stood over the conveyor belts and did their job. The production manager had heard of computer vision systems and commissioned a feasibility study. This suggested that the 12 graders could be replaced by three robots drive by vision systems. The cost of machinery maintenance, depreciation and loan interest was of the same order as the human operators' salaries hence there were no great gains to be had in the profitability of the unit.

Now for the tunnel vision. The eager production manager, rightly from his own perspective, made the case that the automation should go ahead on the grounds that:

\- Productivity measured as output per unit worker would go up (due to the loss of 12 workers, with no expected increase in output);

● Production could be planned on a 24-hour basis;

\- The modernization was ahead of that carried out by competitors.

The board enthusiastically approved the move. Two years after the change, however, the company had lost market share which had seriously eroded its profitability. This, even after the discovery that major quality errors were due to the machinery that missed fruit that was lying on 'rot' and appeared healthy to the computer through its camera. This could only be put right by restoring three quality controllers to their posts which also made the production technique more expensive than before.

Standing back from this, not only did the shareholders suffer, but clearly there was a net gain for the dole queue, which, in a closed system would have to be paid for by the shareholders in increased taxation, not to mention the tribulations of those who became unemployed.

Did the production manager need sacking? Probably not, the problem lay with his inability to see the whole, which explains what I mean when I say that high-tech causes tunnel vision.

My research in the Kobler Unit has convinced me that this force can be very much at work in the application of IT to business. Indeed, Office Automation not only imports inappropriately the notion of automation into management, but also brings in some of the blindness. In fact it may be the word 'automation' that is causing the damage. There is no element of automation in the proper use of information technology in the office. In fact, any attempt at 'automating' unveils cavernous dangers. We may recall foolhardy predictions in the early part of this decase by those who believed that secretaries would be replaced by word processors. This is a clear example of the overreaction to the word. I don't know of such successful replacements, but I do know of the boon that a properly used set of wordprocessors is in a properly organized office.

The real effect in a successful operation is to upskill secretaries to the level of officer managers, where largely time has been saved by a proper allocation of responsibilities for the generation, production, debugging, distribution and filing of documents.

The commandment that might be drawn from all this could be:

The proper introduction of IT into manufacture or business relates to the raising of the quality of what is being done. This is a recognition of the value added by human reason whereas the drive towards human replacement must be seen as a sign of despair.

## The danger of robotic defence

The relationship between the general and the boffin has always been an uneasy one. It is often the case that the military play the apprentice to the scientist's sorcerer. Unfortunately the sorcerer is not the splendid order-restorer of the fable. He has much lesser powers, if any, to control the effect of his pronouncements once the rather powerful apprentice has taken over. Indeed, the military takes on the character of an unstoppable robot that not even his creator can control. I feel that it may be salutary to recall that the development of the atomic bomb contains well-documented lessons that seem not to have been learnt.

It is well known that while most of the scientists collected by the US military at Los Alamos were in tune with the reasons for developing the A-bomb – to pre-empt the same possibility in Nazi Germany, they were not so sure about its deployment in Japan and even less certain of the further development of the Hydrogen bomb.

It is within easy living memory that in 1939, Leo Szilard wrote the letter also signed by Einstein alerting Roosevelt to the possibility of a destructive chain reaction and the danger of this being used by Germany in a weapon. It was he who in 1945 foresaw the dangers of further development of atomic weapons. They again initiated a letter to the President, after all, Germany was now defeated:

Perhaps the greatest individual danger that faces us is the probability that our 'demonstration' of atomic bombs will precipitate a race in the production of these devices between the United States and Russia.

Whether as a result of the death of Roosevelt or the momentum that the project had by then gathered, this piece of extraordinarily clear vision had gone unheeded: the technology had to be proven, first in Hiroshima where up to 200,000 people may have died and then (as further 'proof'?) in Nagasaki where 100,000 died. The sorcerer's apprentice was well on his unstoppable way: one of the sorcerers, in the defeated shape of Leo Szilard, had lost his magic wand.

Better known perhaps, is a similar fate suffered by Robert Oppenheimer. As the Japanese capitulated, Oppenheimer left Los Alamos and delivered a speech against the buildup of atomic arsenals:

... The people of this world must unite or they will perish ... By our works we are committed, committed to a world united, before this common peril, in law and in humanity.

The 30-year-old William Borden, Director of the joint Congressional Committee on Atomic Energy interpreted this attitude as evidence of subversiveness in his submission to the FBI which eventually led to Oppenhimer's indictment:

... He has worked tirelessly ... to retard the US H-Bomb program ... More probably than not, he has since (the end of World War II), acted under a Soviet directive influencing the United States' military, atomic energy, intelligence and diplomatic policy.

This is how the robot of state policy takes over: human reason in the shape of the scientific concerns of an ageing mastermind has been subjugated.

But what has this to do with information technology? My thesis is this. Because, as Michael Bywater put it (Observer, November 1985): 'artificial intelligence is a two-word phrase which makes US Department of Defense officials salivate when they hear it', the exaggerators keep exaggerating to keep the funds flowing and Presidents base their hopes for initiatives such as SDI (strategic defence initiative) on the exaggeration. Any scientist seen as not going along with the hype is in danger of being seen as being subversive.

The robot marches on – the danger is immensely greater than that foreseen by Szilard and Oppenheimer. Their fears were based on the state takeover of a well-calculated outcome of known physics. The current takeover is based on the fund-raising hype of a minority of information technologists.

But is it really possible that responsible innovators are capable of such deception? Unfortunately the evidence is only too clear. Not long ago Ed Fredkin, director of the AI lab at MIT went on a Horizon film to enunciate that no principle of science or engineering stood in the way of building machines that were 'millions of times smarter than man'. I shall return to this later. Now, suffice it to say that not even the most arduous supporter of AI would believe this knowing how deucedly difficult Fredkin's own labs find it to do even the simplest AI tasks (language understanding for example).

I hasten to add that the Department of Computing at Imperial College has an almost impeccable record of integrity in this matter. The commitment to making explicit the limitations of its lines of research, and the dedication to the establishment of rigorous principles for the development of advanced information technology, have made it into one of the foremost and most reliable centres in the world. I feel comfortable working here.

To sum up this argument a second commandment comes to mind:

Information technologists are responsible for handling the most 'human' of engineering resources: information. This is also the youngest and least understood. They are under obligation to study and publicize the limitations of informational machines and direct their work towards providing a good match between such machines and human reason.

I must now practice what I preach.

## The limitations of artificial brains

Much of my own work in computer engineering has been fuelled by the thought that no matter how good some of our programming techniques may be the behaviour of artificial systems seems always to be highly distinguishable from the behaviour of human brains. Yet it is unlikely that the brain would have a ghost in its machine which could be blamed for the difference. This is not to deny the major strides that have been made with artificially intelligent systems: it simply makes it worthwhile to ask the question as to whether the way that the brain does things leads to alternative ways of designing computers.

Primarily, artificially intelligent programs rely on searching through a large number of likely possibilities to arrive at an answer. Our introspection if nothing else, tells us that most of the time we do it differently. Think of a simple coloured-brick stacking problem or the solution to a cryptic crossword puzzle. The brain seems to bring a lot of information to bear on the problem simultaneously through its neural nets and supplies the answer by getting into states that satisfy all these conditions at once. This is the fascination that has given rise to the new school of connectionism that seeks to unravel the nature of neuron net computations.

My own interest goes a bit further back and may be useful to illustrate both the limitations and the fascination of this approach. If there has been a blinding revelation in my scientific existence, it was the notion that the function of a brain cell can be approximated by a random access memory (RAM given that it is 'turned on its side' that is, with the address terminals as (synaptic) inputs and the data output terminal as the axonal output. Nowadays one can hear 12 year-old children comparing the amount of RAM that their game playing computer has. Unfortunately this realization took place in a traffic jam in the Blackwall tunnel on a grey day in February 1965. Then, a RAM was a carefully handknitted mass of thousands of ferrite rings costing about £4,000 apiece. To think of making networks of thousands of these seemed even to me to be a clear sign of insanity.

So, at Queen Mary College we made a simple electronic device in which the memory medium was thin gold strips that acted as fuses. The function of these predated the read-only memories that are currently used to store software in microcomputers. But gold fuses were expensive and VLSI was on its way, so we persuaded Derek Roberts at the Plessey Co to allow some of his engineers to build experimental read-only memories in silicon chip form. They did and this turned out to be the first silicon chip RAM made in the UK. We called them SLAMS (Stored Logic Adaptive Microcircuits).

In 1968, at the University of Kent we made SOPHIA a single-layer net containing 96 bits of memory. Strangely enough much of our current understanding of such systems was formulated then. Thirteen years later, after a string of simulations and bits of hardware, Bruce Wilkie's engineering, John Stonham's watchful eye and my foolhardy optimism gave rise to WISARD, a 1/4 megabyte system in which the single-layer net payed off by being able to recognize 512×512 bit patterns in 1/10th of a second.

Then came the saga of technology transfer. Theory has it that dons in their ivory towers are unable to see eye to eye with industrialists in factories. They therefore need government intervention (in the guise of British Technology Group in this case) to get together.

Bill Adaway of Computer Recognition Ltd and I knew exactly how a commercial WISARD could be made. The major battle was the need to cope with BTG's mandate and reduce the interference to a minimum. We won, and in 1984 the first CRS WISARD came into being, sporting 2 megabytes of single layer memory and a professionally designed man-machine interface.

But what has all this to do with connectionism (a term invented in the last few years)? It turns out that a single-layer net with random content in its stores (before training) and feedback, has the ability to be trained to bring several constraints to bear on the solution to a problem in the time it takes for a wave to ripple from one end of the net to the other.

So, by putting feedback into a WISARD have we made a brain? Nothing like it. It turns out that the intellectual power of a neural net (whatever that means) depends first on its size second on its connectivity (the number of neurons that any one neuron receives information from) but most of all on the information that is around for it to absorb. In size, the WISARD is about one millionth of the brain and in connectivity about one thousandth. And most of all the information available to it is still only that which we know about. So even on the basis of falling costs of memory at the known rate of a factor of 1,000 every 10 years, it would take about a million years (doing the sums optimistically) for us to make a brain-sized WISARD. But even then the human intellect would remain a paragon which could only asymptotically be reached.

But for me the most fascinating thought about all this is that one millionth part of the brain could (because of higher connectivity) outperform the considerable ability of the WISARD. It makes me feel that the importance of connectionism lies in the fact that it will lead us to understand and respect our own brains more than anything that has been done by computer simulation or AI to date.

## Conclusion

Despite appearances, this lecture has not been about knocking IT. I have no hesitation in seeing IT as being an astonishing phenomenon in the family of machines that are helpful to mankind. I will never again write without a wordprocessor, do my tax returns without a spreadsheet or make lecture slides without computer graphics. What's more I can do all this at home on affordable equipment.

What this lecture has been about is putting IT in perspective: there is no competition with human reason and there are no mystical, unexplained powers. In other words, IT is a wonderful aid but not a religion.
