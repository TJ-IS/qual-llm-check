# IT Consultancy – Challenges and Opportunities

Paul Thornton, Chief Executive, PA Computers & Telecommunications, London

In a recent television programme devoted to the trials and tribulations of computing, a first time user, exasperated beyond all measure at being unable to get his system to work, finally threw in the towel and went off to become a computer consultant. While clearly none of the consultancies participating in this AIT event would contemplate offering employment to such an inexperienced applicant, few would deny that the boom in computer consultancy in recent years has offered unprecedented scope for the charlatan as well as for the professional. In this paper we look at the circumstances which have given rise to this situation, and at the current status of IT consultancy. The issues which our clients will be facing over the next few years are explored and some pointers are given as to possible responses from the consultancy world.

## Recent trends in client requirements

Compared with the situation 15 or even 10 years ago, our clients now face an extraordinarily wide range of problems in achieving the best results out of information technology including:

\- the migration of IT from pure back office support into front office, and in some cases into customers and suppliers;

\- the growth in user population as professionals, managers and office workers gain access to the technology;

\- the increasing dependence of many businesses on their IT systems, accompanied by a growing sense of unease on the part of senior management;

\- the increasing complexity of the development task, as new systems must not only meet specific functional requirements, but also maintain interfaces with a wide array of other systems of varying vintages.

These developments, coupled with the well-publicized resource shortage in IT, have created a boom in the consultancy field. The major consultancies and software houses are constantly vying with each other to secure the services of the most able IT staff. The situation has been further exacerbated in the UK over the past couple of years by the seemingly unending requirements of the financial services sector, in anticipation of the Big Bang. Small wonder, then, that the situation offers scope for the unprofessional as well as for the professional.

## Structure of the IT consultancy sector

IT consultancy services are provided today by organizations of many different kinds:

\- the IT consulting arms of the large generalist consulting houses, such as PA and Booz Allen & Hamilton;

\- the management consulting practices of the accounting firms, such as Touche Ross, Price Waterhouse;

\- systems and software houses, such as Logica, Software Sciences;

— specialist IT consultancy companies, for example Butler Cox, BIS;

\- the customer support arms of all the major IT manufacturers;

\- small companies offering specialist services into vertical market sectors or in specific technologies;

\- all manner of sole practitioners, ranging from university professors through to primary school children.

Opinions differ as to the size and growth rate of the IT consultancy world. However, many companies have experienced growth rates of 30 to 40 per cent over the past three years or thereabouts. If we assume the upper figure, and a starting point of 5,000 IT consultants in 1986, then within 25 years there will be no other occupation in the UK. (Of course this cannot possibly be true since we will all be IBM systems engineers long before then!).

## Future developments

Things will, of course, slow down, but the question is when? At present there seems little sign of the trends towards increasing use of information technology abating.

\- There are a great many application areas still to be tapped; fields such as computer integrated manufacturing, decision support systems, office technology are still in their infancy. IT is becoming cost-effective in a host of new areas as price/performance improves.

\- An increasingly computer-literate user population is becoming more demanding in its requirements, and prepared to look outside if the in-house IT department cannot deliver.

The problems for which organizations will be seeking external support from the consultancy world could be classified as technological- and business-oriented. Under the former heading we would include:

\- making the most appropriate use of new and emerging technologies - end-user computing and expert systems are the fashionable technologies today, following database in the 1970s and office automation in the early 1980s;

\- defining an overall IT architecture to meet the evolving needs of the organization;

\- technical support in the implementation of major systems projects.

Although more complex and more diverse than hitherto, these problem areas are similar in nature to those addressed by IT consultants for many years.

Some of the more business-oriented issues which our clients will be facing are as follows:

\- how to organize successfully for IT and the balance of responsibilities between corporate headquarters, business unit IT divisions and user departments;

\- how to ensure value for money in the face of ever rising IT costs;

\- security issues in the light of the growing dependence of the organization on the proper functioning of IT systems;

\- how to break the mould of large projects delivered late, overrun budgets and scaled down functionality;

\- how much to do in-house and how much to contract out (increasingly topical with the emergence of major facilities management companies).

Although these problems are not new, the consultancy world's approach to them has largely been ad hoc and pragmatic. The main generic type of approaches are essentially:

\- 'rent-an-expert': identify someone in the organization (or head-hunt someone) who has done it before, and who knows the problems which can be expected on the specific project;

\- 'invent a methodology': devise a methodology specifically for the occasion, and package it so that all the hygiene factors are covered (progress reports, well-defined deliverables, etc.);

\- 'shoehorning': take a real methodology used by the organization and force-fit the client's problem to it. This occurs most commonly with system design methods being (mis)used to address IT/IS strategy issues.

Because consultancies have generally been successful in attracting high quality staff, many clients have been well served, despite the absence of thoroughbred methodologies. However, the viability of these approaches in the longer term must be questioned.

## Where now?

In the absence of economic collapse or other force majeure, then there is clearly potential for substantial further growth in the market for IT consultancy services. Not only will the technological and business problems outlined above continue to stimulate consultancy business, but also there are bound to be further legislative or environmental stimuli such as liberalization of telecommunications, Big Bang etc., which will create sudden upsurges in our clients' need for external support.

In the short term the challenge to the consultancy profession is to maintain quality standards in the face of this growth. Because demand outstrips supply, inadequacies which would be unacceptable in almost any other sector of the economy are tolerated, and in some cases inevitable (e.g. frequent staffing changes on projects as staff job-hop).

As the sector matures, we are likely to see the kind of shake-out that has occurred within other service industries. A handful of larger consultancies will be able to afford investment in methodologies, service development and infrastructural support to address the needs of our clients in a cost-effective way (this investment is also the key to tackling the shortage of appropriately skilled personnel).

International capability is also likely to be of increasing importance to UK consultancy practices:

\- the multinationals are growing in importance;

\- an increasing percentage of the UK economy is owned by overseas companies (e.g. the City in the run up to Big Bang);

\- if UK-based consulting companies do not access technologies and methodologies in use in the leading-edge markets, then we will be vulnerable to competition from companies based in those markets.

Those who cannot afford this investment will have to retrench to niches in which their specific expertise is at a premium, or quit/merge. There will of course always be scope for the entrepreneurial new entrant with innovative ideas and marketing flair. However, the barriers to new entrants becoming market leaders will be considerably greater than has been the case in the past.

For the large IT consultancies, this transition through adolescence into adulthood will not be comfortable. Any move toward offering standardized ready-made prescriptions is likely to be resisted by clients, and also by consultants who welcome the challenge of thinking through solutions for themselves. However, this may be a price which the IT consultancy profession has to be prepared to pay if it is to avoid being prey to the damaging slurs alluded to at the start of this paper.

Paul Thornton studied Mathematics at Liverpool University, graduating in 1967. He worked for the Operational Research Executive of the National Coal Board (now British Coal) and for merchant bankers Hill Samuel & Company, before joining PA Computers and Telecommunications in 1974. He spent a number of years helping to build up PA's Germany operation, becoming its Managing Director in 1981. Since returning to the UK he has managed the company's Management Sciences and Complex Systems Divisions before becoming Chief Executive in 1986.

During his consultancy career he has worked on a wide range of projects for information technology users, suppliers and regulatory authorities. Particular areas of interest are the international growth of telecommunications services, the use of information technology to gain competitive advantage and the management of large, complex information technology projects.

Address for correspondence: PA Computers & Telecommunications (PActel), 33 Greycoat Street, London SW1P 2QF.

## Discussion

A contributor from Scicon: I would wish to disagree with the statement that IT consultancies do not do market research, because we certainly do at Scicon. Also, I would wish to contradict the statement that IT consultancies do not have adequate methodologies. I would suggest that most of us do follow methodologies in our consultancy work.

Colin Leeson: I did not say that IT consultancies do not do market research, but that most of us do not do market research about where our own business area is going and the implications of that for our consultancies. Regarding your comment about methodologies, yes, you have every right to disagree with me.

Michael Braithwaite: In the area of strategy consultancy it is difficult to say whether there are standard methodologies that can be applied in the same way that they can in some of the more structured IT projects. Certainly there are techniques and methodologies but their application and choices about when they should be applied relies very much on the skills of the individual consultant.

A contributor from New Bank Priest: I would suggest that part of the disagreement about whether there are adequate methodologies is caused by the fact that different types of IT projects form the basis for the contributors' statements.

Colin Leeson: Part of my job is to interview consultants coming into PActel from other consultancy organizations. It is obvious from discussion with such people that the methodologies that do exist are very simple and are not adequate to be called methodologies in the context of high level IT consultancy. I suggest that IT consultancies are just putting defensive smokescreens around themselves when they suggest that effective methodologies exist or are widely used.

Malcolm Iliff: I would support that view from the perspective of an IT consultancy user. We have been seeking to buy a methodology for a particular project and have not been able to find a good one.

Igor Aleksander: There has been much preaching about IT consultancies helping people to run their businesses. Could you give me an example of a result produced by IT consultancy and how you measure it?

Colin Leeson: We could be invited in by a company to solve a particular computer issue, such as designing a particular capacity. This can, therefore, be done and a result seen.

Igor Aleksander: How do you measure the result in terms of financial improvement of the company?

Colin Leeson: We may not necessarily do that with that particular example. To take another example, however, a company may ask us how we can reduce costs in a computer area.

Malcolm Iliff: Would you be prepared to take on a computer project that would drive a company to the wall?

Colin Leeson: Obviously not! We need to look at specific issues. If one were doing a capacity planning exercise and this was going to send a company to the wall, then there is something strange about the capacity planning exercise.

Michael Braithwaite: There is a spectrum of questions that can be put to IT consultancies. Looking at one end of the spectrum let us suppose that a technically-based consultancy is offered a project with clearly defined terms of reference. That project may be associated with a larger strategic project within the client's organization that is likely to fail. In that circumstance I would not blame the consultancy for taking on the particular job. One has to make allowance for how much the consultancy is likely to understand the impact of a particular computer project on the client's business. Also, one has to consider how much knowledge the clients have regarding their own business strategies and implications of IT projects for them. The question is, when should one warn a client that he is risking putting his hand in the fire?
