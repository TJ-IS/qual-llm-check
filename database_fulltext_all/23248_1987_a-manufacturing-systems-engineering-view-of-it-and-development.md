# A Manufacturing Systems Engineering View of IT Research and Development

M.W. Dale, Lucas Engineering & Systems

Abstract: This paper presents a manufacturing systems engineering view of important issues relating to IT research and development. It argues for an approach to the next phase of information technology development which is heavily based on real-world applications with the dominant influences held by educated users and engineers who have added computing skills, rather than information technologists. It argues for ‘consolidation’ with particular attention to total systems integration and an emphasis on the need to professionally engineer the human interface.

It is essential from the outset to define clearly what is meant by manufacturing systems engineering. It is the design, development and commissioning of effective means of manufacture to meet business needs both in the short and long term. It therefore involves an integrated view of the materials, machines, processes, assemblies, products, jobs, people, information, facilities, control systems and computers which together form the complete manufacturing system. It most definitely is not just the control systems and computers in manufacturing, neither should it be confused with its subset of machine systems engineering which is concerned with a lower level of integration around automated machines and processes etc., with their real-time computers and controllers.

It is only by taking this professional, strategic and integrated approach that competitive manufacturing performance can be achieved. Piecemeal applications of production engineering, materials management and information technology to elements of a system inevitably lead to lower levels of performance of the complete system.

The second important premise is to recognize that manufacturing industry is being driven by international competition with ever increasing competitive pressures (Figure 1). This requires a survival strategy which is based on large companies being international players focusing on best practice performance, with the objective of equalling or surpassing the top competitor in their sector. The illustration indicates top business measures of performance which are relevant to manufacturing companies. While smaller companies may not necessarily have to be international players, they need to be either supplying customers of this type or operating effectively in a niche.

These competitive pressures result in the need for high performance products which, in many cases, have short product life cycles. So development lead times need to be short and the manufacturing units must have the ability to deliver the high variety, high quality products against short and reliable manufacturing lead times.

This highly desirable state can be achieved by clearly defining and then implementing appropriate marketing, product development and manufacturing strategies while restructuring and redesigning the business. The Lucas approach to this task has a strong emphasis on simplification which results in complex factories being transformed into a number of simpler product group modules making use of plant and equipment which is flexible, reliable and process capable. This can usually be achieved without heavy capital expenditure on new equipment and high technology. However, where heavy investment is justifiable, plant is operated continuously around the clock. In addition, the businesses are designed with simplified organization structures containing fewer job roles for multi-functional staff who work increasingly in cross-function teams rather than functional departments. Such businesses need to fit neatly into integrated supply chains for maximum effect. Finally, they also require integrated modular control systems which make use of appropriate information technology.

It is important to consider briefly the kind of organization a redesigned factory can have and its implication for information technology (Figure 2). The simplest structure results in only three offices with integrated job functions, i.e. manufacturing operations, product and manufacturing development and, finally, commercial operations. Full extension of this approach brings marketing into the development office and puts sales (an operations activity) into the commercial office. This offers a simple and effective organization structure which may also need a very small quality assurance function, but no other top level functions.

The approach also provides the right foundation for integration of the information systems and if there are fewer offices there is less need for office automation!

The information systems are there to exercise control functions, as well as to provide information, and it is worth recalling the basic concept of levels of control. This is illustrated in Figure 3 for a manufacturing business. While the details can be debated, the irrefutable point is that each level has different requirements (just as does each function) in particular for sample time interval. The natural conclusion is that control systems should be hierarchical with carefully designed information flows between each level, if 'indigestion' is to be avoided. In addition, the lower the level, the more likely are the information system requirements to be unique because they have to deal in each case with a particular combination of products, processes, machines, people and computer hardware.

When we combine the organization described with its necessary levels of control, we have an overall architecture for the information technology. From this analysis it is now possible to look at the IT implications of the manufacturing trends from whence the argument came, as indicated in Figure 4.

The first issues concern data. It is essential that data (and preferably the systems themselves) are owned by the users, not by specialist support functions. Equally, any attempt to integrate requires a singular dataset, as distinct from a single database which, some infer, means a hardware configuration based around one processor.

As already implied, there should be a matching architecture for the information systems, computer systems and organization. Any alternative makes the data ownership point more difficult to achieve.

The systems need to be hierarchical, distributed systems which can integrate in multiple vendor configurations. The applications need to have many bottom-up subsystems which are at least particular to an industry sector and, in many cases, particular to one situation. This does not preclude a building block approach which makes maximum use of standard elemental parts or standard interfaces between parts. Conversely, there is a diminishing requirement for industry-wide standard and top-down applications.

This leads us to the final step, again illustrated in Figure 4, which concerns the enabling changes required in the IT arena. For example the IT goals cannot be reached without full development and wide application of industry-general (not vendor-specific) standards such as MAP, TOP, etc.

While there are many actual and potential uses for new and clever hardware devices, competitive business pressures mean low cost and reliability are equally as important as (and sometimes more important than) sophistication and function. Additionally, they need to be complemented by user-friendly software macros which do not require highly specialized people to develop applications. Similarly, low overhead relational databases and 4GLs which can be integrated with existing languages, databases and software packages (where possible) are essential. Only then can we expect packaged applications to be written in these environments with the result that users can adapt and tailor top-down systems and integrate them with bottom-up systems.

The design activities need changes in a number of directions. Growing use of IT and growing data mountains argue for low-cost work stations supported by hosts for data management. Again, the opportunities for bottom-up product design and process design aids is increasing rapidly. There is a strong need for easier methods of integrating CAD, CAPP, CAM and database. This is not just a question of communications, but very much one of applications.

Considerable benefit could be derived from consolidating work in knowledge-based, rule-based, expert systems to make them slick and robust. This is highly preferable to pushing back the frontiers of artificial intelligence.

The overriding changes concern people. In manufacturing industry we need more multi-disciplinary engineers with IT skills. We need manufacturing systems engineers, product design engineers and project engineers with IT skills. We need professional engineers and technician engineers. We need educated users together with these computer-literate engineers to lead and control applications work; in part we need such engineers to actually carry out the applications development. Recognizing this, and the diminishing half life of IT knowledge, we need a shift in emphasis for initial education and training, as well as a big investment in continuing education and training, including distance learning methods. Once we have these people, we then need to keep the majority of them in industry.

Far from being just an issue about screen formats, contents and ergonomics, the human interface extends to, for example, the ease of product support and the dependency of this on software development tools.

In summary, this manufacturing systems engineering view is concerned with two main issues. The first is that information technology is here to serve needs and therefore in manufacturing industry the IT strategy is sixth in logical sequence:
