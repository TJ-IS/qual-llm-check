# IT Research Direction in the United Kingdom: the Danger of Future Collapse

Igor Aleksander, Head, Kobler Unit for the Management of Information Technology, Imperial College of Science and Technology

Abstract: It is argued that directed research programmes such as Alvey and IT86, being focused on product generation and 'pull-through', do not provide the much-needed environments in which new ideas can grow. In other words the 30+-year cycle of research-to-product is either ignored or not understood. This paper justifies the above statement from case histories and personal experience. It also suggests some essential remedies that are required to reinstate such GREENHOUSE research.

## The central theme

Had Einstein hit upon the idea of general relativity in an age of research funding directed towards products, he would probably have had to justify further study by quoting industrial partners, deliverables and exemplars. Had he held any interest in the by-products of his discoveries, he would probably have given up being a theoretical physicist and sought some gentler pursuit such as philosophy.

The thrust of this paper is that the notion of research, directed towards product generation and wealth creation per se, is a crudely ill-conceived idea which, if acted upon to the exclusion of supporting knowledge-generating research (with no mandatory product implication) contains a self-destroying time bomb for the future.

Some examples drawn from technological history and personal experience are used to justify this central hypothesis.

## A new competitive product

Let us say that directed research is seen as being responsible for the creation of a new market-capturing, IT-related product by the year 2000. Also, as this is a topical subject, let us say that this will be an expert system for doctors, such that no surgery can do without. This type of product (in common with others in the expert systems area) would require several years of field testing and market build-up. As a conservative estimate, a usable prototype would have to be ready in 1995. The marketing and esting would involve the IT maker (or consortium) and a group of medical partners (the users). It would therefore have the character of something envisaged as the central thrust of the IT86 programme.

A precondition for the generation of such a prototype may well be an extended period of research and development that would have started in 1985. This too fits in well with the Alvey/IT86 models as the collaborators required here are universities and IT makers. But what is the information and the methodology on which this latter group can base their work?

In the case of this medical application, the answer probably lies in the idea incorporated in MYCIN (the first medical expert system) published in 1975. Of course this system is ad hoc, and a host of incoming methodology from work in computing and artificial intelligence would need to go towards the methodical development of our product. It is worth noting that MYCIN was developed almost as an act of bravado. This was essentially driven by its university inventors who wanted to show that their methodical thinking could harness the powers of computation into something useful – a computer-based repository of human skill that could be passed on to less skilled humans.

The questioning can continue even further. For example, where did the need to prove the power of computers come from? In 1975 everyone assumed that computers were powerful and useful. The need arose from a paradigm shift that took place in 1965 – the establishment of artificial intelligence as a worthwhile research pursuit. This largely came from John McCarthy's belief in the logical as opposed to numerical powers of computers. Stepping back for an instant, it is worth remembering how fanciful a step this was. The year 1965 was the era of the on-line digital controller, the multi-user system and so on. A shift away from this paradigm seemed to be of value only to the intellectual needs of researchers and was thought to contribute nothing to the salient computational issues of the day. The Lighthill report of the UK Science Research Council still held this view in 1974: no new products were emerging from AI nor were they likely so to do. In fact, this may still be true and our medical expert system of the year 2000 may be a fiction. What is evident, however, is that the paradigm shift of 1965 has led to the ready acceptance and use of novel research methods, logic programming and associated new classes of language in the declarative domain (e.g. HOPE, PROLOG).

Clearly, the quest for the research that underpins our medical expert system could go even further back in time, touching on the invention of the transistor and the computer itself and eventually disappearing meaninglessly into the distant past. My contention, however, is that the atmosphere of research freedom that led to the paradigm shift in 1965 is the most critical inventive factor that leads to the new knowledge-based products of the year 2000 on which everyone in the Western world seems to be pinning their ambitious hopes.

In this case (which is not atypical) the research life-cycle, from inception to product, appears to be about 35 years. The obvious conclusion is that unless the UK is creating (and funding) an environment in which uncommitted research can flourish now, prospects for new products that are based on UK innovation and that might pay off in the year 2022 look pretty bleak. All evidence (e.g. the brain dra worsening student-staff ratio in universities, cutbacks on research in industry, manpower shortages in mid-intellect technological tasks) points to the fact that such an environment does not exist in the UK now.

Curiously, however, even this may be an optimistic view. For how long has such an environment been neglected? Strangely, the key point in time may be the inception of the Alvey Programme, when research without industrial partners was suddenly no longer supported and granting procedures became subject to convincing statements by applicants about deliverables, exemplars and milestones. Had John McCarthy in 1965 been required to justify his spending time with his team in pursuing languages such as LISP, and studying abstruse mathematical proofs of logic computation without industrial partners and no deliverable except increased understanding of what computation is all about, he would not have been funded if 1982 UK grant rules had been applied. Indeed, it is likely that he too would have been labelled by the Alvey director as 'just another academic boffin in need of firmer direction towards wealth-creating processes'.

Therefore one arrives at an obvious interim conclusion: if there is any technological future for the

UK, today's 'boffins' need to be provided with a good (expensive) working environment. Such people are not easily identified and they are not likely to be involved in current Alvey projects.

## Some examples from the past

The very roots of IT and the reason for US/Japan domination in this field appear to come from a history of free and unstructured research of environments. A good first example is the case of John Von Neumann who, with Burks and Goldstine, wrote the foundational report in 1947 on computer structure centred on the stored program. The idea had come from Von Neumann himself, and the US Defense Department was quick to fund something that it recognized as being an important notion in comparison with previous card-controlled computer programming methods.

Maurice Wilkes at the Cambridge Mathematical Laboratories in the UK was not so lucky. In the period 1945-49, his application to the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) was refused on the grounds that an automatic computing machine was not seen as having a market as a product. The wise men of the CSIR were too busy attending to what seemed to be the major research directions falling out of wartime experience: physics or nuclear power and radio/radar for astronomy. The curt reply to Wilkes suggested that instead of spending the man-hours on soldering relays together, he and his colleagues could solve all the country's calculations by working with manual calculators.

Even the invention of the transistor, the device that underpins the semi-conductor technology on which IT is based, was not part of a directed, planned effort. William Shockley at Bell Laboratories was trying to check his predictions of the survival time and diffusion distance of minority carriers in a semi-conductor (the Shockley-Haynes experiment). It was a flash of inspiration that led John Bardeen to realize that surviving minority carriers could be re-collected by the use of a third terminal, leading to the concept of the transistor. This success is clearly due to the fact that Bell Laboratories were supporting the environment in which more could be understood about semi-conductors. The transistor was a product of this environment and not a product of a directed plan.

A similar sequence led to Leo Esaki's invention of the tunnel diode. His brief was to develop Zener diodes and, as part of this, he was curious to see what would happen to the reverse breakdown voltage of a p-n junction, as the impurity doping of the material was increased. He discovered that tunnelling of majority carriers would take place, causing a current to flow which then diminished as the reverse voltage was increased and normal breakdown processes set in. This mechanism is responsible for the famous negative resistance that caused so much interest. Esaki's industrial 'lords and masters' in Japan did not immediately appreciate the potential of his discovery (it was a poor Zener diode). Eventually it gained the Nobel Prize for Esaki. The relevance of this to IT is that early fast memories for computers were made in the early 1960s both at RCA (250 M.Hz clock speeds) and Cambridge University in the UK. These triggered off the notion that semi-conductors might supersede the ferrite core – a notion without which the microcomputer boom would never have happened.

The accidental nature of discovery is not a revelation. Many, including Arthur Koestler (The Act of Creation, Hutchinson, 1976) have pointed to it. The seedbed for such important advances is a healthy, relaxed intellectual scientific environment. Without it, the patently low probability of a significant invention is reduced to nothing.

## A personal case history

It is said that scientists can consider themselves lucky if they have an inventive thought once during their lifetime. My own experience lies in the development of the WISARD (Wilkie, Stonham and Aleksander Recognition Device) pattern recognizer which I quote here to illustrate the lack of co-ordination between the inventive process and government-directed product generation programmes. The inventive thought occurred in the area of what is now called 'neural computing' and took place in 1965. It centred on the fact that a random access memory could form the node of a network which had pattern recognition properties similar to those of the brain. This thought took place in an international atmosphere that was antagonistic to neural computing, largely due to the fact that the paradigm of artificial intelligence was in the ascendant and this was being directed towards the simulation of surface intelligent behaviour as a logical process for (large) conventional computers. A feeling clearly articulated eventually by Minsky and Papert (Perceptrons, the Principles of Computational Geometry, MIT Press, 1969) was that such computation was limited. This theme will be returned to later.

Suffice it to say that although this work could survive within a supportive local departmental environment (and limited resources) any attempts to develop the idea through external funding proved to be unsuccessful. Of particular note was the fact that there were no solid state random access memories (RAMs) available at that time, and suggestions that one might be patented were rejected by the National Research and Development Corporation (NRDC) on the basis that ferrite magnetic memories, being non-volatile, would be dominant in computer usage, leaving no room for solid state replacements. Therefore, the NRDC argued, it would be difficult to show that a patent for a solid state RAM would be used outside the inventor's laboratory. Interestingly, industry's reaction was more positive. The research laboratory of the Plessey Company at Towcester, keen to flex its chip-making abilities, agreed to make a dozen silicon chip RAMs provided that my laboratory could provide some manpower. This gave rise to the SOPHIA machine built at the University of Kent, which enabled us to prove the soundness of the neurally-based image processor (Aleksander Microcircuit Learning Computers, M & B Monographs, 1971).

In the meantime, in 1969, the Fairchild Corporation in California had taken out the patent for the silicon chip RAM and the early RAMs began to appear on the market.

Fortunes turned for this work in 1970 when the Control Sub-Committee of the Science and Engineering Research Council (SERC) provided a grant for the building of the first reasonably sized adaptive pattern recognizer (MINERVA: 2 kilobytes of RAM memory). The grant was given to explore the techniques involved, particularly as they might lead to a way of capturing human experiential knowledge such as may be necessary 'to drive a crane by recognizing the pattern of what is being seen and sensed'. There were no strings, deliverables or industrial partners – only the requirement for a greater understanding of the techniques involved. This funding was renewed on several occasions allowing my team at Brunel University, then much enhanced by John Stonham and Bruce Wilkie, to develop in 1981 the prototype of the WISARD, a system containing 256 kilobytes of RAM memory connected as a network. Its performance was sufficiently impressive to attract a manufacturer of vision systems (Computer Recognition Systems – CRS) to consider buying a patent taken out by the NRDC. This occurred after several years of NRDC's arguing that there was no industrial interest in this type of work. Indeed, CRS approached the University team from its own initiative and not through the intervention of NRDC (which became the British Technology Group – BTG).

The point of all this is that in 1982 both SERC and the newly formed Alvey directorate refused to continue funding this work — first because there was no industrial interest (they had not heard of the CRS involvement, and when this was brought to their attention they argued that CRS was too small a company to be supported by the programme) and second because it did not fit into the pattern of development of advanced information technology as envisaged in the Alvey report. After much protest on my part and my behalf by others, the project was again funded and is now in a relatively healthy state.

The reason for presenting this case study is not to point at inadequacies of assessment but at the fact that directed frameworks are inadequate if not accompanied by plans to consider (and in some cases fund) projects that are not of immediate interest to the majority of industry, yet may have importance as a seedbed for new ideas. It is interesting that such research is often referred to in perjorative terms: 'blue sky', 'long term', 'speculative', 'high risk' etc. I would recommend that these be called GREENHOUSE projects that are protected from the perceived exigencies of industrialization.

## Connectionism: a curious development of IT research

June 1987 saw the first IEEE Conference on 'Neural Networks' being mounted. Its proceedings contain 300 papers and the conference was attended by over 3,000 delegates. To one involved in this field for over 20 years, the conference slogan: 'The Dawn of a New Era' seemed curious if not amusing. The broader name of this field is 'connectionist computing'. Connectionism is the study of the knowledge acquisition properties of networks of adaptable nodes that are, in some ways, like the neural nets of the brain. The knowledge acquisition potential in these networks had been known since 1943 through the work of McCulloch and Pitts. Attempts at making machines have consistently been seen as being out of line with the thrust of computer science developments of the day. So why is the change taking place now?

The partial reason is that in 1982, John Hopfield, a neuro-psychologist at the University of California, San Diego, was able to analyse the cyclic behaviour of such systems by insisting that the nodes be reciprocally connected, which made possible the use of statistical mechanics in the analysis. Others such as Geoff Hinton at Carnegie Mellon, and David Rumelhart also at UCSD, followed and soon established the revival.

The main reason for the now exhaustive funding of this field in the US is the hope that such techniques will overcome what are now being seen as the inadequacies of knowledge-based, rule-bound or logic-bound systems. Speech and vision have always been the 'Achilles' heels' of such techniques, and connectionist systems look promising due to their ability to develop their own computing procedures merely from examples of the task in hand.

This development, in the context of this paper on the future of IT research, is mentioned here primarily to draw attention to the fact that were the connectionist method to be taken seriously in the UK, much more attention should have been directed towards it in the past. Outside my own group of researchers (who were probably the only ones funded in this field during the Alvey period) a handful of others who understand what this methodology entails have found it resolutely difficult to get funding.

A second reason for presenting this case is that it would be wrong to fund only those connectionist projects that are likely to lead to immediate products. Not enough has been understood of these systems: the theories based in physics are inadequate as they only explain the behaviour of a few elements among a vast class of similar systems. For example, connectionist systems made of RAMs are not only well suited to implementation but perform as well as, if not better than, Hopfield Nets. But statistical mechanics cannot be used to analyse their behaviour due to the gross non-linearity of RAM nodes. Also, the limitations of such techniques are not clearly understood. How large must a system be? How densely connected must the node be? How can such systems be interfaced to classical knowledge-based systems? These questions must be answered over the next few years before manufacturers can begin to have meaningful discussions about products. The wording of the Bide, IT86 report, and the financial levels it recommends are such as to inspire no confidence in the possibility of funding such fundamental work. Connectionism may be just one (perhaps minor) example. Others ranging from fundamental studies in physics and materials to the development of mathematical methodologies in computing are also at stake.

## Conclusion: can the collapse be avoided?

The answer is positive, although neglect over the last few years may lead to gaps. However, if there is any hope of the UK being seen as an industrial high-technology nation at all, beyond the year 2000, the following points seem to require immediate action:

1. The fact that GREENHOUSE research is expensive must be recognized. It implies a coordination not only of research councils but also of ministries. The dual funding ideology for underpinning university research (where GREENHOUSE projects are most likely to be) has broken down and the failure of communication between the Department of Education and Science and the Department of Industry over this point cannot continue on its destructive path.

2. IT86 must be followed rapidly by a companion programme, probably of equal value. Such a programme should simply allow SERC to function with elbow room for funding IT GREENHOUSE projects.

3. The expectation for funding GREENHOUSE projects from industry should be adjusted to a realistically low level. However, it may be just as important for such projects to be funded in industrial research laboratories which should come under the purview of government funding agencies in the same way as centres of learning.

4. GREENHOUSE research in technology is as important to the future of the nation as more traditional basic sciences such as nuclear physics and astronomy. A rational funding policy should recognize this and develop its strategy accordingly.

The current argument that the UK is a small nation and therefore cannot take this ‘across-the-board’ view of funding, cannot be taken entirely seriously. Belief that biasing resources into immediate ‘pull-through’ is all that can be done is like saying that the UK is a small nation and therefore cannot afford to invest in its own future.

## Biographical notes

Igor Aleksander obtained his first degree in Electrical Engineering at the University of Witwatersrand, South Africa, and his Doctorate in Computer Design at Queen Mary College, London. After three years in the telecommunications industry, he returned to the academic world and was a lecturer at Queen Mary College, London, Reader in Electronics at the University of Kent, and Professor of Electrical Engineering at Brunel University where he was also Director of Research and Head of Department. He is the first holder of the Kobler Chair in the Management of Information Technology at Imperial College. His research has centred on the design of advanced information technology equipment of an artificially intelligent kind. He has developed this research largely in conjunction with industry, an example being the WISARD Adaptive Recognition System. He has published over 100 papers and nine books.
