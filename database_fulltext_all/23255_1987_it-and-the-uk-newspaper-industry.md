# STATE-OF-THE-ART REPORT

# IT and the UK Newspaper Industry

Henry Holloway,

Consultant, Kobler Unit for the Management of Information Technology, Imperial College

## Introduction

Printers and publishers tend to refer to 'new technology' rather than 'information technology' in printing. This can be confusing as there have been various waves of new technology in printing although currently the term new technology is taken to mean the use of digital electronic techniques for inputting and editing copy. Thus it can be seen that the term information technology is appropriate here, particularly if one uses a definition such as that of King:¹ 'Information technology is a recent and comprehensive term which describes a wide range of processes for the acquisition, storage, transmission, retrieval and processing of information . . .' Indeed, using this definition, newspaper publishing can be seen to have a claim to being one of the first industries to use information technology, as computers were used in the 1960s for inputting, editing and composing text.

This paper examines the present use of digital technology in the production of newspapers in the UK. Information has been gathered by literature search and contact with organizations involved in newspaper publishing. The experience of the Midlands News Association Limited, the leading provincial newspaper publisher in the UK, provides particularly informative reading. The events at News International during 1986 are illustrative of changes in the national newspaper industry. Case studies of these companies are included in this paper.

The paper begins by presenting background material necessary for an appreciation of information technology in UK newspapers. Following a description of the UK newspaper industry, a brief outline of technology is given (a more detailed look at new technology and newspapers is provided in the Appendix). Some comparisons are then made with overseas experience, particularly the USA. The importance of advertising, the effects of the introduction of rival media and the industrial relations record are also discussed.

Having set the scene, the next two chapters look at the two industrial sectors: national and provincial publishing.

The final chapter analyses the situation, providing pointers to the benefits and otherwise of information technology in newspapers.

## Newspaper publishing in the UK

The UK newspaper industry is usually split into two sectors: national and provincial. This is very convenient, since not only are these sectors aimed at two different target populations, but also there are differences in attitudes to technology, industrial relations and, in particular, advertising. Therefore, after a discussion of technology in newspapers, each sector will be examined in turn in the following chapters.

## Technology in newspapers

Various waves of technology have affected printing. These are discussed more fully in the Appendix. However, most innovations have not had a revolutionary effect on printing, modifying production methods to some extent without causing any violent upheavals. The present use of technology, digital methods for input, editing and preparing copy ready for printing, presents a different aspect. This pre-press technology is totally different in nature from previous equipment because:

1. it eliminates the job demarcation which was an integral part of previous production technologies;

2. it offers tremendous scope as to who uses it, how it is used and what it is used for.

Item 1 is central to many of the industrial relations problems associated with new technology in printing. Obviously, it is closely linked to item 2, and both relate to the flexibility which digital technology provides. In addition to these assets, digital pre-press also offers the benefits which are more usually examined: high volume and rate of input, throughput and output. The latter are remarkable, but on their own would lead to changes which are evolutionary rather than revolutionary. For example, high input and editing speeds might lead to fewer compositors, but the job role 'compositor' would still be there. In some newspapers, where digital pre-press technology is used, compositors do not exist.

The present range of equipment has developed from the application of photocomposition in printing. This, in turn, was prompted by the switch from letterpress methods of printing to lithography. The foundation for these developments occurred in the 1950s and 1960s but the type of equipment we are discussing was in use in US newspapers in the 1970s. There have been considerable improvements and enhancements in these systems since their introduction, plus large-scale reductions in equipment prices, but the basic system components are as follows:

\- a computer system which accepts, modifies and outputs text which is received from a variety of inputs;

\- multiple terminals for inputting and editing text;

\- special terminals, with large screens for creating display advertisements;

\- output of copy in a form suitable for making plates ready for printing.

The more sophisticated systems can deal with graphics as well as text and make up full pages entirely within the computer system. In order to achieve this, they require scanners to digitize the graphics and terminals which enable pages to be made up on-screen. A further step in sophistication is the creation of plates directly from the computer without any photographic intermediary.

An integrated computer system for newspapers, incorporating graphics and full-page make-up capabilities, completely changes the pre-press production procedures of a newspaper. Job roles such as compositor and the many separate functions in graphic reproduction and forme preparation disappear, many functions being shared by other workers. For example, text may be keyed in by journalists, editors or tele-ad personnel where a direct keying policy is operated.

## Comparison with overseas newspapers

There have been three Royal Commissions on the Press since the end of the Second World War. All three were highly critical of the national newspapers of Fleet Street; overmanning, restrictive practices and outdated equipment were singled out as the main causes of very low productivity.

A 1981 comparison of the newspaper industries of the UK, West Germany and the USA $^{2}$ measured the productivities as 8.2, 17.6 and 21.1 respectively; the unit used was short tons of newsprint per employee per year. The poor performance of Fleet Street newspapers brought the overall figure down, but the performance of provincial newspapers was still below that of overseas newspapers.

US companies have consistently taken the lead in introducing new technology in newspapers, not just to boost production but to change the product. Until fairly recently, the US did not have national newspapers of the UK model. New technology has been harnessed, however, so that the newspaper USA Today can claim to be a national. Pages are digitized and sent via satellite to various sites in the US.

Newspaper unions have consistently fought against the introduction of new technology. The experience in the US indicates that in opposing the introduction of digital technology they may be acting against the long-term interests of their members. In 1972, US payrolls in the industry were \$3.1 billion and were more than doubled in 1981 when the figure was \$6.3 billion. Capital invested climbed in the same period from \$554 million to \$1.02 billion. Circulations have soared, most dramatically in weekly newspapers which increased from 21 million in 1960 to 49 million in 1985; the circulation of daily newspapers increased from 48.6 million to 63 million in the same period. The number of people employed has actually risen from 345,000 in 1965 to 443,000 in 1984.

Of course these figures are tied in with the increased prosperity of the average citizen, but new technology has assuredly played a part in increasing circulations, providing a wider range of jobs and even increasing the overall number of jobs.

A characteristic of UK newspapers, particularly the national section, is that they have tended to operate on much lower profit margins in comparison with overseas companies. The profit margins of UK national newspapers have been in the region of 2 to 4 per cent.

## Advertising

Advertising revenue forms a large percentage of the overall income of newspapers (100 per cent in the case of a few newspapers mentioned below). In addition to its contribution to the profits of newspapers, advertising has a rather more subtle influence on the newspaper product we receive. The national titles are usually divided into two types: quality and popular. Quality newspapers, selling mainly to the higher social grade of reader, attract a higher advertising rate, and rely on advertising revenue far more than the popular newspapers do. One effect of this is that quality newspapers can afford to assign much higher figures per copy to editorial expenses. Another effect is that newsprint forms a much higher proportion of costs in popular newspapers (up to 40 per cent of costs) than in quality newspapers (as low as 15 per cent of costs).

The relationship between advertising revenue and the newspapers is best illustrated by an example (pp. 78-80). $^{11}$ Lord Thomson gained control of The Times in 1966 and attempted to aim for a much higher circulation. This rose from 256,000 in 1965 to 432,000 in 1969, but a result was a reduction in the proportion of AB readers from around 50 to 43 per cent, in consequence of which The Times lost much of its appeal to advertisers. A new policy was adopted in 1971 and circulation dropped back to 340,000 in 1972, but the proportion of ABs was soon back to 50 per cent, once again attracting advertising revenue so that losses, which in 1971 were £1.4 million, had been cut to £187,000 in 1973. Hirsch and Gordon (p. 79) $^{11}$ say:

The Times is a classic example of the necessity for a newspaper to get all the elements of the formula right if it wants to make a profit, or keep its losses down: an increase in circulation, which seems such a natural objective for a newspaper, proved treacherous since it threw the delicate interrelationships of costs and different kinds of revenue out of balance.

## Rival media

The newspaper industry began to get very alarmed in the late 1970s when commercial videotex systems first appeared. Conferences were organized to examine the potential of the threat. A similar state of panic occurred in the 1950s when television began to expand its sphere of influence. In both cases it transpired that the new media were not rivals but provided complementary information services. In the case of television, this is now recognized by advertising agencies who plan many campaigns to use newspaper and television advertising in concert. Videotex appears to be acquiring a special niche, providing up-to-the-minute details of business information, particularly that of a statistical nature: prices, indices etc. Reports and comments are usually obtained in printed form.

A more significant rival to the traditional, paid-for newspaper is the free newspaper. The earlier form of free newspaper, dating from the late 1960s, consisted almost entirely of advertisements. The current free newspaper is a very different product. A 1983 analysis by Boase Massimi Pollitt indicated that 40 per cent of free newspaper titles gave at least 20 per cent of content to editorial, of which at least half was local news. This is probably the reason for the phonemenal growth in free newspapers. Of course, free newspapers rely entirely on advertising for their revenue so this provides an accurate measure of their success. In 1979 advertising revenue was £35 million. This reached £136 million for 1982, the year in which the circulation of free newspapers overtook that of paid-for weeklies. The predicted income for 1986 was £300 million. The current circulation of free newspapers in Britain is 36 million copies per week.

The free newspaper industry has had a dramatic effect on the regional press. In some cases it has caused the closure of an existing newspaper. For example, the Morning Telegraph, a 130 year-old Sheffield newspaper, closed in 1986 with the loss of 240 jobs. However, not all the effects have been damaging to the regional press. The threat has revitalized many newspapers, forcing them to improve products. In many cases this has been achieved partly as the result of the adoption of new technology. Many regional newspaper publishers now produce one or more free titles in addition to their paid-for titles.

## Industrial relations

National newspapers have a poor record of industrial relations. It is estimated that in 1985 the national newspaper industry lost a total of 95.6 million copies in different disputes. All the major titles were affected. $^{16}$ Recently the conflict between unions and management has centred on the introduction of new technology, but disputes have been occurring for some time. These have been a major factor in the decisions to form three Royal Commissions on the Press since the Second World War. Consideration of new technology featured prominently in the 1974-76 McGregor Commission. $^{3}$ One point made by the Commission was that the 7.7 per cent fall in employment in printing and publishing from 1969 to 1976 was due to the recession, not to the introduction of new technology.

Disputes have continued in Fleet Street newspapers since the McGregor Commission. The Times began to plan for computer composition in 1975 and installed a £3 million system. Efficient use of the system necessitated a reduction in the number of compositors from 333 to 186 and substantial inducements were made to effect this. $^{4}$ However, disputes continued to plague The Times and eventually production was stopped for 11 months in 1979. $^{5}$ It is interesting to note that the dispute began over loss of production on Saturday nights. This led to the suspension of the men involved and the strike started. In the course of the strike, however, the negotiations focused on new technology rather than the initial cause of the trouble.

The paper was acquired by Rupert Murdoch's News International Group, but disruptions continued. There were also disputes at the other newspapers in the Group (the Sunday Times, Sun and News of the World), not only about new technology, or even pay and conditions. On occasion the unions dictated what the papers could print. For example in January 1984, during the miners' dispute, the Sun was forced to run a 300-word article by Arthur Scargill. There was also a stoppage following an editorial attack on the miners (p. 38). $^{6}$

The Daily Telegraph was off the street for two weeks in October 1978, losing 13 million copies and an estimated £1.5 million. The original cause of the dispute was a demand for extra payment for operating telephone equipment by 24 wire-room men. Throughout the dispute, northern editions of the newspaper continued to appear. These were printed at the Manchester office, not in Fleet Street. $^{7}$

The Financial Times suffered production losses in a lengthy dispute from December 1980 to May 1981. Unofficial stoppages were caused by 19 machine managers who were seeking a 33 per cent pay rise. The issue was taken to ACAS after a 10 per cent pay offer, recommended by union officials. $^{8}$

These are just some of the disputes, but they indicate that there are many causes of newspaper disputes, not merely the introduction of new technology.

A turning point in the relationship between newspaper management and unions was reached in 1984. A free newspaper group, the Stockport Messenger Group, successfully took a union, the NGA, to court over a dispute at the Group's new plant at Bury. The Group's owner, Eddy Shah, had a closed-shop agreement with the NGA at another plant, Fineward, from its opening in 1979. He tried to reach a similar agreement at the Bury plant but the talks ended in deadlock. By August 1982, the plant was using some non-union labour because of the failure to reach agreement, and this led to a strike on 4 July 1983 by six NGA men at the Stockport plant who sought to impose a closed-shop agreement at all the Group's sites (then numbering three with the addition of one at Warrington). The men were dismissed and there followed mass picketing at the Warrington site, the scene of some violent clashes. Based on the 1980 and 1982 Employment Acts, the High Court granted two injunctions against the NGA. The union did not comply with these and on 30 November 1983 the union's funds were seized, and the NGA was fined £525,000 for contempt of court on 9 December 1983. On 18 January 1984, the National Council of the NGA voted to end its unlawful actions, although it still remained committed to working for closed-shop agreements. $^{9}$

This success was probably the incentive that Mr Shah needed, as shortly afterwards plans were announced for the launch of Today, a new national newspaper. In turn, this has led to a revitalization of the London-based nationals. The first to move was Rupert Murdoch's News International which moved to a site in Tower Hamlets, East London, following a strike call on 24 January 1986 by the two main unions involved in negotiations at News International: the NGA and Sogat '82. Journalists and members of the electricians' union, the EETPU, moved to the new site and only 26 hours after the strike call all the presses were printing at Wapping. As with the Messenger dispute, there has been mass picketing and violence, and attempts to stop distribution of papers, the rail unions co-operating with Sogat '82 members in the latter. $^{6}$

Disputes may arise from various causes but in most cases it is not a simple case of management versus unions. News International could not have moved without the co-operation of members of the EETPU. It was only the NGA and Sogat '82 which opposed the move. Where new technology exacerbates industrial relations problems is not so much on the issue of job losses, which have been highlighted in the News International dispute, but in the erosion of boundaries between different unions' activities. Thus in 1981 there was a dispute between two unions at the Daily Mirror over who does what in relation to new production methods.

The obvious way out of this situation is to have only one union. This ideal is far away but there has been some rationalization. In 1961 there were nine unions involved in discussion in Fleet Street newspapers. This was reduced to six in 1974 (four printing unions and two engineering unions). In 1982 two unions, Sogat and NATSOPA, merged to become Sogat '82, so there are now five unions in negotiations. However, the situation becomes further confused by the adoption of direct input, when journalists and other non-printing union personnel become involved.

When Rupert Murdoch dismissed 5,500 News International workers in January 1986 they gained much public sympathy. This appears to have been misplaced. In 1984, following three years of negotiations aimed at rationalizing the pay structure of compositors, the linotype operators at the Sun are reputed to have been guaranteed wages of £43,000 a year (p. 36). $^{6}$ However, even these pay scales were lower than other London newspapers. By the end of 1985, linotype operators at the Daily Express were getting £1,100 for a16-hour week! (p. 34) $^{6}$ Evidently, these payments were based on a ‘London scale of prices’. Originating 200 years ago, this scale provided extra pay even for leaving spaces between lines. Thus a compositor could set 50 lines and be paid for 250 lines! (p. 35) $^{6}$ Moreover, new technology has not overcome the problem of irrational pay but has made the system worse as it has been used in a grossly inefficient manner. For example, when the Systems Development Corporation computerized compositor system was brought into use at the Gray’s Inn Road site, the procedure for setting advertisements was utterly ridiculous. Two unions were involved in the setting: NGA for the text and Sogat for the headers. The system had to be programmed so that each union could access only its requisite file.

Before leaving the subject of industrial relations, it is important to dispel the notion that disputes occur only in UK newspapers. The US newspaper industry has experienced many strikes over the years. In 1978, the West German newspaper industry suffered three weeks of selective strikes, costing the printing union, IG Druck and Papier, over £4½ million in strike pay alone. Only after government intervention and hard bargaining, which had started 18 months before, was agreement reached. This dispute was related to the introduction of new technology. $^{10}$

## National newspapers

Recent developments in Fleet Street newspapers have focused attention on problems in national newspapers. However, the situation is not new. The newspaper industry appears to have had more than its fair share of problems – since the end of the Second World War no less than three Royal Commissions have been set up to investigate the press (in 1947-49, 1961-62 and 1974-76).

The national press has tended to concentrate in London, and Fleet Street in particular, for historical reasons. Production centres were set up near the railway termini from which the major channels of communication radiated to the rest of nineteenth century Britain. They were also close to the sources of news: government and the business community. Of course, for some time it has not been necessary to locate close to the source of news — telecommunications facilities have long made this redundant — but in the early years of the newspapers, the proximity of production centres to news sources must have saved hours in meeting deadlines. Traffic conditions in London have been congested for many years, making Fleet Street a most unlikely location for a manufacturer - delivery of raw material and distribution of products have become extremely difficult. For this reason alone one would expect the dispersal of Fleet Street newspapers, yet it has taken until 1986 for national newspapers to move or initiate moves away from Fleet Street.

The major problem of the Fleet Street newspapers — overmanning — has been known for many years. This has become increasingly important as other, external factors have affected the profitability of newspapers. Declining circulations, the deterioration of the economy which caused a fall in advertising revenue, and the rapid rise in the price of newsprint (doubling in price between 1972 and 1974) were the factors which led to the setting up of the McGregor Commission in 1974. In the national newspaper industry, 1986 may be viewed as a key year. In addition to movement away from Fleet Street, which has been mentioned above, no less than three new titles have been launched. Moreover, these titles were produced by new companies, not existing newspaper publishers. The new titles are Today, the Independent and the Racing Post. Whatever the differences of the three publications, they shared a common marketing mistake. A sample of retailers, which included both the north and south of England, indicated that all three publications were short of copies on the launch day when interest was highest, particularly as television advertising had been used to focus interest on the launch.

Undoubtedly, the most interesting and influential is Today, the brainchild of Eddie Shah, since it was the catalyst in the large-scale move out of Fleet Street and into new technology.

Mr Shah founded a successful chain of local newspapers, the Stockport Messenger Group. He received considerable attention in the press in 1983 because of his opposition to 'closed shop' union practices, sacking some employees and winning the court case which followed. He then planned the new national daily newspaper, Today, which was to be the first completely new title for many years, the last being Sunday papers – the Sunday Telegraph and the Mail on Sunday. The Sunday Telegraph was launched in 1961 but was a stable companion of the Daily Telegraph, one of the then most profitable national newspapers.

Today was intended to be free from overmanning resulting from the restrictive practices which had caused many of Fleet Street's problems. The paper was to be produced utilizing the most up-to-date equipment and would feature colour each day. The paper was launched in March 1986. Today is published by News (UK) Limited, which was set up in 1985.

Today is published seven days per week. From Monday to Saturday it contains 40 pages with 16 in full colour. The Sunday edition is much larger, containing 80-96 pages with 32 in full colour, the cover price being raised from 20p to 30p on that day. Staff number around 700, including 155 journalists. The total original investment in News (UK) was £22 million but a new financial package was announced in June 1986 which involved the injection of £13½ million by the Lonrho Group. This saved the paper from closure, but further finance was needed and in August 1986 Lonrho took control of News (UK). Lonrho is also the owner of the Observer.

Today suffered various troubles from the outset. Union opposition to new technology meant that an independent distribution network had to be set up. This had very high overheads, and by August 1986 distribution had been switched back to traditional, unionized distribution channels. The electronic production system also created problems. In the early days, the colour pictures were very poor and a target for satire. Probably the biggest failure was that the publication lacked identity. By September the circulation was around 335,000 copies per day for the Monday to Saturday newspaper, well below the break-even figure.

News (UK) claims that Today has the most modern newspaper production system in the world. The computer system can handle both text and graphics electronically. The two main suppliers are Crosfield and the US company, Hastech. Hastech supply the terminals 'Magician' to handle text and 'Page Pro' to handle full page make-up and graphics. Photographs are scanned and digitized. They are stored temporarily in the computer system while pages are made up. Once a page has been approved on a page layout terminal it is sent via Mercury's data communications service to the three print sites in Manchester, Birmingham and West London. Datrax equipment is used in this process, creating film from the electronic signals which is then used for making the litho plates ready for use on the presses.

The Independent was launched in October 1986, having been conceived mainly by Andreas Whittam Smith, former City Editor of the Daily Telegraph. Planning of the paper began early in 1985, and by April 1986 £18 million had been raised in what is claimed to be the UK's largest ever venture capital start-up from scratch. The Independent is published by Newspaper Publishing plc. The title was chosen after market research and reflects its editorial freedom from political influence.

The computer production system for the Independent is supplied by Atex, the US company which is the world's largest supplier of newspaper terminals. The system allows direct input and full page make-up, some 170 terminals being involved altogether for editorial, advertising and page make-up. Once the pages are made up in electronic form they are sent to printing sites via British Telecom landlines using Datrax equipment. The newspaper is then printed under contract at various printing companies in Bradford, Portsmouth, Sittingbourne and Peterborough.

The Racing Post is a specialized interest newspaper. Published weekly, it is aimed at the betting public and competes with the Sporting Life, which previously had the field to itself. In response to the launch of the Racing Post, Sporting Life immediately cut its cover price.

## Exodus from Fleet Street

The launch of Today was the spur to the creation of the Independent and has also sparked off considerable activity among Fleet Street newspapers. News International (The Times [503,000], the Sunday Times [1.1 million], Sun [4 million] and News of the World [4.8 million]) moved into a new plant at Wapping, East London in January 1986. The cost of the move was around £70 million, of which around £7 million was the cost of the computer composition system.

The move was attacked by the printing unions, who failed in their attempt to get the EEPTU members, who had transferred to the Wapping plant, expelled from the TUC. Mass picketing took place throughout the year and there were many violent clashes at the picket lines. However, the company was able to print and distribute its newspapers.

The success of News International in moving out of Central London prompted other newspapers to plan to follow. The Daily Telegraph is to move its printing from Fleet Street to a £75 million plant on the Isle of Dogs, East London. Agreement was reached with unions in September 1986 on new working conditions and manning levels. Negotiations on these points had been in progress for two years. The agreement will result in 970 redundancies out of a printing workforce of 1,630. The newspaper has not yet decided on a new location for its editorial offices. The Daily Telegraph has a circulation of 1.14 million and also produces the Sunday Telegraph with a circulation of 675,000. The last trading figure showed a total loss of £36.4 million.

Associated Newspapers, publishers of the Daily Mail (1.83 million), Mail on Sunday (1.63 million)

and London Standard (517,000) plan to move its printing from the present location just south of Fleet Street to a site in what was formerly the Surrey Docks. Outside estimates $^{12}$ suggest that in the year to September 1985, the Daily Mail and the Standard made profits of £2.3 million and £1.5 million respectively, while the Mail on Sunday made a loss of £8 million.

The Guardian (538,000), presently with offices in Farringdon Road, is printed under contract at The Times presses in Gray's Inn Road. A £23 million plant in London's docklands is planned to come into full production in 1988. Negotiations are in progress to reduce the workforce of 1,000 by 200. It is estimated that the paper makes a profit of £500,000. $^{12}$

The Observer (782,000), currently located at Blackfriars, planned to move its editorial offices to Battersea in March 1987 when printing would be carried out under contract at four regional centres: Portsmouth, Bradford, Peterborough and Worcester. The Observer is owned by Lonrho which had profits of £158.3 million. The paper is losing money, however, and is not expected to move into profit until two years after its move. $^{12}$

The Financial Times (258,000) was, in a sense, the first national to move part of its printing out of London. An agreement with the printing unions allowed it to send pages by facsimile to Frankfurt for its European edition. At the time, production of the Financial Times was a peculiar mix of old and new technologies: hot metal typesetting in London, computerized photo composition in Germany for the remainder of the pages which are not sent by facsimile (the agreement allows only 20 pages out of the 40-page Frankfurt edition to be typeset locally, and is the reason for the use of facsimile). Negotiations are in progress for a move to an East London printing site, which is estimated to cost £55 million. Redundancies of 400 out of the current 1,500 employees are expected as part of the package. Editorial will remain at the present site. The Financial Times is a different type of publication from the rest of the national newspapers. Since 1979 it has been international, styling itself 'Europe's business newspaper' when it started production in Frankfurt. At present 49,000 copies are printed in Frankfurt and 10,000 in New York, while 199,000 are printed in London. The paper is part of the Information and Entertainments Division of the Pearson Group which made a profit of £49.9 million. The contribution to these profits made by the Financial Times is not known but it has been suggested $^{13}$ that the newspaper is basically profitable.

The Financial Times has had a new rival in Europe since 1983: the Wall Street Journal. The US newspaper has used digital technology for some time: it beams full pages from five production centres to a satellite in geostationary orbit 22,000 miles above the equator and down to 17 printing centres throughout the US. The American editions have a circulation of over 2 million and are produced by only 650 production staff. Having successfully launched an Asian edition, the Wall Street Journal turned its attention to Europe, as the next stage in its attempts to become the world's first global newspaper. Copy for the European edition is received at a land station at Lessive in Belgium, from where it is sent via landline to an editorial office in Brussels. Once a story has been edited it is then sent via landline to Heerlen, 70 miles away in Holland, where it is automatically output on a phototypesetter. After paste-up and platemaking, the newspaper is printed at that site. The use of digital technology gives the Wall Street Journal considerable advantages in deadlines. Copy for the Frankfurt edition of the Financial Times must be sent by 8.30 pm from London; the presses in West Germany commence running two hours later. The Brussels office of the Wall Street Journal then still has up to 1½ hours to its deadline. $^{13}$

The two other major national newspaper groups have not yet announced any plans to move out of Central London. Fleet Holdings, publishers of the Daily Express (1.8 million), Sunday Express (2.3 million) and Daily Star (1.4 million) have not revealed any plans to move away from Fleet Street. Mirror Group Newspapers (Daily Mirror [3 million], Sunday Mirror [2.99 million] and Sunday People [2.98 million]) are to continue to publish from the Holborn offices. Mirror Group Newspapers are in a rather different category from the other national newspaper publishers. Printing facilities are owned by BPPC, a separate company in Robert Maxwell's publishing empire. These are all located outside the City. Both groups are profitable, Fleet Holdings making £9.9 million profit in the year to June 1985. Mirror Group Newspapers made a profit of £5.7 million in 1983-84. Both companies have also reduced staff in recent years.

Thus all the UK nationals are now using, or announcing plans to use, new technology. Atek is supplying a major share of the front-end systems. Its equipment will be used at News International, Associated Newspapers (Mail and Standard), Telegraph, Guardian, Financial Times, the Independent and Sunday Sport. News UK use Hastech systems; Fleet Holdings use Ferranti and Mirror Group use Crossfield CS1. Printing is carried out on a wide variety of presses using letterpress, offset and, at Associated Newspapers, flexographic printing processes.

For some time, most of the national newspapers were not profitable but kept in business because they were part of larger groups which earned vast profits from such sources as North Sea oil. The use of new technology, or perhaps we should say more efficient production, should enable the national newspapers to become more profitable. Indeed, the financial company, Phillips and Drew, has forecast that profit margins of national newspapers could rise, as a result of the moves and negotiations in progress, from 2-4 per cent to 10-14 per cent. $^{12}$

The national newspaper industry is entering an interesting phase. In addition to the new titles which have already appeared, there are plans for others. Sunday Sport and News on Sunday are two new Sunday newspapers scheduled to appear in 1987. This will raise the number of titles to 21.

A new newspaper, the London Daily News, was published on 24 February 1987, two weeks after the original date scheduled for the launch of the new title. Protracted negotiations with the NGA had caused the delay. The London Daily News is an addition to Robert Maxwell's publishing empire. The agreement with the NGA will allow direct input not only at the London Daily News but, later in the year, at the Daily Mirror, Sunday Mirror and the People. The London Daily News will provide 48 jobs which would otherwise have been lost at the other titles. $^{17}$

The appearance of the new title prompted a press war. Associated Newspapers, publishers of what had been London's only evening paper, relaunched the Evening News. (This title had disappeared from the streets in October 1980, following a merger with the Evening Standard.) The cover price of the London Daily News was dropped from 20p to 10p. Editorials in the newspapers have viciously attacked the other group's publication. The Evening Standard took a full page advertisement in The Times of 6 March 1987 to indicate that its sales were holding up and suggest that sales of the London Daily News were disappointing.

It is too early to say whether the London Daily News will be successful or not. What can be said is that all the new titles launched under the umbrella of new technology have found it difficult to reach the circulation figures necessary for continued survival. The problems at Today have been mentioned above. After a reasonably successful launch, the Independent is also starting to struggle. It sold 650,000 copies on the first day of publication, 7 October 1986, but by the end of the year estimates of the circulation were 262,000 or lower. This would be more than 100,000 below its circulation target.

New publications are expected to lose money in the first year or two. During this period, however, they should show signs of reaching a target circulation which will eventually ensure profitability. So far, Today, the Independent and the London Daily News have not shown that they will attain the requisite circulation levels. Based on results so far, it appears questionable to launch a new newspaper on the platform of new technology. On the other hand, the use of new technology in more efficient production methods at existing titles is already seen to pay off. Profits at News International, following its move to Wapping, are believed to be well above levels predicted at the time of the move.

The national newspaper industry sells nearly 15 million newspapers each weekday (around 12.2 million popular and 2.4 million quality) and nearly 18 million on Sundays (15 million popular and 2.6 million quality). These figures have been stable over the past few years and are not expected to decrease. The market share of each newspaper, particularly with the advent of new titles, could change considerably. The situation will be watched closely by advertising agencies who will also vet the composition of each newspaper's readership.

## Case study: News International

News International, owned by Rupert Murdoch, publishes four UK national newspapers: The Times, the Sunday Times, Sun and News of the World. The parent company, News Corporation, publishes in the USA and Australia and reported a turnover of £1.84 billion for the year ended June 1986, an increase of 56 per cent. Net profits were also increased by 52 per cent.

The UK company is unique in that it produces newspapers at opposite ends of the publishers' spectrum, The Times being perhaps the premier quality national title while the Sun is widely regarded as being at the bottom of the hierarchy of national titles.

The Sun has a rather strange history. It is an example of a complete transformation of a newspaper. The Daily Herald was founded in 1912 by George Lansbury who designed it as the 'conscience of the Labour movement'. It was plagued by financial problems throughout its lifetime. In 1922 the Trades Union Congress and the National Executive Committee of the Labour Party assumed responsibility for it. In 1929, Odhams acquired a 51 per cent share. An excessively expensive promotion campaign pushed circulation to a peak of

2 million which slowly declined to 1.7 million in 1955 and 1.4 million in 1960. In 1961 the Daily Mirror Group gained control of Odhams and, along with it, the Daily Herald, which they promised to continue for seven years. However, the Daily Herald reflected the image of the traditional, cloth-capped Labour supporter, which was not the image sought by the Labour Party under Harold Wilson. A publishing metamorphosis occurred in September 1964, the tabloid format of the Sun taking the place of the Daily Herald. Despite the successful election of a Labour government, however, the Sun was not an overnight success. Indeed, in 1965 its circulation reached only 1.3 million, the same as the Herald in 1963. It was estimated that a circulation of 1.7 million was required in order that the newspaper should be profitable so the newspaper's poor financial performance continued. In 1969, News International bought the Sun and the scene was completely changed. 'Politics went, naked ladies arrived, and the circulation soared' (p. 67). $^{11}$

The Times has a much longer pedigree, as well as a better one, its publishing life covering over two centuries. The newspaper was founded by John Walter, the first issue appearing on 1 January 1785. Originally titled 'The Daily Universal Register', the now familiar title was adopted for the 940th edition on 1 January 1788. The history of The Times is virtually a history of newspaper publishing. The first edition cost $2 \frac{1}{2} d$ (including $1 \frac{1}{2} d$ stamp duty) and circulation was approximately 1,000. By 1815 circulation reached about 4,500, price 7d (stamp duty 4d). Stamp duty was reduced to 1d in 1836 and the price reduced to 5d, and probably this helped to boost sales; one issue in 1839 (reporting the Queen's betrothal) sold 30,000 copies. Circulation reached 65,000 in 1865, a year before the first 'display ad' was printed, but circulation then fell for a number of years.

The Times was owned by descendants of the founder until 1908 when it was bought by Lord Northcliffe. He brought in a new administration and re-equipped the outdated printing plant. The price was reduced to 2d, and then to 1d in 1914 – the circulation soared immediately to 150,000 and soon reached 300,000. A decline followed during the First World War and afterwards so that the circulation was 110,000 in 1922, but by 1938 it had recovered to 203,000.

The Times was bought by Lord Thomson in 1966, some seven years after he had acquired the Sunday Times. Circulation was 294,000 in that year. The price rose over the next few years reaching one shilling in 1970, 15p in 1977 and 20p in 1980. In 1981 Rupert Murdoch bought Times Newspapers Limited. A new record daily sale of

464,000 was reached in 1984 when the price was 25p. The development of newspaper technology is also intertwined with the history of The Times. The first editions were set by hand and comprised four pages. In 1800 new Stanhope presses, printing 250 copies, were introduced while in 1814 the first steam-driven presses in the world were used at The Times. Faster and better presses were introduced in 1828, 1858, 1866, 1895 and 1914. Stereotype plates were used to print the whole paper in 1859. Various typesetting machines were introduced. The Times became the first London daily to set type mechanically in 1873 using Kastenbein typesetting machines. The Kastenbein equipment was replaced by Monotype machines in 1908. Intertype machines were introduced in 1914 and Linotype equipment in 1926. In 1970 The Times was the first British national to publish pages (Stock Exchange) by photocomposition and in 1982 the first national to be set entirely by photocomposition. In 1951 Parliamentary reports were sent by teletype and set automatically. Pre-printed colour pages were used in 1967.

There have also been disputes in the history of the newspaper. The first was possibly in 1810, following which unions were excluded until 1912. In 1816 a dispute arose over extra payment for handling very small (6 point) type. Disputes have become progressively more serious in recent years. They occurred during 1977, and in 1978 publication was suspended following a succession of disputes. Production resumed 11 months later after an agreement which entailed a 15 per cent reduction in staff, production improvements and a new dispute procedure. In 1980 it was the journalists' turn to strike, while in 1983 the electricians stopped production for eight days.

Following several years of negotiations, aimed at achieving more realistic manning levels, proposals for a new industrial relations formula led to a strike in January 1986. This was in breach of contract and led to the dismissal of 5,500 workers. In just over a day production was switched to a site in Wapping, Tower Hamlets, which had been acquired in 1980 for use as a press centre only. During 1985, pre-press equipment had been moved into the plant, ostensibly for producing a new newspaper, the London Post. This equipment, of course, was fully utilized for the four existing titles when the overnight move to Wapping took place.

Following the move to Wapping and production of the four newspapers, mass picketing took place at the gates of the plant and neighbouring areas. There were also violent attacks on delivery vehicles throughout the year. At the time of the visit to the plant, in December 1986, the picket at the gates was reduced to five people although there were more stationed in Wapping High Way. The former politely informed callers that they were crossing an official picket line; the latter shouted much stronger abuse.

The front-end system chosen for the Wapping site was supplied by Atex. An original short list of three potential suppliers had been reduced to two (the other supplier was SII) but only Atex could meet the required time. Twenty-five DEC 11/34s provide the computing power. These are configured in three networks of 11,7 and 7 cpus; one for The Times, one for the Sun/News of the World and the third for classified advertisements. It is possible to communicate between networks.

Some 540 terminals are connected to the three networks. Each user has a password which provides entry at an appropriate level. There are two types of keyboard (which are easily moved from one terminal to another); 40 per cent are designed as writers' or input terminals and have 94 keys. The larger proportion are editors' keyboards and have 134 keys. These have more single-key functions, and as writers become familiar with the smaller keyboard they seek to gain access to the 134-key layouts. It is now felt that it may have been a mistake to opt for two different types of keyboard.

The system is operated in the direct input manner — journalists key in their reports. In addition, there are 22 keyboard operators (12 for the Sun/News of the World, 10 for The Times/Sunday Times) who key in reports received over the telephone and other copy.

The systems are also able to receive input from various external inputs. Journalists in the field are using Tandy 200s or Epsom portable microcomputers or IBM pc compatibles at home. The company is experimenting with a number of options along this line and encouraging the use of personal computers.

Computer facilities set up at the Houses of Parliament provide a direct link into the system for 14 terminals. Similar facilities were set up as needed at each of the party conferences. Other direct links to the system supply input from Datasolve World Reporter database, and wire services. Telex may be input and output from the system.

For production, the system outputs through six phototypesetters; two Auto Logic APS5s at The Times/Sunday Times composing room, two APS5s at the Sun/News of the World composing room, and two APS micro 5s at the classified department. Pages are then pasted up according to a dummy layout and sent to the plate-making area where Letterflex (photopolymer letterpress) plates are made. These are then mounted on Goss Headliner presses. There are 48 press units at the site.

The site consists of two main buildings, situated a road-width apart. The larger building houses the editorial offices of the Sun and News of the World on separate floors, as well as the plate-making and pressroom areas. Delivery and distribution vehicles have easy access for setting down or picking up their cargoes. The Times and the Sunday Times editorial offices are located in a smaller building, formerly a rum warehouse built by French prisoners of war in the early nineteenth century. The Atex system is devoted to pre-press operations. Two other separate computer systems are in use: a Siemens configuration for circulation/distribution and PCS system for billing.

In view of the rapid set-up of the system, the need to train people while working and constraints on the system (e.g. the average number of terminals per CPU is 32 against the figures of 28 [maximum] and 24 [optimal] recommended by Atex), the system has performed creditably. Measures of the performance of the system are that it receives $1 \frac{1}{2}$ million characters per day via wire services alone and that 200,000 words from The Times are archived on disc and kept for six months. The main deficiencies lie in the response time but improvements are being added all the time to make the system go faster.

In addition to the Atex terminals, Xenotron terminals are used to make up classified advertisements. Also, there are five Atex GT 68s which provide some page make-up facilities, although graphics are not displayed. Presumably, at an opportune time, electronic page make-up will be introduced.

## Provincial newspapers

The provincial press is a very important publishing body which generally receives much less publicity than its national counterpart. Of the publishers in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, 90 per cent belong to the Newspaper Society which was founded in 1836. Scottish newspapers have their own association. The 267 member companies of the Newspaper Society publish some 1,300 titles, made up of 22 morning, 73 evening, five Sunday, 850 weekly paid-for and 380 weekly free newspapers. Most free newspapers belong to their own organization, the Association of Free Newspapers, and the total number of free newspapers is about the same number as paid-for weekly newspapers, 850. $^{14}$

Evening newspaper circulations averaged nearly 5,800,000 in the six months of January to June 1986. This was nearly 37,000 down on the 1985 figures, some 13,000 being accounted for by the disappearance of the Evening Despatch (Darlington) which ceased publication. The leading publication was the Express and Star with sales of 333,823, exceeded only by the Standard (London)

with 523,000. The Express and Star was also ranked number one by mil rate. $^{15}$ Generally the most successful regional newspapers are amalgamations of more than one newspaper. There are four groups in particular which dominate the provincial newspaper scene, Thompson Regional Newspapers, Westminster Press, Northcliffe Newspapers and United Provincial Newspapers. All four publish titles in more than one region of the country. For example, Thompson Regional Newspapers publishes in Newcastle upon Tyne, Cardiff, Belfast and Scotland. These groups together publish 48 daily newspapers, 194 weekly paid-for newspapers and 92 free newspapers at 74 locations (Associated Newspapers do not publish any free newspapers). $^{14}$

The regional press relies heavily on advertising revenue and is one of the country's largest advertising media. It captured one quarter of the £4 billion spent by advertisers last year, an amount exceeded only by television. In contrast, national newspapers attracted 18.6 per cent of this total while magazines accounted for 15 per cent. The share of posters, radio, cinema and directories together totalled less than 12 per cent.

Technology has played a part in the success of regional newspapers as advertising media. At one time, regional newspapers found it difficult to attract advertising from outside their immediate area but this has now changed. The Regional Newspaper Advertising Bureau (RNAB) was set up in 1980 to promote the regional press to advertisers and also to simplify the procedure for booking space in a number of titles. Advertisers may place advertisements in many newspapers with just one booking and one invoice, using the RNAB's centralized booking system. Advertisers who have used the system include the Co-operative Wholesale Society, the Bank of Scotland and the government's Central Office of Information. The system incorporates census-based data, classifying the type of people living in a particular area, together with the 8,900 post code sectors and the sales of local newspapers in these sectors. The recent experience of a holiday company provides an example of the way the system helps to place effective advertisements. The company had a special offer for retired people. The system provided a list of newspapers by identifying areas with a high proportion of retired people. When the holiday firm advertised in these newspapers it received five times the response it received elsewhere. $^{14}$

The provincial press introduced new technology long before national newspapers. The News of Portsmouth claims to be the first newspaper in the world with a circulation over 100,000 to introduce computerized phototypesetting, which it achieved in 1969. The Nottingham Evening Post was the first to successfully institute direct input. This was achieved only after confrontation with the unions, and other companies were reluctant to take such a radical course. There was a switch to computer composition but the NGA firmly controlled the input of copy which reduces many of the benefits of computerized systems. Forty-two members of the Newspaper Society reached agreement or were in active and urgent discussions with their employees for the introduction of direct input by the end of 1986. In some cases, (see Express and Star below) the transition to new technology has been relatively painless. In other cases, there has been considerable opposition. For example, Thompson Regional Newspapers experienced considerable disruption at its Reading branch in 1985. In the same year, Northcliffe Newspapers had a four-week dispute involving the NGA.

## Case Study: Express and Star and Shropshire Star group of newspapers (Midlands News Association Limited)

It is often said that newspapers are fierce independent in pursuing their own ways of doing things. The following outline of the Express and Star provides a clear example of the veracity of this statement, particularly in relation to the introduction of new technology in newspaper production.

The combined circulation of the Express and Star series of titles (ten editions) is 333,823 (January-June 1986 figures), making it the leading provincial newspaper in the UK. In addition, ten free newspapers and a sports newspaper are published. The company is a family-run concern, the Graham family being involved in its management since 1880 when Thomas Graham was one of the founders of the Evening Star (Graham became sole proprietor nearly 20 years later). The Graham family also have interests in the control of News Shops Limited, Press Computer Systems Limited and Precision Colour Printing Limited, which have been formed as a result of Midland News Association's attitude to business and technology. These companies are controlled by the parent company, the Claverly Company.

The Evening Star was quite successful almost immediately, establishing a circulation of 10,000, sufficient to provide a solid financial base without generating enormous profits. This was at a time when it was easy to publish a new title. The West Midlands was no exception to this, Wolverhampton alone having had some 28 newspapers in 100 years. Some came and went almost overnight — the Wolverhampton Mercury survived for only two issues! The paper outsold the Evening Express, a rival newspaper founded many years before the Evening Star, and this led to a merger in 1884, the name Express and Star being used from 1889. Circulation grew steadily reaching 70,000 by the early 1920s, nearly 140,000 by the end of the Second World War, and passing 200,000 in 1960.

A factor in the success of the Express and Star is undoubtedly the influence of the Graham family. Thomas Graham established the independence of the paper from political parties when he became sole proprietor. Although he was a Liberal he ensured that the newspaper was liberal (with a small 'l') and the editorial staff were free to make up their own minds on any issues. His grandson, Malcolm Graham, the current chairman of the Claverly Company — the parent company — has backed the technology policy of the Express and Star. At an early age he was sent to earn his living in the newspaper industry in Canada. This must have helped to shape a more enlightened attitude to innovation, and certainly a less parochial attitude than could be found in the managements of other UK newspapers of the time.

The adoption of new technology has been most dramatic since the early 1970s. However, the Express and Star's enlightened attitude to innovation has a much longer pedigree. For example, newspaper history was made when a telephone line was booked to relay news of the FA Cup Final from Crystal Palace to the Wolverhampton office. The people of Wolverhampton learnt of the Wolverhampton Wanderers' win against Newcastle United before the news circulated in London. Another 'first' was the introduction of a 'tele-ads' service: the receipt of advertisements over the telephone. This was initiated in the early 1950s when very few homes had a telephone.

A spur to the more recent integrated approach to technology was the launch of the new paper, the Shropshire Star, in October 1964. This utilized a new web-offset plant and photo-composition, the first in the UK. At that time, the Express and Star was still using hot metal composition. To rationalize the situation it was necessary to develop a system of production which would facilitate the sharing of resources at two sites and enable the efficient production of different editions. This required some 20 years to achieve and then only by embracing new developments in technology.

One important requirement was a computer system for inputting, editing and composing. The objective was to develop the system in-house, rather than adopt and modify existing systems (at the time these were only available from American companies and were not really suitable), and this led to the formation of Press Computer Systems Limited in 1973. This is an autonomous company within the group, although it supplied the computer composition system used at the Express and Star. It has expanded to become the leading supplier of integrated computer systems to UK newspapers.

The intention to feature colour in its newspapers also required the involvement of new technology. Again, this led to the formation of a new company, Precision Colour Printing Limited, in 1981, which produces colour pages for the group's newspapers and for other newspapers throughout the UK. The Express and Star newspapers were the first in the UK to print full colour photographs on a daily basis (always five days a week and sometimes six).

At this point it is pertinent to describe the current use of technology in the group's newspapers. Readers unfamiliar with printing technology may wish to refer to the Glossary and Appendix: Digital Newspaper Technology.

The cornerstone is the integrated computer system (Press 11) developed by Press Computer Systems Limited. Computing power is provided by DEC 11-84 computers and the system is specifically designed for single keying of both advertising and editorial copy. Advertisements are keyed in directly, many of them from telephone requests, the ad-girls receiving prompts from the system. For example, if the advertisement is for a house sale, the computer prompts so that the house is fully described – detached, semi-detached or terrace, garage, centrally heated etc. This reduces the chances of errors, minimizes the time needed to place the ad, ensures that the advertisement will conform to the Express and Star's style and, what is more important, gives the customer the best opportunity to sell the advertised article. Journalists key in stories and copy, and news is also fed directly into the system from news agencies.

All the terminals used by the tele-ad girls and journalists have a standard format displaying 28 lines of text, each 80 characters in length. Although the system provides aids to copy fitting there are two tasks in particular which can only be carried out on larger, more detailed screens. Terminals are provided by Xenotron, a UK supplier. These tasks are the creation and input of display ads and full-page make-up. Display ads have been created in this fashion since May 1986. Full-page make-up of classified advertising pages was expected to commence in November 1986.

Editorial pages were also to be made up in this fashion. Photographs and drawings can be scanned into the system. This is mainly used at present in the creation of display advertisements, but could be used for full-page make-up of editorial pages which include photographs. At present, paste-up is the method of assembling pages.

Once the page has been pasted up, a plate is made by photographic means and positioned on the press ready for printing. Page images are also sent electronically via facsimile machines to the West Bromwich printing plant where plates are made and used on web-offset presses. (The presses at the Wolverhampton plant were originally letter-press machines which have been converted to use a process called direct lithography – Di-Litho.) The West Bromwich plant at Black Lake was opened in 1976. Some ten miles from the Wolverhampton office, it is linked by a British Telecom line to facilitate the facsimile transmission of pages.

Colour pages are produced at a third plant, at the premises of Precision Colour Printing Limited at Telford. Advertisements, news and sports photographs are re-printed, re-reeled and taken by road to the other two plants. Generally, the same quality of newsprint is used as for the standard black and white pages. Usually, colour printing is carried out overnight but on occasion, same-day insertions have been made. For special supplements, such as that for the Royal Wedding on 24 June 1986, a higher quality paper is used.

Provincial newspapers rely on advertising for a major part of their revenue. A feature of the system is that every customer placing an advertisement is automatically checked for credit rating. The Express and Star also have an electronic library, brought in to replace the collection of 'clippings' which is the traditional newspaper library. This is run on a separate VAX 750 computer although stories are transferred automatically from the Press 11 system. The first task of the library staff each day is to sift through the 800 or so stories which have been added, decide which to keep (usually about half) and add keywords to aid retrieval. Operational since 1985, the system permits full-text search as well as search by keyword. Each item is given a temporary or permanent status when entered into the library, temporary items being deleted when necessary.

From the above description, it can be seen that the company has an extensive commitment to digital technology. What then are the benefits of this technology? Apparently not simple financial advantages, although turnover has climbed steadily from 1980 to 1985, the annual figures being 23.2, 25.3, 26.8, 29.3, 32.3 and 33.8 million pounds (rounded to 1 decimal place). For the same periods the profits have been 3.3, 2.4, 1.9, 3.0,

3.4 and 2.8 million pounds yielding profit to turnover figures of 14, 9, 7, 10, 10 and 8 per cent.

The 1985 accounts indicated that staff costs (wages, social security and pensions) accounted for 45 per cent of expenses, while new materials accounted for 32 per cent. Newsprint alone adds $4 \frac{1}{2} p$ to the cost of each copy.

The paper's circulation has been stable over the past few years and the number of employees (around 1,000) also has not changed. However, the newspapers have kept their readership while other newspapers have lost substantial numbers of subscribers. The use of technology must have played an important part in this, although technology alone will not sell newspapers.

## Conclusion

Information technology has played an increasingly important role in newspaper production for more than two decades. For most of this time, UK newspapers have lagged behind their overseas counterparts. There are some notable exceptions. The News of Portsmouth claimed to be the first newspaper in the world with a circulation of over 100,000 to introduced computerized phototype-setting, which it did in 1969. The Nottingham Evening Post adopted a direct input system in 1976 and claimed that it could get news from source to street in 20 minutes. The new printing plant opened by George Outram at Glasgow in 1980 claimed to be the most up-to-date newspaper printing plant in Europe, if not the world. For almost the whole of this period, however, Fleet Street newspapers have been produced by what is virtually nineteenth century technology. Even where some modern equipment such as facsimile was used to transmit pages to other sites both in the UK and overseas (e.g. Financial Times), pages were made up by casting molten metal on machines which had changed little since their introduction in the 1880s. The use of production methods based on this outmoded technology was one of the reasons for the low profitability of national newspapers but, undoubtedly, the main reason for the plight of Fleet Street newspapers was generations of weak management. For nearly 100 years, Fleet Street proprietors conceded virtually every demand made by the unions to ensure continuous production of their very profitable papers. As a result the unions became more and more powerful. In 1979, management at Times Newspapers Limited tried to turn back the clock but this only led to a closure for 11 months.

Printing unions continually claim that the main reason for their opposition to new technology is to avoid redundancies. This has won a large measure of public sympathy, particularly at a time of high unemployment. The outmoded technology necessitated a level of overmanning although the unions have pushed up this level far beyond the requirement for normal production. If copy was received close to the deadline, management had to ensure that there was a sufficient number of compositors to key it in. Most of the time, however, the edition would be 'put to bed' long before the end of a shift. Thus, although personnel worked unsocial hours, the working day was very short (a 16-hour week was commonplace). These inefficient practices would make any production method unprofitable, however effective the technology. In the wake of the News International dispute, unions have proclaimed a commitment to a 'job-for-life' policy. This appears to be an outmoded policy in the present social and economic climate. Certainly, more people lost their jobs at News International following the strike call than would have been the case if the move had taken place by negotiation. In the US the introduction of new technology may have caused loss of jobs at particular newspapers but a profitable, expanding newspaper industry eventually created more jobs. This pattern might have been followed here if the print unions had adopted a more forward-looking policy.

It is clear that Fleet Street is a special situation where the benefits of new technology are actually the benefits of improved management. The situation in the provinces provides a better viewpoint for judging the effects of information technology. Newspaper managements in the provinces, however, still tend to view new technology only as an aid to more efficient production. At the Nottingham Evening Post, for example, it was felt that technology was an aid for profitability, and that the use of new technology had no effect on editorial except to allow input closer to the deadline. The Nottingham Evening Post was the first newspaper in the UK to introduce direct input but after ten years' experience it is felt that new technology has not provided any other benefits such as increased market share.

This view of new technology is widely held in newspapers. It is refreshing to find the view of David Humphreys, General Manager of the Express and Star, who feels that you cannot count the benefits of new technology in financial terms. New technology is an integral part of company policy and is tied in with the marketing strategy. It is not used specifically to save production costs. Indeed, it has required a considerable financial investment, but without its use the paper would not be able to tailor its different local editions to its readers. However, even at the Express and Star, there are no moves to use digital technology to provide new products. Newspaper publishers seem intent on using new technology solely for the purpose of producing newspapers and thus ignoring the spin-off advantages which might ensue. (See report No. 2: Electronic Publishing.)

Perhaps the move to information technology is too recent a phenomenon for benefits (other than faster throughput) to have been realized. After all, only one of the Fleet Street newspaper publishers has moved and News International is still carrying out improvements and modifications to its system. It is considering what use may be made of the new equipment. One of the first ventures may be in selling typesetting services, perhaps to other companies in the same group. Another possibility is the creation of databases which might then be made accessible by outside users. The Times at present uses Datasolve's database but is investigating the possibility of creating its own. Once ventures such as these become commonplace then other products might appear.

One change has already affected the nationals. Greater use is being made of outside printers to print papers at different locations around the UK, thus saving on distribution costs. This is made possible by facsimile transmission. Technology is thus altering the structure of the newspaper publishing industry, which once consisted solely of integrated publisher/printer companies. It is also subtly altering the product, since localized distribution allows later deadlines.

New newspapers, such as the Independent and Today, have been launched, at least partly, on the basis of more efficient production as provided by new technology. These new titles are likely to erode the market share of other newspapers. One may then see a price war, or new marketing ploys such as the various forms of 'bingo' indulged in by most of the nationals. Alternatively, we may see newspapers diversifying into new publishing products.

## Appendix: Digital newspaper technology

The newspaper publishing industry (or at least its innovative companies) can lay claim to being among the first, if not actually the first, to use information technology. The first application of computers to composition in newspapers is believed to be that at the Los Angeles Times in 1962. The company had acquired powerful computing facilities for business and accounting use and wished to explore the possibility of using computers in composition. Unfortunately, the experiment was not a success. This was not a fault of the computer composition system but was due to the unsuitability of the letterpress printing process for computer composition. This is an important consideration, as the widespread use of letterpress has been a factor in delaying the introduction of electronic composition.

At the end of the Second World War newspapers were printed by letterpress. In the 1950s technical developments of the lithographic process improved its quality. At the same time photocomposition was being developed and this was much more suited to lithography than metal letterpress. This resulted in an extremely rapid changeover, so that by 1976 nearly 70 per cent of US newspapers had converted to lithography. These were mainly the smaller newspapers so that in circulation terms it represented only a 30 per cent share, but nevertheless the statistic is quite startling in view of the former dominance of the letterpress process. As with most technology trends the UK has followed the US although lagging some years behind. The trend has continued although it has been checked in recent years by the development of photopolymer letterpress, designed for use with photocomposition. Flexography, another form of relief printing which uses a lighter press, has also made an appearance on the newspaper printing scene.

These technology developments were necessary prerequisites of the introduction of digital prepress technology, which began with the use of computer composition systems in the 1960s. The development of digital phototypesetters in the mid-1960s was the final link in the chain.

Today it is possible to capture text within a composition system via keyboard or any electronic medium. Graphics may also be digitized and stored in electronic form, although only the most advanced systems utilize this approach at present.

Once an item has been edited, it is output on to photographic film or paper via phototypesetters. This is used to create a plate which is then mounted on the press and printing commences. Some advanced systems use a laser plate-making process which eliminates the need for the photographic intermediate. Using such a system all production stages from input to plate-making are carried out within the system.

The US news magazines Time and Newsweek developed systems which allowed them to transmit pages via satellite relay to various print locations. This concept has been adopted by USA Today and the Wall Street Journal for printing newspapers throughout the US and at other locations round the world.

UK companies have contributed to these technology developments in the newspaper industry – perhaps most notably Crosfield Electronics Ltd which supplies Datrax equipment for transmission of newspaper pages to remote sites. Xenotron is a major supplier of page make-up terminals.

## Glossary

composition: the input and preparation of copy in a format suitable for printing.

direct input: a system whereby journalists, editors and tele-ad personnel can input copy; in contradistinction to the traditional working practice in which only special operators — compositors — could prepare copy.

Di-Litho: the trademark for a system which converts letterpress presses to the direct lithography printing process.

direct lithography: a printing process in which the blanket cylinder is omitted and the litho plate directly contacts the paper surface during printing.

EETPU: Electrical, Electronic, Telecommunications and Plumbing Union.

electronic composition: a computer system which allows composition on a computer terminal.

facsimile: the transmission of a document over a telecommunications link. Newspaper facsimile equipment can accept full pages at the receiving station ready to make printing plates.

front-end system: a powerful computer configuration designed for composition.

full-page make-up: the assembly of a newspaper page in its final form, ready to produce a plate.

hot metal composition: the traditional method of preparing copy whereby letters were cast from molten metal.

laser plate maker: a device for transferring printing image on to a plate prior to printing.

letterpress: a traditional printing process in which ink is transferred to the paper surface from the raised portions of the image carrier; also called a relief process.

litho: the commonly used abbreviation of lithography.

lithography: a planographic printing process in which the image areas of the printing plate accept ink and the non-image areas reject ink (usually because they accept water which then repels oil-based ink).

NGA: National Graphical Association – the principal union of compositors who were formerly responsible for preparing all copy.

NUJ: National Union of Journalists.

offset: the customary shortened form of offset lithography. Until fairly recently, nearly all litho printing was offset. The ink from the plate is transferred to an intermediate blanket cylinder and hence to the paper surface.

phototypesetting: a method of creating the image which is to be printed on photographic film or paper (also called photocomposition). Electronic composing systems most commonly output via phototypesetters.

plate: the carrier of the printing image. Usually of metal, the plate is normally imprinted with the printing image by a photo-chemical process. Plates are mounted on press cylinders. Plates are most commonly used in lithography but photopolymer plates are used in a form of letterpress printing as at The Times.

pre-press: any and all of the operations carried out prior to mounting plates on presses.

single-keying: the same as direct input.

Sogat '82: Society of Graphical and Allied Trades which in 1982 merged with another print union, NATSOPA.

tele-ad: the receipt of advertisements via telephone. Computer systems can aid the tele-ad operators with prompts, ensuring rapid input of accurate and good advertising copy, and also provide credit checking facility.

web-offset: litho presses that print on a reel of paper which is cut into pages after printing.
