# A User's View of IT Consultancy

Malcolm Iliff, Information Systems Department, Coutts & Co

## Introduction

A paper on a user's view of consultancy will almost certainly have a bias dependent on the user's personal experience, or by virtue of that user's experience of the corporate environment in which consultants have been employed. A user's view might even be considered too one-sided, though what I wish to demonstrate is that this ought not to be so.

My personal perspective is coloured by the fact that I previously worked as a management consultant before taking on my present appointment as Manager of the Information Systems Department in Coutts & Co. Here it might be said that I act as an internal consultant on information technology, a user of consultants and occasionally, it might also be said, an abuser of consultants.

This paper is prescriptive, as it is simple in its probably over-ambitious objective, namely, to suggest ways of increasing the effectiveness of the consultancy assignment and the consultancy profession. I have a desire to stem the flow of wasted efforted, wasted paper, wasted presentations, wasted recommendations and wasted reports that gather dust, spurn serious consideration, and simply rest unnoticed on the shelves of corporate executive offices.

## Skill sets for user as well as consultant

It was Andrew Carnegie's epitaph on himself that 'here lies a man who knew how to enlist into his service better men than himself'. Anthony Jay, who quoted this in his article in the Harvard Business Review of July/August 1977, also went on to say that 'the correct qualification for success is not to be one of the brightest people but to know how to use them.'

To be able to use a consultant to best effect, the user should recognize that there are skill sets which make individuals into successful consultants. There are also skill sets or a craft that enable one to be a successful user of consultancy services. This appears to me to be a point frequently missed, especially by those who believe that holding the purse strings in a corporation is the only qualification needed for initiating and running a successful consultancy exercise from the user's side. Far too frequently, consultant and client work together with the skill set of neither partner complete, understood, nor practised. Neither, indeed, do I believe that there is a correct perception in many cases of what the skill sets should be on both sides for completion of a successful and mutually profitable assignment. Perhaps, less surprisingly, there is frequently lack of understanding of the role and skills of the other party in the relationship. Anthony Jay in his article listed some 25 principles and pitfalls for the potential and actual user of consultants; it was an article particularly useful for those users-to-be to be better able to select and make proper use of the consultants they wished to employ. Perhaps somebody has also elsewhere discovered and listed 25 or so common shortcomings in the skill sets of consultants themselves. How often has it been the case that when one skill has been present in a budding adviser it has been regarded as sufficient to justify the accolade of 'consultant'?

Management consultancy is not in my view the presentation of a single skill to a willing user, whether in the area of telecommunications, computer architecture, functional analysis, organizational structure or any other topic in the realm of IT or outside it, even though an assignment may major in one of these areas and be pivotal on a deep technical grasp of that topic.

## The critical factors for success

So, as a critical user of consultants, what do I consider to be the most telling issues that are fundamental to successful IT projects? I have chosen two headings: first, congruence of understanding and second, client culture. My thesis is that many consultants provide sub-optimal output and therefore inferior value for money, because they lack a true understanding of what task really needs to be undertaken, and after the project has commenced they are unable to assist their clients to best effect because they do not understand the clients' cultural environment. (There may be many other reasons that contribute to a client's dissatisfaction but it is neither appropriate nor timely to address these points here.)

## Congruence of understanding

The stages of a project from beginning to end might be listed thus:

\- conception of a project by the user;

\- preparation of terms of reference

— selection and appointment of consultants;

\- project work;

— presentation of findings/recommendations;

\- implementation.

Regarding the first two stages listed, I would advocate that it is not unusual for the user's conception of the project to be incomplete, sometimes incorrect and not infrequently misleading. I would also suggest that the preparation of terms of reference is a sadly neglected activity. Well structured, clearly stated terms of reference are critical to the success of a project, but they are often imprecise and lack incisiveness. I have seen this to be true where terms of reference were prepared on the client's behalf by the consultant as well as by the client for the consultant.

Hence my suggestion that congruence of understanding needs to be achieved in terms of:

\- the true objectives of the project;

\- the outputs expected;

\- agreeing on what are feasible inputs;

\- agreeing the modus operandi for the project members;

\- the basis of the cost.

Each of these facets, and maybe others, need to be incorporated clearly in the terms of reference.

That may seem simplistic, and even obvious to all. Why choose the arguably pleonastic term 'congruence of understanding' to describe the approach to this aspect of the work? The answer is that it is not so simple a thing to achieve.

I am employing the term ‘congruence’ to highlight the need for views of what has to be done in a consultancy assignment to be as nearly coincidental as possible, from whatever perspective each party might assess the assignment.

Let me illustrate the concept thus: the characteristics of a company might be represented by a pyramid, there being in this case three dimensions in which it can be considered. I am almost certainly abusing mathematics here in such distinguished company, but whatever face or combination we see, we see the same pyramid. Translating the metaphor, the problem is that the different perspectives will always offer different analyses of the same situation. The colours, the patterns, the characteristics of each face will be different and, moreover, in the real world one is talking of 'n-dimensional pyramids' in space where the significance of the perspectives for the different viewers, and the conclusions they draw about the nature of the problem, may vary considerably. So, returning to my metaphor, the two viewers of the pyramid (the consultant and the user) must first, for full understanding, each recognize from where the object, the problem, the situation is being viewed by the other and must then, together, move round the 'n-dimensional pyramid', viewing the same problem from as many observation points as is necessary to see the context as well as the size of the problem that exists. Such a basic exercise can itself radically change users' conceptions of what ought to be done, and how.

Regrettably, the individual who employs or negotiates with the consultant is himself frequently blind to these other perspectives and therefore needs much hand-holding by the consultant who should, by his previous experience, be able to take the user 'round the corner of the pyramid', so to speak.

I have personally been involved in IT consultancy, with a client company, where the blindness of the user (who drew up the original terms of reference) was perhaps compounded by vertigo, as well as a fear of the unknown. In that case I was obliged to find in the user organization a better partner with whom I could traverse the land in which the problem lay and who assisted in agreeing a more rounded set of terms of reference.

It may seem strange that I speak now from the point of view of the consultant and not the user. But it is not so strange, for what I advocate is that consultants take on a greater responsibility for assisting the user in the definition of the terms of reference, the real problem, and for setting out a feasible modus operandi for the assignment. Users may often be inexperienced, or even in a situation where their colleagues will not allow them to state to the consultant in the first instance what the true issues may be. Consultants, on the other hand, have the advantage of political and organizational independence, objectivity, and past experience which enable them to assist in the preparation of those terms of reference. They know (or should know) the multi-dimensional or multi-disciplinary aspects of any potential assignment. They have the responsibility of exploring those aspects before committing themselves to an assignment and taking it forward in partnership with the client company.

So my first conclusion, under the heading of 'congruence of understanding', is that the first point of failure in the client-consultant relationship is that insufficient attention is paid to the provision and definition of the terms of reference. Where adequate attention is given to this important aspect and where there is congruence of understanding, then good project work and a profitable outcome on both sides are more likely. It is up to consultants to educate clients on how to define and set out the terms of reference for each assignment.

## Understanding the client culture

My second point relates to understanding the client's corporate culture, a topic far more difficult to circumscribe and come to terms with.

What does one mean by the ‘culture’ of an organization? And why is a lack of appreciation of the elements in that culture critical to users in terms of their ability to get good value from the relationship with their consultants?

Culture, like my pyramid (mentioned earlier), is multi-dimensional, with many facets. A few examples are:

\- Management style: Is it autocratic, democratic; dominated by particular concerns such as status or mobility?

\- Organizational structure: Is it hierarchical, project-orientated, divisionalized; and what is the nature of the relationship between the divisions, the departments, the units and the branches?

\- Decision mechanisms: is delegation downwards, or is it upwards?

\- Innovation: Is there a history of receptiveness to new ideas, inventions or lateral thinking?

\- Strategic direction: Is there any? Is it understood? Is it really a strategy or does the client 'talk tactics'?

\- Intellectual qualities: Does management have any? And what about the staff – are graduates employed or encouraged?

\- Planning cycles: Do they exist? Are they understood or are they simply a ritual?

\- History and tradition: Does the company have any?

\- Social factors: Does management work after lunch? Only at lunch? Or does it miss lunch?

— Precedents: Are there precedents for the sort of work that is being proposed?

\- Technical infrastructure: Is the client a follower or a leader in the realm of technological applications?

There is a multitude of factors to consider, representing a whole range of dimensions along which to measure the temperature and shape of a company's culture. Interactions between all these dimensions and the fact that the user – the employer of the consultant – has his own unique position within that company's culture make for a complex set of issues to be recognized. Not only must the issues be recognized but also the consultant must adapt to or at least work within the culture – consciously – while he carries out his assignment.

I have sat in on a presentation of great skill and wisdom given by an eminent firm of consultants where the proposals were rejected by the listeners on the grounds that 'the presentation was just too slick, too smooth – we don't like it'.

The consultant has to get under the skin of the client culture: I refer here even to situations where, in IT consultancy, the discipline being sold to the client is narrow and technical. The content of the report and the conduct of the technician are critical. The packaging of the whole assignment – the written word, behavioural aspects, verbal communications and social interaction – is as important as the technical content.

I conclude with two illustrations from my own experience of where lack of understanding of client culture has harmed an IT consultancy project. In each case the prime technical skill was present.

In the first instance, I recall a situation where the client project co-ordinator on a functional and systems analysis project was himself not in total sympathy with the culture of the organization for which he worked. He was a strong character, confident, forceful and technically competent. He provided most of the data needed by the consultant, who consequently presented in his report conclusions recognized by management as being highly influenced by this individual. The result was that the report was never published but was rewritten by the client management with a different set of conclusions.

The second situation: a firm of consultants was asked to carry out an extraordinarily complex technical review for a financial institution in the city, and the consultancy (not having the local expertise to undertake the exercise) imported an American consultant, highly competent, highly vocal, highly opinionated and totally unsympathetic to the culture in which he was being asked to operate. Perhaps he found the client organization quaint; his clients found him difficult; the project was never completed.

It is the failure of the consultant to recognize the significance of the cultural environment into which he is drawn and the impact this failure can have on the acceptance by the client of what might otherwise have been a brilliant assignment.

I recognize that the culture of the user can have its bad as well as its good aspects. Indeed, the reason for a consultancy assignment may be to engineer a shift in just one major or minor dimension of that culture. But beware the consultant who finds the challenge of that potential shift so great that he ignores a ground rule about corporate culture. That ground rule says that if a foreign body burrows deep into a corporate culture and tries to move it, then it is at risk of being swept away by the corporate cultural stream and by those determined to protect it. A far better practice is to take it slowly and understand and engineer the responses in the client with a little land-fill on one side of the stream and a little excavation on the other.

## Summary

In summary my perspective as a user of IT consultancy is that its effectiveness can be enhanced, first, by recognizing the skill sets needed by both the user and the consultant for a successful project; second, by consultants encouraging and assisting clients in achieving ‘congruence of understanding’ of what projects are really about; and third, by encouraging consultancies, at all stages, to burrow under the corporate cultural skin of their clients.

I recall for you my earlier quotation: 'The correct qualification for success is not to be one of the brightest people but to know how to use them.'

## Discussion

A contributor from New Bank Priest: I would like to comment on the problem of getting the project right from the start, i.e. with regard to the terms of reference. There is a danger that if you, as a consultant, 'go in too strong' in clarifying terms of reference at the beginning, the client will give the project to someone else.

Malcolm Iliff: Yes, in a way the consultant is in a 'no win' situation. It is a matter of providing the client with confidence. Some consultants say, 'What are the terms of reference?'; others say, 'Can we spend some time talking around the terms of reference?'. Sometimes it becomes apparent that the terms of reference are specifying an impossible project, and on these occasions it is clearly better to turn the project down. This is an alternative that is easier to choose in the larger consultancies than in the smaller ones. There is also a need to educate the users, and not enough of this is done during IT consultancy projects.
