# Characteristics of IT Innovation

Peter Monk, Centre for Urban and Regional Development Studies, The University, Newcastle upon Tyne

## 1. Introduction

This paper outlines the results of an interdisciplinary analysis of the characteristics of information technology (IT) and the process by which IT changes (Monk, 1987). The purpose of characteristics analysis, and the reasons for focusing on innovation in IT, are described in section 2 below. IT and information itself are closely related; their technical and economic characteristics are discussed in section 3. Section 4 deals with the process of innovation in IT and its implications for existing concepts of technological change. Finally, a summary of conclusions from the Monk (1987) study is presented in section 5.

## 2. IT and technological change

Current debates in the UK and elsewhere about future economic development are centred on the implications of rapid technological change. While differing opinions are expressed on the precise effects of such change – notably the effects on employment – there is a widespread view that economic prosperity requires the exploitation of new technologies. In particular, information technology is seen as the means of radically improving and expanding opportunities for wealth creation, and thus social welfare. These potential economic and social changes have been variously described as the ‘information revolution’, or the transition to a ‘post-industrial economy’, or an ‘information society’ (Marien, 1985).

These concepts presuppose a fundamental change occurring in the nature of technology. In general terms, ‘technology’ may be considered as the means used to process or transform resources. Historically, industrial development was founded on technologies for processing physical resources: first coal, iron and textiles; then petroleum and synthetic materials. Steam power, followed by electricity, provided the means for transforming physical resources into economic commodities. However, IT does not process physical resources; it processes an abstract resource – information.

The growing economic importance of IT suggests that economic analysis must address the implications of technologies which process different types of resources. It cannot be assumed a priori that information and IT may be treated in the same way as physical resources and the industrial technologies which process them. Neither can it be assumed that innovation in IT is necessarily the same as previous forms of technological progress. It is first necessary to account for the characteristics of information as a type of resource, and those of IT as the technology which processes information.

There are two reasons why previous work on innovation and technological change has not accounted for the characteristics of IT. A review of work in this area can be found in Monk (1987, sections 1.2 and 2.2). First, the characteristics of IT are new. Its history has been brief, even if spectacular. Clearly, IT cannot boast the same historical pedigree as, say, commercial aviation, gas and electricity supply, or steam power technologies. It is these more established technologies which have served as paradigms for economic historians and theoretical economists. Indeed, by far the greatest amount of the literature on technological innovation relies heavily on historical precedent and long time-scales – the very qualities which IT has yet to acquire. Second, IT is essentially an information phenomenon. This puts it outside the scope of mainstream thought on technology, which is still wedded to the physical resource processing paradigm of technology. With apologies to Nathan Rosenberg (1982), IT is not so much a 'black box' as an 'abstract box'.

Two similar reasons account for the significance of IT innovation as a radically new or ‘revolutionary’ phenomenon. The first reason is that the rate and extent of technological change in IT is unprecedented. All branches or aspects of the technology are developing extremely rapidly; major new advances and applications appear month by month, rather than over a period of years. Indeed, IT is arguably the most generally applicable technology ever developed - more so even than steam or electric power technologies which underpinned the historical development of industrial production.

The second reason concerns the peculiar nature of the technology itself. Not only does IT process abstract resources (i.e. information) but also the technology is itself partly information. Innovation in IT occurs typically through the production of new information resources — including abstract machines in the form of software — rather than the development of new types of physical machines.

IT innovation is, therefore, an information process. It is a form of technological change that is shaped by the characteristics of information — and information processing — rather than by the characteristics of material production.

## 3. Characteristics of IT

IT is defined here as that machine-based technology which actively processes information, rather than merely storing or transmitting it. Two points should be noted about this definition. First, a 'machine' in this sense may be physical or abstract; the term is meant to include software systems as 'virtual machines'. Second, 'machines' do not constitute the whole of IT; the technology must also include non-machine information resources such as relevant theoretical and practical knowledge (e.g. formal definitions of programming languages, systems analysis and development methodologies).

IT is just one of a set of information-related technologies which do not, however, share all of IT's characteristics. The definition above separates active information processing from simple communications technologies (e.g. telephone, television) and from non-technological information handling activities (e.g. library services). The purpose of doing so is to isolate the characteristics of IT that are different from those of physical resource processing (PRP) technologies and physical commodities.

In the context of technology modelling, IT displays the following general technical characteristics:

1. Hardware and software are mutually substitutable yet complementary aspects of IT; both aspects are required for any IT system. Hardware and software have a flexible symbiotic relationship. Further, the development of new instances of IT depends directly on existing hardware and software (among other factors): IT is essential to its own development.

2. Form and function in IT are flexibly related. There is no unique mapping of sets of forms and functions into sets of hardware and software; nor is there a unique mapping between forms and functions. Any set of functional requirements may be satisfied by a variety of system architectures, each of which may be implemented in a variety of forms. Conversely, similar architectures or similar forms may fulfil different sets of functional requirements. Differences in function are not easily related to simple differences in form.

3. Although digital computation is currently the dominant mode of IT, the definition above does not preclude other modes so long as active processing of information is involved. In current practice, however, IT is virtually synonymous with digital computation.

4. To the extent that it is composed essentially of information, IT has the capacity to process itself as a resource. In this sense, IT may be described as a ‘reflexive technology’. (The question of whether other reflexive technologies exist is left open.)

5. IT systems are probabilistic: information or data generated by IT is subject to uncertainty. Although IT systems may generate 'reproducible results', those results may not be predictable before a system is used. Indeed, if all such results could be predicted accurately in advance, the technology would offer fewer practical applications. In practice, effective instances of IT are those where the probability of error in results is kept within 'acceptable' limits.

6. IT systems interact within their environments both through direct information transfers and through 'learning-by-using' (see for example, Land and Somogyi, 1986).

7. IT is characterized here as an abstract technology that is flexibly constrained by the form and function of the physical machines which support it. The degree of hardware dependence varies greatly from one system to another.

8. IT physical machines (hardware) are subject to fundamental limits of size, speed and circuit density. However, these technical hardware limits are likely to be less significant than non-hardware factors in the future development of IT.

9. IT abstract machines (software) and their applications are constrained by the range of computable information problems, and by the nature of possible solutions to them. Some computable solutions are inherently not feasible; solutions that lie outside the 'human window' must remain opaque to human analysis (Kopec and Michie, 1983).

10. At least some limits to IT are dynamic, in that they depend directly on the state of available information resources (e.g. theoretical knowledge) rather than solely on observable characteristics of physical resources or phenomena. Dynamic limits to IT are reflected in paradigm shifts in theoretical knowledge about the technology.

Unfortunately, the economic characteristics of IT are less straightforward. There are two problems here. First, physical and abstract (information) resources or commodities have radically different economic characteristics; second, various aspects or components of IT represent different relationships between hardware and information – that is, the economic characteristics of each IT component will vary according to the particular relationship between physical and abstract resources.

Information resources and commodities display four general economic characteristics:

1. Information is 'non-appropriate': the seller of information is not deprived of its possession.

2. Information is 'non-divisible in use': some, if not all, sets of information must be complete if they are to be usable. For example, half of a chemical formula or half of a FORTRAN compiler would not be usable commodities (even if such information sets had value in exchange).

3. Information is 'beterogeneous': unlike quantities of homogeneous physical resources, 'more information' means different items of information, not further copies of the same items.

4. Information is 'context-dependent': the value of an information set as a resource depends on the contexts of its interpretation, use and/or exchange.

In practice, however, these four economic characteristics are not absolute; they are displayed to varying degrees by different types and forms of information. For example, the degree to which real abstract commodities (e.g. software products) are non-appropriate depends on their information content, the form in which they are implemented as economic commodities, their relationships with other commodities, and the technical and economic context of their use.

It follows that IT is not a single type of commodity with easily identifiable economic characteristics. Instead, it is composed of both abstract and physical commodities of various types, each of which has different economic characteristics. These abstract and physical commodities are complementary; they are mutually substitutable within limits; their form and function are not uniquely determined, but both are flexible and context-dependent. The degree and direction of interdependence between abstract and physical components of IT vary widely.

Moreover, any single activity, process or product in IT may involve more than one type of interdependence between those components. Since information sets exhibit variations in economic characteristics, information itself need not be considered as a single type of resource or commodity. Certainly, it would be difficult to classify the diverse range of information components of IT under a single meaningful heading. As those components display different economic characteristics, there is a strong case for classifying them as different types of economic resources.

Movements across information categories thus represent transformations of information resources. As economic production necessarily involves resource transformations, the economic production of information must involve the transformation of abstract resources. Since the production of IT requires the creation and use of new information, technological change in IT must involve transformations of abstract resources.

The implication here is that the process of innovation in IT necessarily involves changes in the economic characteristics of the technology's constituent parts. The creation of new information sets — software, blueprint methods, algorithms, and so on — which constitute additions to IT means creating information resources with different economic characteristics from pre-transformation resources. For example, the process of software development may require up to eight different types of abstract resource input (i.e. all except the top left box in Table 2). None of these eight types of input has exactly the same economic characteristics as software, since each represents a different relationship between abstract and physical resources.

For example, consider advances in IT-related theoretical knowledge, such as formal analyses of software testing methods (Ince, 1985). As long as such information is not classified (in the military sense), it is in the public domain and thus it is non-appropriate. However, the production of that theoretical information requires observation of implemented software which need not be entirely non-appropriate. Indeed, any information set that results from the transformation of abstract resources does not need to share the same economic characteristics as any one of its inputs.

It is for this reason that the economic characteristics of IT are not static or even steadily changing.

While information in general may appear to exhibit a definite set of characteristics, the information constituents of IT do not. The development of the technology itself implies changes in the economic characteristics of its constituent information resources.

## 4. Innovation in IT

Innovation in IT is essentially an information process — one which is broader than the process of hardware and software product development. This information perspective on IT derives from two points. First, any technology is more than just a collection of techniques. IT comprises both existing products, systems, services and information sets (i.e. techniques) and the principles from which those techniques have been developed (i.e. technological knowledge). Second, the distinction between techniques and technological knowledge in IT is not at all clear cut. For example, software development tools may be considered both as marketable products and as sub-sets of the principles under which IT techniques are developed. The purpose of an information perspective on IT is to allow consistent and useful description of the technology itself and the process by which it develops.

In section 3, a variety of types of information resources were considered as components of IT. Each type of component may display a different set of technical and economic characteristics, notably due to differing relationships between abstract and physical resources. As new components are developed — through the transformation of information resources — so the characteristics of IT change: that is, innovation in IT changes the relationships between the abstract and physical components of the technology.

However, when looking at the future development of IT, hardware itself may be treated as an information resource. First, as Tannenbaum (1984, p.11) remarks: 'Hardware and software are logically equivalent'. In the design of any IT system, the roles played by hardware and software are a matter of choice. Although all such systems must ultimately involve the use of hardware, the role of specific physical resources with particular characteristics is not predetermined. Second, hardware development is itself essentially an abstract process. It is common practice for new hardware designs to be tested through software simulation. This means, in effect, running pieces of IT hardware which do not exist as physical resources!

In this sense, IT hardware appears to be analogous to software but with a different set of characteristics. For example, performance would be constrained by circuit switching speeds rather than the efficiency of programming language implementations. Similarly, if a hardware design is constructed in a physical form – rather than remaining as an abstract machine – the characteristic of quantity of that resource would change: units of physical hardware can be counted but quantities of information are not easy to define.

To simplify the problem of representing the innovation process, IT may be considered as an abstract technology, consisting of five types of information resources:

1. Hardware: the design and characteristics of IT physical machines.

2. Software: the design and characteristics of IT abstract machines.

3. Theoretical knowledge: the formal theoretical foundations on which IT systems and methods, and information processing itself, are based.

4. Blueprint knowledge: the semi-formal knowledge and empirical information which are necessary for the creation and use of IT systems in practice.

5. Tacit knowledge: the informal knowledge and skills developed through experience of creating and using IT.

These five information components of IT are interrelated. The development or extension of any one of them requires input from one or more of the others. Equally, such developments are likely to create knock-on effects; changes in available software, for example, must affect the knowledge and skills of IT practitioners and users.

From an information perspective, innovation in IT occurs whenever any one or more of the technology's abstract components is developed or extended. While some changes to those components may appear trivial (e.g. routine amendments to software) they still contribute to the innovation process. The development of structured programming techniques, for example, owed a lot to cumulative experience of problems of testing and maintaining software constructed by ad hoc methods. Most importantly, however, the innovation process occurs with the use of IT – not just with its creation.

In general terms, then, IT innovation occurs through transformations of the technology's component information resources. These transformations may happen in a number of ways, yet eventually they are all constrained by the characteristics of the various forms in which information resources are embodied. Physical and abstract machines, on-line data sources, off-line documents and human knowledge must all be considered in the analysis of IT as an abstract technology.

IT innovation processes may be represented by network models (Monk, 1987, chapter 8). The technique of network modelling allows both macro (technology) and micro (technique) innovation phenomena to be portrayed in a consistent fashion, using given sets of components or inputs to technology. Network models of IT are essentially maps of actual or potential transformations of information resources. In each case, the five information components described above form the nodes of the network. IT innovation is represented as patterns of 'traffic' in network models, i.e. as sets of information resource transformations.

Modelling changes in the effective state of IT in the economy is a more complicated business. It requires a representation of the technology itself (e.g. a network model), the innovation environment (including the distribution of knowledge among labour) and interactions between the two. These internal and external aspects of IT innovation are not directly comparable. Internal and external factors cannot be simply put together as nodes in an enlarged network model of 'effective innovation' in IT. However, it may be possible to consider both internal and external factors as information phenomena. If so, effective innovation in IT might be modelled in terms of stocks, flows and transformations of information resources.

## 5. Summary of conclusions

This section summarizes briefly the conclusions drawn by Monk (1987), including those points not covered in the discussion above.

1. IT is defined as that machine-based technology which actively processes information (where a 'machine' may be a physical or abstract artefact). IT is distinguished from (a) physical resource processing technologies and (b) information-related technologies and information-handling activities.

2. Information plays a dual role in IT: it is the resource to be processed and it is the principal form of the technology itself. To the extent that it is composed of information, IT is a reflexive technology (i.e. IT has the capacity to process itself).

3. The role of information in IT invalidates conventional economic theories of production and technological change as explanations of IT innovation. Those theories cannot account for the production and distribution of information resources or commodities.

4. Economic concepts of invention, innovation and diffusion are criticized on the basis of observation of IT innovation. The widely accepted simple model of innovation as a three-stage process is rejected here as an explanation of IT innovation, for two reasons. First, the development of some new instances of IT bypasses one or more stages in the invention-innovation-diffusion process — for example, in-house system developments are not ‘diffused’ into the economy in the normal sense, whereas public domain technique developments are not directly subject to commercial innovation. Second, even ‘failed’ inventions and innovations affect the state of IT information resources through the accumulation of knowledge and experience by practitioners.

5. Rosenberg's (1982) analysis of the characteristics of technologies is criticized and extended to account for IT. A new analysis of IT is presented to account for three aspects of technology:

(a) technology as information;

(b) technology as macro and micro phenomena;

(c) technology as systemically complex phenomena.

6. Based on case study observations of IT innovation (Monk, 1987, chapter 4) and an analysis of Rosenberg's work, a new analysis of the technical, economic and developmental characteristics of IT is presented. Information resources, and their characteristics in IT, are defined and categorized.

7. Consistent and unified descriptions of the technology and the innovation process are developed from analysis of the central role of information in IT. IT is composed of interrelated sets of information resources. IT innovation occurs as the transformation of information resources. The 'skills shortage' in the IT industry is analysed as an information resource problem, and options for its solution are discussed.

8. Compatible macro and micro perspective models of IT are developed as new applications of communication network theory. New forms of stacked and sequenced models of IT and its innovation environment are discussed, as a framework for the development of further analysis of IT.

9. Potential applications of characteristics analysis and network modelling techniques for a range of technologies are presented as research recommendations.

In conclusion, there is a clear need for the nature and role of information in economic activity to be analysed more thoroughly. This includes both the role of information in technologies – notably IT – and the nature of information phenomena in the economy. To echo Stonier's (1983) conclusion, an 'information economics' is urgently required. It is suggested here that information economics and information technology will be two significant areas of study that are closely related.

Finally, the central purpose of this study has been to contribute towards better understanding of a new and radical technology. IT has enormous potential as a tool in economic and social activity. The better the technology is understood, the greater will be the chance of its beneficial and effective use in society.

## Biographical notes

After graduating in Economics from the University of York in 1978, Dr Peter Monk worked as a programmer in the financial sector. His interests in the development and implications of IT led to a period of lecturing in further education on the subject, and the use of computers in education. His consultancy experience includes both technical and commercial aspects of systems development, and reporting on IT employment and training issues. In 1983 Dr Monk started part-time research at the University of Bradford (School of Science and Society) on economic aspects of IT, and gained a PhD in June 1987. He is now Senior Research Associate at the Centre for Urban and Regional Development Studies (CURDS) at the University of Newcastle upon Tyne.
