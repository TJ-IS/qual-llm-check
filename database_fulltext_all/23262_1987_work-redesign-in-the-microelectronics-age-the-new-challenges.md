# Work Redesign in the Microelectronics Age: the New Challenges

Amin Rajan, Institute of Manpower Studies

## 1. Introduction

At the workplace, the impact of information technology has proved all-pervasive. Its inherent ability to integrate and automate a range of disparate functions has influenced not only the quantity of jobs but also their quality. The qualitative features of interest here are skills attributes of various jobs, their functional content, and the organizational structure within which they are performed.

An earlier paper covered the impact on jobs and skills (Rajan, 1985). This paper extends the analysis by covering their functional and organizational contexts. Both these aspects are perhaps best assessed through an empirical examination of the ways in which information technology has either inspired or facilitated the redesigning of work so as to render it more productive or interesting. The central focus of this paper, therefore, is work redesign in the microelectronics age.

In a seminal article, Leavitt and Whisker (1958) offered four distinct predictions on the impact of the nascent computer revolution on the content of managerial work in the 1980s. First, there would be increased structuring of middle management, directly in response to the standardization and/or automation of many decisions hitherto made by middle managers. As a result, they would have less autonomy, skills and career progression. Second, and in marked contrast, there would be an enhancement in status for certain categories of middle managers. Constituting a small minority, they would involve those performing non-standardized knowledge-intensive functions such as planning, research, development and systems design, all of which offer rapid upward mobility. Third, there would emerge more distinct differences between top and middle management, with the former concentrating on strategic and the latter on routine tasks. Finally, corporate structures would become recentralized, with faster and ever-increasing volumes of information flowing to the top. There would be a growing concentration of power among the most senior executives, as a result.

Sixteen years later, Braverman (1974) weighed in – with predictions this time concerning non-managerial employees. Information technology, he argued, would ‘proletarize’ the craft and clerical work that had hitherto relied on the development of innate skills through years of apprenticeship and work experience. The conception of such work would be divorced from its execution as management achieved total control over its design, planning and execution. As a result, employees would be reduced to performing simple manual operations that no longer required discretion, self-control or brain power: that is, their work would be deskilled.

Since then, such predictions have featured prominently in academic and public debate on technology, not least because of their alarmist undertone that work will become highly standardized through the use of technology: the attendant redesigning will be aimed at simplifying work so as to make it highly repetitive and amenable to economies of scale through specialization.

In the UK, diffusion of information technology first became apparent on a notable scale in the last decade. At any rate, it has been in progress now for a considerable number of years for us to assess how the earlier predictions measure up to outcomes so far. This paper attempts an assessment by surveying two sets of research studies. The first of these covers those studies which examine the motivation and, to a lesser extent, the nature of work redesign. The second set covers studies that have probed into the nature of redesign and its consequences. Between them, they serve to shed light on motives, nature and effects of redesign that have occurred in the recent past. It is worth stressing that the second set receives more prominence here, given this paper's emphasis on the impact of technology at the workplace. Indeed, in order to underline this preoccupation, the paper describes in detail two case studies emerging from the second set. Only thus, it becomes possible to illustrate how complex and varied the outcome on redesign has turned out to be in practice so far.

More importantly, perhaps they also show how unnecessarily alarmist the earlier predictions now appear in retrospect.

Accordingly, the plan of this paper is as follows: section 2 surveys some of the recent studies. If it appears short and selective, that is only because among the voluminous literature on redesign, no more than a handful has a distinct orientation towards empiricism and information technology at the same time. Sections 3 and 4 describe the two case studies that have the requisite orientation. The final section draws out the main implications and conclusions.

## 2. Survey of recent studies

The recent years have witnessed a renaissance in the study of work redesign. In the 1960s and 1970s, its thrust was prescriptive: how to ameliorate human relations at work or to improve quality of working life. Robertson and Smith (1985) consider four forms of redesign in this context. The first of these involves job enlargement occurring through the addition of further, often similar, task(s) to a person's current job. The second involves job enrichment, resulting from the addition of new tasks which may have been done previously in another stage of the work process or at a different level of authority. The third involves job rotation, requiring the movement of individual workers (often within a group) around a variety of tasks. The final form involves the creation of autonomous (self-managing) groups comprising individuals responsible for their own working procedure and production systems. Each of these forms has the express objective of securing more variety, responsibility or status for job holders. As such, they stand in sharp contrast to the functional specialization through technology-led work simplification as envisaged by, for example, Braverman (1974). Indeed, Robertson and Smith (1985) cite a number of examples where the prescriptive approach has been put into practice. They do this to emphasize that even in the microelectronics age there is nothing inherent in the nature of new technology to force a deterministic outcome.

Much the same theme runs through the studies carried out in this decade. Most, if not all of them, accept implicitly that not only redesigning a la Robertson and Smith is possible, but also that it is occurring in various industries. The only sense in which they differ from the studies of the previous decades is in terms of the underlying rationale of the emerging redesign. They appear to put less emphasis on socio-psychological imperatives and more on the political, economic and technological ones. Specifically, they question the view that the redesign process is necessarily dictated by considerations of improving job satisfaction and quality of working life. That, at any rate, is the primary message from a collection of papers edited by Knights et al. (1985). Under one cover, the papers encompass the views of all the leading researchers in the field of redesign. Their contention that competitive market pressures are forcing firms to reorganize work to render it more productive is well taken. However, from the point of view of this paper, the nature rather than the rationale of redesign is more important. In this respect, only one paper in the collection has much to say. In it, Professor Child summarizes the evidence showing that the earlier predictions by Leavitt and Whisker have yet to materialize. At the same time he identifies managerially-initiated developments that have been facilitated by information technology. These are directed towards (a) elimination of direct labour and spread of sub-contracting and (b) weakening of traditional job or skill demarcations. Their objectives are two-fold: reduction in unit labour costs and greater functional flexibility. As to the nature of the redesign, none of the papers in the collection has very much to say.

To a lesser extent the same observation can be made about a book by Blackburn et al. (1985). It, too, expends a great deal of effort on whether the recent initiatives in job design, aided by technology, imply a departure from Taylorism — a body of thought that preached extreme functional specialization that still characterizes many mass production or assembly line operations today. However, in the process the authors leave us in no doubt at all that redesign is occurring and that its rationale extends beyond economic imperatives. They conclude that 'It would be unwise, however, to write off work redesign as just management's latest fad for intensifying work. It does also focus attention on the human aspects . . .' (p.106). Whereas the authors grant that information technology offers considerable scope for redesign, it is left to Professor Child (1984) to spell out the details in a separate paper.

In his paper, Professor Child agrees that the use of technology enables management to pursue various commercial goals in a way that has implications for corporate structure. These are, however, mediated by management's views on the scope workers should have to use their skills. In turn, such views are influenced by the role of technology in enhancing management control through three mechanisms: by providing further and more accurate information on operations; by reducing the scope for idiosyncratic judgement; and by permitting a unification of previously segmented control systems. As such, technology offers an opportunity to resolve some of the tensions between efficiency and flexibility that have featured in the long-running debate on centralized versus delegated decision-making. This it does by providing a two-fold capability: (a) in the form of monitoring the activities of the decentralized units, and (b) in the form of an analytical system for the decentralized units so that they can make sound judgement in their decision-making. Either way, the capability offers organizations a choice which they did not have before. The way the choice is exercised depends on, among other factors, managerial ethos and task contingencies such as variability of production or the number of environments served. So, no definite outcomes can be postulated.

Although the paper falls short of considering different forms of redesign associated with the possible outcomes, it is significant in two respects. First, it shows that technology does not have a deterministic impact on work design; rather, it merely provides a structure of opportunities — some good, others not. The outcomes depend on management foresight and competence. Second, the job redesign initiatives extend beyond humanistic imperatives to cover various external forces.

From the foregoing discussion, it is clear that recent studies have a strong implicit presumption that redesign is occurring and what probably matters more is the underlying rationale. A detailed study on the employment prospects in the UK over the rest of this decade, however, reverses their emphasis (Rajan and Pearson, 1986; Rajan, 1987). In assessing the impact of information technology, it encountered numerous examples of redesign in the 450 detailed case studies that were carried out. They prompt three observations:

1. Some kind of redesign has been attempted in as many as a quarter of the firms in the private sector of the economy.

2. Its form is highly varied between as well as within firms. As a result, it is not possible to categorize firms by the form of redesign.

3. The role of information technology has not been unambiguous in this respect. It has facilitated the change in some cases, and inspired it in others.

The outcomes have been favourable in some respects and not in others. Either way, the use of technology per se has not been the dominant factor: instead it is the way it has been used by management against the background of conflicting pressures in internal and external environment.

In order to substantiate these observations, we now move on to describe two of the case studies. At the outset, it is worth emphasizing that their object is not to glorify technology but to illustrate the complexity of its interaction with other factors when assessing its impact. More positively, they also serve to show that the detractors of technology have too often overlooked its various merits in ensuring business survival in the short run and growth in the long run.

The case studies cover a major clearing bank and a manufacturing firm engaged in the production of brake pads for motor vehicles. Apart from their industrial backgrounds, they have two other differences. First, the bank has a predominance of female employees, a quarter of whom work part-time. Most of them possess social and procedural skills that characterize clerical work in banking. Also, the density of union membership is low. In contrast, the brake pads manufacturer has mostly male employees with a strong background of craft skills, full-time work and high union density. Second, the bank provides a wide portfolio of services, both banking and ancillary. Over time, these have come to rely on the use of information technology on an incremental basis. The pads manufacturer, on the other hand, has a narrow product range but with a high degree of differentiation between types of vehicles and markets. The company has a high technological usage through the wholesale upgrading of its main plant. That is where the dissimilarities end, for they both share two common attributes as well. Both organizations have been subject to strong competitive pressures in this decade that led to a restructuring of capacity and redesign of work. Also, in their respective ways, they both typify to a large extent the nature of redesign that has been occurring in their respective industrial sectors — namely, services and manufacturing. The following two sections describe their experiences.

## 3. Case study I: a bank

Being a clearing bank, this organization is one of the largest in the UK. Its employment size, business turnover and profitability are deliberately omitted here in order to conceal its identity.

The bank experienced a growth in business volume of around 7 per cent in the last decade, benefiting from the general prosperity of the banking industry. In the economic recession of 1979-81, however, the volume growth continued but profitability nose-dived because the bank's customer base had a disproportionate representation of manufacturing firms with severe cash flow problems. Underlying the cyclical problems there was also a secular trend which the bank wanted to reverse. Last but not least, it has facilitated the redesign of work, as summarized in Table 1.

It shows that the redesign has come mainly through job enlargement. Job enlargement was occurring in the pre-rationalization days, but rather gradually because functional specialization had hitherto been the dominant form of work arrangement. Now technology enables individuals – be they managers or clerks – to perform a number of tasks under one job. For example, through the use of elaborate management information systems, managers at the regional head office can supervise more branches with enhanced product portfolios. Within individual branches, too, the introduction of computerized credit scoring systems and front office terminals has relieved managers from routine lending and supervisory functions, so they are now able to spend more time on staff training, business planning and marketing — that is, activities essential in implementing competitive diversification. Branch-based clerks are also freed to perform a range of tasks, with the automation of cash balancing and close-of-business bookkeeping. These typically include customer counselling, sale of ancillary services, data input and foreign exchange transactions. Indeed, certain senior clerical workers in area corporate offices have achieved a measure of job enrichment by being made responsible for servicing all the needs of individual large corporate customers. For example, as well as handling day-to-day money transactions, they effect currency flows, prepare trade documents and offer information on economic conditions in various countries.

Alongside such job loading, however, there is now also significant functional specialization among non-managerial groups at regional head office and the operations centre. The former provides back-up to the network in common services such as recruitment, off-the-job training, security and property management, all of which are performed on a specialist basis because they either require special expertise or are amenable to economies of scale. At the operations centre, specialization is dictated entirely by the mass production nature of the operations. Typical functions here include organizing cheques in special sequences, monitoring their processing through computers, and arranging their physical transportation to the storage vaults.

From the foregoing explanation, it is clear that work restructuring has led to more than one form of design. As far as routine or highly specialist functions are concerned, the dominant form is specialization based on extreme division of labour. With such functions performed either at regional head office or the operations centre, branches are left to concentrate on activities which justify expensive high street presence — namely, money deposit or withdrawal and other services that require face-to-face encounter with customers. Technology has helped to bring these activities under one job.

The process of redesign is still in progress and is expected to be completed by the end of the decade – it is therefore difficult to give an assessment of its long-term impact, but so far a number of outcomes are already evident. On the negative side, just under 10 per cent of the branches have been closed so far in this decade. In the early phase, employment also contracted by about 2 per cent. Since then, however, it has stabilized and then shown a marginal increase. The overall job reduction has been kept deliberately low because the bank has a considerable investment in staff through continuous informal training and work experience.

On the positive side, four types of gain have become evident. First, labour productivity, measured as a number of payments transactions per employee, has accelerated; the annual rate increased from an average of 4 per cent in the last decade to about 7 per cent in this. There has not been a pari passu increase in profitability because the restructuring of customer and product base is taking time, and therefore many transactions continue to remain low-value and high-volume. Second, the growth in business volume, too, has accelerated at a rate faster than the industry average, implying an increase in the market share at the expense of the competitors. Third, the redesign initiatives in branches are reported to have increased employee morale and motivation, partly because of the decline in boredom through variety and partly because of the extensive reskilling and retraining that has occurred. As the final column in Table 1 shows, many categories of employee are now required to have multiple skills and competences, all of which have involved a substantive training effort. Last, but not least, the quality of service to customers has improved. The introduction of the automated teller machines, the so-called 'self-service' bank, and the front-office terminals have served to automate a number of remaining routine functions with a two-fold impact: speedier service and greater access to staff for counselling and informational services.

In the first instance, the outcome so far has stemmed primarily from the rationalization process speeded up by the recession. But that is not to say that technology's role has been neutral --far from it. It has facilitated the implementation of the redesign initiatives that took the back-office routine functions out of the branches and thereby served to underpin a large measure of job loading over most of the new structure. In that sense, technology's role has been beneficial. At the very least, now that the bank has become more competitive, the risk of large-scale job losses is minimal compared to the position seven years ago. In this respect, too, the beneficial role of technology cannot be denied.

## 4. Case study II: a brake pad manufacturer

A subsidiary of an American multinational enterprise, this firm operated nine factories in 1970, producing brake pads for domestic motor vehicle manufacturers. Its market share then stood at 20 per cent. However, with the rising import penetration throughout the last decade, the demand for pads remained virtually static. Against the background of strong economy-wide inflationary pressures, closures became inevitable. By 1978, four factories were phased out, with production concentrated in the remaining five. With the onset of the recession in 1979, demand fell by 20 per cent in one year alone, and cost pressures intensified.

Accordingly the firm had three options: 'moth-ball' the unused capacity and hope that things would improve; have a complete and permanent shutdown in the expectation that the UK vehicles industry was in a state of terminal decline; or totally restructure the capacity so as to be able to penetrate the overseas markets. It chose the final option.

With the aid of a capital injection of £30 million from the parent company, it embarked on a rationalization programme that dictated the closure of four factories and a wholesale upgrading of the remaining one in the north-west. By late 1983, all production was carried out from one factory. Equally significant was the scale of automation and integration within the upgraded capacity. This is best appreciated by comparing the pre- and post-1983 production systems.

In the last decade, the production process typically had the following 12 distinct stages:

1. weighing of asbestos and resin, the two key ingredients;

2. mixing of these ingredients;

3. putting the ingredients into special 'shoes';

4. transferring the shoes into baking ovens;

5. baking;

6. cutting;

7. grinding;

8. chamfering;

9. washing;

10. drying;

11. quality inspection;

12. packing.

This system had two characteristics. First, each stage involved a distinct activity performed on a specialized basis. Each stage was also spatially distinct, there being a considerable physical movement of work-in-progress between them. Such movement was manual between stages 1, 2 and 3 and between 10, 11 and 12; and mainly mechanical between the rest. Second, production was done in batches, partly because the production runs were earmarked for the local vehicle producers, and partly because of the manual transfer of work-in-progress. For example, for reasons of health and safety, weighing and mixing were usually done in a building 450 yards away from the main factory. Also, being manual processes, only a certain amount could be weighed and mixed in one shift.

However, the restructuring changed the system radically. All stages were mechanized and organized in an integrated sequence which obviated the need for manual transfer of work-in-progress. The first four stages were integrated through computer-controlled machines. Baking was isolated physically at the end of the shop-floor because of the intense heat it generated. However, the work-in-progress to and from the ovens was carried out through computer-controlled transfer automation. Thereafter, the next five stages were integrated into multi-purpose machines directly controlled by operatives via computers. The last two stages remained stand-alone because of the high degree of human input in the inspection phase.

Thus, the original 12 stages are now collapsed into five with total automation of the transfer of work-in-progress. From the outset, the new system relied exclusively on experienced long-serving craftsmen, each of whom now has responsibility for multiple machines or multiple functions. The new system relies on three eight-hour shifts in order to avoid the expensive shut-down of the ovens. Each shift starts with a production planning meeting attended by all the crews. The craftsmen who operate and control the machines then make the necessary programming changes relating to the planned rate of the throughput and the variety of pads. Thereafter, they monitor the performance of the machines and do first-level repair and maintenance as and when necessary. Their jobs, as a result, have undergone an enlargement, underpinned by significant training into electronic, programming and planning skills.

There are two other groups of employees whose jobs have been redesigned: technicians and quality inspectors. The first of these are engaged essentially in higher-level repair and maintenance. However, with an elaborate computer-controlled instrumentation system there is now less need for physical inspection of machines. As a result, the technicians work closely with the design department, where product changes are conceived. As such, they serve to bridge the divide between design and production. In the process their jobs, too, have been enlarged. In contrast, those of the quality inspectors are rendered more specialized. Under the old system, quality inspection was not viewed as an important activity and was informally left to the individual craftsman. Now that the firm's throughput is closely aligned to that of its major buyers through the 'just-in-time' principle, quality assurance has become vital. A new cadre of quality inspectors has been created, all of whom have had elaborate training that has also involved spells at the customers' plants where the vehicles are assembled.

As in the case of the bank, the dominant form of redesign is job enlargement, with a measure of functional specialization in the expert area of quality checks. Here, too, outcome has had negative as well as positive features.

On the negative side, the rationalization reduced the employment level from around 3,000 in 1979 to 1,200 in 1986. Its composition, too, has changed: the brunt of the decline has been borne by semi-skilled and unskilled employees whose jobs have been automated. On the positive side, labour productivity has increased by 50 per cent in the last three years. The semi-open use of asbestos in the last decade led to a high natural wastage rate, averaging around 25 per cent. The integration of the stages has vastly reduced exposure to that ingredient: now the wastage rate runs at around 5 per cent. Furthermore, automation and redesign have reduced unit costs: in inflation-adjusted terms they are about 70 per cent of their 1980 level. This has enabled the firm to increase exports and reduce the reliance on the domestic market. Currently 15 per cent of the output is exported, a figure that is targeted to double in the early part of the next decade.

Finally, the human capital content of work has increased substantially. Single-discipline craftsmen of the last decade now have multiple skills through extensive retraining in electronics, electrical, mechanical and hydraulic trades – both on and off the job. The unions concerned have accepted these changes in exchange for job guarantees and generous productivity-linked bonuses. With the bulk of the rationalization process complete, the firm is now more confident about its future success. As if to underline this point, it sharply increased its apprentice intake in 1986, thereby causing an increase in the overall headcount.

## 5. Implications and conclusions

The two case studies serve to highlight the form of work redesign occurring in the UK. Although its scale should not be exaggerated, there is little doubt about the role of information technology in it. The driving force came from the market and cost pressures, but technology has gone a long way towards facilitating structures that are essential for business success. In this respect, the first set of empirical studies in section 2 are right to emphasize the complexity of the forces that have inspired the redesign initiatives in this decade.

It is equally clear that the initiatives stand a better chance of success through the use of technology. After all, competitive pressures are constantly conspiring against labour-intensive operations to the extent that the delay in the adoption of technology becomes a matter of choice between survival or extinction. But the adoption per se does not guarantee survival. It has to be accompanied by the redesign of work such that it becomes more varied, interesting and productive. For example, in the 450 case studies mentioned in section 2, many firms remain sceptical on the scale of gains from technology. For them, the outcomes so far have fallen well short of expectations. Yet on closer inspection it transpires that in most of them the adoption of technology was not used as an opportunity to redesign work. The experience of the two case study organizations as described in the preceding two sections suggests a number of implications and conclusions.

First and foremost, there is nothing inherent in the nature of information technology that dictates predetermined outcomes, in a way implied by Leavitt and Whisker (1958) and Braverman (1974). In the case of the bank, the work done by most middle managers and clerks has, if anything, become more varied and reskilled. In the final analysis, much depends upon how the work is redesigned. Like all else at the workplace, technology is under human control.

Second, the initiatives for redesign can and do usually lead to higher productivity and motivation. But they also involve job losses, at least in the short term. They could be minimized, however, if the initiatives are particularly successful because the resulting competitive advantage can lead to new jobs or redeployment opportunities. For example, it is instructive to note that in both organizations studied, the employment levels have already begun to recover after falling initially. Whether they would reach their pre-1979 levels in the foreseeable future is a matter for conjecture. The substantive point is that both are now distinctly more optimistic about their business prospects in the medium term. The role of technology in this context cannot be underestimated.

Third, improvements in the level of employment in the long term can be achieved if two pre-conditions prevail. In the first place, the use of technology has to be justified in terms of certain business imperatives, such as organic growth or profit maximization. This has the effect of focusing corporate attention on how to achieve an optimal return on all corporate resources, including the human ones. Adoption of technology for its own sake has little to commend it. This statement sounds tautological. Yet it is worth labouring because many firms in Britain are adopting technology as a competitive response without due regard to the full structure of opportunities it presents. The second requirement is that considerable resources need to be earmarked for retraining in order to cement the partnership between man and machine. Corporate commitment to redesign work and retrain employees is the best means of reinforcing the partnership.

Finally, that some degree of redesign is occurring in most industries is not in doubt. At the national level there still remains a need for an elaborate awareness programme that focuses on opportunities and good practices. For there is little doubt that information technology can play a vital role in reducing job demarcations that have plagued the UK economy for too long.
