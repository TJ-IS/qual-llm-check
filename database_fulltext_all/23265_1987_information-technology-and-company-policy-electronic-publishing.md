# Information Technology and Company Policy: Electronic Publishing

Henry Holloway, Consultant, Kobler Unit for the Management of Information Technology, Imperial College of Science and Technology

## Introduction

This is the second in a series of papers examining the application of information technology (IT). In this series we are interpreting the term IT in a comprehensive way to mean the whole range of computer-based facilities for the acquisition, storage, transmission, retrieval and processing of information.

The term ‘electronic publishing’ is used in two ways in publishing. First, it is taken to mean publishing in a computer-retrievable form so that copy is called up on to a screen. Examples of this form of electronic publishing include databases and videotex (viewdata and teletext). More often the printing and publishing industries use the term to denote any activity in which a computer system is used to create a document, whether it will appear as an on-screen publication or in printed form. Since it is becoming easier to change the output form of a computer-stored document, there is some merit in the second definition. However, electronic publishing also requires a technological capability that at the minimum level involves publishing from a database, whatever output medium is used.

To present a clearer picture of what is happening, first the publishing industry and methods used in electronic publishing are examined, and then certain peripheral issues such as electronic manuscripts and generic coding are examined, as these are pertinent to a discussion of the issues arising from the use of electronic publishing.

## Publishing

The UK publishing industry is estimated to comprise some 12,000 publishers. The vast majority of these are very small, in some cases publishing only one title. Very few have their own typesetting capability, fewer still a printing capability, and the number that can handle electronic publishing is very small indeed. To a certain extent, the electronic publishing companies are a subset of those companies with printing capability, for a powerful computer system is an essential for electronic publishing. For example, dictionary publishers have the technical capability to provide electronic publishing. Additional expertise is required and this is sometimes supplied by external service or consultancy companies.

Electronic publishing can be traced to two separate origins. Publishers of print-based products have utilized computer systems increasingly so that today such systems are commonplace. Recently they have found use even in Fleet Street newspapers (see the first paper in this series $^{1}$ for a discussion of these events). Computer composition was used in newspaper publishing as long ago as the early 1960s. Database publishing originated at about the same time. Technology convergence has led to the present-day situation where a document may reside in a database and be retrieved in electronic form or output in printed form, enabling the modern publisher to output the same data in a multiplicity of forms. Different publishing activities often take place within the same enterprise. However, it is easier to review the technologies in two categories: print-based publishing and on-screen publishing.

## Print-based publishing

Computer control of composition was introduced in the 1960s. Although newspapers may claim to be the very first to use this technique, they were quickly followed by other publishing innovators in book and commercial publishing. Today most typesetting is computer-controlled, although once typeset output is achieved, printing is still very much based on mechanical rather than electronic operations.

The publishing industry comprises two main classes of enterprise: those that cater only for publishing aspects (editing of manuscripts and marketing) and those that do at least the type-setting, if not the printing, in-house. The latter tend to be larger companies and though they are a minority in number they account for a major share of the revenue or quantity of pages produced.

Even those with in-house typesetting/printing capabilities also make considerable use of external agencies for typesetting and printing.

The provision of an in-house typesetting/printing capability requires a powerful multi-terminal computer system. Typically, this will be powered by several minicomputers, the exact configuration being dependent on the number of terminals and other factors. Proofs can be obtained via matrix printers, usually laser printers. The system is also linked online to a phototypesetter so that output is almost immediate once copy is satisfactory. In some instances, particularly in the later stages of production, proofing may be carried out by photocopying typeset bromides. The method chosen is dependent on house policy.

Text can be input via the keyboard, of course, but there are many ways of capturing text from other computer sources: via telecommunications, disk conversion and other methods. The multiplicity of computer types, operating systems and magnetic media posed problems several years ago to the publisher seeking to capture copy from extraneous sources. Today, although difficulties remain, copy can be captured from practically any computer source. Once this is achieved then copy can be easily called up to terminals for editing.

## On-screen publishing

Once information is available in electronic form, it may be stored using a variety of magnetic or optical media, transmitted over a cable or broadcast. Thus, information can be called to a screen from local or remote sources. As new media have emerged, different publishing activities have been developed to take advantage of each technology/medium. The different electronic publishing activities are usually classified as follows:

\- Online: access of database information from a computer terminal via telephone link;

\- Videotex: access to a database from a television set via telephone link or broadcast signal;
- Videodisk, CD-ROM: examples of activities centred on a particular storage medium which are linked to a computer to access the stored information.

Online databases were the first form of electronic publishing. The user accesses the database from a computer terminal, using keywords and logical connectives to select files. Despite a cost of £50 or more per hour, online searching may be more cost-effective than manual searching, a few minutes online replacing hours of manual searching. Online publishing is an international activity. Users and databases are often located in different countries, this being feasible because of the existence of networks which reduce telecommunication costs.

Users do not have to subscribe to individual databases. Online services provide the facility to access many databases provided by separate companies. There are about 4,000 online databases usually classified as reference or source databases. Reference databases provide the basis for further search and are subdivided into bibliographic (abstracts or indexes) and referral (addresses and other details listed in directories or catalogues). Source databases contain complete sources of information so that it is not necessary to search elsewhere. Source databases may be subdivided into three types: numeric (statistics), text-numeric (e.g. company reports) and full-text. $^{2}$

The earliest databases were of a bibliographic nature. The lowering of the cost of storage and faster computing systems saw the introduction of full-text databases which have a phenomenal storage requirement. At first these focused on traditional database topics — law, science, patents and medicine. In the past few years news databases have become very popular. The text for these databases is taken from newspapers and magazines. Datasolve's 'World Reporter' includes information from the Financial Times, the Guardian, the Economist and others.

Two fairly recent developments have helped to make online access more popular: first, the ability to download selected files to the user's system, where the information may be manipulated, and second, the emergence of personal computer networks, so that databases can be accessed by humble and inexpensive computer systems costing a few hundred pounds (e.g. Micronet or Compunet, the Commodore-based network).

Videotex is the access of databases from television sets via telephone or broadcast. Each set requires an adapter to decode signals and, in the case of telephone videotex, a method of connection. The first examples of either system to be commercially available were launched in the UK in the late 1970s. Prestel was developed by the (then) UK Post Office and became fully operational in 1979. It is now run by British Telecom (BT) and offers users access to both Prestel's own database and to other databases supplied by 'information providers'. Each frame is charged at rates which vary from 0p upward, in addition to the various telephone and subscription charges from BT.

At the same time as the Post Office was developing Prestel, both the BBC and IBA were developing a system which sent information by television broadcast. Negotiations between the Post Office, BBC and IBA ensured that a standard format was agreed, so that Prestel sets can also receive the broadcast videotex. Broadcast videotex is usually known as teletext and utilizes the spare capacity of television signals. All four of the UK's public television channels provide teletext: Ceifax on the BBC and Oracle on IBA. Both can be said to be successful; in particular Oracle has attracted advertising revenue and offers services such as hotel booking information (prices and availability) and sending greetings.

Teletext can only be used to send signals to the user, whereas Prestel is two-way, albeit with the user only able to transmit at a much lower baud rate than the incoming signal. (Prestel was designed in this way to reduce the costs of the modem required.)

Although designed for television access there are considerable advantages to accessing videotex from a personal computer. As with online services, there are substantial cost benefits to using a service such as Micronet.

As well as providing information, Prestel can be used to make a number of transactions such as transfer of money, making reservations, or requesting information by post. Private videotex systems are widely used. These may be set up as an individual network of terminals connected to a database or operate through a Closed User Group on Prestel.

Although the first in the field, Prestel was not a successful service, at least until the introduction of Micronet and home banking. The most successful public interactive videotex service is probably the French Teletel system, the French PTT supplying some 1.5 million terminals to users. The French system is based on Antiope; other systems in use are Telidon (Canada), CAPTAIN (Japan) and NAPLPS (USA). Prestel's standard is used in a number of countries in Europe, South Africa and elsewhere either in its entirety or, as in the case of West Germany's Bildschirmtext, with some modification.

## Videodisks, optical storage and CD-ROM

Floppy disks have been available for many years. While adequate for publishing software, they are not suitable for publishing text as their capacity is limited, and this has prompted publishers to search for other storage media. The first of these was videodisk, its greater capacity compared to floppy disk giving promise of a bright future. As videodisk is an analogue medium, multi-media publishing has been its greatest success. For example, a videodisk might contain illustrations and tabular data to back up text received via teletext or online.

Videodisk has been overtaken by newer and better technologies before it could carve out a substantial market. One of these, the optical disk, promises great things as it has the advantage of being a digital medium and longer working life as optical disks are read by laser.

A development of optical disk technology is the CD-ROM (compact disk – read only memory). CD-ROM has been developed from the audio compact disk. Since there is already a substantial market for audio compact disks, CD-ROM disks and players are not expensive and prices are likely to fall dramatically if the market develops. A CD-ROM player is attached to a computer, most usually an IBM pc or compatible machine, and an information retrieval program allows access to the data held on the disk.

CD-ROM disks are manufactured by creating a master and then replicating using a stamping process, so that the cost per disk falls dramatically as the number of disks increases. Few companies have the in-house capability to manufacture CD-ROM disks so the task usually goes to outside agencies. CD-ROM offers substantial memory capacity (around 500 Mbytes per disk) in a compact form and at low cost.

CD-ROM is attracting considerable attention. Many titles have been released and more are becoming available every day. Such well-known online services as Dialog are providing information in CD-ROM form. Examples of titles from a range of suppliers are AGRIS, ERIC and Info-Trac (an index of the New York Times and news magazines). $^{8}$

At present CD-ROM suffers from the disadvantage that the memory is unalterable. WORM (write once, read many) disks are available and appear to offer a useful function. A WORM system, the Intelligent Archive, is reported to have been put to effective use in two large UK computer installations. $^{9}$ Companies are known to be developing erasable memories. These may be two or more years away from commercial application. $^{3}$

## Electronic manuscripts

There is a vast and growing amount of information in electronic form as more and more people gain access to computers. Publishers have been aware of the situation and particularly in the USA have made attempts to capture information in electronic form rather than re-key information. Just as typescripts replaced manuscripts with the emergence of the typewriter, electronic manuscripts or compuscripts promise to do the same to typescripts. Technical difficulties, due probably to a lack of computer expertise within the UK printing and publishing industries, caused the UK to lag in this application but the situation is much more promising today. In most cases the compuscript is captured for output, after editing, via a phototypesetter but there is no technical reason why the same text should not find its way into electronic publishing.

## Generic coding

It is easy to output the same computer file via different output devices and different media, if the copy is coded appropriately. Codes may be inserted within the text, but separated from it by markers called delimiters. This is often done to provide specific output instructions, particularly for phototypesetting. There is a body of opinion that coding should not be output specific to one medium or machine, but allow for any output option. Generic coding, as this process is called, achieves this by coding the structure of a document rather than output parameters. For example, in typesetting a particular typeface such as bold or italic would be chosen to emphasize a word or phrase. In generic coding a marker such as <et> would be interpreted by computer program as emphasized text and output in appropriate manner according to the style sheet for a particular medium.

In the UK, the publishing industry is approaching generic coding with caution and few publishers have adopted it. Those who do often develop their coding system in-house, rather than use a standard one. It is suggested $^{10}$ that the main reason for the cautious approach to generic coding in the UK is that many publishers and printers do not understand the precise nature of generic coding.

An ISO standard for generic coding was published in 1986, called SGML (standard generalized markup language). $^{4}$ This has not achieved widespread use at present.

## New strategy

In the late 1970s Senders $^{5}$ suggested that technology was ripe for application to publishing learned information. He envisaged a scientific journal published in electronic form. Research papers would be sent online to a host computer system where they could be called up by the editor and referees, and if acceptable made available for public access. Technology has improved in the ten years since Senders published his paper but the electronic journal is still not feasible. The reasons are not so much technological as ergonomic. The BLEND project $^{6}$ demonstrated that people are not prepared to access large amounts of text on-screen.

Electronic publishing has evolved in the course of 20 years or so and is now firmly established. Some companies, notably Pergamon (see Company

Profiles) are making profits on their electronic publishing activities. However, electronic publishing is developing new market areas rather than impinging on print-based markets. These new market areas are growing but not in the dramatic way of other areas of IT applications such as wordprocessing. Is this because there is no market for it, or is it because companies have not made the most of the opportunity? Electronic publishing is quite different in nature from traditional publishing. Holloway $^{7}$ examined models of publishing and applied one to compare various forms of publishing, showing that electronic publishing was not only faster, but was more direct and also changed the order of some of the operations followed in traditional, print-based publishing. Given that there are dramatic differences between print-based and electronic publishing, perhaps a new strategy is needed to enable electronic publishing to realize its potential.

## Company profiles

Electronic publishing is still at an early stage of development and so it is interesting to examine the way companies are dealing with this marketing opportunity. Two companies were selected as representative of the present state of the industry: Pergamon and John Wiley. Both of these companies are superficially similar; both are UK arms of multinational publishing groups operating internationally and both were existing publishers of printed material before they moved into electronic publishing. However, it will be seen that there are significant differences, as well as similarities, in their approach to electronic publishing.

## Pergamon Press

Pergamon Press is part of a group of publishing companies controlled by Robert Maxwell. The group is split into three sections: Pergamon, British Printing and Communication Corporation (BPCC), and Mirror Group Newspapers. Pergamon Press is one of the largest publishing houses in the world, publishing journals, books and software for the international scientific community. Publishing offices are located in Oxford, New York, Toronto, Sao Paulo and Sydney, with sales offices in Beijing, Frankfurt, Tokyo and Hong Kong.

Butterworth and Company (Publishers) Ltd — one of the oldest publishers in the UK — and Springer-Verlag, the leading European scientific publisher, jointly founded Butterworth-Springer Ltd in London in 1948. Butterworth took control in 1951 and the name was changed to Pergamon Press. Pergamon is taken from the Greek town noted for the Altar of Pergamon, dedicated to Zeus.

In 1951 Pergamon published five journals and two books, with a turnover of £30,000 on which it made a loss. In 1986, this had grown to 6,000 book titles in print, 390 journals and serials, employing a workforce of 20,000 with annual sales in excess of £750 million. The company was reorganized in 1986, so that there are today four separate operating companies: Pergamon Journals Ltd, Pergamon Books Ltd, Pergamon Group Services Ltd and Pergamon Holdings Ltd. Pergamon Journals Ltd was acquired by BPCC, but all companies continue to operate from Headington Hill Hall, Oxford.

Complementing the printing activities of Pergamon are a number of electronic publishing activities. Pergamon Database Publishing includes 11 journals generated from the Current Awareness in Biological Sciences Database (CABS). Together with Infoline, services are offered for the design of information systems to handle capture, processing and storage of text and typeset output. Infoline provides the capability to disseminate information via magnetic media or, through databases, international online access.

Pergamon's ventures into database publishing have progressed to the stage where products can be obtained either online or as a hard copy. Some of these activities began in 1981 as joint ventures with research associations such as Pira and Rapra. Since then the number of databases has expanded at a rate of one or two per year.

The acquisition of Orbit from McLean has produced a company offering one of the largest online hosts in the world. Combining the two systems will create some difficulties initially as two different sets of operating commands and routes to references need to be reconciled. Work is proceeding to produce a joint operating system. Both services will be improved by the cross-fertilization process; a new improved Orbit will contain Infoline elements such as teleordering. Orbit-Infoline offers database information in three areas:

## 1. patents;

2. science and engineering;

3. business.

Specific subjects covered by category 2 include printing, packaging, photography, geology, water industry and paper manufacture. Category 3 includes information from sources such as Dun and Bradstreet, Jordan and Sons, and BIS. Information is provided on company accounts, company size and who owns whom.

A recent venture was an investigation of the use of CD-ROM in publishing. A product called Compact Solution was launched in the autumn of 1986. Compact Solution is a service for processing existing information, converting it into CD-ROM form. This entails product design, data conversion, data preparation, disk mastering and replication. Once the information is available in CD-ROM form, it is a very compact information package; a single disk contains around 500 megabytes of information, roughly the equivalent of 1,500 floppy disks or 200,000 A4 pages. The CD-ROM disks are played through a CD reader linked to an IBM pc or compatible computer and information retrieved by the Knowledge Retrieval System (KRS), licensed from Knowledgeset Corporation.

Other ventures in which Pergamon has been involved include software publishing. An example of the type of product in this area is Manuscript Manager, a wordprocessing package with added facilities to aid the academic author. Features include reference and citation insertion, and cross-referencing.

A new product, expected in September 1987, is Chemquest, a buyers' guide containing information on costs, where to obtain, etc.

## Pricing policies

The company has not set out to differentiate between prices for electronic and printed products. The company has learnt through experience, and the ability to provide information in both electronic and printed form has been useful. While the intention was not to subsidize online services through printed publishing, for a long time printed products were the main source of revenue. This remains true although the buying patterns are interesting. Initially, sales of the product in electronic form accounted for less than 10 per cent of revenue. Today electronic products account for between 30 and 40 per cent of the total revenue.

It is not only the growing commercial importance of the electronic side which is interesting. Revenue has grown despite the pricing of online access which makes it expensive when compared to printed products. Thus while a hard-copy subscription may be, say, \$200 per annum or much less, online access charges may be \$50 per hour. Despite this quite significant difference, online use is growing. Moreover, while hard-copy subscriptions are still undertaken mainly by libraries or some other central purchasing office of a customer, online subscriptions are largely placed by individual departments within organizations.

The majority of Pergamon's databases are in profit. The growth pattern is also different from that of hard-copy products. While it may take five years for a printed publication to realize a profit, it may take only three years for an online product. ## Manuscripts in electronic form

The use of authors' manuscripts when supplied in electronic form was investigated but has not been introduced generally as it was found that there were a number of difficulties. These may not apply to other publishing areas. Generally, the publisher does not know the authors, who may have chosen a journal according to a variety of factors such as prestige or time of publication. There is a need for an industry standard covering the use of manuscripts in electronic form but this has not materialized as yet. SGML has not reached to the author level; something is needed which automatically functions at this level. There is some progress towards this in the USA but an international journal receives input from all around the world including Asia and the Soviet Union and there will be problems when dealing with input from a mixture of sources. Possibly, a procedure could be set up to deal with production from floppy disk. This would entail the author initially sending a hard-copy printout to the editor. This would be revised and sent to the author for correcting, after which the author would send the corrected manuscript in both hard-copy and floppy disk form.

The situation is different in the case of review journals where the author is known and often already conforms to a standard laid down by the publisher when supplying camera-ready copy. It will be easier to move to electronic manuscripts in this case but there are still matters that have to be resolved. The academic community complain that if they correct text they are doing the publisher's work, and seek some reward for this. Perhaps the publisher will pay for the work. On the other hand, it has been suggested that the author is rewarded in other ways: for example by getting the manuscript printed more quickly.

Pergamon is fortunate in that it has the requisite expertise within its catalogue of companies to tackle any aspect of electronic publishing and also already figures in the areas of publishing where this can be put to the best use. However, it must be conceded that it is putting its expertise to good use. For example, it is envisaged that articles commissioned for a review journal, Pharmacology and Therapeutics, will find additional use as part of an encyclopaedia covering the same topics. Other spin-off products may seem more surprising at first. Quite popular is a mailing list generated by the journal Current Advances in Plant Science.

## John Wiley and Sons Ltd

John Wiley and Sons Ltd of Chichester has been in the business of publishing books and journals for nearly 180 years. It is part of an international group with offices in New York, Canada, Australia and Singapore. The company has been involved in electronic publishing for a number of years. A few years ago it followed the mood of excitement surrounding the technology and made a major commitment to electronic publishing. Today it is more cautious on the issue of electronic publishing. There is no significant funding within the company for experimentation with non-print products.

The first database to be published from Wiley's Chichester base was introduced in 1984. This was a directory of 25,000 medical research products being undertaken in the UK. Online business has so far been disappointing, as has been the involvement with software publishing in the educational and scientific areas, although there have been one or two notable successes. There are grounds for optimism concerning a new product, the Interactive Science Laboratory. This is a videodisk program, controlled and enhanced by a microcomputer. Designed for sixth-form or first-year undergraduates, the program covers four topics in physics and chemistry: chromatography, distillation, electrolysis and AC circuits. Students are offered various 'help' facilities and there are questions for assessment. The student's progress is monitored; sections studied, answers, use of 'help' facility and time taken are all recorded. It is thus possible to assess whether the program has been studied properly or whether the student has skipped sections or guessed answers.

The company is watching developments in the

CD-ROM market. A trial CD-ROM disk was produced in the USA but there is no major commitment to this form of publishing at present. This area presents special difficulties as support is more expensive. It is necessary to demonstrate the product: some potential users might not even know how to turn the machine on.

John Wiley and Sons Ltd has investigated the capture of authors' copy from floppy disk. There appeared to be potential for saving on typesetting costs. In practice this has not worked out. In the past not every typesetting company could handle floppy disks, although the situation is much better now. It is also difficult to decide how to put in format codes; should the author or printer do it? So far things have not worked out satisfactorily -- any external savings have been eroded by increased internal costs. In the right circumstances there could be benefits for author, publisher and printer but this would need organizing.

One area in which John Wiley and Sons Ltd is committed to information technology is in its use in-house. Warehouse stock control/distribution and the marketing/educational database are computerized on two separate but identical IBM mainframe systems. Computing was initially the province of a data processing department. This has grown in size but as there has also been a need to deal with other aspects of computing, particularly personal computers, an information services department has been created. Staff have welcomed the arrival of the personal computer and it has been company policy to provide micros where needed. Currently there are around 100 micros between 250 people. Although it is difficult to justify the purchase of a pc in terms of cost/benefits the feeling is that individual desk top.computers add to a beneficial working environment at a relatively low cost.

## Conclusion

The convergence of technology is aptly illustrated by electronic publishing. Not only is the technology employed itself a mix of computing and telecommunications, but aspects of both publishing and information science can be perceived. However, for a long time electronic publishing could be seen to have only a limited market. While information scientists could perceive the advantages of database searches compared to manual searching, electronic publishing did not reach society in general. Expensive equipment created a barrier to wide usage of electronic publishing which could be seen as another example of technology push.

Lower cost of equipment and widespread changes in the marketplace have led to a situation where electronic publishing is now establishing itself and could be poised for the long-awaited breakthrough. No longer is it necessary to use very expensive equipment to access databases. Practically any personal computer can be used with the addition of an inexpensive modem and networks have been set up especially for these home users, offering low subscription rates.

Electronic publishing is in an almost unique situation as it has utilized IT to develop new products rather than use the speed and throughput advantages of IT merely in a continuation of existing work practices. This gives electronic publishing both advantages and disadvantages with respect to other industries using IT. The market is still relatively new and, as it grows, the vendors of products and services (still few in number) have the opportunity to mould the market to their wishes. At the same time, few companies are really sure what products to develop or what price policies to follow.

Pricing policies for database products have followed a rule of thumb, appropriate in the days of expensive equipment and specialist users but possibly in need of revision in the present situation. At one time database publishing was supported financially through the printing and other activities of the companies involved. Of course, now that databases are making a profit in their own right, publishers are reluctant to change price structures. Publishers are likely to adopt new pricing policies only if it can be demonstrated that drastically reducing prices of database services would generate sufficient increase in usage to compensate. This is unlikely to happen unless a new vendor enters the arena who is prepared to adopt this pricing strategy. Another factor in the equation is that many successful database owners also publish the information in printed form and although, to date, electronic media have not appeared to compete with print media, the situation could change if electronic products were to become price competitive with traditional media.

Online searching was a completely new product when launched. After many years it can claim to be financially successful as well as much appreciated by the library community. The market is more suited to the launch of new products now than it was in the 1960s and 1970s, so that vendors might expect a more rapid movement into profitability than occurred with database publishing. However, few more recently launched products have shown immediate return on investment. Poor marketing has been one cause. Prestel was widely criticized for its failure to capitalize on a product that was highly regarded in many quarters and expected to perform much better than it did. Launched as an information service for the home, Prestel found a better market niche when segmented marketing of more specialized information reached the business community. Even so, Prestel is still way behind the French equivalent and, with the availability of less expensive database access via home computers, the advantage of television-based videotex may have disappeared.

Multi-media publishing has not been a great success. In this case other factors, such as a smaller potential sales target population and relatively expensive products and equipment, have contributed to product failure. Since many of these products are directed towards educational establishments the tight control of public sector finance in the UK during the 1980s might also be suggested as a detrimental factor.

Similarly, publishing on magnetic media has had its difficulties. In addition to the factors contributing to the poor performance of multi-media publishing, successive waves of technology have overtaken products before they could become established. Video disk was followed by optical disk and one form of this, CD-ROM, is now the focus of attention. This offers the promise of better things. It provides high capacity storage at reasonable cost. Large-scale manufacture would further reduce costs and the market for CD-ROM might explode. One disadvantage of CD-ROM is that the information stored on it cannot be altered. Erasable memories are under development but are believed to be two or more years away from the marketplace.

Another suggestion is that new electronic products are not really new but re-packaged forms of print products. When someone produces something that is really new and tailored to electronic media, electronic publishing could then really take off.

A major difference between electronic and print-based publishing is that electronic publishing requires that the user has access to equipment that can receive information in electronic form. The cost of this equipment has fallen drastically in the past few years and the potential user-base grown in inverse proportion, but the distinction still exists. At the moment the user provides this facility. In future the publisher of expensive printed products such as encyclopaedias may wish to consider providing, say, a CD-ROM player as a marketing ploy.

Electronic publishing is at an interesting stage in its development. Lower equipment costs, the development of expertise by product and service vendors, mature technology and reliability of products and, above all, a potential user-base of vast proportion, give promise of a rapid expansion of this sector of publishing in the near future.

## Biographical notes

Henry Holloway received his first degree, in Physics and Mathematics, from Durham University, and taught in England, Uganda and New Zealand. Returning to the UK, he studied part-time at Chelsea College for the MSc in Cybernetics of London University. Later, he received his PhD from the University of Aston in Birmingham, which followed a period at Pira, the research association for the UK paper and board industry. An independent consultant on printing and information technology, he has worked on a wide range of projects concerned with the impact of computer-based technology on the dissemination of information.
