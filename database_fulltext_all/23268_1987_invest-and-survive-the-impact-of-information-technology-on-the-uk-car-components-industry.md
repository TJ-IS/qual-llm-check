# Invest and Survive – the Impact of Information Technology on the UK Car Components Industry

Nathan Haynes, Coventry (Lanchester) Polytechnic

## Introduction

There can be no doubt that information technology (IT) is making great changes to the way we live and to the society we live in. The manufacture of products is no exception. Indeed it is in business that IT seems to have its strongest foothold. Its effects on the business environment are endemic. As Keen (1985) puts it:

IT intentionally and radically changes the office landscape, the nature of work, the dynamics of competition, and the economics of doing business.

This report seeks to highlight some of the strategic effects IT is having, or will have, on the UK car components industry, by pulling together current trends in the car component industry with the rapid advances in IT which have occurred over the past few years. It then goes on to forecast the possible effects that IT may have on the future of car component manufacturers and the structure of the industry itself.

## The UK car component industry

Owing to the fact that a car component can be anything from a complete electronic engine planning system, produced by a multinational company, to a small plastic-name plate used for cosmetic purposes, produced by a man in his garden shed a few yards down the road from the car assembly plant, it is difficult to produce any meaningful definition of the car components industry. However, the UK government (Minutes of Evidence, 1985) defines participants in the industry as any firm manufacturing:

... components forming part of or fitted as standard equipment to on-road vehicles powered by internal combustion engines, but excluding in-vehicle entertainment, accessories and liquids.

The car components industry in the UK – despite the wide variety of different car components – is highly concentrated, with many car component firms specializing in particular areas of car componentry (i.e. Chloride – carbatteries, Champion – spark plugs, and Pilkington – safety glass), and in many of these separate areas one or two companies have either a monopolistic or oligopolistic hold on the market. A further round of recent mergers, for example the takeover of Automotive Products by the BBA Group, have led to an even more concentrated industry.

One of the causes of this increasing concentration must have been the severe recession the car component industry has weathered recently. Figure 1 shows the output of the UK car component industry over the years 1979 to 1984.

Naturally enough the car component industry is linked very closely to the car industry itself and so any trends within the car industry will be reflected in the car component industry. The UK car industry has suffered badly in recent years with the UK now being the home of only one large indigenous car producer (Rover Group) and three foreign-owned multinationals (General Motors, Ford, and Peugeot-Citroen-Talbot) plus a number of smaller producers. This situation has been brought about by the increased internationalism of the world's car markets following the creation of General Motors' 'world car' concept.

The trends for the world automobile industry can be pin-pointed as follows:

\- the increasing competition in world car markets, especially in Western Europe (Bhaskar, 1979);

\- the increasing multinational nature of car production and car markets (Bhaskar, 1980);

\- the increasing influence of low wage and Eastern bloc producers (Altshurer et al., 1985);

\- the continuance of a large number of major car producers because of the high barriers to entry and exit (Altshurer et al., 1985);

\- the increase in inter-company projects and agreements (Altshurer et al., 1985).

Unfortunately, space does not allow for further discussion of these points but they do have an effect on the long-term trends affecting the car component industry. It has been estimated (Bhaskar, 1979) that anything up to 70 per cent of a British car's value can be attributed to the cost of its component parts, and this dependence at first brought about an uncompromising attitude among the purchasing departments of the large car producers. However, this attitude has now changed, and with the introduction of Just-In-Time (JIT) supply methods and single sourcing, the relationships between car component suppliers and their customers have become more open, friendly and mutually beneficial to both parties (Lamming, 1986). Such moves have led to the following state of affairs in the car component industry:

\- competition is on the basis of lowest cost and differentiated product;

\- increased rationalization is occurring within the industry;

\- there are fewer companies chasing larger, but fewer, orders;

\- there is an increasing multinational feel to the industry;

\- a high level of technical competence is now required to compete effectively within the industry.

Firms compete in the car component industry in one of two ways — most usually by cost advantages but sometimes by producing a product which is technically superior to any other in the marketplace. Traditionally costs have been all important and the rationalization of the industry in recent years can be seen as an attempt by firms to use economies of scale to try to keep costs under control and remain competitive. The pressure for such moves has been strengthened by the 'multinationalization' of car producers, once again to ensure economies of scale. Only companies large enough to be able to supply sufficiently cheaply the large amount of components that the car producers require, delivered or produced where the car producers want them to be produced, will remain competitive on a world scale. This last point has also led to many UK component manufacturers placing manufacturing facilities outside the UK so that they can be close to their major customers and still supply them by overcoming stringent home-produced car content restrictions prevalent in many car producing countries: examples of this include GKN in West Germany and France, and Armstrong Equipment in Canada and Spain (Minutes of Evidence, 1986).

## Advances in information technology

Once again IT covers such a broad area that a definition is difficult to formulate. Keen (1985) highlights the problem in the following way:

The field of information technology (IT) is now too broad for anyone really to master it. IT – computers, communications, data, work stations and the analytical and technical disciplines that shape software and its applications – is like the word transportation, which runs the gamut from bicycles to airplanes and involves many hidden economic and social issues.

Recent advances in IT have been in four previously separate, but now converging, areas:

\- product improvements;

\- manufacturing process improvements;

\- communications;

\- office automation.

The advances of IT within products can be seen by the multiplicity of items, used both at home and within businesses, which either have used microprocessors to make them more complex or efficient, or are completely new products. Examples of these two kinds of advance are numerous: take for example the use of microprocessors in washing machines, televisions and business telephone systems, and the introduction of pocket calculators and personal computers which would have been only a dream 20 years ago. IT has also had an effect on the goods produced by the car component manufacturers — for instance electronically controlled anti-lock braking and fuel injection systems and on-board car computers. It has been said that apart from the use of new materials the use of electronics will have the biggest impact on car component design and manufacture in the years to come (Minutes of Evidence, 1985).

Changes in manufacturing processes have probably been the most important for manufacturing in general, and the car component industry in particular, that IT has brought about. The use of computer-aided design (CAD) systems has been prevalent in the industry for some time (Golding 1985). Nowadays, however, IT makes it possible to pre-program what a machine on the shop-floor will do (computer-aided manufacture - CAM - using direct numerical controlled - DNC - machines), to link the information to the original design process (CAD/CAM), and to have a computer tell the operator when the goods should be made (computer-aided process planning - CAPP). Work-in-progress can now be moved about using automated storage and retrieval systems (ASRS) and automated guided vehicles (AGVs); indeed, some companies have linked CAD/CAM systems to automatic handling equipment to produce flexible manufacturing systems (FMSs) which can handle changes in the requirements of a number of parts very easily (Huang and Chen, 1986). On the horizon, although no company has yet reached this level, is computer-integrated manufacture (CIM) where artificial intelligence (IA) will allow FMS manufacturing facilities to be linked into other business systems to enable entire factories to be run 'automatically' using advanced IT techniques (Mather, 1986; Boaden and Dale, 1986).

The major strategic importance of IT in the manufacturing process is that it allows companies to produce smaller batches of products and still not suffer from a lack of economies of scale. Thus smaller companies, with a smaller cost base brought about by using IT in their manufacturing processes, will be able to compete against the larger companies who have the advantages of large economies of scale.

Communications have also been improved by IT in two ways: it is now easier to communicate, both between firms' computer systems and between people, with the universal use of the networking of personal computers, electronic mail and facsimile machines. Such improvements make it easier for firms to co-ordinate between manufacturing units which are geographically widespread and, in doing so, to control business policy and manufacturing strategy more accurately. Porter and Millar (1985) put it this way:

Finally, the new technology has a powerful effect on competitive scope. Information systems allow companies to co-ordinate value activities in far-flung geographic locations.

The advances in office administration brought about by IT, such as the introduction of personal computers and wordprocessors, should not only reduce a company's cost base by making its paperwork less burdensome, but should also help a company to be more competitive by improving the decision-making capacity of management.

## Information technology and the UK car component industry

How, therefore, do these changes in IT link up with the trends seen in the UK car component industry? And how will IT strategically affect the industry at large? In the long run, IT will change the whole structure of the industry by changing the ground rules by which the component manufacturers now compete. The shift to a new industrial structure has already begun and the increasing use of IT in the industry will certainly speed up the process.

Because of the future structure of the car industry, car component suppliers will survive only if they can secure the fewer number of larger orders the car companies are offering. Traditionally competition for such orders was based solely on price, and only those companies with cost advantages — brought about through large plants and economies of scale, or with great flexibility, through being small — would get the business. Thus, within the traditional structure of the industry very large or very small car component manufacturers were successful while medium-sized companies found profits hard to maintain.

However, the implementation of IT has meant that instead of just competing on price, car component companies are now more likely to win large orders from car makers because of a higher level of customer service or a better delivery performance than their competitors. This has come about for the reasons that the buyer/seller relationship between the car producers and their component suppliers has become more friendly, and that IT now holds the key for car component firms in such a market.

It is the component suppliers who have the technology to give their customers better service by being able to link in with the buying company's engineering systems who are more likely to be the car makers' key suppliers for particular products in the future. Already companies such as the Rover Group are linking their CAD systems to those of their suppliers and computerized scheduling systems are now following the same path: the company's ARG Metro replacement was originally intended to be the first 'paperless car' (Lamming, 1986). As McFarlan (1984) and Porter and Millar (1985) point out, suppliers who have the technology can link up with car producers and are usually given advanced warnings of new designs which can effectively lock other suppliers out of lucrative contracts.

Because of the increased multinationalism of the car industry, the use of communications technology is becoming increasingly important for the competitive supplier. The decline of the UK motor industry has forced UK suppliers to site factories abroad, close to the production sites of foreign car makers, in order to give better customer service and delivery performance, and this trend is likely to continue (Minutes of Evidence, 1985; 1986). A high level of communications technology is therefore imperative if a supplier is to make a profit in the future by effectively controlling its manufacturing facilities which increasingly will become more geographically widespread.

In addition, to be successful in the car component industry, firms in the West will have to use IT to produce a technically advanced product; otherwise their competitive edge will be taken away by firms producing in the low wage countries of the developing world (Altshurer et al., 1985).

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, IT will effectively change the structure of the car component industry by making a medium-sized firm one which is the optimum size for competitive cost advantages within the industry. As was mentioned previously, the use of CIM or FMS will obviate the need for car component companies to seek manufacturing economies of scale; flexibility will be the key. Hence large firms in the industry will tend to shrink in size, while small firms will have to grow larger to accommodate the large capital outlays incurred when implementing an FMS.

To sum up, therefore, to be successful in the UK car component industry in the future a company ideally should:

\- be medium-sized;

\- have advanced manufacturing process capabilities (CIM or FMS);

\- be able to link up with car producers' CAD and CAPP systems;

\- have a good communications network to allow for flexibility in the positioning of future manufacturing sites;

\- have a technologically advanced product offering.

## Opportunities and threats

It is clear, therefore, that there are plenty of opportunities for firms in the car component industry taking on new technology. IT could easily give them the competitive edge in an industry which is rapidly changing shape; large orders, closer links with customers and a secure long-term future may rest with suppliers who are bold in their approaches to implementing IT. However, IT may also bring with it some hidden threats: for instance IT may push a company towards operational ineptness before any real strategic gains can be made. In fact three threats must be tackled before strategic gains using IT can be made. These are:

\- the problem of people;

\- the problem of organization;

\- the problem of computer protocols.

First, many writers (Evans, 1979; Watson-Gandy, 1986) have written about the problems of IT and its effects on the workforce, both on the absolute loss of jobs and the deskilling of the jobs that remain. In an industry which has been plagued by industrial relations problems in the past, and where the unions see the introduction of IT as another bargaining tool (Watson-Gandy, 1986), executives in the car component industry must treat the problem with sympathy, yet with authority, to see that long-term strategic gains in using IT are not squandered by short-term policies to placate the unions.

Second, as well as the possibility of having to change their size, car component companies will also have to face the probability that their organizational structures will also have to alter quite considerably. Increasingly integration is the key word in IT for business (Data Management, 1986) and companies which continue to work on purely functional lines will find it increasingly difficult to implement new technologies which require a heavy use of project teams (Wild, 1986). Therefore companies within the car components industry will be forced increasingly to undertake programmes of reorganization in order to implement IT and should emerge from the process smaller, but probably more flexible and responsive, as their structure will most probably be based along the lines of matrix organizations with many separate project teams looking after each branch of the new technology (Mather, 1986; Wild, 1986).

Finally, many of the car component manufacturers will face problems of computer protocol, between different machines within their own factories as well as with links into their customers' systems. The problem has already arisen with CAD systems where, because each major car company insisted that its own system should be used by suppliers in order for contracts to be won by these suppliers, car component makers have had the awful choice of either trying to support a multiplicity of CAD protocols or losing one or two major orders (Golding, 1985).

Despite all this, it is clear that the car components companies who manage to resolve these problems and implement new IT concepts successfully are those who will form the basis of a more flexible car component industry in the future.

## Conclusion

IT will increasingly change the structure of the car component industry by reducing the importance of economies of scale, and increasing the need for flexibility and technical competence of firms, in the industry. Medium-sized firms, with the advantages of production and design flexibility and a competitive cost structure, which the introduction of IT will bring, will replace the traditional large corporations and the small 'back-street' operators. The onset of improved communications with IT will mean that car component firms will increasingly be locked in more closely with their suppliers and this will mean that car component manufacturers will become either dedicated to particular car producers or, as is more likely to be the case, increasingly multinational.

Despite recent reports that the car market may be in a slow decline (Gooding, 1987), it is almost certain that there will continue to be profits to be made in the car component industry for years to come. IT will lead to great opportunities for competitive advantages for car component firms who implement the new technology. There is only one real threat that IT brings to car component makers, and that is the threat to those companies who do not use it. Such companies will be swept away by the rapidly rising tide of increased flexibility and productivity that IT is bringing to many car component firms. As Siegfried Höhn (1986), the Director of Strategy and Investments at Volkswagen, has said:

The rapid change in the programs for industrial production on the one hand, and the explosive development of new information technologies on the other hand, do not allow companies to adopt a 'wait-and-see' attitude.

It is important for car component companies to make sure that they do not 'wait-and-see', whether or not Höhn is proved correct.
