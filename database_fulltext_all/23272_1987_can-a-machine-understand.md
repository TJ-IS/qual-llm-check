# Can a Machine Understand?

Paul Taylor, Kobler Unit, Department of Computing, Imperial College

The claims, aims and achievements of workers in artificial intelligence (AI) are often made the subjects of discussion in other fields. One such field is philosophy, which represents a radically different kind of enterprise from computer science, concerned with resolving conceptual confusions. Some of these are the traditional problems of philosophy; others are taken from newer, more dynamic disciplines. A question of the latter class, which will concern us here, is whether a machine can be said to understand.

Considered as a philosophical question this is not the practical or empirical question it might be for a computer scientist. It is not, however, merely a linguistic question, but one to do with what we can work out about the concept of understanding just by thinking about it. This obviously lacks something in comparison with an approach where the problem is attacked, soldering iron in hand, since if we use only Occam's Razor we can never even fail to build the machine; the most we can hope to produce is a readable book! Philosophical discussion can, however, serve an invaluable function as a critical force aiding those trying to build such machines – but only if it is recognized within the AI community that a weak point has been found, and if the philosophical analysis can be translated into the conceptual language of AI.

The philosopher whose arguments have provided the focus for most of the recent philosophical debate about AI is John Searle. Searle (1980) was concerned to criticize the script-based approach to story-understanding practised by Schank and his colleagues. The details of this work are not important here, since what is under attack are general assumptions providing the rationale for the research: that a machine can be said to understand if it appears to understand, that running a program is sufficient to cause this understanding.

The subsequent debate generated a fair amount of misunderstanding, so it might be as well to be quite clear about what Searle was not saying. His point was not that a machine could never be said to understand — for in a sense we are machines. Nor did he claim that a man-made machine or a digital computer could never be said to understand. The claim was simply this: that a machine could not be said to understand by virtue of the program running on the machine — an assumption apparently being made by a number of AI workers.

The force of the argument in Searle's paper is carried by a powerful thought-experiment. The reader is asked to imagine a room into which Chinese symbols are fed and out of which Chinese symbols are passed. Inside the room a person is consulting a set of rules telling him or her how to put together symbols for output, which are related to the input symbols in such a way as to generate the illusion of understanding Chinese. Searle's point is, simply, that it is an illusion. The person in the room, by following the rules, is implementing a formal program, just as a computer programmed to 'understand' Chinese would be implementing a formal program, but is not thereby able to understand Chinese.

Many people in the AI field have criticized Searle's arguments by taking up the analogy, and trying to show that although the person in the room does not understand Chinese, the room as a system does; or that if the room was in a robot able to interact with the world, then it would understand. Searle anticipated these objections and maintained that, in the first case, even if one imagined the whole system operating inside someone's head, it still would not be the case that the person understood. Such a person would be unable to relate the word 'hamburgers' (in Chinese) to the objects we know as hamburgers, and simply would not know that the word referred to the thing. To deal with the second case Searle simply says that, in his view, adding perceptual and motor capabilities simply adds perceptual and motor capabilities, not understanding.

The responses to Searle's paper (Commentary, 1980) show that these defences did little to convince people opposed to Searle's thesis. It seems that once the thought-experiment is modified our intuitions about it are less clear, and those who are disposed to disagree feel they can find some basis for their convictions. It might be as well, therefore, to leave the analogy and consider what Searle is trying to say about understanding, whether he has identified a conceptual difficulty for AI and whether his analysis can be of value to those working in the field.

It is certainly the case that when we understand, we often have a related conscious awareness, which may seem the sort of thing that a computer would not get by running a program. However, it is not clear that this subjective feeling is an important part of understanding. I would say that if this was all the computer lacked then it would still have to be argued that the computer did not understand, and since Searle does not argue this point he must believe that there is something other than the feeling of 'what-it-is-like-to-understand', other than behaving as if one understood and which is crucial for understanding.

Searle's consideration of what understanding is, is a part of his consideration of intentionality (Searle, 1983). Intentionality is a characteristic of certain mental states, including understanding. Briefly, a mental state is intentional if it is about something. It is characteristic of understanding, believing, desiring — that when one understands, believes, desires, one understands, believes, desires that something. This property of 'aboutness' is a characteristic of certain mental states which does not seem to be shared by the non-mental (a book is not about something by itself; it only borrows intentionality from an intentional agent). This might suggest that only brains can cause it and that it could never be shared by a computer, but it does not require this and Searle did not argue this. He argued only that his thought-experiment showed that running a computer program could not guarantee that it would arise.

Our question must now be whether a philosophical analysis of intentionality can help us to see if a computer could understand. Searle makes two points of crucial importance: that an intentional state can only exist within a Network of such states and that an intentional state can only exist against a Background that itself is not intentional.

Imagine a Neolithic man uttering the sentence, 'I wish to stand as Republican candidate in the next election'. Searle would say that a person could not have such a desire without having an indefinite but finite number of related beliefs and desires about America, elections, primaries etc. These related beliefs and desires are the Network.

A belief is a representation; however, the capacity to use such representations must consist of something other than knowledge, contained in representations, about how to use representations. Otherwise we have an infinite regress. So we need a Background of non-representational capacities. The Background is not knowledge or belief, but a precondition for having knowledge or belief, or any intentional state. Searle wishes to hand over quite a lot to this Background. For example, he would say, 'I do not believe the floor is able to take my weight; I just act as though it is because just-acting-as-though-it-is is part of my Background'. The ideas expressed here are very similar to those associated with Wittgenstien. Wittgenstien (1953) wrote that: 'If a lion could speak we would not be able to understand him', for understanding depends upon shared 'forms of life'. Forms of life are not known or believed—they just are. And we just act accordingly.

If these ideas are accepted, and there is much in the above that I find convincing, then could a computer be built which would relate 'hamburger' and hamburgers? The question is that if Searle has found a weak point, can we translate his analysis into the conceptual language of AI?

There exists a belief within part of the AI community that machines with a parallel architecture, or a certain type of such architecture, may offer the key. These machines look much more like brains. There is no passing of information to and from addresses via a central processing unit, but rather computation depends upon the connection structure of neuron-like computing units. These 'Connectionist' machines respond holistically, in that states change suddenly, not bit by bit, and so they fit with the idea of the Network. They fit too with the hypothesis of the Background, as much more depends upon how the units are connected than on what is explicitly represented.

Whether these analogies are telling and whether building small, simple connectionist machines helps us when the brain is so large and so complex — and intelligence perhaps an all or none affair — is an open question. It may be that the future of AI lies with machines that have an increasing capacity for appearing to understand or with machines that serve as intelligence amplifiers, having no Background of their own. It would be more exciting, however, if Searle's philosophical analysis could be translated into the concepts of Connectionist machines and could help us to build real artificial intelligence.
