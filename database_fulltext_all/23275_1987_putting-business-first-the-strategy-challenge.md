# Putting Business First: the Strategy Challenge

David Harvey, Editor, Business Computing & Communications

## Introduction

Business Computing & Communications was launched in November 1983 specifically to address the management issues associated with information technology (IT). It is written primarily for senior executives, as well as management information services and data processing managers, concerned with the way computer systems are changing the corporate, business and social environment. The magazine deals with all aspects of the impact of information technology on companies. IT's significance for business strategy has become an increasingly important theme over the past year or so. Interest in conducting a survey that explored how the leading consultancies tackled the problem of aligning information technology with business objectives was a natural extension of Business Computing & Communications' concern with the strategic dimension of IT.

## Background to The IT Consultancy Report\*

A preliminary survey was carried out by Business Computing & Communications in February 1986 covering 26 consultancies, and the main conclusions were summarized briefly in an article in the May 1986 issue. Since then, the scope of the survey has been significantly enlarged in a number of ways, in the belief that the growing interest on the part of companies searching for a pay-off from IT meant that more would be looking to consultancies to help them with this crucial quest.

All the respondents were re-surveyed, using an extended questionnaire, and additional consultancies were brought into the survey. Thirty-nine consultancies were contacted and 35 replied.

Respondents were asked to provide names of client organizations for which information technology strategies had been carried out and these were invited to comment on the results of assignments in practice. Eleven client companies give their verdict on the consultancy assignments they commissioned.

## The current problem

Research carried out on both sides of the Atlantic demolishes the simplistic notion that merely investing in information technology guarantees business improvements. On the contrary, the evidence suggests that there is no simple correlation between IT investment and improved performance.

A long-term project being carried out in the USA is measuring corporate returns from expenditure on information technology. Provisional findings of the Product Impact of Marketing Strategies (PIMS) study, based on the analysis of 60 companies, show that only a handful have achieved returns on their investment in computer systems. The study sets out to identify the strategic advantages gained from the use of information technology to gain improvements in market share or other performance measures relevant to a given company. Although the negative aspect is that the majority of organizations studied merely increased their overheads, the encouraging finding is that the few which demonstrated better performance saw a threefold, or higher, return on their investment. The few were those who were able to identify and support activities which were critical to the success of their businesses.

A major UK survey conducted in 1984 by

A T Kearney for the Department of Trade and Industry, The Barriers and Opportunities of Information Technology, tells a similar story. An estimated 20 per cent of the £5 billion annual expenditure on IT was wasted, through failure to target high priority business functions, disregard for customer service, and other factors. By contrast, the study, corroborating the PIMS findings, indicated that the positive result comes when IT supports strategically significant goals, such as increased market share, better customer service or increased product acceptability.

Given that the challenge is to achieve this happy marriage of technology with business goals, it would seem that many companies have failed in the attempt to realize this objective if, that is, they have even tried.

Price Waterhouse's annual Information Technology Review 1986-87 reports that half the companies covered in its survey are computerizing their activities without reference to what they are trying to achieve in business terms. The review makes the comment that ‘...any IT study undertaken outside the framework of what the company is trying to achieve must either founder or, worse, succeed in making a plan to achieve technological objectives for their own sake’.

Further confirmation of the failure to relate information technology to corporate objectives comes from recent research into the practice of information systems planning in 130 UK organizations. Bob Galliers, head of the School of Computing and Quantitative Studies at the Western Australia Institute of Technology, who conducted his study while on secondment to the London School of Economics in 1986, found that in up to 50 per cent of companies covered by his survey, information systems planning was either totally divorced from, or tenuously linked to, corporate strategy.

As many as 80 per cent of those responsible for information systems planning made no distinction between strategic and tactical planning. In only 20 per cent of the organizations surveyed was the desire to match information systems to business need advanced as the prime motive for information systems planning. Not surprisingly, in the light of these findings, 41 per cent of the sample felt that their planning was unsuccessful and the single most important factor that was attributed to this failure was the absence of senior management support. In these circumstances, information technology is being implemented in a management vacuum, sealed off from the real needs of the organization.

## The consultancy marketplace

Any organization trying to identify suitable consultancies to help with the formulation of an information technology strategy is faced with a fundamental problem: who to turn to. In this relatively new area of consultancy, the players are constantly growing in number. Pinpointing those who specialize in strategy, as opposed to solely technical, assignments is no simple task and it was part of the survey's objectives to discover the main consultancies which claimed to be active in the field today. Respondents fall into four broad categories:

\- First, there are those who have a background in traditional management consultancy including Arthur D Little, Binder Hamlyn, Inbucon, A T Kearney, Handley Walker, Nolan Norton, PA Computing and Telecommunications, PE Information Systems and SRI International. Some, including Atkins Planning and SRI, also have broader interests in other areas such as industrial, technical and economic research.

\- Second, there are the management consultancy arms of the audit-based firms such as Arthur Andersen, Coopers & Lybrand, Deloitte Haskins & Sells, Grant Thornton, KMG, Thomson McLintock, Peat Marwick Mitchell, Price Waterhouse, Stoy Hayward, Touche Ross and Arthur Young.

\- Third, there are the computing services and system houses: BIS Applied Systems, CAP, CMG, Hoskyns, Logica, James Martin Associates, Scicon and Wootton Jeffreys.

\- Fourth, there are the specialist information technology consultancies set up to provide consultancy services, and sometimes also research: Business Information Techniques, Butler Cox, Database Consultants Europe, Eosys, Index, LBMS and Paul Strassman Inc.

The significant differences in opinion about the scope of information technology strategy assignments are, to some extent, explicable once the background of the consultancies is taken into account.

## Main findings of survey

The survey revealed the following points:

\- Client organizations find that IT strategy studies are often only partially or marginally effective in identifying opportunities for competitive advantage. Only two client studies appeared to have been concerned primarily with realizing the objectives of an IT strategy study which directly relates information technology to corporate goals.

\- One of the most striking findings of the survey of consultancies operating in the UK is that the respondents interpret the scope of an IT strategy survey in widely contrasting ways. The major division is between business-oriented and technologically-driven consultancies. At one end of the spectrum there are consultancies which will deliver a strategy recommendation based exclusively on IT opportunities arising from business needs. At the other end, there are consultancies which will deliver what is essentially a long-term plan for the information systems department, usually based on a data architecture study. The majority of firms were somewhere in between, although their bias tended towards technologically-led strategies.

\- The terms of reference under which consultancies conduct strategy studies range from an insistence on the managing director's active support to a readiness to proceed under the sponsorship of the data processing or management information services executive alone.

\- While most consultancies insist on proceeding only when business priorities have been established, some will go ahead without a corporate strategy, sometimes in the hope that the planning which goes into an assignment of this sort will lead to the formulation of a business strategy.

\- The elapsed time for a study varies significantly for client companies. For example, where large organizations are concerned, the time-scale ranges from three to twelve months. Overall, assignments take from a month to a year to complete, according to the size of the organization.

\- The majority of consultancies expect to follow through a strategy assignment with the implementation of systems, ranging from advice on hardware and software selection to systems design and building. Few consultancies are concerned solely with IT strategy.

\- A minority of respondents explicitly proclaim themselves to be independent consultants who offer no other services. In several other cases, respondents also supply products and computing services either of their own or as agents for a third party source.

## The strategy conundrum

Part of the problem associated with discussing information technology strategy is that it means different things to different people. Although the term was defined for the purposes of the survey as 'a programme for the introduction of computer and communications systems in support of activities which have been established as central to the attainment of pre-defined corporate objectives', it is clear from respondents' replies that this leaves plenty of scope for interpretation.

All the consultancies subscribe to the principle that an information technology strategy must be business led. But it is apparent from their answers to the survey questionnaire that they believe this condition can be satisfied with varying degrees of stringency. In practical terms, there is clearly some confusion as to whether an information technology strategy is being carried out primarily for the company or the data processing or MIS department.

Few consultancies offer their own definition of the term ‘information technology strategy’. One of the exceptions is A T Kearney, who affirm: ‘An IT strategy is an enabling device to allow the business to be transacted most effectively.’

While many consultancies see an information strategy as a sub-set of corporate strategy, a number felt that an information technology strategy can still be worthy of the name even when business goals have not been fully defined.

PActel was one of the few firms to highlight alternative interpretations of the term. While arguing that something less than a strategy may be justified in certain cases, PActel believes that it is important to be clear on the difference: 'We tend to differentiate between a set business strategy for which an IT parallel is required (an IT strategy) and an assignment where IT issues are included with business strategic planning (IT positioning), although IT and business should never be totally divorced.'

Another respondent, BIT, claims that the terminology is potentially confusing and argues that an information technology strategy must be understood as part of the business plan. 'An IT strategy without business goals is a meaningless concept', it says. 'The key misconception here arises because many "IT strategies" are developed by/with the MIS function as the "client". In this case, the project is actually a business strategy for the MIS "business unit", whose client is the parent organization. This is not an IT strategy for the parent organization itself. Consultants who confuse these two quite different activities (the MIS business strategy and the parent company's IT strategy) mislead both themselves and the client.'

There are some sharp differences in the way consultancies both view assignments and carry through their thinking in terms of the techniques involved in client studies.

On the one hand there are those such as A T Kearney, BIT, Index, Nolan Norton and Strassmann who see the primary aim of a strategy assignment as finding the major points of leverage where information technology can be used to improve business performance. Consultancies in this group often take the client only to the stage where the opportunities for capitalizing on the use of information technology have been established. Implementation of the system is often beyond their scope.

On the other hand, there are respondents who see an assignment of this type as a study which essentially leads to a long-term strategy for the information systems function. Typically, consultancies in this group place great emphasis on the need to develop a comprehensive data architecture and their studies include activities such as data modelling and analysis. CMG, LBMS, Logica, Scicon and SRI are some of those who place an emphasis on these aspects of strategy studies.

In contrast, Strassmann maintains that only a very small number of strategic factors make a difference to the company's performance and rejects the large-scale architectural studies as strategically irrelevant. In his view, the consultant's role is to 'objectively sort out the few improvement programmes which should be emphasized and to reconcile differences of opinion about priorities'.

Most consultancies take a line between these two extremes, many claiming to have a pragmatic approach to work in this field. P-E Information Systems, for example, say that their studies are 'driven by the terms of reference'. This flexibility of approach is also found in the case of Deloitte Haskins & Sells, who claim to link IT to the direction of the client's business in a number of ways: a client can build future plans on the basis of its current systems; or use IT to support individual business functions; or adopt a technology-led strategy, or a data architecture approach. Finally, the client has the option of adopting a strategic linkage employing some, or all, of these approaches in achieving the organization's main objectives.

## Business or IT strategists?

One of the ways in which potential clients can assess consultancies is according to the weight they give to aligning information technology with corporate strategy. While all respondents are prepared to help an organization define its corporate strategy or identify its priorities, although with varying degrees of enthusiasm, by no means all see the absence of an agreed strategy as an insurmountable obstacle to carrying out an IT assignment. A few respondents will continue with a study on the assumption that business goals will emerge in the course of the assignment.

Respondents differed significantly as to how they see the relationship between corporate strategy, or business goals, and an IT strategy assignment. Among those who regard an IT strategy explicitly as a sub-set of the business plan is Butler Cox: 'Our definition of an IT strategy explicitly refers to its relationship with the business strategy. Hence, it is not conceivable that an IT strategy could be developed without a clear understanding of the client's business strategy.' Index not only insists on a corporate strategy but goes on to amplify its argument with the assertion that 'A key new question for companies is "Where in our business do we need to build a competitive technology edge in the next five years?"'

LBMS is one respondent who maintains that an assignment can still be profitably followed through without a pre-defined corporate strategy: 'Even when circumstances are not ideal, it is possible to demonstrate success with partial strategic planning approaches, which improve the likelihood of a more comprehensive review a year or so later.'

Arthur D Little, one of the 13 consultancies which will ‘occasionally’ embark on a strategy study without a fully-fledged corporate plan to work with, makes the point that: ‘Quite often, the assignment can profitably focus on specific profit improvement opportunities and the IT needed to capitalise on them.’ The consultancy goes on to say that ‘This may be sub-optimal, but a valid return on investment nevertheless.’

Several respondents qualify their readiness to help a company arrive at a set of agreed corporate objectives. A number say that they would only do so if this is explicitly included in the brief. Some argue that it is not their role to extend the terms of reference of a study.

PActel makes the practical point that 'if a client requests consultancy support for an IT strategy, it rarely takes kindly to the consultancy attempting to enlarge the assignment'.

Insisting on working within a corporate strategy is one thing; being confident of its validity is another. While James Martin Associates claims that 'A mechanism for identifying business goals must be an agreed part of the assignment', the consultancy goes on to say that where business goals have already been defined, these need to be 'validated' by subsequent interviews. A number of other consultancies point out that what is codified is not necessarily the final word on the subject. Inbucon states that a formal statement can be 'next to useless if high on wishful thinking and low on strategy development'.

Strassman goes as far as to argue that it may even be positively misleading. He favours a different way of obtaining a fix on corporate objectives and argues that analysis of the evidence of what an organization actually does yields more useful information about strategy than paying heed to what is written down about its objectives. 'Every company has a strategy', Strassmann says. 'It is revealed in the way the company has been making its investment decisions. Any piece of prose that articulates the company's strategic intent should not be accepted as a satisfactory statement of what to expect. What matters is what the company does, which can be evaluated from a thorough review of its financial and competitive results.'

BIT maintains that ‘there is absolutely no need for a formal business strategy before the study’, provided that the consultant has established through discussions with the chief executive officer ‘what business he is in’ and ‘key business goals, issues/problems’.

In practice, there can be real problems for consultants who attempt to sort out the issues for their clients. PActel is straightforward in its recognition of the fact that it must deal with clients as the consultancy finds them. It confesses that 'IT strategy activity is frequently very divorced from the business planning activity, both organisationally, timewise and culturally.' But in such cases, the consultancy feels that it is usually 'possible to get sufficient statements of business direction, even if they are not formally written out to undertake a strategy study.'

In the same way that respondents are in different camps on the issue of corporate strategy, consultancies can be grouped according to the level of management involvement they insist on. Clearly, any major decisions about the business require the support of the board, and it follows that those consultancies which seek to ensure that an information technology strategy is business led, place great emphasis on the participation of management. Respondents will, variously:

\- never undertake an assignment without the managing director's involvement;

\- sometimes do so;

\- accept some form of board sponsorship; or

— undertake an assignment without board sponsorship.

Most of the respondents say that they expect the active co-operation of the client's managing director. But among the exceptions are some who make interesting comments. Arthur D Little, CAP, SRI and Stoy Hayward all say that they will 'occasionally' take on an assignment without the managing director's co-operation. Eosys comments that some degree of board-level support is needed: 'If the MD is hostile, the study is not viable, but an uncommitted managing director is acceptable.' Atkins Planning says that it will occasionally proceed in the hope that the managing director's attitude will be changed in the course of the study.

Two of the most revealing replies come from Butler Cox and PActel. Butler Cox maintains that it will not proceed with a study, unless the aim is to win the managing director's support. PActel's point is similar — at least one relevant director must be associated with the study, although the consultancy had sometimes assisted the MIS head to put his case in the face of the managing director's lack of interest. Significantly, PActel also reports that most of its assignments come from MIS managers, or their immediate bosses, and rarely from chief executives.

## Competitor analysis

Respondents reveal that they have differing policies on examining the use of IT by a client's competitors. Only five firms say that they always do a competitive analysis; sixteen do so generally and eight do so only occasionally.

Of those who always conduct a review of the technological trends in the client's sector, Coopers & Lybrand carries out market research through the consultancy's marketing consultants and the company's extensive client database. Literature searches and other forms of desk research are also undertaken.

Butler Cox is another consultancy which normally undertakes a comparative review, based on research conducted for the Butler Cox Foundation or included in the company's reports. Supplementary information may be added through additional interviews and discussions. The amount of effort which goes into research varies considerably.

Touche Ross is prepared to conduct special surveys, or commission them from specialist organizations. Eosys, by contrast, will generally make a comparison with the use of IT by a client's competitors, 'but not in great detail'.

## Methodologies

Many of the consultancies use one or more formalized methodologies to provide a framework for establishing their clients' needs. Broadly, methodologies fall into two categories: those intended to elicit the most profitable targets for the application of information technology and those designed to model the main operations of the business to generate the design of a corporate-wide information system.

Methodologies in the first category are used by several consultancies. Critical success factors is a means of identifying those areas of the business which are central to the success of the enterprise and could most profitably benefit from the application of information technology. Value chain analysis offers another means of highlighting areas of the business to which IT can be usefully applied. These are among the methodologies most widely used by respondents. They have also clearly influenced the development of proprietary methodologies of several consultancies: Index, A T Kearney and Coopers & Lybrand are some of the consultancies which make use of critical success factors.

In general, however, the respondents who lay greatest stress on methodologies are those who carry their strategy assignments through to implementation. James Martin Associates employs a 13-phase methodology, Information Engineering, which, in their words, 'supports system development from information strategy through to business systems implementation. Each stage evolves into the next, thus avoiding "translation errors".'

LBMS' proprietary LEAP methodology generates, among other things, a portfolio of ranked applications and physical and ideal data models. LEAP feeds into the firm's development methodology, LSDM, which is used in over half their studies.

Similarly, Hoskyns has an elaborate methodology called Strategic Information Systems Planning which links into a series of proprietary development methodologies, while BIS Applied Systems uses a five-stage approach, Information Resource Planning, which includes technical and resource planning phases.

These examples from the second group of methodologies share a common assumption that the key objective is to provide the information systems function with a conceptual and technical infrastructure for their long-term activities. They generally assume that the study will be company-wide and tend to be data-orientated, and will typically result in the implementation of database management systems. This is reflected in their emphasis on activities such as data gathering, functional decomposition, entity analysis and other steps on the road to automating the organization's operations on a grand scale.

The automated business approach contrasts markedly with the information technology opportunists' philosophy which seeks to identify the points of optimum business leverage.

Although some consultancies clearly place a great deal of reliance on methodologies, one respondent, P-E Consulting Services, makes a highly pertinent comment: ‘... while we have developed and used standard methodologies where appropriate for business analysis, data analysis and programming techniques, we do not believe in their blanket application. We employ consultants with considerable practical experience and skills to apply their expertise to our clients' problems. The potential danger is that a standard methodology can be used as an excuse for using a junior consultant, leading to a superficial study of little benefit.' Assurance that a consultant would be able to field a multi-disciplinary team where necessary is rarely mentioned.

Of the handful of respondents who said expressly that they brought in consultants from other disciplines, Coopers & Lybrand claims always to involve non-IT consultants to focus on the business needs of the client. PActel also cites its ability to draw on other sources of skills within the PA consulting group.

## Strategy assignment track record

Consultancies were asked to indicate the number of strategy assignments they had undertaken to date and in which sectors they claimed particular expertise. In some cases, the number of assignments seems disproportionately large, but this is almost certainly a reflection of how the respondents interpret the all-important term ‘information technology strategy’. Significantly, those consultancies which specialize in studies focusing exclusively on the business potential for IT have conducted far fewer than the ‘grand-plan’ systems designers.

## Client involvement and extent of the study

Respondents who concentrate on identifying the strategic opportunities for applying IT see the scope of their work in different terms from those who see an assignment as a route to the development of company-wide systems. Interviews with board members and senior managers are part of all the consultancies' methods of identifying needs and assessing business opportunities. But respondents who concentrate on strategy, rather than systems architecture, generally see less need to extend their investigations further down the managerial hierarchy, at least in such detail.

Accordingly, the number of people which a consultancy expects to interview can vary widely. In the case of Index, the number ranges from 12 to 20; for Arthur Andersen the total is likely to be from 60 to 120.

## Senior management awareness of IT's business potential

One significant finding to emerge from the survey was that only a handful of consultants specifically describe their role as providing any kind of education of senior management in the strategic business potential of information technology.

Index, for example, favours a collaborative method of working through a series of management workshops. During these meetings, Index attempts to 'educate' the client in competitive use of IT. Hoskyns begins its assignments with a senior management workshop to open directors' eyes to the importance of IT. A T Kearney states that the first stage of an assignment involves briefing managers about the potential of information technology.

Peat Marwick Mitchell also takes a broader view of its role as a consultancy. 'Our role is to effect change in organizations, so educating the client can be important', it states.

Even fewer consultancies suggest that an important part of the consultant's role is both to educate the managing director and to stimulate a reappraisal of business objectives in the light of information technology. One which does, BIT, says that the consultant should challenge the managing director's existing preconceptions: 'It is essential that the CEO understands the breadth of impact/opportunity created by IT change in his marketplace. It's essential the consultant understands the boundaries and parameters of "what business we are in" so he does not waste time on good ideas that are outside his framework.'

## Client references and clients' rating of assignments

With the exception of one, all the respondents said that they would provide potential clients with the names of references from their list of past assignments. Most will do so subject to their clients' agreement to act as a reference. Hoskyns, though, says that it will not do this under any circumstances, on the grounds of confidentiality. BIT says that it would prefer to provide references only after a new client has agreed to undertake a strategy study.

The acid test of any consultancy work is the impact it makes on the client organization. In addition to stating their policy on references for potential clients, respondents were also asked to give the names of clients for whom strategy assignments had been carried out. Several consultancies declined on the grounds of confidentiality, while others simply ignored the request. In the end, eight respondents suggested 13 organizations which could be contacted for the purposes of this survey.

Client organizations were asked to comment on the bottom-line effect of the assignments: whether they had been successful in identifying key business objectives, aligning IT with these objectives, pinpointing new opportunities for gaining competitive advantage through IT, and so on.

They were also asked to say whether they thought the study had struck the right balance, whether they had implemented the recommendations, and if the business had improved as a result. All 13 replied, although two did so only to say that it was too soon after the assignment to make any sensible comments.

Although this sample is clearly too small to draw hard and fast conclusions about the overall effectiveness of strategic assignments, many of the replies reinforce the concern about the consultancies' interpretation of the scope of an information technology strategy study. They also reinforce and support other evidence about the ineffectiveness of IT strategy studies, as shown by the findings of the Price Waterhouse and Galliers studies.

Only two of the studies appear to have the scope of an IT strategy assignment, as defined for the purposes of the survey. Elsewhere, it was not always clear from the answers given by a number of client companies to what extent the assignment was a business- or technology-led study.

There was also evidence of a difference of opinion as to how successful assignments had been. The answers suggest that there is a discrepancy between what the consultants regard as an entirely successful assignment and the clients' estimation of the results achieved. This was very much in line with Galliers' findings: while 93 per cent of consultants thought that their assignments had been successful from a senior management point of view, only 59 per cent of the client companies shared this view.

Most disappointingly, only one of the reference companies was in a position to evaluate the assignment on every count, including the impact of the strategy recommendations on the performance of the business: 'IT changes have supported major rationalization of our manufacturing and distribution facilities in the UK, which in turn have significantly reduced our costs. IT is now established as a tool of senior management aimed at improving customer service, improving manufacturing efficiency and reducing cost on an on-going basis.'

In every other instance, the client organizations said that it was too soon to make a firm judgement on the business impact of the changes that had been made. One client, however, did feel it was worth pointing out that while it was impossible to quantify the bottom-line impact, there had been a positive benefit ‘in terms of IT awareness and objectives’.

A key question in the client survey related to the identification of new opportunities for competitive advantage. Client companies consistently scored their assignments as less successful in this area than in others. There was also a greater divergence in their replies. Only one company claimed that the assignment was 'wholly' successful in identifying competitive opportunities. Three said that the assignment was marginal in this respect, while two said that the study was only partially successful.

There was a uniformly higher level of rating on the issue of aligning data processing spending with key business activities, although there were contradictions here. Two companies gave their consultants a higher rating for aligning data processing spending with business activities than they did for their success in eliciting business objectives and translating these into IT terms.

## Issues for discussion

A number of central issues are raised by the findings of the survey. The starting point for the survey, on which the IT Consultancy Report is based, was the recognition of the increasingly urgent need for companies to discover the right formula and the right source of help to back business goals with the resources of information technology. With increasing numbers of companies looking for assistance in fashioning IT strategies, the assumption was that more consultancies would be coming into this field and offering to carry out work in this highly important and complex area. Identifying consultancies with the right blend of skills is no simple task. As the survey confirms, the range and diversity of consultancies is, indeed, extensive; the different ways in which they attempt to link information technology to the business of their clients are equally diverse.

Many consultancies have devoted considerable resources to developing elaborate methodologies for implementing IT strategies. As a result some, although not all, of these firms appear to lay greater emphasis on the tactical issues of information systems planning as opposed to strategic business issues. The large number of IT strategy assignments (50-100) cited in some cases is typical of the confusion in some respondents' minds between strategic and tactical studies.

By contrast, those consultancies whose approach involved the more rigorous assessment of corporate goals and strategy, and was clearly focused on identifying the competitive advantage opportunities for the application of IT, claimed far fewer assignments. Significantly, these firms were generally also prepared to challenge the client's preconceptions about IT and its relevance to the business and marketplace in which the company operates.

The problem does not lie with consultants alone. There is mounting evidence to show that senior managers either do not see IT as a priority business issue, or abrogate their responsibility by leaving IT planning to the information systems function without providing adequate direction.

In this connection it is significant that several consultancies cite greater management awareness as the major, or the principal, benefit of an IT strategy study. This results from the fact that many studies appear to have been commissioned by MIS managers with the goal of establishing a strategy for the MIS department.

It follows that a company which is seriously attempting to lift company performance through the application of IT needs to be aware of the extent to which its own attitudes and policies may facilitate or frustrate the successful outcome of a study. Most consultancies will accept the given terms of reference and do not see it as their job to contest the scope of the proposed study.

This suggests a number of key points for consideration by any organization planning to call in consultants to assist in IT strategy formulation:

\- As with all consultancy work, it is imperative that the client decides precisely what is wanted from the assignment, in particular to what extent assignment objectives are strategic or tactical. A golden rule is to invite a shortlist of consultancies to tender for the work against specific terms of reference. This should elicit proposals which can be evaluated meaningfully.

\- The central issue is how effective a consultancy will be in the demanding task of strategic analysis, that is, identifying the opportunities for harnessing the business-boosting potential of information technology. This, in turn, raises a major concern. Clearly, the ease with which any assignment runs will depend on the client's certainty about its corporate strategy and goals. If these are well defined, the consultancy's job is that much easier.

\- If an information technology strategy is to be firmly embedded as part of the overall business plan, it assumes that the consultancy must be skilled at eliciting the business potential of IT. How sound are the consultancies in business strategy skills? As indicated earlier in this report, IT strategists need to be masters of two cultures: business and information technology. Can shortlisted consultancies demonstrate real competence and success in aligning IT with corporate goals?

Part of the value of an information technology strategy study could well be in the challenge which IT brings to the business. How far is the client prepared to go in allowing its thinking to be challenged and reviewed in searching out a way forward for the business? How skilled will the consultant be in fulfilling this role? Rubber-stamping an existing corporate strategy is a different matter from bringing creative, constructive suggestions to the table. Some, but by no means all, consultancies expressly see their role as catalysts for change.

In this connection, it is worrying that a small number of respondents were prepared to go ahead with a strategy study in the absence of the active support of the client's managing director or without a rigorous review of the client's business strategy or primary goals.

Only a handful of respondents advocated the use of multi-disciplinary teams which include consultants skilled in corporate strategy. Surprisingly few made specific claims for the expertise of their IT consultants in this area. Yet as experienced practitioners know, this is the single most important factor contributing to the success of a study of this nature.

Again, although consultants pay lip service to the need to involve senior management in the planning process, comparatively few provide an explicit stage in their assignments devoted to raising management awareness and providing guidance on the business potential of information technology.

Above all, the survey has underlined findings made elsewhere that aligning information technology with business goals is no simple task. While the consultancy world represents an immensely useful source of expertise and growing experience in this area, no organization should expect consultants to provide an easy answer to their problems. Ultimately, the quality of an IT strategy assignment reflects the shrewdness of the client organization in establishing the correct terms of reference in the first place and selecting a consultancy which is best able to assist in identifying ways of delivering the results it is hoping to achieve.

'By the 1990s there will be no information technology strategies, only business strategies.' Paul Strassmann, founder of Paul Strassmann Inc., made this prediction during his address to the National Computing Centre's annual user conference in 1985. His point was simple: unless companies discover how to use information technology to support activities which improve performance, the exercise is a waste of time, even counter-productive. 'Information technology strategies conceived in a corporate vacuum do not take you anywhere', maintains Strassmann.

This can be seen from the hard evidence emerging of the frequent failure of investment in IT to improve corporate performance, summarized earlier, which brings us back to the basic premise of the whole survey: the only information technology strategies worthy of the name are those which support the goals of the business.
