# IT Consultancy from a Technological Viewpoint

Alan Knight, Logica Consultancy Ltd

Logica has established a reputation over the years for being one of the leading UK systems houses. The main thrust of the company is developing and implementing computer-based systems for our clients. How does this sort of background influence our approach to IT consultancy?

The first thing to note is that we have a separate company, Logica Consultancy Ltd, which undertakes our front-end consultancy work. One reason for this is that we have recognized that consultancy is a totally different business from our systems business. It has different characteristics in terms of the scope and size of projects, project life cycle, project management and control procedures and, last but not least, the staff profile. Therefore we have established a separate company which is, in many ways, organized and operated differently from our systems companies. Another very important reason for establishing Logica Consultancy Ltd as a separate company is independence. We have a high degree of autonomy and independence from the other parts of Logica to the extent that we can, and on occasion do, recommend to our clients that the solution to their problem can be met by a particular product or system provided by non-Logica companies.

Our objective as a consultancy company is to provide our clients with a complete consultancy service in the field of information technology. We provide consultancy for both suppliers and users of IT products and services. In doing so we believe we can achieve a high degree of synergy which benefits both supplier and user.

For suppliers we provide a range of marketing-oriented services helping them, for example, determine the size and characteristics of particular markets, identify the right products and services to offer, and decide how best to market these products and services.

For users, we provide consultancy across the whole range of information technology — communications, data processing, office systems etc. — to help clients identify, evaluate, specify, operate and, above all, benefit from applying IT to their business. Again, our objective is to provide a complete consultancy service providing support across the full life cycle of IT systems. Typically this will include:

\- strategic planning;

\- feasibility studies;

\- detailed technical design;

\- specification;

\- procurement;

\- project management;

\- training;

\- post installation and operational support.

The scope of these activities covers a wide range, from fairly abstract conceptual studies to the highly detailed technical analysis of specific items of equipment. As stated earlier, our background is rooted firmly in the technology of IT, and this obviously colours our approach to providing the consultancy services we offer. So, how does Logica's approach differ from that of other practitioners?

First, we limit ourselves to operating within the field of information technology. This is where our reputation and our key strengths lie. While much of what we do is at the strategic and feasibility study end of the spectrum, and could rightly be described as management consultancy, it is still constrained very firmly within the context of information technology. We would not, for example, be involved in advising the government on the organizational aspects of privatizing a public service. We might well, however, be heavily involved in advising on the information and communications systems that such an organization might need during its first five years of life.

Our background not only influences what we do but also determines to a large degree how we go about doing it. Our culture, as a company, greatly reflects our systems house heritage and focuses considerably on team work combined with individual responsibility and targets. Our approach to consultancy is aimed at identifying problems, and hence defining tasks to be undertaken within a project. Our objective is to solve a specific problem or to undertake a specific task within agreed budget, time and resource constraints. We tend not to get involved in long-term advisory/hand-holding consultancy assignments where the consultant has a role to play in the client's organization but his specific objectives are ill-defined.

A second characteristic of the way we approach assignments is closely linked to the task orientation of our work. We tend to use small teams of specialists, with complementary skills, to undertake projects rather than assigning the 'complet consultant' to undertake the entire assignment.

Although we focus on targets and have evolved a highly effective set of project management and control procedures we also, perhaps paradoxically, encourage a high degree of individualism within our consultants. We do not, for example, have highly standardized structured methodologies which our consultants are obliged to use. Rather, we have developed a range of techniques, tools and guidelines for undertaking IT consultancy assignments. These techniques cover areas such as:

\- long- and short-range forecasting;

\- information gathering techniques;

\- identifying decision criteria.

These techniques are well documented and made widely available to consultants but the decision on how, or when, to use the individual tools is up to each project manager in consultation with his client.

Finally, another characteristic of our IT consultancy which firmly reflects our technological background is that our consultants are encouraged to produce pragmatic, workable solutions to our clients' problems. Solutions are proposed which we know could be implemented effectively by a Logica systems company, should the client so require. To this end, we will often include staff from other Logica companies in our project teams to ensure that our proposed solutions accurately reflect up-to-date developments in IT.

Perhaps the most effective way of illustrating this approach to consultancy is by describing a typical assignment. About 18 months ago we were approached by the Alvey Directorate to carry out a study of the requirements for a high speed network. The Directorate felt that much of the work being undertaken within the Alvey programme could benefit from the participants having access to a high speed communications network. A second objective was that the provision and operation of such a network should give the UK a valuable and unique experience in advanced communications technology. As it was put to us by the Directorate, 'We want to identify the type of communication facility which will be coming into use in ten years' time – and build one in 18 months.'

This project certainly met all our criteria in that it was firmly rooted in communications technology, and the client had a defined problem that he wanted us to address. Our initial approach was to mobilize a four-man project team. All the team members were experienced consultants but they had complementary skills. The project manager was in fact the least experienced person in terms of age and depth of technical knowledge. In particular, his background was in the economics of communication systems rather than in the technology. He was also a well-disciplined and organized project manager. The three other team members were each very experienced in various aspects of communications networking, including:

\- user requirements analysis;

\- traffic forecasting;

\- circuit switched technology;

\- packet switched technology.

The first problem we identified was that although the Alvey Directorate was our client, it would not be the user of the network. The Alvey Directorate would in fact become a Value Added Network operator. The problem was that the potential users and their applications were unknown and could not be identified readily. Our approach to this problem was to set up a 'user forum'. This comprised some 25 people whom we identified as having an interest in the concept of 'high speed' (i.e. greater than 2 MBit/s) networking. They came from users, suppliers and academia. The members of the forum were each sent questionnaires on potential requirements for high speed networks, and a number were interviewed face-to-face or over the telephone.

As a result of this exercise, we were able to gather a considerable amount of data on the type of applications that might require a high speed network, and on many of the characteristics needed from the network, e.g. availability, reliability, security, delay time etc.

These potential requirements were documented in a working paper which was circulated to the user forum. We then convened a one-day meeting to discuss the network and user requirements. This combination of information-gathering and analysis techniques enabled us to identify and classify the potential user requirements quite clearly. Unfortunately, what emerged were two distinct groups of user requirements which, in terms of network characteristics, were mutually incompatible.

The project then entered a phase of fairly detailed technical analysis of the technologies and systems that were, or would shortly be, available to meet the user requirements. After several weeks we were still unable to reconcile the conflicting user requirements to the state-of-the-art technological solutions that we had been able to identify. We decided to set up a 'brainstorming' session to see if we could find a way forward. As is so often the case our brainstorming presented a solution that, with hindsight, was obvious in its simplicity.

What was actually required was not a single high-speed network to support the Alvey community, but a true ISDN based on high speed bearers linked by packet and circuit switching to provide the different network characteristics. This solution was proposed to the Alvey Directorate as the strategy for supporting the Alvey programme, and was accepted.

Subsequent stages of the study involved detailed analysis of the economic aspects of setting up the network — what the costs were likely to be; alternative charging mechanisms; and how the network could be funded over a five-year period. We also needed to address the detailed logistics of planning, building, managing and operating a network which would be not only a semi-research vehicle but also a semi-commercial network committed to providing a defined level of service to its users. Finally, we addressed the detailed network design, i.e. that part of the project most likely to be identified with Logica.

This project also met our criteria of being a workable solution, and the Alvey Directorate subsequently commissioned a consortium of Logica Communications and Electronic Systems Ltd, British Telecom, and GEC to design and build Alveynet. From the feedback received on the progress of the network, it is understood that one of Alvey's primary objectives has been met by providing the UK with invaluable experience of building a true high speed ISDN environment.

## Biographical notes

On leaving school, Alan Knight took a sandwich course in electrical engineering at Borough Polytechnic (now Polytechnic of the South Bank), sponsored by the Greater London Council.

After gaining his degree, Alan joined the GLC as a project engineer working on a wide variety of assignments in London's schools, courts, offices, and for the fire and ambulance services. It was during this period that he first became involved in planning and designing communication systems. In 1974, he joined Logica as a Communications Consultant, spending several years undertaking consultancy assignments in the UK, Europe, USA and the Far East. These assignments involved the design, specification and implementation of communications facilities, both voice and data, for clients in all sectors – banking, manufacturing, retail, government etc.

Over the last six years he has undertaken a number of sales, marketing, and general management roles within Logica's consultancy company. He is now a director of Logica Consultancy Ltd, and responsible for the communications assignments undertaken by the company.

## Discussion

A contributor from British Telecom: Our experience of the design of new systems is that it is of critical importance to examine what the potential implications are for the users of the system – that is to say, in the matter of how the technical change will require the organizational members to adapt their style of operations. It is not simply a matter of their being given a capability and learning how to play with it, for example in people learning how to exploit conferencing. This raises the question of how valid and valuable the technological analysis is in itself without some assessment of the necessary tactics for nurturing the potential users in the build-up to the change period. Can I ask if much attention is given to that in the project that you described?

Alan Knight: This was indeed the crux of the problem. If you ask potential users (of a service not yet conceived), 'Is a high-speed 2-megabit switch network a good idea?' most people say that it is. If you ask them, 'What would you use it for?' they reply, 'All sorts of things; this company would be definitely constrained if we didn't have one.' Then you have to actually repeat, 'What would you do with it?' until you break through the barrier of getting specific answers. This was why we had the idea of getting together with a potential user forum who we put in a room to hammer out some of these issues.

The contributor from British Telecom: How about changes in the actual working practices?

Alan Knight: Yes, this is one of the specific issues that the forum looked at. For example, looking at conferencing within this context there was some very interesting debate in the requirements of conferencing. Some people had a much shorter horizon in looking at this issue. They would view it in terms of money saved in travel, but this did not allow for the social implications of conferencing and its impact on social interaction between organizational members.
