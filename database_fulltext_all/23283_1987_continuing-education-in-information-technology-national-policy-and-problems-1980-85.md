PERSONAL VIEW

# Continuing Education in Information Technology: National Policy and Problems 1980-85

Ian Bryant, Department of Adult Education, University of Southampton

This paper reviews some of the public arguments expressed between 1980 and 1985 for the development of continuing education to meet the needs of a society rapidly changing under the impact of information technology (IT), the assumptions on which government policy support of this development have been based, and the opportunities and constraints faced by educators in the university sector in responding to the challenges posed by IT. Before examining the record, two general definitions are perhaps needed:

\- Continuing education (CE) refers to any period of structured learning subsequent to a break from initial continuous education (to whatever level), and encompasses both vocational and non-vocational study.

\- Information technology (IT) refers to any aspects of computing and telecommunications concerned with the generation, process application and transfer of information in digital form.

In the first major assessment of the impact of the microelectronics revolution on national education, Maddison (1980) reported that the impact of IT in terms of restructuring the procedures and content of education had thus far been minimal, the principal reason being 'an ingrained unwillingness to accept that there is an indissoluble nexus between the technology of communication and the process of education in the modern world.' Maddison considered that in many parts of the educational system it was as if the 'revolution' had not even begun. He raised the question of whether the history of the universities, which had played only a marginal role in the industrial revolution in Britain (in contrast to the universities of the USA, Germany and Japan), was to be repeated. In the literature available to Maddison, not much thought had been given to the implications of IT for adult and continuing education — with the exception of promoting education for leisure, an increase in which it was generally assumed would follow in the wake of the revolution.

By 1980, the government had committed itself to two modest programmes of financial support for some form of continuing education, through the Microprocessor Applications Programme (MAP) and the Microelectronics in Education Programme (MEP). MAP had both a retraining and an 'industrial awareness' component. It was a Department of Industry crash programme for short-term results, aimed at a restricted social group of 50,000 'key decision makers' in industry. MEP was launched in 1980 as a Department of Education programme of curriculum development and in-service training for teachers, which recognized the potential contribution of new educational technology. No public funds were available at that time to pump-prime continuing education activity in IT in the higher education sector.

Other than a few teachers and selected senior managers, mature students were not deemed to require or to be suitable for continuing education in IT. Indeed, the thinking of the University Grants Committee (UGC) and the Computer Board during the late 1970s was to support the emerging trend away from a central campus and specialist-user mainframe computing service towards the idea of supporting the accessibility of local microcomputing facilities for all undergraduates (not just those in electronics, computer studies or engineering). In a revelatory statement from their Joint Working Party Report of 1983, the Computer Board said, 'We think it important that young men and women entering upon their careers from universities should accept computers and be able to understand them as the sophisticated tools they are, and the undergraduate stage, in our view, is the time to do this when what is learned is assimilated in a way not possible later.' [emphasis added]. Support for mature students in professional updating in computing and related studies was not even considered.

In 1980, the influential Finneston Report expressed the need for the ‘continuing formation’ of engineers to take advantage of new technology, and it attached great importance to increasing CE provision and uptake at all levels in the profession. In specifically recommending a commitment to technical updating as a condition of continued registration as a chartered engineer, the report suggested that employers be required to grant paid study leave. It further recommended the establishment of regional engineering centres responsible to a new Engineering Authority in order to encourage, inter alia, the development of distance learning schemes in liaison with the Open University and other bodies. In a message to the government and universities, Finneston stated that 'the DES and UGC should be required to ensure that engineering schools have adequate resources to free senior staff time for continuing formation activities.'

DES did not take up the Finneston recommendations. Later in the same year, in its discussion paper on continuing education, it affirmed that the government's approach to professional and executive vocational education (PEVE) would be based on the principle that 'the beneficiaries not the State should meet the full costs involved.' But DES left the door open to the possibility of pump-priming funds for courses with a low initial but high potential demand. The paper did not consider the implications of developments in IT, though there were passing references throughout to encouraging institutions to develop 'distance learning provision' [left undefined] and to develop a co-ordinated system of information on institutions' short course provision.

The limited support of CE in IT was a split responsibility of the Department of Education and Science (DES) and the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI). This was viewed as slowing down developments by some advisory groups. Also in 1980, the Advisory Council for Applied Research and Development (ACARD) produced a report which described developments in IT and saw the constraints operating within the UK as stemming largely from ‘critical skill shortages’. ACARD proposed a government programme of resources to stimulate IT training in firms. It recommended that one government department should take an overall co-ordinating role for the development of IT, with education and training as the key promotional vehicle. As if in total disregard of this advice, in 1981 DTI became heavily involved in planning for the promotional year, IT '82, at the same time that the UGC were implementing the first round of cuts in university funding.

With a gradual realization of the need for adaptable models of professional continuing education, the Advisory Board for the Research Councils (ABRC) recommended in 1982 the Science and Engineering Research Council (SERC) scheme for continuing education as one on which to base other research councils' activities in advanced course training. ABRC took note of the 1980 DES and Finneston documents and described with approval the SERC Integrated Graduate Development (IGD) scheme of joint industry/higher education experimental short courses. But these were to be 'end on' to initial higher education. Money for IGD came largely from a diversion of funds from conventional taught courses in engineering and did not represent new financial support for CE. The report explicitly stated that the Research Councils did not see it as their job to support part-time post-experience courses.

Also reporting in 1982, the Alvey Committee – established as a direct response to the perceived competitive threat from the Japanese fifth generation computing programme – listed a number of IT development priorities, adding, 'we believe that it is particularly important to improve the capability of the users of IT. The understanding in all sectors of society concerning the scope for the use of IT is very poor, despite the many [sic] measures already taken.' In costing the overall package, £57m over five years would be earmarked for research and training support in academic institutions, to be 100 per cent government funded. Alvey identified the major problem of CE in IT as that of employee release and suggested the dissemination of instructional material through a distance learning communications network, linking IT specialists in industry with those in the academic sector.

By 1983 the Manpower Services Commission was formulating a revised adult training strategy, partly as a consequence of the limited success of TOPS in meeting either company or individual needs for new skills. In their April discussion document it was noted that 'the potential of IT to improve handling of and access to information is there to be exploited, as a quite major new element in the continuing education and training system. Linking information and advice or diagnosis with training provision must be a key feature of the [adult training] strategy.' IT was now beginning to be seen as both an object of, and a means of securing, continuing education. An additional urgency for developing IT updating was pointed to by the National Economic Development Council (NEDC) in 1983 when a working party recommended that the government follow up on IT '82 by announcing a five- to ten-year strategic plan for IT development through a fully co-ordinated policy of public procurement for IT products, training and education, publicly-funded research and development, and inward investment. NEDC was concerned about the UK's declining world market share of IT hardware products (from 10 per cent to 4 per cent in a decade).

At the very time that universities were being urged to become more responsive to a continuing education market, to develop self-supporting, full-cost courses to meet industry's needs for updating to compete in a fast-paced 'high tech' economy, the Computer Board for the Universities and Research Councils (1983) raised serious questions about the ability, let alone the willingness, of universities to respond in a manner likely to satisfy either government or industry. Its report raised serious doubts about the capacity of university computing facilities to keep pace with the putative training requirements of the IT 'revolution'. Emphasis was placed on the inadequacy of existing facilities in terms of both quantity and quality to meet even the needs of undergraduate students. The Board's survey of universities revealed that they (the universities) did not know what departmental microcomputing facilities were available. It was further estimated that only 35 per cent of central computing facilities were available for teaching purposes, and that UGC-funded departmental equipment grants were inadequate for the purchase of microcomputing and networking hardware and software. The Board was pessimistic about the chances of speedy improvements, reporting that 'there is a critical lack of awareness in the academic community of the potential of the new technology and the likely changes it will bring to higher education. There is insufficient staff development in this area. At present, no organised programmes of development exist to change the situation ... Cutbacks in academic staff have exacerbated the situation. As staff take on a greater workload they will not have time to explore these new paths ... the tendency will be against change.'

The situation in non-vocational continuing education was equally gloomy. In October 1983, the Advisory Council for Adult and Continuing Education (ACACE) produced its report 'Microtechnology and the Education of Adults'. This was based on a survey to gauge the response of local education authorities (LEAs), the Workers' Education Association and university extra-mural departments to the demand for part-time microcomputer courses. Approximately 2,000 courses in England and Wales were recorded, serving some 23,000 students in 1982-83. Student enrolment in these courses accounted for no more than 3 per cent of all enrolments. Adult education centre facilities for computing were said to be 'poor'. Classes tended to make use of the tutors' own equipment, and for demonstration purposes rather than to gain hands-on experience. ACACE suggested (with little foundation) that university extra-mural facilities were probably much better! The use of computers and other educational technology with part-time adult students was described as being 'very low'. There appeared to be virtually no demand for courses on the implications of microelectronics. ACACE suggested (on the basis of the public response to the BBC computer literacy programmes) a potential adult education market of some 150,000 students for practical courses. A major problem was seen to be the lack of tutors who combined microcomputing competence and an experience of teaching adults.

Urgency and despondency were evident in equal measure in 1984. In January, the Government White Paper 'Training for Jobs' reaffirmed the 'full cost to beneficiaries' principle for continuing education that had been enunciated four years earlier. It was intended to restructure the £250m Manpower Services Commission (MSC) adult training programme, to further develop the Open Tech programme, and to look at the feasibility of a loan scheme for those in jobs who would be 'willing to pay for training of their own choice which they would not otherwise take up.' In summary, the White Paper confirmed the MSC strategy to be correct, namely 'to secure an adequate supply of people with up-to-date skills to meet the demands of the new technologies ... as well as to increase the capacity to cope with continuing change.'

Continuing education interests within both sectors of higher education produced their responses to government and market messages with two major reports in 1984. The UGC Continuing Education Working Party Report (1984) reiterated the importance of CE to cope with, and take advantage of, technological change. It based its case for additional public funds for CE largely on economic and employment grounds. Engineering, science and other technology PEVE had accounted for only 10 per cent of all university-generated PEVE in 1981-82. It was necessary to increase this proportion but, to do so, the Working Party considered that the status of CE in universities needed to be made equal to that of undergraduate teaching, postgraduate teaching and research. A change in university staff attitudes was recommended, together with the expansion of staff development programmes to cope with the issues of training adults. Coordination of distance learning materials was seen as important, as was the development of modular courses, flexible entry and credit transfer arrangements. Significantly, the UGC contribution to PEVE courses via recurrent grant/FTE student was reported as amounting to less than 4 per cent of the resources provided for full-time degree students. UGC was urged to press the government for a pump-priming grant for PEVE of £500/FTE, or an immediate gross increase of £2.5m rising to £10m by the year 2000. It was emphasized that this should be additional money, and not transferred from other uses. More comprehensive than the UGC Report, the National Advisory Body (NAB) Continuing Education Group Report (1984) described a much higher proportion of mature students served by the public higher education sector. NAB concentrated on identifying the non-financial barriers to the realization of the potential of continuing education to meeting economic, social and personal needs. In some ways it is a more 'honest' document, the largest number of recommendations for change concerning the revised attitudes and behaviour of individual institutions. Continuing education should become a senior managerial co-ordinating responsibility, with a focus on marketing and short course development. Each LEA was urged to establish its own mature student information service, and each institution a course counselling service. On the financing of PEVE, NAB generally accepted DES policy on the clients bearing full costs, but it recommended that an additional £5m be added to the advanced further education pool from 1985 to 1986 for pump-priming. An additional, if somewhat misdirected, recommendation was that DES should consider the possibility of a tax relief system for PEVE participants.

In August 1984, the Institute of Manpower Studies (IMS) reported to NEDC and MSC on the comparative training effort of Britain's major competitors. In the USA, Germany and Japan, higher priorities were given to company and employee training. IMS noted that more private money was being put into vocational updating, at the same time that public funding remained level; i.e. the former was not expected (as was judged to be the case in the UK) to bear the consequences of a reduced government commitment to continuing education. IMS recommended as a minimum level public funding and repeated the (by now) ritual exhortation to employers to increase their training effort. Evidence of links between training and economic benefit to individual companies and industrial sectors should be sought in order to promote the virtues of updating.

The context for the simultaneous NEDC Report (1984) on the IT skills crisis in Britain was the worsening balance of trade in IT products, estimated as reaching £1 billion by the end of 1985. NEDC regarded the establishment of a 'National Training Focus for Information Technology' as a priority. This would 'help educational and training establishments to obtain clear signals from their markets and to develop a co-ordinated approach to satisfying them.' Calls for further government funding were made, particularly in the areas of collaborative and pre-competitive civil research and development. A major constraint on the ability of the education system to respond to the needs of the IT industry was considered to be the reluctance of companies to forecast their medium-term training requirements in an uncertain economic climate.

In key high technology areas companies were not training but, according to the MSC Report (1984) on the labour market for new skills in Newbury and Milton Keynes, leaving vacancies unfilled or 'poaching'. Major shortages were reported in software engineering and technical management. Company training was regarded by some respondents in the survey as adding to the attractiveness of their own specialist IT personnel to market rivals.

By the time the Butcher Committee had completed its Final Report on IT skills shortages in mid-1985, all parties had been blamed for the poor state of affairs in continuing education: higher education for its failure to market or even to consider CE as a serious responsibility; the government for its insistence on self-financing of CE and its reduced financial support of the enabling infrastructure; employers for their conservatism and unwillingness or inability to identify updating needs; and society as a whole for failing to recognize the revolutionary implications of IT for work organization and practice. Government funding under PICKUP, Open Tech, 'new blood' schemes and IT conversion courses has been piecemeal and hedged with restrictions. In total it has not even begun to compensate for the losses in recurrent grant to higher education since 1980.

What is to be made of all the arguments for developing a competitive IT economy via continuing education? The first thing to be said is that educational and training policy can be only one component of a concerted political strategy to induce change, but that in the UK a relatively unsupported educational system has been asked to bear a disproportionate amount of strain in the attempt to develop IT capabilities. Second, the arguments outlined above can all be represented in terms of a fairly simple yet misleading model of what 'ought' to happen, but in which some misleading assumptions are embedded. This market competitiveness has been the backdrop to most of the educational policy considerations regarding IT, as is evident in the reports cited in this paper. Educational institutions are being pressed harder then ever before both to initiate (in research and development) and to respond (through active marketing, continuing education and training) to this change. It is argued that the effect of a declining national economy and loss of market share — especially in new technology products — combined with a rise in consumer expectations which are increasingly being satisfied via foreign technology imports, increases the urgency of a domestic response to include educational institutions and which must match the (educational) efforts of our competitors.

Because of the pervasive character of information technology, there is an unchallenged presumption that it automatically generates new sets of educational needs. The argument is carried at logical and rhetorical levels; that is, in principle we know both that this is and that it ought to be the case. Yet the substantive connection between IT and the need, say, for continuing education is problematic; it contains elements of both reality and rhetoric. It is real in the sense that a general failure to meet educational needs carries with it the undoubted social and economic consequences of not keeping up to date in a world where competitors are busily doing so. It is rhetorical in the sense of failing to settle the arguments about how these needs might be defined and met, and who should take on this responsibility.

A major problem lies in the fact that not all the educational needs that could be presumptively derived from the introduction of IT are in reality demanded – whether by employers, employees, or those whom IT has made redundant. In a state of information ‘volatility’, generated by that very technology, do they perhaps lack the appropriate information on which to act? Quite apart from the particular educational policies implemented by government, therefore, IT in itself increases the pressure on educational institutions to simultaneously educate and service a market that may not know what it wants or needs.

The next link in the policy argument is that conventional educational delivery systems do not have the capacity to make appropriate responses to changing market conditions. For example, the representation of universities as lacking in competitive business acumen is undoubtedly correct. Yet it fails to acknowledge what is more fundamentally true – that at root universities are not businesses, and that an adequate continuing education ‘solution’ to the needs generated by IT (even if it could be agreed upon) may not be found simply by grafting ‘new’ delivery mechanisms on to ‘old’ institutions. Universities do not have the attitudinal, structural or productive capacities to generate self-supporting continuing education on the scale which is exhorted. The argument that technological changes can in themselves offer new educational possibilities is partially true, but only partially. IT has a role in solving the educational problems that it creates – for example via networked distance learning and computer-aided instruction. However, these are unlikely to become the routine means of delivering training and professional updating; developments in these areas will occur at the margins, where the possibility of diverting resources from other activities permits.

The chain of policy reasoning is completed by implication and association, rather than substantive demonstration. New knowledge, it is argued, will be generated through new and more efficient educational processes. Research will be funded on a new basis, and projects judged in terms of their industrial and commercial utility. Clients for continuing education will demand applicable knowledge. In IT, new applications will be found which lead to further changes. Ideally, these will result in increased competitiveness and IT market share.

It is the nature of universities and new educational technologies, and the relation of each to the marketplace, that both call this set of assumptions into question. Many universities have not yet been forced to innovate in continuing education and Hoggart (1982) has spoken of a ‘rooted disinclination to change’. In Response to Adversity, Williams and Blackstone (1983) maintain that 'there is little reason to suppose that most academics are inherently hostile to new technology.' Nevertheless, many are justifiably suspicious of the inflated claims made on behalf of IT, especially by product manufacturers. Some academics see the market as a source of additional funds; others are concerned about a possible decline of standards in the pursuit of profit, though this concern may be less valid in the case of continuing professional education. The top-down changes demanded have encountered an institutional inertia which can be cleverly intellectualized. Bottom-up changes have in some cases been marginalized, with the use of computers in continuing education being the province of individual enthusiasts.

In contrasting the rhetorical with the operational, the only way to give effect to the former is through the actions of individuals. This must be considered from the point of view of work within the particular cultures of academic departments. Burgess (1985) has made a useful distinction here, namely that higher education organizes knowledge by subjects whereas candidates for updating confront problems. This incongruity is the source of society's 'permanent disappointment' with education. It is a latent tension, concealed by the predominance in higher education of those who are completing their initial education, but which becomes manifest in continuing education. The tension is heightened by the cross-disciplinary developments in, and implications of, IT. One can point to many examples of individual 'state-of-the-art' developments in educational technology. Reports on these form the bulk of contributions to academic journals such as Programmed Learning & Educational Technology or the British Journal of Educational Technology. Some are employed in delivering continuing education, but they have not entered the mainstream of provision as routine modes of curriculum delivery. There are good reasons for this, often ignored by those who demand a rapid educational response to change. Customizing distance learning material is time-consuming and expensive; it has been estimated, for example, that courseware development times for computer-aided learning are increased by a factor of at least 25 over the time to prepare conventional material (Kocher and McLean, 1985). High development costs can only be offset by pump-priming and/or low production and delivery costs in a larger market for continuing education than is evident. Quite apart from the question of quality, educational technology is usually machine-specific. The private training sector has taken advantage of this lack of portability in providing a plethora of courses of brand-based and task-specific instruction in both hardware and software usage.

By prioritizing its limited support for continuing IT education in terms of, first, hardware, then (rather less) software, then (less again) human and infrastructure resources, actual policy has been a reversal of what has been required. In failing to understand both the limitations of IT and the room for manoeuvre within higher education, the circle of assumptions on which policy success depends cannot be completed. Existing government thinking wants universities to play a major role in continuing education, yet it is that same thinking that is likely to result in universities having a marginal influence in the IT revolution.

## Biographical notes

Ian Bryant is the PICKUP Research Fellow in the Department of Adult Education, University of Southampton. He is currently investigating aspects of university continuing education in information technology. Trained as a sociologist in England and Canada, he has recently researched and taught adult education in Scotland. His research experience includes examining various aspects of post-secondary and continuing education – especially paid educational leave, professional updating, and educational provision for the unemployed. His teaching experience includes Open University tutoring, social science for mature entrants to university, and research methods courses for postgraduate adult education students. He is interested in the personal and social contexts of conducting adult education research, both as a way of establishing the learning/development needs of professionals, and in developing the methodological and conceptual tools to evaluate the effectiveness of different delivery systems.
