# Microcomputer-based Networks in Libraries and Information Services

J. Howard Petrie, Information Industry Consultant, Department of Library and Information Studies, Loughborough University

Abstract: A microcomputer on everyone's desk is the norm in some organizations and is rapidly becoming so in many others. The incentive to link them together into networks is also growing. Reasons include: access to databases inside the organization and from elsewhere, electronic mail, and making the most out of available equipment, among others. Until recently, some computer equipment suppliers paid little heed to please from users for flexible networks to link their machines. On the other hand, others have emphasized the ability of their equipment to be networked together and with that of other manufacturers.

## Introduction

Most computer manufacturers have now woken up to the need for networking. We have reached a stage of development at which the microcomputer on the desk can rapidly gain access to databases and work in conjunction with other machines, both within and outside the organization.

This paper concentrates on the role of the company information service in the development of databases and the provision of access to them in addition to others outside the company. It argues that these online services have provided a stimulus to the use of both internal and external electronic networks. The rate of use of on-line databases within the company is likely to grow rapidly and the role of the information expert will grow.

## The changing role of the library and information services

The traditional image of the library is one of a store of books accompanied by a passive attitude towards the supply of information. In reality, and particularly in industry and commerce, information services have been active for many years in the collection, management and supply of information relevant to all aspects of the companies' activities. They are playing an increasingly important role in the management of the company's information resource.

Databases are created as libraries of printed material, microfilm, etc, in the computer. Information services have often been at the forefront of in-house computerized database developments. They frequently began by automating card files of bibliographic references to articles, reports, etc.

## From printed indexes to on-line databases

The growth in the output of published literature initially led commercial abstracting and indexing services to automate their procedures for production of printed journals. At the same time as generating a paper output using computer typesetting techniques, a computer-readable database was created. Some of these databases became commercially available in the early 1970s.

Today, several thousand databases are publicly available on-line from vendors in Europe, the USA and elsewhere. They cover a wide variety of subjects and vary in their depth of coverage. Some contain the same information as an equivalent abstracting and indexing journal. Others contain the full text of documents. Some are never published in paper form and can contain specialized data aimed at a specific market. They are usually stored on large time-sharing computers and access can be through private or public national and international networks, the latter method often using the packet-switched technique.

Industrial information services are generally heavy users of these on-line databases, often with data being down-loaded into local centrally held company databases and/or transmitted electronically to the client within the company.

## In-house databases

The software developed for the commercial provision of on-line information began to be used for the production and searching of in-house databases. Initially, minicomputers and larger machines were used, but nowadays information retrieval systems are available for microcomputers as well. Current software prices are low enough to make it feasible for the individual to have an information retrieval package on a personal desktop machine. Database management systems, such as dBASE III, have also incorporated some of the searching features, such as the use of Boolean logical operators and searching for parts of words, developed for information retrieval systems.

These hardware and software developments have enabled in-house databases to be created and made available on-line to company personnel. Initially, databases were mostly bibliographic, covering patents, standards, internal company reports etc. They are increasingly becoming full-text and being extended to other company data.

An example is in the pharmaceuticals industry in which the company information service invariably provides a chemical structures database for drug design and structure-activity purposes. These databases can be available on-line to the research worker alongside the patents, reports and other databases. On-line chemical structure search databases are also available commercially. Software has been developed for microcomputers to assist with searching these databases, displaying structure diagrams on the visual display screen, and incorporating them into documents with wordprocessors.

All these services depend on efficient internal and external electronic networks.

In some companies, this demonstrated expertise in both managing databases and providing information has, in some cases, helped the information service to assume the management of other corporate databases. Again in the pharmaceuticals industry, the information department in some companies manages the database which stores details of the drugs produced by the company, including manufacturing processes, testing, history, etc. This might extend to the physical storage and management of samples of compounds and related information. Again, such information is likely to be made available on-line.

## Electronic mail

Some organizations use electronic mail for intra-company communication (memos etc). Information services can be heavy users of such services, providing answers to in-house queries from research, marketing, production etc departments. Information will be generated from in-house databases and downloaded data from commercial and other databases. Data will be edited using wordprocessing and other software before sending to the requestor by electronic mail.

## Information retrieval software for the end-user

Information retrieval systems are not easy for the inexperienced person to use. It is usually necessary to learn a command language which is easy to forget if it is not used regularly. In addition, if the database is created by the user, techniques for building and maintaining it need to be memorized. For instance, inverted list techniques are often used to provide fast retrieval speeds, but this leads to large file sizes as the inverted lists can be larger than the original database. Such difficulties can be a disincentive to the creation of personal on-line databases.

Developments in user-friendly interfaces are simplifying the search procedures, although they are at an early stage of development and are not yet widely available. They will be valuable in increasing the use of information retrieval systems in general, and in cutting down on the costs of using external (charged) services, by reducing the time wasted in learning the peculiarities of individual on-line systems.

Subsets of databases can be downloaded and stored in local machines. Information retrieval and database software packages can accommodate different record formats, although there are problems with merging records from different databases, and copyright restrictions sometimes inhibit the demand for the transfer of records from commercially available databases to those developed locally.

Some producers of information retrieval packages for microcomputers have extended their capabilities so that they can operate with local area networks. Thus, even with low-cost local-area networks, databases, created in part from material supplied through the information service to (say) a research worker, can be used by others in the organization.

## Future developments

The amount of information stored in the computer at all levels of the organization will undoubtedly increase. Information retrieval, database management and office systems will play an important part in these developments. Databases will increasingly be available at personal, group, departmental and company levels. Management will need to ensure these bases, which are a fundamental asset, are managed correctly. Information services will play an ever-increasing role in this activity in many companies. Easy-to-use software and simpler network protocols will eventually lead to a rapid increase in network traffic associated with information retrieval.

It is very likely that the amount of information commercially published in electronic form and transmitted will increase. Some factors which support this view are:

\- optical storage devices, eg WORM (write once, read many), are now available for microcomputers providing much greater storage capacities. Hence, more documents will be captured from networks;

\- bio-medical journals are already being put on to

CD-ROM (project ADONIS), and large databases can be delivered to the user with CD-ROM;

\- telecommunications developments, such as ISDN (Integrated Services Digital Network) and OSI (Open Systems Integration), will enable documents, with images, etc, to be transmitted and more computer systems to be networked together;

\- developments in desktop publishing (eg Postscript – the most popular page make-up language) and other publishing standards (eg SGML – Standard Generalized Mark-up Language) will enable documents to be created locally and transmitted to other computer systems in a compatible format.

It is difficult to forecast the rate of growth of such developments. One would expect that by the turn of the century much of the current printed material used by business and industry will only be put on to paper at the local level, apart from quality colour work. The use of electronic networks and optical discs for communication of information will have become the norm. The extent of the use of paper by the individual will be dependent on the quality of the display and portability of lap-top computers.

The above trend is already in evidence. It will be imperative for companies to protect this growing electronic information resource by ensuring that all staff have some knowledge of information handling and, more importantly, that the number of professionals with advanced skills in information handling increases steadily.

J. Howard Petrie is an information industry consultant specializing in information technology for libraries, information services and publishing. He is also carrying out research at the Department of Library and Information Studies, Loughborough University, into the use of electronic mail for research into the effects of information technology on the provision of information for the public. He was previously a University Librarian, and has held posts in university and national libraries, and worked on the EURONET DIANE information services for the Commission of the European Committee.
