# A Study of the Use of Micro-based Networks in British Schools

Helen Pain-Lewins, Department of Library and Information Studies, Loughborough University

Abstract: During the summer of 1987, a study was made of the use of Prestel and The Times Network for Schools (TTNS) as electronic communications systems in British schools. The results discussed here provide information on school use of networks and on the activities in schools where good practice has been identified.

## The systems

## TTNS

Introduced in 1984, TTNS provides education with an electronic communication service readily accessible to both pupils and school staff. In addition to being able to send correspondence, this service offers numerous other possibilities, including reports on matches, inter-school sports fixtures and leagues; dates, details and confirmation of in-service training sessions; school orders for equipment and up-to-date details of county contracts; local authority handbooks; centralized filing of school and area-based data and diary events. Links are made between schools in the same authority, between authorities, and even abroad.

## Prestel

Established in 1979, Prestel was the world's first public viewdata system. Information is provided by many sources including government, public bodies and major companies. The system is interactive, the users being able to send messages via a response frame to other users or to information providers. There is also 'Noticeboard', an open access message service operated via the Board's operator, and 'Bulletin Board', a telemessaging service permitting conversations between two or more parties.

## Setting the scene

In this country it is considered by many that our education system is inadequately funded – and that this is particularly apparent in the information technology sphere where the necessary equipment is expensive. However, the evidence from abroad confirms that similar problems with funding are experienced in all countries where information technology has been introduced into the education system. Certainly though, the situation in Britain appears to be serious. The results of a survey of microcomputers in schools carried out by the Department of Education and Science $^{1}$ in Autumn 1985, makes depressing reading. Five hundred secondary and 500 primary schools were surveyed and the findings revealed that the primary schools had an average of only 1.7 microcomputers per school, and the secondary schools only 13.4 per school. Not all of the schools responded – only 53 per cent of secondary and 75 per cent of primary schools. Hence, it seems likely that the situation is even more gloomy than it appears in the survey, as it seems reasonable to suggest that schools are more likely to have replied if they were relatively proud of their provision of microcomputers.

However, it must be said that the Technical and Vocational Education Initiative (TVEI) has had a major impact on the provision of hardware and its use in secondary schools in Britain. Schools and colleges involved in the scheme have received (in education terms) a considerable injection of finance and much of this has been used to purchase information technology products such as Prestel and TTNS hardware, and their essential concomitants including microcomputers and disc drives. However, Dean $^{2}$ in her study of the impact of TVEI on education, is most critical of the way this technology was under-exploited, particularly from a library viewpoint. She found that as librarians were not generally consulted when TVEI finance produced an injection of information technology hardware into their libraries, and that they had little involvement in the planning of its uses, it was not fully exploited. But there are a few bright spots; in the Isle of Wight, for example, TVEI schools are networked together.

Turning now to experience of networks worldwide, it is here that most hope and excitement is generated, as the potential of such systems for education in general, and libraries in particular, becomes apparent. In the forefront are studies, particularly from the US, of the role of networks in the development of literacy skills, especially among those with learning difficulties. Closely related is the use of networks for those who are 'turned off' education. Most notable among these is a study by Riel $^{3}$ of a network system linking California and Alaska called 'The Computer Chronicles Newswire'. Riel argues that to develop literacy skills children need interaction, and traditional English writing exercises contain very little of this vital element. Computers in the past have been essentially a source of one-way communication, but now, with the development of networks, we are moving into the era of two-way (or more) interaction. Third- and fourth-grade students in schools in California and Alaska, two culturally very diverse areas within the USA, were linked in a network and developed a creative writing co-operative. Writing for these students ceased to be a chore and became an interesting experience. The students were, probably for the first time in their school careers, successful. Their learning had some meaning, it was in context and it had a purpose. In addition to improvements in literacy skills, the students' reporting, reading, editing and writing skills improved greatly. Other skills, such as the ability to co-operate and familiarity with information technology, were developed also. The most striking change, though, was in students' attitudes to writing both on and off the computer. They wrote more freely and with more confidence, and the skills acquired on the computer were found to be transferable.

The findings of Riel are largely confirmed by a similar study in another part of the US by Kumpf. $^{4}$ His study of similarly aged students (third and fourth graders) showed that the use of wordprocessors and the graphics of electronic mail were used successfully as communication tools to share thoughts, ideas and feelings. His research concluded that great improvements were shown in the development of these skills among the students participating in the experiment. Electronic mail has, he argued, a major role to play in children's learning.

A further study by Mackey $^{5}$ in 1984 concentrated on the possible topics that are most likely to spark off creative writing among younger students – but offered broad support for the view that literacy skills can be greatly enhanced by using electronic mail as a vehicle for their construction and transmission. He, too, saw the importance of the development of ‘spin off’ skills – for example, computer literacy.

Clearly, then, there are great benefits in networks for English classes. However, the weight of research demonstrates that these benefits do not end there. Bluhm $^{6}$ points to the improved communication between schools in general, and their libraries in particular, that can be achieved. Details of meetings and information relevant to staff and/or students can be networked cheaply and effectively. Probably the greatest problem in the US is the lack of standardization of networks – there are 40 educational network systems and, even though most work on the IBM PC, they are not compatible. This problem is mirrored in the software available, which is also incompatible. Fortunately, no such difficulties of incompatibility exist in Britain yet, as there are so few systems available here.

It seems useful, at this point, to outline briefly some research into networks in American public libraries, as the school library can learn much from this experience. Dewey, $^{7}$ in his study of electronic bulletin board systems, highlighted some of the ways such a system can benefit libraries and their users; many of these are transferable to the school environment. Benefits to library organizations included calendars of events of interest to libraries, continuing education services, inter-library loans and other electronic mail. Benefits to the public directly included details of library services, advice columns, puzzles for children, library events calendars, on-line library newsletters, details of new books acquired, book sales and reference requests. Clearly, the system he outlined has tremendous benefits as one public library dealt with 9,000 uses of the system in a little over a year. However, he was not unaware of the drawbacks of such a system, pointing to problems for library staff in responding to a greatly increased number of requests; day-to-day maintenance of the system; electronic graffiti and 'hackers' as the most serious of these.

Support for the view that schools can learn from public libraries is given by Carroll $^{8}$ in her study of local area networks in US school libraries. The main benefits of local area networks she pointed to were better library user access to more materials and services, and improved cost-effectiveness as more information was provided for the same money. The school libraries she studied, in Washington and Maryland, saw their role as a whole community resource, so they were in a good position to benefit from resource sharing via networks.

Staying with the American experience and changing the emphasis somewhat, Johnson, $^{9}$ in his study of the staff of schools' (administrators and teachers) attitudes towards computer networks, recommended that three areas be critically examined when installing networks. These are (1) teacher preparation; (2) technological change and (3) availability of resources. While these seem sensible suggestions, it is interesting to note that Dean, $^{10}$ in her study of TVEI and school libraries in Britain, argued that such critical examination has not taken place here and this is why the systems are not being fully utilized for the benefit of the institutions involved.

Unfortunately, research into networking in Britain's schools has been less voluble, but two studies are particularly interesting. Gilman, $^{11}$ in his study of the role of microcomputers in school libraries, points out that as early as in 1982 Prestel was being used as a mailbox by, for example, physical education teachers to compile fixtures, results and league tables of inter-sport sports events. Further, Stride, $^{12}$ points to the uses

Derbyshire makes of the electronic mail facility, for example, in curriculum matters to arrange forthcoming meetings for groups of teachers, and in administration for circulating up-to-date details of county contracts.

## The questionnaire

Using electronic mail, a questionnaire was sent to 124 TTNS System Managers covering each of the local education authorities in Britain, excluding Northern Ireland. Questions relating to the following were asked:

1. The number of primary, secondary and other schools in the authority connected to TTNS and Prestel.

2. Whether separate financial help is available for TTNS and Prestel-positive responses asked to specify.

3. Whether TVEI provides financial help with TTNS or Prestel – positive responses asked to specify.

4. Local education authority plans for developing TTNS and Prestel.

5. Identification of schools in the authority most active in promoting TTNS and Prestel.

6. Existence of any research into the use of TTNS or Prestel in the authority – positive responses to give brief details.

The TTNS electronic mail system allows subscribers to send questionnaires which when completed can, in theory, be returned via the system. The sender is also able to ask for an acknowledgement when the message has been read by the recipient.

The results were disappointing; although only 11 (8 per cent) of the System Managers replied, the figure for 'read rate' is revealing and depressing, as the following table shows:

One week is the maximum time that it is reasonable to allow for the reading of messages, as any greater length of time means that electronic mail loses its advantage over more traditional forms of communication. To work efficiently subscribers must read their messages at least once a day and respond to them quickly. It is worth noting that all 11 replies came from System Managers who read their mail within a week of receiving it.

The System Managers who replied to the questionnaire represented Wirral, Hampshire, Warwickshire, Salford, South Glamorgan, the Isle of Man, Essex, Kirklees, Devon, Grampian and Croydon local education authorities. Six of these had been identified already by TTNS as the most innovative authorities in their use of the system, and it seems reasonable to conclude that the authorities who replied were generally the ones which had a proud record in this sphere – indeed, this is borne out by the results.

The analysis of the responses demonstrated no clear pattern of use of either TTNS or Prestel. Some authorities appear to have concentrated their resources into primary schools, some into secondary. With only two exceptions, the authorities which replied all provided some form of central financial support, such as offers of free subscriptions, free hardware or part payment of costs. Only three of the authorities received help from TVEI for either system, which struck us as a surprisingly low figure.

While most (seven) of the authorities which replied planned developments for TTNS, none were considering plans for the further development of Prestel. However, it would be unwise to read too much into this result, as the questionnaire was handled by TTNS Systems Managers, who clearly had less involvement with Prestel. One encouraging finding was that six authorities reported research into the use of either Prestel or TTNS.

It is hard to draw conclusions from this questionnaire due to the small response rate; however it does seem evident that in the area of TTNS and Prestel there is a small number of innovative, active authorities in Britain. As to those which did not respond, it is possibly fair to assume that had they a record to be proud of they would have responded, and for that matter would have been more regular in the checking of their 'mailboxes'. This seems a pity as it is quite obvious from the case studies also undertaken as part of this research that, when Prestel and TTNS are exploited to the full, or even purely as an electronic mail service, they have much to offer education today.

## The case studies

Four schools were visited – these were a Buckinghamshire special school, an all-girls comprehensive in the Wirral, a Warwickshire Church of England junior school, and a Nottinghamshire comprehensive school. The intention was to investigate the quality, as well as the extent, of the use of Prestel and TTNS electronic mail systems. These visits demonstrated that the networks are being used to encourage and develop computer and writing skills; to create understanding between people from very different environments; as a local education authority's main communications medium; and as a means of obtaining information. Obviously many users covered more than one of these areas at a time. TTNS was found to be the system most readily adopted for most of these purposes.

## The development of writing and computer skills

The reinforcement of English and computer skills acquired elsewhere in the curriculum was being achieved by pupils in the Hampshire junior school through the use of TTNS electronic mail. They were participating in a 'chain story', in which each school produced (with the help of a wordprocessor) 300 words of a story and then sent this on to the next school in the 'chain', where the same process occurred. The exercise also encouraged the children to work cooperatively in groups and, in fact, had some interesting 'spin offs' in that children, enthused by this exercise, used their ideas in art, craft and drama classes.

Pupils at this school were also taking part in an interactive adventure game called 'Island Adventure'. The children had to solve quizzes set by pupils at other local junior schools, and then contribute to the adventure by setting their own quiz. The object of the game is to arrive at an unclaimed area in the 'game board' and then create a puzzle page, text screen and graphics screen. 'Island Adventure' is soon to be launched in the whole of Hampshire, a sizeable undertaking as 240 of Hampshire's 800 schools are on-line to TTNS. The educational benefits to this school are considerable, especially in the case of less-able and less-motivated children. Previously difficult children in this school blossomed, they had an incentive to achieve something they saw as worth while and had access to a medium untainted by past failure.

The encouragement of writing skills was also an impetus for the small rural Warwickshire junior school to use, first a microcomputer and then networking through TTNS. This school has been highly active in the introduction of microcomputers and their use in the school, and it now has one BBC microcomputer per classroom. The school has pioneered the use of wordprocessors in schools, arguing that for infants (four- and five-year-olds), they are invaluable in encouraging young children to write. A child of this age has little incentive to write 'by hand' as the work produced is hard to read, adults find such output difficult to follow, the children themselves have difficulty reading their own work, and other children do not even try to do so. By traditional writing methods, these children's creative ideas cannot be adequately expressed. For the first time, using wordprocessors, a child's work can be read by other children. For the first time, the audience becomes important to the author. The results of this process were very encouraging, as children began to criticize their own work, and their written output matured as a result. This process was continued for younger juniors (six-, seven- and eight-year-olds) using the MicroElectronics Programme's 'Infant Writer' package. Quickly, other schools in the area began to realize the potential of the scheme, and a story exchange was instigated in which four local junior schools passed a microcomputer disc in a chain, adding to the story at each school. This proved highly successful. So, by 1985, the children at this school were used to working with microcomputers and wordprocessors and were used to exchanging stories with children from other schools. The time was right for TTNS which was introduced in 1985, and enabled links not only with local schools, but with others further afield.

Eight children in a Buckinghamshire special school use the Prestel electronic mail service, under the guidance of the school assistant, for two hours a week. Enthusiasm has grown, with children who had previously been reluctant to write letters now keen to do so and, in some cases, unable to wait until the next lesson to find out if their replies have arrived. It is the two-way nature of the communication medium that most attracts these children. Over the last year they have learnt how to write a letter and, in some cases, how to type it. Once again, children with learning difficulties are offered the chance of success by information technology. These children quickly became aware that they, despite their difficulties, have the opportunity to communicate on equal terms with those at 'ordinary' schools. A letter written with care, compiled on a wordprocessor and sent via electronic mail looks the same regardless of the type of school from which it is sent. The children quickly realize that they leave their previous stigmatization behind when entering Prestel.

## The creation of understanding among people

The Warwickshire junior school is ‘twinned’ through the TTNS network with a school in the Shetland Islands. Daily, these two schools exchange details of weather conditions and this leads to interesting work compiling tables, graphs, etc. In addition, a ‘Shetlands Survey’ was undertaken through the network. The children used the library to build a picture of life in the Shetland Islands, and were surprised to discover just how inaccurate this picture was when lifestyles were exchanged on the network. The image that the library gave the children was one of a rural economy based on small farms and fishing. The reality in 1987, which the library had been unable to portray, was one of a modern, affluent, thriving economy dominated by the oil industry. An important aspect to stress here is the ‘child-to-child’ nature of this communication. Such direct links mean the survey was seen through the eyes of the children, rather than filtered or mediated by the values of adults. Apart from the computing skills enhanced by this exercise, the children learnt how to construct a questionnaire and developed a better understanding of other people’s lifestyles, which broadened their horizons.

This was again well demonstrated by a similar project 'twinning' the school with another in Newcastle-upon-Tyne. As a result of a similar survey of lifestyles undertaken through the network, children at the school were amazed to discover that 60 per cent of the fathers of similar-aged children in Newcastle were unemployed. The reality of unemployment was brought a little closer to a group of children from a prosperous part of Britain.

Even more ambitious have been the links forged via TTNS between this school and another in Tasmania, Australia. These communications have proved to be very swift (a matter of minutes) and so the children's enthusiasm has not been dampened by long delays in replies. Further links have been established with schools in New Zealand using the TTNS 'Farm Link' project. In this, Warwickshire schools have produced data on farming methods in different parts of the county, and these have then been exchanged within Warwickshire, and recently between Warwickshire schools and their counterparts in New Zealand.

In the Wirral comprehensive school international links also have been forged by pupils. The school has experimented with TTNS electronic mail links with Spain and proposes links with France and Germany. The most noteworthy achievements, though, have involved pioneering links with schools in the US. The school is linked with another in Connecticut, and messages have been exchanged between teachers and between pupils. All these links have been found to lead to a widened cultural experience for all of the children involved, and provided the opportunity for a fuller understanding between people from different environments.

## Local education authority communication medium

The policy of Wirral Education Authority is to develop TTNS as its main communications medium. The system has, for many teachers, replaced paper communication, being more immediate and cost-effective. There is also a marked reduction in telephone charges now that the system is being used effectively. TTNS costs are currently running at a mere 50p per day, per school. Not only is the system efficient, it is also effective. The problem faced by teachers, almost daily, of trying to communicate with other employees of the Authority, such as advisors, and failing to do so because they are 'off site' or 'in a meeting' is alleviated. By using such features of the system as 'express messaging' and 'reply requested', the sender can ensure a speedy service and messages that actually arrive at their required estimation. The Authority also produces an administration handbook on TTNS (meaning it is easily updated and cannot get lost), it communicates information for teachers such as details of Grant Related In-Service Training (GRIST), and produces an electronic diary.

## Access to information

In the Nottinghamshire comprehensive school, the Prestel 'Mailbox' facility provides staff and pupils with the opportunity to send enquiries and requests for publications. For example, pupils and staff use the Sports Council Mailbox frequently for information on such topics as sport sponsorships. This facility is also used for careers enquiries and requests for Health Education Council publications. The librarian pointed also to the potential of the system as a communications medium between schools (eg for inter-library loans), but little use is made of this at present as so few schools use their Prestel Mailbox.

## Conclusion

Electronic mail has the potential for benefiting education in our schools today. However, such potential cannot be realized without strong commitment from school staff, adequate financing and the generation of an awareness as to the contribution the technology can make.

An area which does not appear to have been developed so far, is communication by electronic mail to public libraries and school library support services. With the increasing need for teachers and their students to obtain up-to-date information, often on subjects not readily covered in books, easy access to information sources becomes necessary. A school's own resources can no longer be expected to provide for all likely requests, and yet a speedy response is required. Direct networking, for example, to main central and reference libraries, and to the school library support service could see professional specialist assistance being provided to schools expediently, efficiently and effectively. Once located, information could be relayed back via the network, and articles, pages of reference books etc faxed to the school. The technology is available, all we need is the foresight to use it for the benefit of education. We cannot afford not to make this investment if the children in our schools today are to be educated for tomorrow's society.
