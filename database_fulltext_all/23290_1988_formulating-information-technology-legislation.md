# Formulating Information Technology Legislation

Georgios I. Doukidis and Zisis Giannakos, Information Systems Department, London School of Economics

## Introduction

As the use of information technology (IT) grows in the modern world and solves a large number of problems, it becomes at the same time the source of new problems. Many of these are directly or indirectly related to the law. Legislation on IT is made necessary through practical implementation of specific issues, which means that it is developed within existing areas of law. One example is the Data Protection Act.

In this paper, the authors suggest that what is necessary is a panoramic approach to this phenomenon, an approach that will lead to the invention of a separate legal field of IT issues. This new framework will face head-on an enormous subject matter – both overall and in depth.

After summarizing some very basic reasons that justify the adapting of a separate legal field on IT, we sketch the character of such a field, which we name information technology legislation (ITL). We include a number of issues related to the understanding of ITL, like the relation of ITL to reality, the creation and development of ITL based on scientific example, the objectives of the Laws of Information, and the political dimension of ITL.

After examining the basic characteristics of ITL we examine a number of special areas which will have to be embodied in an ITL. The issues covered by ITL are very wide and diverse. Here we identify important areas, and examine current practice and legal thought as well as the development of legislation. We continue with consideration of the practical development and implementation of ITL, and include the branching nature of ITL, the area boundaries of ITL, priorities, and its methodological basis. The epilogue contains some final comments that concern more general organizational issues.

## The need for separate legislation on information technology

Up to the present time, at a practical level, the creation of new laws concerning IT is characterized by attempts to legislate on special issues or ‘targets’ (eg information privacy protection law). Criticism of this practice is twofold.

First, legal regulation of issues such as data protection will most certainly be inadequate, principally because in the social field there exists a complex mesh of problems rather than autonomous problems. To satisfactorily tackle only one of these problems is to ignore the parallel arrangement of a great number of problems that are related to the original problem. An attempt to give a solution to a special issue without taking into consideration the rest of the issues that comprise the mesh may generate new problems or transfer the problem into another area entirely.

The successful arrangement of all problems generated from the existence of IT in society is not carried out through the solution of each problem individually, but depends on the total arrangement of the whole mesh. What is required is a panoramic approach to the subject of the legal problems of IT. For the approach to be systematic it must take place within a separate legal field - ITL.

Second, the development of special legislation for IT is done within the traditional legal fields (eg commercial law). However, IT is a phenomenon which brings radical and irrevocable changes to social practice and to the quality of life.

Consequently, traditional legal thought, which was primarily before IT, is unable, to some extent, to follow the rapid rate of change in society brought about by IT. Traditional terms like theft (where the stolen goods disappear after the theft) are inadequate since, for example, illegal copying of data from a computer storage device does not mean that the device will disappear after the 'theft'. A separate law field on IT will be constructed from first principles for problems like the one above and their relevant importance will become greater and greater with time and the invasion of new technology throughout a society's organization.

ITL can be considered as a framework within which, prior to the formulation of a new law, extensive consideration of the problem's parameters will be taking place. Within this framework theoretical and empirical tools of thinking will be present that will allow an overall study of the phenomenon, as well as giving access to corresponding experience of other countries. The relation of ITL and IT is bi-directional: on one side ITL regulates the IT services within the social environment, on the other side IT specifies to a certain degree the quality of the relevant law research through international data banks, expert systems etc.

In addition to the above there are still some important arguments that justify the existence of a separate ITL.

1. IT has already stopped being considered a mysterious technology in the hands of a few experts and has spread to applications which are comprehensible to the most inexperienced users. As time progresses more social fields are influenced by this new technology which comes in and modifies, abolishes, or introduces new practices at the production level, the information level, the research level, etc. The traditional legal fields will begin to lose entire areas which prior to the introduction of IT were under their jurisdiction. As an example we would like to mention 'Electronic Funds Transfer' within credit organizations and the resulting existence of 'electronic signatures' that cease, to an extent, the current legislation on business dealing. The role of ITL is very important as a reference point every time a change or modification is made to a law in the traditional legal fields. For example, the creation of new law to regulate issues arising in commercial law, if made in the light of ITL, may reveal new and important areas.

2. Within the framework of ITL, a time plan could be developed to achieve the systematic introduction of legislation according to priorities specified by realistic criteria. Because of the large number and the heterogeneous nature of the issues, there is a need for balanced ITL development. The use of IT in both public and private sectors of the economy demands the existence of a legislation framework that will regulate a wide range of issues, most certainly not confined to that of personal privacy.

3. As time progresses the effects of IT on all aspects of society, will cause an accumulation of problems that will require regulation. However, as the number of problems to be regulated increases the existing framework with which to deal with these problems will remain stable, thus giving rise to a legislative bottleneck. The delays caused by such a bottleneck will result in further negative effects of the existing problems. In an area like IT where there is rapid development, the issue of timely legislation is of utmost importance. The existence of ITL reduces these negative consequences by decreasing the bottleneck and eliminating queuing time. This is achieved by reducing the time taken for the study of the problem and the formulation of the legislation.

4. ITL offers the framework for sensible production of IT laws. Today the production of such laws is identified as extractive (it refers to issues 'targets') and non-coherent (there is not an acknowledgement of the fact that every law is part of a more general plan). The prior existence of a legislation framework contributes to the elimination of the extractive and non-coherent production of laws. It also contributes to the minimization of common areas overlapping between laws and to the maximization of the 'space' relevant to IT that is spanned by the legislation.

5. One further advantage that ITL can offer as a complete legal field is its contribution to the formulation and realization of National Policies on IT. This is a result of the overall consideration of the issues relevant to IT that ITL offers and the heritage of fruitful thought that an absolutely complete legal field like ITL has.

## The nature of the information technology legislation

Having looked at some of the main advantages of adapting separate legislation for IT, an effort will be made to sketch the character of such a field. A very large number of issues that are related to the understanding of the purpose of ITL exists. We mention here some of the most important issues that were the primary source of intensive discussion as yet unsolved.

## ITL and reality

ITL is a concept that comprises the peculiar characteristics of every legal field. Its purpose is twofold: on one hand, it tries to regulate the problems and the framework from which they stem and, on the other hand, it tries to lead people towards socially desired directions. The ways ITL will approach these two fundamental objectives are directly dependent on how ITL is influenced by reality.

A number of researchers (Tsatsos 78) support the idea that legal acts have an ‘idealistic’ dimension, in other words they are concerned with what should ideally be done. According to these there is a gap between everyday practice and legal acts. Everyday practice acts as the initiative for the construction of an act but it does not specify its contents. The legislator should take into account the real conditions, but the basis for the development of legal acts lies in fundamental origins that are not necessarily related to reality.

The opponents of this theory argue that, on one hand, 'idealistic' acts refer to some abstract system of principles which are not clarified and, on the other hand, the system of these principles is not an entity that remains unchanged with time (as its supporters claim) but is the product of a given historical reality within which these principles were formulated. As a result, the legal acts and consequently ITL must be connected directly with reality to be in a position to arrange in a satisfactory way the problems encountered in everyday practice. During the last few years, this view seems to have predominated in wider legal areas.

On the other hand, it has to be stated that ITL is not a

true reflection of reality for the following reasons:

1. The complicated and heterogeneous structure of reality is inevitably reduced in a communication code (language). This code forces us to realize the surrounding world in a certain way which cannot be an image of reality, if such reality exists.

2. The methodology and the theoretical pedestals upon which the scientist lawyer relies are products of some specified political and historical reality that influences the refutation of the reality image in a specified code of thought.

3. There is always a time-lag between everyday practice and the development of legal acts. The fast change in reality that IT brings about imposes modifications on the relevant legal acts in order that they can respond to the new conditions. This cannot be done immediately. This leads to the creation of a gap between current reality and that reality for which the act was formulated.

4. In those cases that an attempt to engrave socially desirable directions is made, ITL guides reality rather than being guided by reality.

## Justice and science

Science may be considered as a certain way of thinking about problems that encloses a sum of achievements which are recognized as a basis for further practice (Kuhn, 1962). The example of science is characterized by the reduction of the complicated nature of the real world to a simpler form by making experiments whose results are accepted because of their repetition. The definition of science in this way not only excludes the legislation but also all the other social fields of knowledge from being characterized as ‘scientific’.

The social reality cannot be refuted on the basis of repetitive experiments where some parameters change, whereas the rest of the conditions remain constant. Social problems appear to have a high degree of interdependence and are very likely to remain unchanged even if they are detached from their social framework. If the problems are detached from their framework they lose their original meaning and thus the chance to be explained. This is happening because these problems are something more than the simple sum of their individual components (Checkland, 1976). The summations of social elements exhibit properties unknown for elements if taken individually.

## Objectives of ITL

We may distinguish many levels of objectives of legislation on IT. These may vary from abstract to very specific and include many kinds of such objectives within every level individually. Here we briefly mention four specific objectives that are characterized by their practicality.

Preventive. The law must have the ability to avert illegal acts or omissions that violate the legal rights of individuals. The great time lag caused by the existing traditional law on IT issues reduces considerably its ability to cope with this specific objective.

Corrective. Indemnity payments should be paid by the offender to the physical or legal person that suffered loss. ITL may contribute towards that with the establishment of rational criteria, eg for the evaluation/costing of the contents of a data bank.

Motivational. This may refer to wide spectrum of activities like encouraging the foundation of hardware and software companies, research support at universities and institutes of technology, etc.

Educational. ITL must contribute to the familiarizing of the citizen with IT, eg by encouraging the participation of lawyers and other users in computer networks in order to exchange information.

## Political dimensions of ITL

The four objectives outlined above have distinct political uses. A number of closely related questions arise: Who instigates this legislation? Which real and notional entities acted as incentives to the creation of these acts? What are the principles on which they were based and what were the criteria used in order to give priority to certain acts? To what extent were the interests of minorities embodied in the acts and to what extent are the legal acts – developed by the individual legislator – acceptable to the majority of the people? Additional questions may refer to the particular contents of a certain act, and to the necessity of its existence.

However, the political element does not only refer to the development process of an act, but also to its interpretation by the legislature. To a large extent legislation does not have an objective value but gives directives that are explained in the light of the case under trial. The subjective element is important since the judges themselves are persons with a certain position in the social hierarchy, an identifiable life-style and a crystallized way of thinking formed through the conditions of their work.

## Basic areas that have to be embodied in ITL

The field that ITL should cover is wide and heterogeneous. There are, however, some areas—kernels—around which a large part of the legal research and the preparation of legislation concerning IT tend to concentrate. We give below a summary of every one of these areas by referring to the current practice taken and the state of legal thinking concerning special legislation. We also raise a number of questions that concern, on the one hand, special areas that are candidates for legislation and, on the other hand, the process itself for the development of such a legislation.

## Data protection

Although there is no agreement about the exact areas of possible infringement of personal privacy resulting from IT, it appears that there is agreement as to the kernel of the problem. The concept of hazards refers to the ability of computer systems to collect data from a considerable number of sources, to store them for a long period of time, and to provide direct access to special data as well as capabilities for merging, sorting or searching, for purposes that are characterized as sensitive or harmful for the citizens, all within a short period of time (Shattuck, 1985).

The revelation of personal details and their introduction to computerized systems is often essential and in everyone's interest, as in the case of medical data banks where information available on hospital networks may turn out to be crucial for the life of the individual. What is required is the identification of the 'golden line' between the citizen's right to a personal life and the state's right to implement social policies.

The complicated nature of everyday life and its ability to change rapidly dictates to the legislator the formulation of generalized laws that are enriched in practice from the decisions taken in the courts. In this process the subjective interpretations of judges creep in together with possibly ineffective precedent to interpret a current case that may have arisen in a different framework.

Very recently in western countries a concept of enterprise protection, similar to data protection within the framework of personal privacy, has been developed.

## Employment and work conditions

There has been a lot of discussion about whether IT has had negative effects on employment and work conditions. A lot of people argue that IT has abolished several millions of jobs over the last two decades. However, others argue that IT has also created jobs both in the IT industry itself and in the new services that are related to it. Problems of definition, as well as statistics in the field that are rather uncertain, cause confusion. The whole problem is very closely related to the necessity of introducing IT to a society in a way that minimizes the negative effects on the population.

The issue of work conditions in relation to computer systems is also important and is at present the subject of wide discussions not only in the field of law. In addition, there are two more general areas of interest:

1. The influence of trade unions on the design of information systems in order to improve their control over long-term planning and the strategic decisions of organizations (eg in Sweden (Seipel, 1982)). This, of course, assumes a deep knowledge of new technology from the trade unions.

2. The effect of IT on job satisfaction. Issues such as the introduction of dull routine tasks, isolation from colleagues, tightened control over employees from management, etc.

The meaning, in practice, of the above issues for ITL is that a traditional legal field, like employment law, will use ITL as a guide for the development of employment legislation that will be related to IT.

## Political process

The use of IT by political parties may result in the improvement of their manifestos, eg with better manipulation of data that come out of the electorates' ideological trend survey. IT also offers the opportunity to a political party to improve its image to the electorate, eg by preparing individual letters for a vast number of voters instead of the well-known standard impersonal information bulletin.

As far as the voting process is concerned, IT offers the possibility of developing a new type of participatory democracy. Every household owning at least one terminal that is connected to a nation-wide network may vote through that terminal in an instant national referendum on issues like legislation concerning abortion, etc. From the perspective of law formulation, however, a number of questions are raised, like: Is this a potential future to be strived for and what options exist? Can direct democracy of this kind repair some of the shortcomings of representative democracy as we know it today or would it actually do harm, eg by slowing down the pace of government or by leaving too much room for careless decisions? Here also reside the legal problems of ensuring all those issues necessary for the proper operation of such referendums, eg the secrecy of the vote, one-person one-vote, fraud, identification of voters, etc.

## Contracts

In the area of contracts much practical experience has already been established. The hardware market had a great deal of practice in acquiring and using the new technology. The group of hardware suppliers consists mainly of hardware manufacturers that offer their products for sale or hire, and companies that purchase hardware from the manufacturers and offer it for hire at prices very much lower than the manufacturer (third-party lease).

The software market similarly established a wide area of practice in marketing products and services. Software may be acquired as software packages from software houses, or from hardware companies. For larger or specific applications software may be obtained from firms offering contract programming. Apart from software and hardware provision and installation, there are companies that offer special services which may not be necessarily related to IT. Contracts are also required in areas such as software improvement after its installation, hardware maintenance and repair, special training programmes on how to use the system, etc.

Contracts concerning general purpose hardware play a dominant role as regards both economic significance and influence on contracting practices. The other main type of contracts refers to software development by special order, the purchase of packages for software development, etc (Tapper, 1983).

Some problems are common to most types of contracts, such as transfer of title and payment methods. Difficulties with IT contracts are commonly associated with the description of products and services to which they refer, including specification of computer systems' performance and guarantees of such performance. Other problem areas are organization of systems' design and, in particular, delivery and acceptance in stages, and arrangements of back-up intended to diminish the risks associated with dependency upon a particular computer system (Nieman, 1985).

The issue of standardizing contracts drafted by vendors of hardware and software is very important. In this area IBM dominance is indubitable because the contracts used by this firm have tended to be used as models. However, the vendor-drawn contracts are generally unfavourable from the customer's point of view since customers are mainly partially covered. On the whole, the average computer user can be described as rather unsophisticated and unable to protect his legal interests. The exception is usually provided by specialized government agencies (as, for example, in Sweden) responsible for the acquisition of computer products and services. Such agencies have been rather successful in enforcing their own practices and contract provisions, and have tended to be ahead of other users with regard to legal know-how and bargaining strength.

## Intellectual property law

We can distinguish two basic types of legislation for intellectual property: patents and copyright.

Patents that refer mainly to protecting the methods may be valid in the software development area only if these programs can be considered as items with physical existence. In such a case, the method or process used to develop the software can be safeguarded and thus become established as a patentable item. Consequently, no other software developer, eg software house, individual, etc, may develop software using the same process. One legal opinion classifies programs in the non-patentable category of mathematical methods or book-keeping systems rather than in the category of items with physical existence, ie apparatus that can be patented.

Copyright refers mainly to work protection in context. Computer software can be protected both as a method or process by patents and in context by copyright protection. Computer software in this context should be understood as computer programs in various forms, such as flowcharts, tables, etc. All these intermediate forms of the software should also be included under both's jurisdiction.

The area of copyright problems is not restricted to protection of computer software and its intermediate stages. It also includes protection of computer output such as tables, histograms, etc. There are many other problems and everyday practice makes them more important. For example, to what extent protected works such as text and designs may be stored in machine-readable form and used through data banks without violating the rights of the owners?

## Standardization and compatibility

Legislation for standardization and compatibility of IT products should be another major part of ITL since the lack of compatibility of computer systems is a crucial disadvantage for organizations, enterprises and individual users. As a result, their ability to manipulate efficiently external information systems is severely diminished. This will most probably produce negative effects on their results and their overall functioning.

## Antitrust regulation

IBM's dominance of the IT market raises a number of problems concerning monopolistic competition. An example of reaction to this situation is seen in the pressure against IBM in the United States regarding the practice of 'bundling'. Legal action forced IBM to 'unbundle' its software, that is, not to include software products and services in the purchase or rental price of hardware.

However, IBM dominance is not the only antitrust problem. For instance, the differentiation of IT products and services, as well as the heavy dependency of customers on specific manufacturers, create difficulties for many of them in finding substitute products and services. This issue is related to the whole discussion of the establishment of standards and compatibility for IT products. The various governments can have substantial influence in tackling monopolistic IT markets, always within the current international economic communities or agreements. This could be achieved by controlling imports and exports of IT products, and by influencing the type of products purchased by the public sector.

## Security

IT contracts often contain terms that relate to providing enough security to the enterprise or organization if the system is unable to operate as specified in the system's specification. This may be a result of the hardware system breaking down (eg due to power failure) or a result of software bugs that are 'hidden' in large software systems. Furthermore, security legislation should be introduced to prevent criminal acts in computer systems, eg unauthorized copying of data from the system, sabotage, etc.

Since the evolution of IT, insurance companies have hastened to insure computer systems against various contingencies. However, there is dispute concerning their value which is dependent upon their use. It is also worth mentioning that the probabilities for eventual losses, eg data losses, data ruin, etc, are estimated in an unsatisfactory way.

## Problems of evidence

To what extent data stored, not as paper documents (where there are signatures, stamps, etc) but as records stored in a computer storage device (a computerized system can produce an infinite number of paper documents), are to be considered as evidence, is another area that concerns ITL. There are cases where the speed of transactions is so high that the possibility of keeping paper documents is excluded. As a result, the documentary evidence is kept in computer systems' storage devices.

## Taxation

The way concepts such as 'goods' and 'services', 'systems software', and 'application software' are interpreted can influence the legislation in areas like tax reduction of software development, etc.

## International legal issues

The first issue refers to international agreements on co-operation between several countries to make the best possible use of IT work for their common interests. Such agreements concern areas like international trade, information flow in international computer networks, etc.

Control and use of these networks by large organizations may lead to a loss of control of the network by a country's authorities, and the capital inflow and outflow to and from the country by means of Electronic Funds Transfer (EFT).

Problems of responsibility for any damage incurred may arise, eg in a data bank in country A from a user in country B, that accesses the data bank in A with his or her computer terminal through an international computer network. The European Community has already appreciated (Doukidis and Tsalikoglou, 1987) the importance of the legal harmonization of the European IT sector by initiating the development of the European Community Corpus Juris Informaticae. The above has been seen as a necessary step towards the creation of the Common Information Market by 1992. The three main areas that the European Community is concentrating its efforts on are: telecommunications, transborder data flows, data and software protection.

## Directions for the practical development and implementation of ITL

There are some special observations which can be made concerning legislation development on IT within ITL and also some final comments related to more general issues of functioning and evolution of such legislation.

## The issue of interdisciplinarity

The legislative framework developed in the previous section is the outcome of a synthesis involving several traditional branches of knowledge. We can distinguish between two levels of interdisciplinarity:

1. Intra-legal interdisciplinarity refers to the legal disciplines which contributed to the development of the framework. Such fields include labour law, criminal law, civil law and so on.

2. Extra-legal interdisciplinarity pertains to the non-legal fields of knowledge which were necessary for the development of an adequate ITL. Such fields include economics, sociology, management, linguistics, computer science, systems analysis, etc.

The element of interdisciplinarity in the framework is alleged to contribute to its effectiveness as a framework for legislation development, with real-life problems that are complex and heterogeneous. Due to their nature, real-life problems demand the involvement of a tool which was constructed from the beginning as a multi-disciplined remedial construction.

## The issue of boundaries

Some of the issues examined above, and which must be included in ITL, have overlapping areas. For example, the issue of antitrust regulation is directly related to international issues. There is a need to determine the boundaries of each of these special areas of ITL individually beforehand in order to avoid, for example, examination of the same problem under a different sub-area.

## The issue of priorities

This refers to the parts of the framework that should be given priority when an ad hoc legislation is developed. However, priorities can differ between countries, cultures, regimes, etc. There is, however, a universal trend – at least in the western world – towards legislation in areas like data protection, IT market regulations, etc.

## The issue of methodological basis

We have seen before that the theoretical background upon which the creation and development of ITL relies cannot be the scientific framework because of its inefficiency in the study and explanation of social phenomena.

As a more efficient framework for analysing social phenomena, systems theory is proposed. Systems theory approaches social phenomena from a panoramic perspective by examining their more specific forms within an interdisciplinary framework. Furthermore, its support for principles like 'whole' and 'parts', that do not have absolute existence but accumulate their meaning depending upon the person who views the system, attach to this theory the unique ability of examining compound and extended phenomena like social phenomena.

## Conclusions

We finish this paper with some more comments and observations that concern the practical implementation of an ITL on a national level.

Interdisciplinary issues, boundaries issues, priorities issues, and issues of methodological basis can be considered as the basic forms for the development of balanced ITL that depends on the country's special needs.

Current legislation on IT in all countries covers only a small domain of the legal fields that are necessary for the successful introduction of IT. The major disadvantage we believe is the lack of appreciation that existing law is part of a more general IT framework that is specified within ITL.

The development of satisfactory legislation that will cover a wide spectrum of areas will result from legal research into existing law. In most of the countries this research is almost non-existent.

The participation of those people directly involved with IT (analysts, programmers, users, special academics) in the formulation of acts is crucial. The creation of IT laws only by lawyers, whose experience of IT is occasional, diminishes the possibility of making really effective laws. Real practice of the workplace remains unknown to the legislators for many reasons.

Because of the interdisciplinary nature of ITL there is a need for contributions from people who are knowledgeable in many areas of the development of legislation. This is usually done by formulating committees made up of experts. However, this does not guarantee the design of truly effective laws because of the lack of deep communication between the experts. The ideal case would be the existence of persons with educational and practical experience in more than one field who can have a panoramic view of the overall issues and problems that fall under the jurisdiction of an information technology legislation.
