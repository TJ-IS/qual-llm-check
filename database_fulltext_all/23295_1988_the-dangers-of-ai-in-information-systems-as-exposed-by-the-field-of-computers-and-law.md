# The Dangers of AI in Information Systems as Exposed by the Field of Computers and Law

Ronald Stamper, $^{*}$ London School of Economics and Department of Information Management, University of Twente

Some intriguing problems lurk at the meeting of those polar opposites: computers and law. There are speed versus sloth, the mechanical versus the social, the formal versus the informal, syntactic conformity versus semantic dispute, and other contrasts, all of which should find their way into a new series of books: Artificial Intelligence and Legal Reasoning, edited by Thorne McCarty and Edwina Rissland, published by the MIT Press.

I believe that this field of enquiry will sharpen our perceptions of a range of problems, not only in computing and law but in many aspects of the social sciences and philosophy. The law provides a laboratory where we can observe our social reality made explicit in the shape of norms being made, broken, interpreted and remade. Computing is the instrumentation to make these observations more precise. This series should attract many readers in the information systems field, provided that the editors adopt a fairly critical view of AI.

Consider the book that inaugurates the series: An Artificial Intelligence Approach to Legal Reasoning, by Anne von der Lieth Gardner. Dr Gardner has chosen a well-focused problem: to build an AI system to tackle examination questions in the sub-domain of contract law, concerned with offers and acceptances. A successful system would display at least a measure of legal erudition that any law teacher could accurately judge. She shows how such a system could make the key distinction between clear and hard cases.

Dr Gardner has chosen to work with common law rather than 'add a layer of complication' that statute law would introduce, but regrettably she does not expand upon the nature of this layer. Common law has its own layer of complication: the problem of reasoning from particular cases. I particularly looked forward to the treatment of that problem. The system requires both common sense and legal knowledge to be handled. So this research, although focused sharply, casts light over an exciting range of problems.

A four-level processing strategy is adopted:

\- The first characterizes legal events in the problem statement using a transition network of possibilities (e.g. offer, counter-offer, acceptance).

\- The second uses rules to decide whether an arc in the network can be followed. The interesting, hard questions yield conflicting results, but the main source of hard questions is level three, invoked when the rules run out.

\- At the third level, predicates that cannot be tested by legal rules of procedures have to be examined using common sense knowledge or knowledge drawn from cases.

\- Finally, having recognized the genuinely hard legal question, the fourth level (not attempted in the research reported) would adduce arguments on both sides of these issues.

The robot law student still lurks beyond Dr Gardner's horizon.

Unlike many of the expert systems community approaching legal applications from the computer side, she is not blind to the dangers of trying to mechanize legal reasoning. Her Chapters 2 and 3 on legal philosophies, which have informed her thinking on the design of her system, should be read by anyone who is tempted into this problem area. The central problem which her strategy illuminates is that of matching the formal consensus embodied in the law to the informality of the human context where dispute is the stuff of legal reasoning. The jurisprudential theories she sketches (the mechanical, the realist and the positivist) differ dramatically. When applied to AI and legal reasoning their differences are sharper. The same range of theories is important in the study of other kinds of information systems. We can expect legal philosophy to be stimulated by issues raised by the encroachment of computers into legal activity. I hope that the same ideas, sharply focused in computers and law, will influence thinking about all kinds of information systems.

Although the relationship of legal expert systems to people is covered, it is not treated primarily as a social issue but one for the individual decision-maker. This is a mistake that is now generally made by the systems designer who attempts to mechanize other kinds of decision-making.

The representation of knowledge in this system uses a relatively informal frame-based, problem-statement language (KRL). Another language (MRS) based on predicate calculus is used to encode the knowledge for the computer. This strategy lies securely within one of the standard AI research paradigms which accepts predicate logic and its derivatives as beyond reproach in all essential aspects.

From this point on, eschewing any further examination of underlying assumptions, the research, lucidly explained in terms of knowledge representation, processing and program performance, glides comfortably to its conclusion. The one disappointment is in the treatment of cases. Individual cases are not represented and used in processing at level three. Instead simple general rules called 'examples' have been distilled from the case material, and these are treated in essentially the same way as other rules except that a condition not matched by examples does not fail — it is reported as a hard question. Legal rules, on the other hand, define the scope of the law; failure to be matched by them indicates that the problem lies outside the scope of the law.

Within this volume we have a fine example of 'normal' science in Kuhn's sense. For a number of reasons, this is the field where the anomalies in the normal AI paradigm will first become most evident. This series of books could continue by reporting AI research from the same methodological mould, but filled by other legal problems, other 'user' communities, different knowledge representation and processing strategies. The academic AI industry will ensure the supply of material. Work of Dr Gardner's quality will be welcome but the series as a whole has the potential to tackle a wider range of issues, and to question the basic assumptions of AI and perform a service to all concerned with information systems.

Workers in computers and law will eventually be forced to ask questions that upset the established paradigm. AI aims to replace human intelligence by computers. It has blossomed recently into the production of expert systems that claim to encapsulate knowledge. Their makers rely upon epistemological assumptions, valid in routine engineering, medicine and various applied sciences, that do not extend to the law. Recording and sharing and consensus about how to describe the physical world differs radically from interpreting contentious prescriptions about social behaviour. The justification behind an expert system for diagnosing engine malfunction does not work for an AI system to resolve legal disputes. Applied scientists can conveniently overlook the social processes that originally led to their agreed body of knowledge, but in the legal domain the social processes are always central.

Consider a few problems visible but not discussed in this first volume of the series: the treatment of facts, the uncritical use of predicate logic, obscurity introduced by the formal representation of knowledge, the belief that knowledge can be encapsulated in logical formulae, and the multiplication of problems by the very process of trying to solve them.

The type of examination question being investigated gives the facts of a case. From the true facts and the valid law, the deductive reasoning performed by the computer leads to true legal conclusions. Not infrequently, however, legal disputes have more to do with deciding the facts than adducing arguments. The anticipated consequences of the rules lead each side to characterize the world in terms that would lead a court to find in that side's favour. The facts are the product of legal consequences, not their producer. The truth has to be decided by social mechanisms of negotiation, arbitration and court decision. The facts are also outputs of the legal process and not only inputs; consequently the nature of the world is to some extent a product of the law.

Predicate logic cannot adapt to the social creation of truth and reality. It is a tool developed for the totally different task of formalizing mathematical arguments — it is not an instrument for creating new mathematical concepts. Predicate logic assumes that already settled are all questions of what individuals exist in the world and how they are characterized, problems that are central to some legal disputes. If the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — that one's language determines how one perceives reality — is difficult to maintain for natural languages, it surely does apply to a logic that imposes iron constraints on what one can conceptualize within it. From a social and therefore legal point of view, predicate logic is semantically naive and misleading.

All programming languages create problems that are shared by the new logic programming languages. The programmer invents a whole new vocabulary for characterizing the problem, making it more difficult for users of the system to penetrate. Another programmer, working independently, would not produce the same results. In the program-writing culture, no one would think of commenting adversely on the licence to use 'STKLVL' for 'stock-level' or 'CUSTNO' for 'customer number'. In routine business data-processing it may not matter; in the law, where the interpretation of words is central, this kind of obscurity becomes a dangerous disease. Dr Gardner's examples reveal far less of this 'Humpty Dumpty' syndrome than does the work of most logic programmers. However, predicates such as 'produces-manifestation-with-content' (p.156), although they use familiar words strung together, are not natural language but part of the programmer's private code. Someone else might use, in a related system, the predicates 'produces-manifestation' and 'has-content', making it incompatible with the first system. This serious problem afflicts business data-processing where many incompatibilities among constituent applications are rooted in their arbitrarily chosen vocabularies. Practical legal AI systems will have to be constructed as a patchwork of small systems (like Dr Gardner's) but the consequences of each designer behaving like Humpty Dumpty is that the patches will never imitate the 'seamless fabric' which the law is supposed to be.

Obscurity may be endemic in the law. Nevertheless it is open to comment and critical scrutiny, compared with AI representations of legal knowledge. Expert system builders believe that objective knowledge may be captured, stored and sold as a product. Even a body of scientific fact and theory ceases to be knowledge if it is not constantly interpreted, rehearsed, tested and used by comprehending people. The law would be reduced to mechanical, bureaucratic nonsense even more quickly by hiding it in a black, electronic box. Present AI techniques place encoded legal knowledge beyond the intellectual reach of the community responsible for its critical appraisal and justifying the way it is applied.

Workers in computers and law suffer from the 'Wicked Problem' syndrome where, like the sorcerer's apprentice, they find each attempt to solve a problem only makes it more intractable. The syndrome can be evaded by working on toy examples, so that two steps back for each one forward does not look too bad. I have indicated some of the causes of the wickedness of problems of computers and law. They are all traceable to a failure to deal with the social dimension correctly. The goal must be to make legal knowledge less obscure, more clearly structured, more open to the user and the critic. This, I believe, is possible, and Dr Gardner indicates in her work how structure, scarcely visible in the prose, can be revealed in a formal analysis. The irony is that such a revised goal would lead research away from the cognitive machine sought by AI towards a more humble product aimed to support human intelligence used in social cognition.

This more humble product might be thought of as a miraculous extension of our familiar writing implements and printing technology. The extension is miraculous because it adds, to the familiar signs that we write on paper, the articulation of our word pictures so that they can be animated. Our use of writing is an aid to collaboration, an advance upon speech that permits forms of social organization that would otherwise be impossible. The computer takes us just as great a step forward, and it will also lead to new, as yet unimagined, forms of social interaction. It is in the domain of support for collaborative behaviour, not in the imitation of individual human intelligence, that the computer will have its greatest impacts. AI will, I hope, give way, in the imaginations of the information systems community, to CSCW (Computer Supported Cooperative Work, the title of the important recent conference on this theme in Austin, Texas). CSCW will be just as good an excuse for the pursuit of advances in programming as AI has been, but it encourages a healthier approach to information systems. If this shift of paradigm is to happen, a series of books on computers and law has a role to play in bringing it about, but only if the editors can entertain contributions that expose, rather than hide, the anomalies which attempts to apply AI to the law will reveal.

## Biographical notes

After reading mathematics at Oxford, and working in the NHS and the steel industry, Ronald Stamper published his 1973 study of information in business (second edition in preparation for Blackwell, Oxford). His LEGOL/NORMA Project experimented with legal expert system shells (1975/77/79) to create an information system specification language and method, now available as MEASUR.
