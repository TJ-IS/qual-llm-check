# Appropriate Technology for Humans and Organizations\*

Chris Clegg, MRC/ESRC Social and Applied Psychology Unit, Department of Psychology, University of Sheffield

Abstract: This paper introduces the idea of ‘Appropriate Technology’ and applies it to some of the choices organizations make when designing, implementing and managing information technology (IT). It questions whether companies make appropriate choices about the extent of technology, the allocation of system functions between humans and machines, the design of jobs and roles for humans, the design of supporting organizational structures, and the methods used during design and implementation. If inappropriate choices are made in these areas, problems of morale, motivation and system performance can occur. The objectives of the paper are to examine whether these ideas can help to identify and explain problems in the implementation of new technology, and to argue that social scientists have so far achieved relatively little in helping organizations to make more appropriate choices in these areas. The paper makes a number of propositions about new technology from a social science perspective, introduces what is meant by Appropriate Technology, uses manufacturing as an example of an environment within which new technologies are being implemented, and describes five key decision areas for managers. As a result, a new set of propositions is made, this time from the perspective of general management and engineering. The author draws on research and development experiences with a number of companies, and on work undertaken within the ESPRIT and Alvey programmes.

## Introduction

This paper introduces the idea of 'Appropriate Technology', and focuses on whether new information technologies can be designed, implemented, operated and managed in ways appropriate for their users and for the organization. 'Appropriate' here has deliberate ambiguity. First, it is used as an adjective, meaning suitable, in this case for effective human and organizational use. Second, it is used as an imperative verb, and is concerned with ways in which people have the opportunity to take ownership of, and some measure of control over, the design and management of the technology.

The paper draws on a range of research and development projects on which the author has worked with colleagues (including, currently Graham Johnson, Robin Martin, Susannah Ravden, Toby Wall and Peter Warr). This research includes work for ESPRIT on Computer Integrated Manufacturing, for the UK Alvey Directorate under their programme on human-computer interfaces, and in a number of companies implementing new technologies. Most of these projects have been conducted in manufacturing environments, though not exclusively so, and it is hoped many of the points in this paper are also of more general relevance.

The objectives of the paper are:

1. to introduce the idea of Appropriate Technology, and to consider what it may mean when applied to information technology;

2. to examine what social scientists have to offer in the form of models, methods and advice to help organizations to develop more appropriate technologies;

3. to consider whether use of the term adds to our understanding of the choices that organizations make when designing, implementing and managing new technologies.

The paper is organized in six parts. The first consists of a set of critical propositions, written from a social science perspective, concerning the ways in which new systems are typically designed and implemented. The second introduces the idea of Appropriate Technology from the domain of development economics. The next focuses on manufacturing and the context within which some new technologies are being developed and implemented.

Some elements making up appropriate technologies with the firm are identified in the fourth part of the paper, which examines the extent to which social scientists have offered models and methods to influence practice. Particular attention is paid to four interrelated issues, namely decisions on (a) the appropriate type and level of technology, (b) what to automate and what to allocate to humans, (c) the appropriate roles for humans in advanced systems, and (d) the appropriate local organization structures to manage and support them. The next section discusses some of the problems of user participation in these areas, and advocates that ways need to be found for users to appropriate the design and implementation of such technologies. Finally, the paper rewrites the earlier propositions, this time from an engineering and general management perspective, argues that the notion of appropriateness is useful, and identifies some issues for research and development.

## Propositions about IT

While it is difficult to generalize about the ways in which companies introduce their new technologies, a number of interrelated propositions can be formulated with some confidence. This first set is made from a social science perspective.

1. Many new technologies are implemented without careful integration into the company's business plan. There is a lack of strategic thinking and planning about IT (Ingersoll Engineers, 1987; Piercy, 1987).

2. In sophisticated high-tech companies, the development of IT is technically dominated and driven. Technologists and engineers have appropriated new technology, making it their own. General managers and personnel specialists have been too passive in their demands to contribute to the development of such technologies. When they are involved, it is usually after the system is designed, and often after it has been commissioned (Blackler and Brown, 1986; Clegg and Kemp, 1986).

3. In less sophisticated companies, the development of IT follows the 'disjointed incrementalism' (p.303) described by Blackler and Brown (1986), in which technology systems arise by muddling through. This is especially problematic in large, highly differentiated organizations (Mueller et al., 1986).

4. A major objective within company automation plans is often to maximize the role of technology, or the extent of automation. The limits to automation are determined by technical feasibility and financial constraint. Human operators are seen as sources of unpredictability and error, and since technologists seek control and predictability — certainly in their production environments — whenever possible system functions are allocated to machines rather than humans (Clegg, Ravden, Corbett and Johnson, 1988).

5. Whether technologically driven, or designed by muddling through, scant regard is paid to the human and organizational aspects of such technologies (see Clegg and Kemp, 1986); they are usually addressed after the technology is designed, and sometimes after it is running. In many cases 'default' options are taken, such that jobs and local organization structures are designed as they were with old technologies, with minimum levels of skill and responsibility and with highly differentiated structures. Suboptimal performance may arise because of poor system design (that does not meet user requirements); lack of clarity, understanding and consensus about how the system will be operated and managed; poor interfaces; poor job designs and organization structures; and inadequate training. Hutchinson (1986) has argued that new technology applications often perform below their potential because of mismanagement reflected in misuse of the system, misunderstanding of it, and even its disuse.

6. Systems designers report that the most widespread approach to technical change is based on the notion of ‘satisficing’ (Simon, 1969), that is, finding the first available satisfactory solution that meets basic requirements. In part this is because of the difficulty of searching for, defining, and evaluating ‘optimum’ solutions.

Other people would doubtless place different emphases, and clearly these propositions are in direct conflict with how social scientists would like to see the development of IT (see Meister, 1987). Implicit in them is the complaint that technologists, engineers and general managers are not taking social science inputs seriously enough. But do social scientists have something to offer? This issue is considered later when an alternative set of propositions is presented. The paper now introduces the idea of Appropriate Technology.

## The idea of Appropriate Technology

The idea that we should have a contingent and human centred view of technology stems in its most recent and popular form from the writings of Schumacher (1974). In his classic text, Small is Beautiful: The Study of Economics as if People Mattered, he argued the case both for more humanized scales of operation, and for the development of intermediate technologies that are appropriate for their social and cultural circumstances. In 1965, Schumacher set up the Intermediate Technology Development Group (ITDG) to work in the Third

World, developing and introducing technologies that are appropriate for local circumstances. This group has grown and continues to work effectively, more than 20 years later. During this period, Schumacher and colleagues have emphasized the need for technical innovations that are culturally and socially appropriate. For example, in environments where labour is cheap and in plentiful supply but skills and tools for the maintenance of complex equipment are scarce, it makes little sense to import advanced labour-saving technologies.

ITDG's philosophy rests on the primacy of self-help and self-development. For example, the Appropriate Technology Design and Development Group (ApT), itself an independent, small nonprofit making organization which sometimes collaborates with ITDG, has worked in 12 Third World countries designing and developing technologies which can be built and maintained using local materials and skills. Initial training is provided but thereafter the technology and its support are self-sustaining. ApT has designed and developed a number of machines (for example for rolling sheet metal) and these have been used to make agricultural tools, rainwater guttering, stoves, furniture, hospital equipment and other everyday items.

Over the years the idea of intermediate technology has been partially supplanted by the term ‘appropriate’, perhaps because of connotations of ‘second best’ perceived in use of the older term. But the basic ideas still have enormous currency and influence, especially in the developing world, and in the context of international aid.

Recently Jequier and Blanc (1985) and Darrow and Pam (1985) have argued that appropriate technology has become a generic term for technologies characterized by one or more of the following features. They can be produced, maintained and controlled locally; can be understood by users; are flexible and adaptable to particular circumstances; promote organizational simplicity; have high potential for employment; make sparing use of natural resources; and require low investment per workplace and per unit of output. Furthermore, some believe these same principles can be applied to more developed economies, and point to the widespread alienation of users of advanced technologies (Stewart, 1985). Of particular relevance are the emphases on local control, user understanding, system flexibility and simplicity, and cost. Before applying these ideas, the paper first considers one environment, that of manufacturing, within which new technology investments occur.

## Manufacturing context

There is not space to review the market and technological forces operating on companies working in manufacturing, but some generalizations can be made about prospects for the medium term. These comments apply to small and medium batch manufacturing which constitutes the majority (estimates vary around 70 per cent) of engineering output in the UK and the USA. Commentators (e.g. Santucci, 1987; Susman and Chase, 1986) appear to agree that this manufacturing sector, in the medium term, will be characterized by increased competition; smaller batch sizes; increased demand for customization and product variety; less predictable external environments (e.g. markets); greater complexity of production processes, accompanied by the occurrence of more non-routine variances; higher capital cost of production systems; relatively lower labour cost inputs; greater integration and interdependence and tighter manufacturing coupling, both organizationally and technically; and increased prevalence of design for manufacture.

Of particular interest here are predictions that manufacturing systems will become increasingly complex, tightly coupled and interdependent, and at the same time will incorporate higher levels of uncertainty, in the process itself, at its boundaries, and in its relations with the external environment. The implementation of new technology will itself promote some of these trends. Nor are these predictions necessarily restricted to manufacturing: indeed it seems plausible that many business sectors will follow these trends.

## Appropriate advanced manufacturing technology

This part of the paper is concerned with translating the idea of appropriate technology to examine whether it helps our understanding of problems with IT within UK firms. Four main issues are considered and they can be viewed sequentially.

## What advanced technologies are organizations developing?

The issue here is whether or not companies have a strategic approach to the development of new technology, if so, what it is, and whether or not it is 'appropriate' for their circumstances. The first point echoes that made by many others, that most firms do not have an explicit coherent strategy for the development and implementation of IT (Ingersoll Engineers, 1987; Blackler and Brown, 1986). Muddling through in this way, a firm is unlikely to hit upon appropriate choices of technology. Examples are legion of firms with under-utilized and ineffective systems that do not meet the requirements of their users.

But how do more sophisticated companies fare? To focus on manufacturing, many are unduly technology driven, seeing new technology as a cure-all for their manufacturing ills. There is a common assumption that all roads lead to computer integrated manufacturing (CIM) as if according to some evolutionary imperative. This is reflected in the emphasis on ‘automate or liquidate’, and in the attribution of somewhat higher order, mystical qualities to CIM, as in:

Trying to define CIM is like trying to define God (Chalmet, 1987, p.12).

In many firms, however, there is a more mundane focus on money and headcount. Most significant investments in advanced manufacturing technologies, in the UK at least, are subject to stringent payback requirements, often as short as two to three years, and rely on labour cost reduction both through headcount and skill level. Paradoxically, the fact that Japanese manufacturing companies — to date some of the most successful users of such technologies — make no such demands of their investments, has had little impact on UK financial thinking.

Fashion, too, undoubtedly plays a part in shaping the strategic thinking of individuals and companies.

It would not be difficult to justify the application of Cohen, March and Olsen's (1972) garbage can model of decision making. In this view, the decision to implement a flexible manufacturing system (FMS) or CIM can be interpreted as a solution in search of a problem. How many enthusiastic engineers are pursuing FMS and CIM when their problems call for more appropriate solutions? Such cynicism receives some support from experienced leading-edge companies such as Ingersoll Engineers (1987) and GEC (Greenwood, 1987) who are currently preaching the wisdom of simplicity, the virtues of returning to basics, and the dangers of automating existing problems. These ideas are strikingly reminiscent of the features of appropriate technology identified earlier.

Some consultants working in this area point to the uniqueness of strategic choices of advanced manufacturing technology. All companies are different; therefore all strategies should be unique, and no generalizations are possible. But it is hard to believe that there are no generalizable principles to guide companies facing these problems. In fact a number of general propositions are available. The first has its roots in production engineering and points to the importance of rate of output and the number of parts produced. Figure 1, reproduced here from Ayres and Miller (1983), highlights the appropriateness of different technological choices contingent upon these two production variables.

A second schema has been developed by PA Consultants (Chalmet, 1987), and emphasizes the pressures on manufacturing companies towards increased complexity (in the form of number of items on the parts list, number of levels in a bill of materials, number of budget centres in a manufacturing facility, and so on) and higher uncertainty (in the market place). This schema fits technological 'solution' or application to a company dependent on the uncertainty and complexity it faces. This is summarized in Figure 2.

According to Chalmet, FMSs are most appropriate for complex production processes in relatively certain markets, as in the case of audio and video products and cars.

Another set of ideas to help organizations to choose appropriate technologies has been offered by Dale (1986) whose schema uses Perrow's (1970) concepts of predictability (for example of the type and amount of throughput), and analysability (for example of the production process and its control). Dale argues that hard automation is straightforward in predictable and analysable environments. Advanced manufacturing technologies, such as FMS, CADCAM and CIM, can only be made to work in analysable manufacturing environments and are ideal for unpredictable situations.

While there are some discrepancies in these arguments there are many common themes, and one can tentatively hypothesize that the appropriate organizational context for systems such as FMS and CIM includes at least some of the following: (a) relatively analysable (and thereby programmable) products (b) that are similar but varied (e.g. similar sizes and components), (c) that have been designed for manufacture, (d) with constrained customization, (e) with regular repeat orders, (f) with quality premiums, where repeatability is important, (g) in small to medium batches, (h) in companies with existing high tech skills, (i) in strong financial circumstances, and (j) with a culture accepting change.

Otherwise, given scarce resources, investments might be better made in other improvements to the manufacturing system. From a practical perspective, checklists of this kind may prove useful for companies trying to identify the level of technology in which they should invest. What evidence there is suggests that most companies do not have a strategy for making such choices and that, if they do, it is usually too technology, rather than organizationally and environmentally, driven. Companies might usefully ask themselves if they are focusing too much on their technology. Do they expect too much of it? Is there a danger of over-automating? Are they following fashion? Would something simpler, and perhaps cheaper, prove more effective? Can technologies be identified that are more appropriate for their own circumstances?

Two further points should be made. First, there are few systematic checklists, models or methods that aim to help managers and others to identify what sorts of technology are appropriate for them; and second, this perpetuates the technology-driven satisficing approach described earlier.

## What is automated within high tech systems?

Given the investment in a certain type of technology, this issue is concerned with whether or not the appropriate functions in systems are automated or are undertaken by humans. This paper has proposed that most new applications are technically driven, the human aspect being considered late in the day. But the issue is not simply one of timing whereby the technologists make their design then hand it over for others to operate and manage. During design, the systems engineers make choices about what should be automated, and what should be 'left' for the humans in the system. This is sometimes called the allocation of system functions.

For example, in developing a flexible manufacturing system, decisions are made on how materials will move around the system, how parts will be loaded into and unloaded from machines, how production schedules are set up, and how errors and breakdowns in software and hardware are identified and rectified. Usually these decisions are made so as to maximize the level of automation, within cost constraints, and to minimize human interventions.

Underlying such approaches there is the view, prevalent among engineers, that humans are sources of unpredictability and error. One of the tasks of designers of production systems is to reduce and, if possible, remove unpredictability from such systems.

It is recognized that humans may be better performers than machines at some functions. Thus humans are better at complex pattern recognition, at various dextrous assembly tasks, and so on. In the past, ergonomists and others have developed systematic comparisons of what Humans Are Better At versus what Machines Are Better At (i.e. HABA/MABA – see, for example, Fitts, 1951; Nof, Knight and Salvendy, 1980; Price, 1985). Such comparisons are usually in the form of tables but they are not without their problems. They tend to be limited in scope, and there are real difficulties in keeping up with the relative performance capabilities of new technologies, such as robots. But more fundamentally, these tables only consider short-run performance under ideal working conditions, as well as ignoring other important psychological and organizational issues.

In ESPRIT CIM Project 534, Clegg, Ravden, Corbett and Johnson (1988) have been working with systems engineers developing a methodology that can help to allocate functions using a range of criteria. These criteria are technical feasibility; health and safety; operational requirements, including physical, information processing and performance requirements; and function characteristics, including criticality, unpredictability, and psychological importance.

More generally in this project, humans are seen as sources, not of error but of error recovery, as elements that are well equipped to control and respond to complexity and uncertainty. It is noteworthy that humans often have far greater roles to play in advanced systems than designers and others initially envisage. Large and complex systems have to cope with, and indeed sometimes create, high levels of uncertainty — for example in the work process itself, at the boundaries with other systems, or in coping with and responding to external changes. Humans can be highly effective at handling such uncertainties and at making judgements. They can also perform well in the roles of error detection and error recovery. In many manufacturing contexts, for example, certainly those of the kind described earlier, the idea of people-less systems remain a long way distant (see also Sinclair, 1986).

The general point is that many systems are designed with inappropriate allocations of function, because there are few, if any, systematic and usable methods available. In the current technically-dominated climate, this may result in over-automation. Some tasks are better done by humans, especially in the sorts of context involving high levels of complexity and uncertainty. Companies might usefully ask themselves if it is really possible to identify and plan for all production contingencies in the way that complete automation requires. Are there aspects of the system that require human control and intervention? Would better solutions be achieved if humans were seen as controllers of uncertainty and as sources of error recovery?

## What are the roles for humans?

Once allocation of function decisions have been made, the various tasks need organizing into job designs for the operators of the system, and local organization structures need designing to support its activity (see below). Considerable knowledge exists, both generally with regard to job design criteria and more specifically regarding job design and new manufacturing technology (see Ravden, Clegg and Corbett, 1987).

Clegg (1984) has argued that job designs result in part from choices of technology, but also from choices about patterns of local control and from the allocation of roles. Clegg and Kemp (1986) point to the choices that exist even with the implementation of similar technologies, such as CNC machine tools. They argue that some companies use new technology to de-skill operators, but that this is not inevitable. The choices of technology, along with the allocation of function decisions, influence the range of tasks that need doing, such as loading, unloading, programming, proving out, editing, quality checking, and so on, but they do not determine who will do them. There remain significant choices about the allocation of these roles. In part these are matters of the design of local organization structures and they cannot, in practice, be treated separately.

Kemp and Clegg (1987) present a model of role design based on the notions of complexity and uncertainty. They argue that:

... organizations working in uncertain environments characterized by market competition, small batches, high product quality and complexity may choose to give operators local control over key work roles in order to promote effective working (p.111).

Susman and Chase (1986) present a scenario of the integrated factory of the future, in which there are increased levels of vertical and horizontal interdependence, at the same time as higher levels of conversion and boundary uncertainty. They argue for the 'upgrading' of job designs and point to the benefits arising, such as lower overheads, quicker learning, and improved handling of key variances by operators. In similar vein, Cummings and Blumberg (1987) argue that self-regulating autonomous work-groups provide the optimum form of job design and work organization for high technology environments (see also Hirschhorn, 1986).

It is widely held that such work groups are the appropriate form of job design when tasks are interrelated, when a group can take collective responsibility for performance outcomes, when group members can develop a variety of skills, and when evaluation is system- rather than individually-based. Such conditions are typically created by integrated systems.

This scenario is currently forming the basis of two separate ESPRIT CIM projects. For example in ESPRIT CIM Project 534, Ravden, Clegg and Corbett (1987) have presented two alternative job design scenarios for the operation of the manufacturing system, advocating the second which involves control of the system by the operators. A summary of these choices, along with their anticipated costs and benefits, is presented in Table 1.

Nevertheless, it should be stressed that such approaches are unusual, and most implementations of new technology are not systematic in their consideration of job designs. Companies could usefully ask themselves what jobs are designed for the operators of new systems, and whether or not these will help performance. In the sorts of complex and uncertain manufacturing environment identified earlier, restricting the roles of operators to loading, monitoring and unloading chores may well lead to sub-optimal performance. Are more appropriate and effective choices available based on more responsible roles for humans in the system?

## What organization structures are required?

Less is known regarding the design of organization structures that are appropriate for managing and supporting high tech systems, although there is a view that traditional structures, tall (hierarchical) and wide (differentiated), do not fit well with new technologies (Susman and Chase, 1986). Certainly there is evidence from an extensive literature that organization structures need to change to fit autonomous group working of the kind advocated above. Dale (1986) points to the need for goodness of fit and coherence between elements making up the organizational system. In this view, new technologies will only perform well if other parts of the system are appropriately designed to be consistent with them. For example, changes in technology will require changes in people (e.g. skill requirements), and in systems and procedures (e.g. for recruitment, selection and training, and also for control and scheduling). New organization structures may be required and more subtle cultural adaptations may also follow. Indeed, some believe the principal benefit of new technology lies in its catalytic effect, promoting or indeed forcing changes throughout the rest of the organization (see Clegg and Corbett, 1986).

Child (1987) reviewed the evidence linking production contingencies, new manufacturing technology and organization structure, using a typology similar to that of Ayres and Miller (1983 — see Figure 1). He suggests that the appropriate application of FMS and CNC is for production systems requiring high levels of product variability and relatively small output. For firms in this sector (e.g. jobbing engineering companies), flexible decentralized structures are recommended, although Child takes care to point out that generalized recommendations are not yet possible in this area. Care is needed because of the range of other contingencies that managers must heed when choosing their organizational designs. This is an area where many advocate uniqueness and where relatively little prescriptive design work has been reported. Furthermore, what little empirical work has been done in this area suggests that the link between organization structure and the performance of AMT is complex (see Majchrzak, 1988).

The earlier predictions concerning the manufacturing context in which IT is developed, however, provide some useful pointers. Manufacturing systems may become more complex and interdependent, in part because the technologies themselves become less visible and more difficult to understand. At the same time organizational procedures and systems will become more tightly coupled, with less slack and fewer buffers. There will be increased uncertainty in such systems, for example in the production process itself, and where they interface both with other organizational systems and with the external environment. There are several dangers here, for example that complexity leads to the specialization of skills, that tight coupling causes a loss of flexibility, and that uncertainty creates differentiated functions. These pressures themselves may well lead to problems of understanding and co-ordinated effort.

Nor can it be said that they are influencing current practice on any scale: in most cases companies appear to be working without guiding models.

Companies who are introducing advanced forms of technology could usefully ask themselves whether their organization structures will remain appropriate. If the structures have not changed with the technology, it may be that an opportunity to create a flatter and simpler structure is being missed.

## Appropriating AMT

This part of the paper is concerned with the process of technological change within the firm. Social scientists often advocate participative design and implementation procedures. A range of motives exists for participation, from a democratic and moral commitment, to a desire for people to be and sometimes 'feel' involved, through to pragmatic goals aimed at incorporating as broad a range of valid expertise as possible.

In the UK, managers often justify participation in terms of people ‘feeling’ involved, and it does seem that industrial participation has dropped off most managerial, trade union and political agendas with a speed many would not have predicted during the heady 1970s when worker directors, two-tier boards, workers' councils, and other less formal approaches were widely debated. Participative approaches remain rare, certainly in the UK. There are a number of reasons for this, including widespread perceptions that: (a) potential participants lack expertise, (b) problems and their solutions belong to managers, (c) design problems are difficult enough without adding new forms of complexity, (d) participative approaches are slow, (e) participation increases the potential for conflict and (f) that generally the approach lacks legitimacy.

It should be recognized that major technical change involves problem-solving aspects (How do we actually design a new system that meets current and predicted requirements? What is the optimum system?), educational issues (How do users and others get to learn what can be done and how best to do it? How do specialist designers get to learn and incorporate the expertise of others?), and also political difficulties (How are scarce resources allocated? How are the different interests of people reconciled?). For these reasons, participative procedures are very complex to manage.

There are some examples of participative design. In the UK, Mumford and colleagues at the Manchester Business School probably have most experience of participative design approaches. Mumford has developed a method called 'Effective Technical and Human Implementation of Computer Systems' (ETHICS). The objectives of this approach are to enable users to play a major role in system design, to ensure that new systems are acceptable to users, and to help users to manage their own organizational change (Mumford, 1983, p. 105). But the ETHICS model has usually focused on the selection of a system from a set of existing products, and on fitting the social system around it.

These practical problems regarding participation should be taken seriously. In their study of participation across a range of environments, Clegg and Wall (1984) concluded that it needed to be understood in its environmental and organizational contexts, and that too often it was perceived as a vertical and hierarchical set of problems. They pointed to the significance of the lateral dimension to participation and offered two propositions — one applying to predictable and stable environments, the other to uncertain contexts. Given earlier comments on the anticipated manufacturing context, the latter proposition can usefully be applied to the implementation of IT in such environments.

Organizations functioning in relatively uncertain contexts have a heavy information processing load. Their operation requires continual decision-making and, consequently, they offer much scope for employee participation. The implementation of participation systems in this context will be regarded as highly significant and of considerable interest. However, such organizations are also characterized by high levels of differentiation manifest in structural, political, psychological and behavioural differences across interest groups. These groups have little in common and may find it very difficult to share information and interact in a way that allows for mutual influence and thereby promotes a positive climate in which to make decisions. The principal impediment to participation in this context is thus one of process (Clegg and Wall, 1984, p.440).

This prediction of the difficulties of participation regarding new technology seems well borne out in practice (for example, see Mueller et al., 1986), where problems are compounded by differences in values, for example between those who simply do not believe in the legitimacy or value of participative design exercises, and those who do.

It cannot be said that social scientists have offered much more than advocacy in this area, and certainly little in the way of practical procedures and methodologies for managing technical change. Unfortunately, nor does this paper. Perhaps 'participation' is no longer a useful notion, plagued as it is by fixed positions and well-worn rhetorics. And perhaps it starts from the wrong position since it assumes the problem is one of getting users of technology to participate in the design work done by expert technologists. What we might usefully consider is the idea of 'appropriation', where the users of new systems (including those operating, supervizing, supporting and managing them) are the owners of both the problems and solutions. The task is for users to appropriate the design and implementation of new technologies, with the help of experts. In this perspective, the emphasis is on self-help, self-management, and local knowledge and understanding.

Within companies it is worth asking if there is too much reliance on the technology experts for designing new systems. Are the procedures for managing change appropriate? Do the procedures allow inclusion of the full range of valid expertise? And, more fundamentally, who is the system being developed by and for? Would a more effective system result if its design is appropriated by its users?

## Discussion and conclusions

This paper has argued that many organizations in the UK are not effective at developing and implementing appropriate technologies.

Recalling the earlier propositions from a social science perspective, a complementary set can now be presented. This set is written from the viewpoint of the engineer and general manager who have practical responsibilities for designing, implementing and managing new systems.

1. Relevant social science expertise is highly fragmented. For example, business strategists, ergonomists, industrial sociologists, organizational and occupational psychologists, and experts in human-computer interaction, often have little awareness of one another's problems and perspectives. The disciplines and interests are poorly integrated.

2. Human specialists often have scant understanding of engineering problems. Many engineering courses now have social science components, but how many social science degrees, or indeed training courses for personnel managers, have significant technical and engineering inputs? Engineers have to learn something regarding human and organizational issues to meet their job requirements. The same argument regarding the acquisition of technical competences is not necessarily true of personnel and industrial relations experts.

3. Social scientists and others have failed to define what appropriate technologies are, and indeed to provide criteria and scenarios of what might be aimed for. One conclusion that must be drawn from this paper is that social scientists have offered relatively little in the way of models, checklists, methodologies or tools to help practitioners to solve their problems, and this is especially true with respect to strategic choices over the sorts of technology to be pursued, the allocation of system functions, the choice of appropriate organization designs, and the design of methods and procedures for managing change more appropriately. This discussion does not assume that we should aim to develop rules and methods for mechanistic application. Rather, the goal is to generate ideas and alternatives that will inform opinion, and promote discussion of choices that often pass by default. Social scientists have also failed to provide usable sets of tools that enable managers and others to evaluate whether or not their systems meet their human and organizational requirements.

4. Nor have social scientists been effective at educating and persuading engineers and others (such as personnel and general managers) in their views and methods. Perhaps we have also failed to demonstrate it is worth the effort, partly because of the points above but also financially. Justifications are often negative, i.e. concerned with the avoidance of disasters, rather than on the basis of predicted benefits or of positive opportunities (see Cascio, 1982).

Clearly there are exceptions to each of these claims, and doubtless others would place different emphases, but the general point remains valid: that social scientists have failed to meet (and possibly create) the needs of technologists, engineers and general managers.

Unfortunately, such comments sound negative, over-critical, and potentially alienating (in the sense of inducing feelings of helplessness). For these reasons Corbett, Clegg, Johnson and Ravden (1988) drafted a set of more immediately practical plans which could be used in the short and medium term to overcome the differences and conflicts that undoubtedly exist between designers, users and social scientists working in these areas. These plans arose from the authors' experience of working collaboratively with design engineers in the development of a large and complex high-tech system. They suggest the following actions:

1. Organizations should attempt to open up the design process. If possible end-users (i.e. managers, supervisors and system supporters and operators) should ‘appropriate’ the design — i.e. the system and its design should be owned and controlled by the people responsible for its management and use.

2. Design work should be undertaken within mixed disciplinary teams, comprising managers, engineers, users and experts on the human and organizational issues (for example, people from personnel or applied social scientists).

3. If the new system is introduced using some form of project management, the project leader should be a manager who will be responsible for the management and use of the system when it is operational.

4. The design team should develop specific objectives for the system and specific criteria against which it can be evaluated. Human and organizational issues should be included in these objectives and criteria, and also in the requirements and functional specifications of the system.

5. The design team should develop specific methods, tools, scenarios and decision aids which will help systematically to consider the appropriateness of decisions regarding the extent of automation, the allocation of system functions, and the design of jobs and local organization structures. In each case the team should consider the range of design choices that are available and their anticipated impacts on key criteria.

6. When design sub-groups are created, human and organizational specialists should contribute to technical and financial problem solving and decision making, while engineers should contribute to human issues. The specialists should collaborate actively rather than coming together periodically. If design teams require training inputs, all members should participate.

7. All prototype, demonstrator and final designs should be evaluated using a range of technical, financial, and human and organizational criteria. Explicit measures and evaluation tools should be used, and the results should be fed back to all interested parties.

8. Specific examples of interdependency between design choices and human and organizational outcomes should be sought, so that debates during design can focus on specific issues. Practical problems provide a focal point for integrating efforts.

9. The design team should explicitly research the different approaches to design by market leaders and innovators in their business sector. The team should examine approaches to technical design, but also the human and organizational issues covered here. Are there lessons to learn from leading innovators? Are there sources of expertise, for example through consultancies, universities and polytechnics, that may help to solve problems?

10. Financial assessments should be made of critical outcomes so that people can establish whether investment in these issues has proved worthwhile.

11. The design group should be kept together so that they see through the project to the end, and so that they can experience the practical outcomes of their debates and decisions.

12. Consideration should be given to the ways in which the lessons from such exercises can be generalized, both to other parts of the company and to succeeding generations of technological development.

Before closing, it is worth asking what the notion of appropriateness adds to our understanding of these issues, and whether it offers any advantages over terms such as 'human centred'. These questions cannot yet be answered, but the idea of appropriate technology usefully incorporates notions of contingency and fit, making it clear, for example, that all roads do not lead to CIM by way of FMS. And it does place technological issues in an organizational context. Furthermore, it draws on an existing tradition from development economics and offers some opportunities for integration across different ergonomic, psychological and organizational domains: this can only be healthy. It can also be extended to include other areas; for example, organizations need to consider whether they are designing information and control systems that are suitable both for the users and for the desired methods of working, as well as appropriate software, hardware and interfaces. The notion also raises questions of what is appropriate, for whom and under what circumstances, and these provide a useful agenda for future research and development work. At least three interrelated needs can be identified:

\- for more proactive R&D work so that social scientists and others gain experience of designing and implementing appropriate systems;

\- for the development of practical methods and tools to help people make appropriate choices in the areas identified in this paper;

\- for multi-disciplinary work to overcome fragmentation.

Two closing remarks should also be made. First, none of these views are anti-technology. The pursuit of appropriate technology does not necessarily mean the adoption of low technology (Elkington, 1986). And second, these comments are neither anti-technologist, nor anti-management. The view is that social scientists have let technologists and managers down, and one hope is that these ideas help to identify a useful agenda to overcome these problems.
