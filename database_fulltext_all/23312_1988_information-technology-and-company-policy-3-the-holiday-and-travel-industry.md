# Information Technology and Company Policy: 3. The Holiday and Travel Industry

Henry Holloway, Kobler Unit, Imperial College of Science and Technology

## Introduction

This is the third in a series of papers examining the application of Information Technology (IT). In this series we are interpreting the term IT in a comprehensive way to mean the whole range of computer-based facilities for the acquisition, storage, transmission, retrieval and processing of information. Previous papers have examined newspapers and electronic publishing (Holloway, 1987 a and b).

In complete contrast to the last industrial sector examined — electronic publishing, which relies on IT for its very existence — the holiday industry has no intrinsic need for IT. However, skilful application of IT has played a significant part in the acquisition and retaining of the market share by leading companies. A profile of one of the first holiday companies in the UK to exploit IT successfully, Thomson Holidays, is given after a review of the industry in general.

## The holiday industry and the economy

The holiday industry is a very profitable sector of the economy. 1987 was a record year for the UK with 15,600,000 visitors spending £18 billion, also a record with turnover up 5 per cent on 1986 (Elliot, 1988). However, there is also an outflow of currency as more and more people take holidays abroad. Package holidays for British families are expected to cost £3.6 billion in 1988, up 16 per cent on 1987. The UK Civil Aviation Authority has issued licences to tour operators so that they can offer 14.5 million charter holidays, 2 million more than in 1987. Thomson Holidays remain the largest operator, with approval to offer 3,240,000 holidays.

It is evident that the profitability of the holiday industry is largely dependent on the general strength of the economy and more particularly on the level of disposable income. This is shown clearly in statistics relating to the recession of the 1970s. A measurement of the percentage occupancy of hotels showed a fall from 57 per cent in 1974 to 50 per cent in 1980 (Self, 1981).

The British holiday industry recovered from the recession between 1981 and 1984 and continued to expand so that by 1985 there were 14.6 million visitors (Computer News).

A factor in international tourism is the relative strength of currencies. Traditionally, the USA has supplied a large proportion of overseas visitors to Britain and the number of American visitors has varied according to the relative strength of the dollar and sterling. In view of the strength of the pound, it may appear surprising that 1987 was a record year for the British tourist industry. Reasons for this success lie in the diversification and expansion of the British holiday industry, and of international tourism.

An interesting point is that, in September 1985, the Government department dealing with tourism was moved from the DTI, where it had resided since 1929, to the Department of Employment.

## IT applications

Various types of company have made efforts to apply IT to the holiday and travel industry; transport companies, particularly airlines, tour operators and travel agents.

Airlines in USA began experimenting with computer reservation systems (CRS) in the late 1950s and these were in use by the 1960s. At first, the systems were only used by the airlines internally but there was an upsurge in usage and interest in CRS once these were modified to allow access by external users — travel agents and other airlines. Sabre and Apollo were the first systems and are still the largest. CRSs are very profitable, gaining income from other airlines for each ticket booked through the system and from travel agents through equipment rental. Sabre made a profit of \$107 million in 1987, nearly one quarter of American Airlines' total profit (Packham, 1988).

By the mid 1980s, the USA market was becoming saturated so the operators of American CRSs looked for new markets. Sabre, Apollo and PARS terminals have been used in Europe since 1985. This created a threat to the profitability of European airlines in two ways. First, a CRS usually gives some priority to the system's own airline (this has been a source of bitter legal battles in the USA on the ground of unfair practices). Second, CRSs are a major source of income for the owning airline. The European response was to form a group to run a CRS for all members of the group. The initial intention was to have one system. Unfortunately, differences of opinion on system configuration led to the establishment of two European consortia. Galileo was set up in July 1987 by British Airways, KLM, Swissair and Covia. At the same time, Amadeus was founded by Air France, Iberia, Lufthansa and SAS. These systems require substantial investments. Galileo is estimated to cost £75 million for the first three years and Amadeus around £150 million. Despite the two groups initial differences over choice of system equipment, there are many similarities. Both will use IBM mainframes and IBM operating system TPF (Transaction Processing Facility) designed for high volume online transaction processing (Packham, 1988).

In 1977, online booking of British Airways and several other carriers became available via the Travicom system. When introduced, special terminals were needed, which agents bought or rented (at £2000 per month) (Int. Tourism Quarterly, 1983). Travicom will now be integrated into Galileo (Packham, 1988).

Travicom is targeted at business travel. Tour operators and travel agents have also invested in computerized booking. In the UK videotex has featured prominently in leisure bookings, particularly air charter. Tariff and schedule information has been available on Prestel, British Telecom's videotex service since 1977. One of the first companies to use Prestel for marketing travel was Sealink which helped to generate interest in this activity by giving sets to travel agents. Olympic was the first company to offer booking via private videotex system. The entry of Thomson Holidays, the market leader in package holidays, with its system TOP (developed over 2 years at an estimated cost of £2 million) prompted a surge in the market. By 1983 there were 7,000 terminals in operation on various systems; 1,000 of these were on Travicom (Int. Tourism Quarterly, 1983).

Skytrack, British Telecom's airline reservation system attracted 250 subscribing agents at the same time. Skytrack then cost £200 per quarter standing charge, which included free use of Prestel (ibid).

Thomson Holidays introduction of TOP helped to give the company a competitive edge so that its market share of the overseas package holiday market grew from around 17 per cent in 1983 to 29 per cent in 1987 remaining the largest operator in the UK, selling 3.5 million holidays. Its nearest competitor in size, Intasun, had around 19 per cent of the market in 1987 with 2 million holidays.

There are rewards to be obtained in a market estimated to be worth £2 billion in 1985 and growing at 10 per cent per annum (Harvey, 1986), but there are also hazards. A number of companies have failed to survive in an industrial climate where aggressive marketing by some companies has added to the complexities of dealing with currency variations and fuel price increases. The industry has suffered a series of price wars in recent years and although conditions are more stable at present there is always the possibility of further price cutting.

Thomson Holidays was not the first tour operator to use information technology for booking but is generally regarded as the most successful. Although it has released general details of the TOP system, the company naturally keeps the finer details of the operation secret. Other companies are trying to implement a system to match TOP but have not had the same success. Horizon's second system, Horizon which replaced Vista introduced in 1981 but not popular with travel agents, acts in similar fashion to TOP. Intasun, which has its own approach, is using its third system (Harvey, 1986).

There are sound commercial reasons for the development of TOP and airline CRSs. As Holloway (1985) notes:-

The tourism industry in general is ideally suited for computer technology. It requires a system of registering availability of transport and accommodation at short notice; of making immediate reservations, amendments and cancellations on such facilities; of quoting complex fares and conditions of travel; of rapidly processing documents such as tickets, invoices, vouchers and itineries; and of providing accounting and management information.

Holiday and travel companies have been so successful in developing systems that the UK's Department of Trade and Industry launched project Vanguard, an initiative designed to inform other industries of these systems and to use them as models for future developments (Computer News, 1986).

## Company profile — Thomson Holidays

Thomson Holidays is part of Thomson Travel, a group which also includes Skytours, Portland Holidays, Britannia Airways and Lunn Poly. In turn, Thomson Travel is part of the Canadian-based International Thomson Organization which has world wide interests in publishing, information technology and oil.

Thomson Holidays and Skytours are the largest tour operation in the world and expect to carry 3.5 million holidaymakers in 1988. Thomson Travel was founded in 1966 as the amalgamation of several travel companies and changed its tour operating name to Thomson Holidays in 1972.

Information technology was introduced early in the company's history. In 1976 a system was introduced to streamline sales, reservations and booking administration. Thomson Reservation and Administration Control System (TRACS) took three years to develop at a cost of £3 million. Ten regional sales centres were connected online to a central computer at head office. Travel agents in the areas served by the centres telephoned to find out what was available and make bookings.

A manual system was in use prior to the introduction of TRACS. Details of the holiday — the inventory — were kept on cards. This card system was very difficult to monitor for load factor and security. The occasional holiday could be purloined as the removal of a card was difficult to trace. TRACS made instant inspection possible and the load factor could be ascertained at any moment.

Following the introduction of Prestel, Thomson Holidays began to examine the use of videotex which was gaining acceptance in travel agencies. In 1981 a pilot scheme was set up in the South East of England linking 66 agents in ten towns. Results from these agencies were compared with those of a control group of ten similar towns. In the towns where the videotex booking system was in use it immediately accounted for 35–40 per cent of bookings.

This prompted Thomson Holidays to introduce an interactive videotex booking system nationwide. In October 1982 the Thomson Open-line Programme (TOP) was made available to travel agents. Agency agreements were amended to take account of the new booking procedure which cut out most of the paper work. It is worth noting that Thomson Holidays insisted that all the agents were trained to use the system. There have been many instances where the use of high technology solutions has failed due to lack of training.

The system was an immediate success, accounting initially for 40 per cent of the company's business. This proportion increased by 10 per cent per year until 1986 when it had grown to 90 per cent. In 1987, following an extensive expansion of the system, TOP bookings became mandatory.

The present system is powered by two Amdahl mainframe computers; a 5870, with a rating of 20 MIPS, 40 MB and 32 channels and the newer 5890 which is rated at 40 MIPS, 64 MB and 48 channels. These serve 42 videotex ports, 1500 nodes and 20,000 terminals. These are connected via Public Switched Telephone Network (PSTN) to the Microscope regional network controller which feeds the information to the central computers via British Telecom Kilostream. The flow of information is managed by three Amdahl 4705 front end processors. An outline of the system is shown in Figure 1.

The advantages of the computer-based approach to booking adopted by Thomson Holidays cannot be overestimated. Between 1978 and 1986 business trebled with no increase in staffing levels. The system handles an enormous throughput, as much as 200,000 sales per day. This could not be managed without the data handling technology now in place.

The system allows greater efficiency. For example, the load factor of aircraft is critical to profitability. This has risen over ten years from 90 per cent to 98.7 per cent. Thomson Holidays are hoping to improve on even this high level as it represents 40 to 45,000 empty seats on booking of 3.5 million.

The indirect benefit of the introduction of first TRACS and then TOP is that the travel agencies perception of Thomson Holidays altered. The systems ensured that the accuracy of bookings was good. One result is that holidays are trouble free. This is reflected in an annual MORI survey, sponsored jointly by the tour operators, which has rated Thomson Holidays number one in this respect every year since 1980.

The company's rate of growth is exceptional. In 1985 1.4 million holidays were booked, and in 1986 2.6 million. The system developed by Thomson Holidays has provided the platform for this growth but the use of technology must be married to the business. Summarizing the company's attitude to technology, Colin Palmer, Deputy Managing Director, Systems states that "IT must offer an integrated solution which must relate to business"

Thomson Holidays pre-tax profits for 1987 were down £17 million on its 1986 figure of £43 million when it carried 3.7 million people (Unsworth et al. 1988). ## Conclusion

Various companies have experimented with computer systems to aid completing reservations, airlines being among the first. The more integrated approach with the system actually making the booking did not take off until the advent of videotex. The first videotex system, the much maligned Prestel, featured prominently in the development of booking systems for the travel industry. As with many innovations, users had to be won over. Sealink, one of the first information providers on Prestel, stimulated interest by installing Prestel receivers in agencies.

Computer-based booking systems have now become commonplace among package tour operators. The leading product in this area is Thomson Holidays' TOP, which is much admired and envied by rival companies. Attempts to produce alternative systems with equivalent performance have not been successful, so far. Thomson Holidays' dominance of the package tour market must be attributed, at least in part, to the competitive edge which TOP has provided. Thomson Holidays' productivity, measured in sales per employee, is at least 25 per cent better than its nearest rivals.

The holiday industry is an area in which IT has been used imaginatively by some companies to provide a competitive advantage by processing and analysing bookings. As with other successful uses of IT, success does not stem from a reliance on the product itself, but on a clear vision by management of what the technology should be doing. The advantage of an IT approach compared to conventional manual methods is related, in this case, to accuracy and information on what is available and what the competitor is doing. Rapid processing plays its part in this, of course, but there is a difference in nature in what IT is achieving here when compared with the newspaper industry, for example, where increased speed and throughput in production are the essential reasons for the utilization of IT (cf Holloway, 1987a).

## Biographical notes

Henry Holloway received his first degree, in Physics and Mathematics, from Durham University, and taught in England, Uganda and New Zealand. Returning to the UK, he then gained the degrees of MSc in Cybernetics of London University and PhD from the University of Aston in Birmingham, which followed a period at Pira, the research association for the UK paper and board industry.
