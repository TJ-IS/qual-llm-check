# Transborder Data Flows and Law: Questions not Answers

Paul Marett, Department of Library and Information Studies, Loughborough University

## Introduction

I am not going to begin by saying that data have always been transferred across frontiers. I am going to suggest that the situation which we have today is fundamentally different from anything which has gone before. As an undergraduate many years ago, I attended lectures by an eminent philosopher and I still remember his reminding us that a difference of degree is a real difference. To compare the data transmission network of a modern transnational corporation with the courier service of a fourteenth-century Florentine merchant is totally unhelpful.

## Definitions

The term ‘transborder data flows’ has entered the language (or jargon) and it will be used here in preference to ‘international data transmission’, ‘transnational data flows’ or the recently-coined ‘international information flows’. The French talk about ‘les flux transfrontières de donnees’.

Transborder data flows taken literally could, of course, include postal communication and voice communication by telephone. However, a useful definition for our purposes is:

the movement across national boundaries of electronic or machine-readable data for the purpose of processing, storage or retrieval in computer systems (Kirsch, 1983, p.3).

## A more scientific definition is:

Transborder data flows are units of information coded electronically for processing by one or more digital computers which transfer or process the information in more than one nation-state (Novotny, 1980, pp.143-144).

I do not think that this adds very much. The definitions are, I think, sufficiently narrow to exclude telex and facsimile transmissions. They do not specifically exclude the transfer of data across frontiers on such tangible carriers as discs, tapes or punched cards, but these have a physical form which renders them reasonably easy to control. What we really have in mind are data transmitted over telecommunication networks, whether via cable (private or not) or satellite. Particular characteristics of such data transfer are that:

1. it can be virtually instantaneous;

2. it is not susceptible to normal methods of inspection and control.

Furthermore, the transfer of data takes place across international frontiers and hence is subjected to more than one legal regime.

It is easy enough to produce a reasonably straightforward blanket definition of transborder data flows: it is much more difficult to draft any coherent classification which would clarify the issues when attempting to analyse the legal problems. Several approaches are possible, depending on whether we focus on the content, the function, the users or the network.

Classifying by content we might distinguish between personal and non-personal data, or alternatively between databases and business (or institutional) data.

By function we can see data flows which are concerned solely with communication of the data, and those where the application of the data is the main function and the data are manipulated by, or interact with other data in, the system.

If we classify by users, we may list these specifically — transnational corporations, financial institutions, governments, crime detection agencies, other business users, scholars and scientists, to give some examples. Alternatively we may find it helpful to distinguish inter-company (or institution) data flows from intra-company flows; we may separate closed-user groups, a subset of which may be customers (e.g. of a bank); database users may form a separate category cutting across the others.

If we are concerned with the network, it may be public or private; we can think of the record-carrier companies providing the communication infrastructure by cable or satellite; we can also consider the services provided along the communication route by value-added networks (VANs) offering packet switching, data storage, data processing and so on, or by computer service companies which provide their customers with the hardware and software on their own or leased networks for accounting, technological, scientific or commercial operations.

Transnational corporations are the most important sources of transborder data flows: they communicate constantly across frontiers with their branches and subsidiaries, transmitting and receiving the data necessary for their daily operations. These corporations generate, according to recent estimates (British Invisible Exports Council, 1986, p.18), 70 per cent of transborder data flows, and most of these arise within a small number of companies (which have been particularly articulate about restrictions on transborder data flows). Financial institutions are important as well. The Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT) has around 900 member banks in 39 countries and carries out nearly a third of a million transactions a day (Revell, 1983, p.148). American Express could not function without its international flows of data for credit card transactions. The customers of Hongkongbank can now use the Bank's Hexagon system offering market information, electronic funds transfers, account details, electronic messages and other services, practically worldwide (Far Eastern Economic Review, 1986). Airlines generate some 11,000 million telecommunication messages (voice and digital data) annually: a typical Boeing 747 departure from Zurich to New York may involve over 29,000 messages. SITA (Societe International de Telecommunication Aeronautique) operates the worldwide reservation system (Transnational Data Report IX, 1986).

## Legal issues

Transborder data flows take place in a world in which, to a great extent, free flows of information are regarded as a good thing. The desirability of freedom of information – with its corollary, free flows of information – is, like democracy or motherhood, never really questioned. Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights provides for freedom of opinion and expression, and presupposes freedom of information. The founding charter of UNESCO specifically requires the Organization to promote free flows of information. Both the OECD Guidelines and the Council of Europe Convention on data protection (see below) recognize as a fundamental value the free flow of information.

## Privacy and data protection

Of all legal concerns relating to transborder data flows, the one which has received most public attention has been the protection of individual privacy. In this country the Data Protection Act of 1984 is very much in our minds at present. I would remind the reader that the Act concerns personal data relating to an identifiable living individual. It lays down principles of fairness, accuracy, relevance to the specified purposes for which the data are held, non-disclosure and subject access. The Act applies only to data held on computer. There has been some criticism of the fact that data held in manual systems are not covered by the Act. Such criticism is misguided: I shall stress frequently the point that the computer has introduced a totally new dimension in data handling and transmission which demands a totally different legal approach.

Adoption of the Data Protection Act has enabled the United Kingdom to accede to the Council of Europe Convention for the protection of individuals with regard to automatic processing of personal data. The Convention is the first effective international instrument for the legal regulation of transborder data flows, although the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has established non-mandatory 'Guidelines' closely similar to the principles of the Convention. The Convention does not seek to establish a transnational law: each signatory state agrees to apply the principles of the Convention in its own national law. This means that transborder flows of personal data between signatory states will be subject to the same level of protection in the country of destination as in the country of origin.

In the United Kingdom the Data Protection Registrar has certain powers of control over transborder data flows. These are found in s.12 of the Act. The Registrar has power to issue a transfer prohibition notice to prevent a data user from transferring personal data to a non-Convention country, either directly or via a Convention country, when a contravention of the data protection principles is likely. The Registrar may also, under certain circumstances, prohibit transfer of more intimate personal data (race, politics, religion, health, sexual life or criminal record) even to a Convention country.

One effect of this is that the transfer of data to a safe 'data haven' is in theory prevented. I say 'in theory', for one large question hangs over these provisions. The registration form completed by data users requires particulars of countries to which they may have occasion to transfer data. Beyond that, the Registrar has to take positive steps to prohibit transfer after being satisfied that contravention is likely. The question is, by what mechanism will the Registrar monitor data flows? Section 12 was to come fully into effect on 11 November 1987 so we shall have to wait a while before we see how effective it is.

France and West Germany have similar provisions to ours but other countries have approached the problem in different ways. Norway, Sweden and Denmark have adopted systems of prior licensing of data transfers with varying degrees of detailed regulation (Hafli, 1980). Sweden has the longest experience of data protection legislation, having passed a law in 1973, but the first few years of operation produced only a small number of disputed cases on the export of data. An application by Siemens, the German multinational company, to hold employee data in their central system abroad was turned down on grounds of the undesirability of having a large part of the population registered abroad (ibid). This argument illustrates a fear expressed by some advocates of the greatest freedom of flow of information that legislation intended to protect individual privacy may well be misused by governments for other restrictive purposes (Buss, 1984). Incidentally, the Swedish law prevents the export of manually-held data for electronic processing abroad, a loophole which the United Kingdom Act seems to leave unplugged.

In a few countries data protection laws extend to legal persons (i.e. companies and other corporate bodics) as well as to private individuals. The subject access provisions would cause difficulties: imagine a company being obliged to reveal its computer file on a rival's pricing and marketing strategies to that rival.

## National sovereignty

A most important factor which has to be borne in mind is the tremendous dominance of the United States of America in the data processing and telecommunications fields. In the manufacture and supply of computer and telecommunications equipment a few large American companies take a disproportionate share of the market; satellite technology is dominated by the Americans (Hamclink, 1984, Chapter 2). Perhaps even more important is US political and economic dominance. The widespread tentacles of the great American transnational corporations (ibid, Chapter 4) and the myth – or truth – of American government intelligence-gathering organizations have been important factors in the assertion by other states of national sovereignty over information flows.

Information is power, and in times of conflict or crisis a country could find itself cut off from its own vital national information. Thus there are already potential conflicts between the developed and the Third World countries, and between the US and the rest of the world. There is concern about the 'data drain' where important social and economic data are processed and stored abroad; there was, for example, much soul-searching in France when US-based economic modelling programs were fed with French data for projections of the French economy for French government use. Alain Madec, the Chairman of the French Government Commission on Transborder Data Flows, has pointed out that the international exchange of information concerns an asset of value which can generate power and wealth for the holder, which is susceptible to many abuses and which is difficult for a state to control:

transborder data flows, even more than the trade in products, means the decay of the nation state (cited by Kirsch, 1983, p.48).

Concerns for national security, for the protection of home industry, and not least for the cultural and ideological integrity of the nation, are facets of the problems of sovereignty vis a vis transborder data flows. Cultural integrity is threatened by the Americanization of society and the dominance of the Anglo-American language: the first principle enunciated by President Mitterand of France in proposing a World Communications Charter was 'affirmed respect for the diversity of languages' (Garzon Clariana, 1985, p.104). Ideological integrity has been a stumbling block in any attempt to secure accord on freedom of information between the Western powers and the Communist bloc.

This is the background against which legal regulation of transborder data flows for reasons of national sovereignty will take place. It is highly probable that such regulation will increase. Regulation may be indirect, by requiring local procurement of telecommunication and computer equipment, by discriminatory pricing for leases of network facilities from the PTT (usually a government monopoly), or potentially by taxation of information flows. It may be direct, by a legal requirement that data processing takes place locally, either generally or for specific types of data.

I have already mentioned that states may use privacy and data protection laws to provide a legal sanction for restricting data flows in the interest of national sovereignty rather than of personal privacy. In spite of the commitment to free flows of information in both the Council of Europe Convention on data protection and the OECD Guidelines, both accept in slightly different terms derogation from the principles of data protection for the safeguarding of sovereignty, security or public order (Convention, Articles 9(2)(a) and 16(c); Guidelines, Article 4). If privacy protection is extended (as it is in some countries) to legal persons as well as to individuals, the possibilities for national control of transborder data flows are considerable. Moreover, a government may forbid personal data transfers to countries which do not have equivalent protective legislation: in particular, US privacy laws are generally not deemed equivalent (Buss, 1984). Indeed, one writer (Kuitenbrouwer, 1986) sees more widely a subtle but steady erosion of traditional concepts of freedom of information and communication by the informatics revolution: the pretended 'technical' nature of new media regulation may be a disguised attempt to control content.

The country which has taken the strongest steps so far to regulate transborder data flows is Brazil (Kirsch, 1983, pp.57-61; Hamelink, 1984, p.96; British Invisible Exports Council, 1986, p.22). Apart from severe restrictions on purchase of foreign computer and telecommunication equipment, positive controls have been imposed on transborder data flows since 1978. A government body, the Special Secretariat of Informatics, is charged with regulating data flows in accord with the national policy to develop national databanks and data processing facilities. Licences for the transborder transfer of data are granted for a maximum of three years, and in the case of so-called 'commercial' data flows (i.e. where the data or data processing facilities are purchased at a price) are very rarely granted. In general it is required that data be processed locally unless there are strong reasons for allowing transfer of data abroad.

## Economic and fiscal issues

I admit that the distinction between national sovereignty and economic and fiscal issues as areas of justification for legal restrictions on transborder data flows is rather artificial. The policies of the Brazilian government, for instance, derive from factors both of sovereignty and of economics. No one doubts that an indigenous information industry is of considerable importance to a national economy. For the developing countries the very real prospect that industrial jobs will be sited where labour is cheap, while the managerial decisions are taken in far distant countries where the higher expertise necessary is located at the terminals of the international data communication system, is very real.

The question for the world's information industries, then, is not whether that industry is increasingly important - it is - but rather to what extent nations may lawful(ly) inhibit or control the international commerce in information to effect a build-up of their own local industries (Hardy, 1983, p.262).

Much hinges on whether information is regarded as a product or a service: if the latter, then it falls outside the scope of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, and discriminatory laws by signatory states are in order.

Information is increasingly regarded as a marketable commodity, yet, so far, transborder data flows have escaped the direct imposition of tariffs. Indirectly there may be some taxation of computer services (e.g. through VAT on services, or Customs duties on hardware, or on the physical carrier of data, tape or disc). Indeed, volume-sensitive pricing by government monopoly PTTs for leased lines or networks is very near to taxation on data flows (Bing, Forsberg and Nygaard, 1983, pp.105-106; Kirsch, 1983, pp.48-49). In France, a proposal that imported computer software should be taxed has led to fears that taxation of information flows would follow (British Invisible Exports Council, 1986, p.23; Kirsch, 1983, pp.48-49). The possibility of taxation of data flows has aroused predictably strong response from the business community, for example from the International Airline Transport Association whose members pass more than 5,000 million messages a year over telecommunications networks (Transnational Data Report, 1986).

West Germany imposes restrictions on trans-border data flows by requiring that a significant proportion of data processing be performed locally. This seems to be part of a more general restrictive policy applied indirectly to favour local equipment suppliers and data users (British Invisible Exports Council, 1986, p.23). Canada, with a common frontier and language with the United States, feels itself particularly vulnerable. The Clyne Report of 1979 dealt with the need to avoid reliance on US information resources. The Banking Act of 1980 requires banks doing business in Canada to process customer records inside the country and gives the Minister of Finance certain other powers of control (Kirsch, 1983, p.51; Buss, 1984). The implications of data regarding the finances of Canadian citizens being available to business, and other, circles in the United States are obvious. There is also concern at the loss of jobs in the information industries resulting from the large-scale processing of data abroad.

## Private and commercial law

If issues of privacy and national sovereignty have so far attracted most attention, the emergence of legal issues relating to transborder data flows will be most significant in the spheres of commercial and private law in the future. If we have a few answers in the former fields, in the application of private and commercial law to transborder data flows we can still do little but ask questions.

Let us look first at copyright. The rules of intellectual property do not protect information as such. Copyright protects information as it is represented by the text. Use of a major databank will involve large numbers of transactions by large numbers of individuals in many countries accessing it. The information is displayed briefly; there will be no record of the information extracted; users may store it in their own computers where it is in effect invisible; they can then retransmit it to perhaps a thousand different locations. How can traditional ideas of copyright operate here? What happens if the owners or licensees of copyright offer their material internationally and it is retrieved in a country where other licences hold exclusive rights? (Coditel decision of the International Court of Justice, cited by Kirby, 1983, p.39.) In the UK the Copyright (Computer Software) Amendment Act of 1985 extends copyright protection to material stored in a computer. But the provision is placed within the context of the Copyright Act passed 30 years ago when a 'literary work' could cross frontiers only in a discrete tangible form (unless slowly read out across telephone or radio links). The situation has been expressed thus:

Application of established legal concepts of copyright to computer communications in the same way as they have been applied to print media will create more problems than it will solve (Pool and Solomon, 1980, p.113).

As far as I know there has not yet been any litigation on international libel by computer. The prospect, however, is not just fanciful and the consequences could be serious. A transient message on a computer terminal screen could do untold harm to the business or personal reputation of an international figure. The legal problems arising would be tortuous. UK law makes a distinction between written and oral defamation: is a computer screen display libel or slander?

So far, litigation on errors or omissions in transmission of data has been rare but it is certain to develop. The difficulties of assigning liability for errors are compounded by the many 'hands' through which data may pass in the course of an international transmission. An apparently simple transmission may involve a sender and his equipment, a record carrier, perhaps leasing the carrier medium from an international cable or satellite network, a value-added network operator, and the receiving party and his equipment. The few cases so far have involved banks and electronic funds transfers (e.g. EVRA Corporation v Swiss Bank Corporation, details and reference in Kirsch, 1983, p.82). When the transmission takes place in a contractual context, information providers or senders will doubtless try to safeguard themselves by disclaiming liability for failures of the suppliers of equipment, the carriers and any other intermediaries.

Over something like 13 centuries the courts have built up rules and expertise regarding the validation and authentication of commercial documents of all kinds. Indeed the science of palaeography originated in the law courts and not in the scholar's study. The replacement of parchment or paper — sealed, signed or notarially attested — by electronic data flows is going to raise a large number of legal questions (Kirsch, 1983, pp.78-83; Bing, Forsberg and Nygaard, 1983, pp.106-109). We are already familiar with electronic funds transfer (EFT), airline bookings and credit card transactions, all performed by electronic data flows across national frontiers. Already the process has begun replacing the documentation in international trade transactions (bills of lading, for example) by electronic data transfers. The international contract evidenced only on the parties' computers, with no paper document, will doubtless become general in the next couple of decades (perhaps with each party receiving the text in his own language, automatically translated en route).

Unlike paper documentation, electronic data transfers will leave no physical record. The most obvious legal question concerns authentication of a computer message. This is a question where law and technology impinge: can technology produce authentication devices which the courts will find acceptable? So far, technology has not provided anything for use outside a very secure closed user group better than the plastic card plus personal identification number. The public key encryption system where a message can be encrypted only by the holder of a secure code thus providing authentication — though it can be deciphered by the use of a second code which need not be secure — seems promising if the courts can be convinced of the security of the encryption code.

Questions of evidence of computer records and transactions have already been faced in national law (in the UK in s.5 of the Civil Evidence Act 1968 and s.69 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984). The questions which may arise in international transactions are frightening. Who, for example, can give evidence that the system was functioning properly when the data may have been handled by half a dozen or more intermediaries en route?

I have already mentioned the problems of allocating liability for defects or omissions. With paper documentation the point where the fault occurred can usually be identified and the courts have built up rules for allocating responsibility. Now a whole new set of legal and business rules will have to develop and there is as yet no clear indication how the problems will be approached.

Not only the allocation of liability but the locus of liability and the applicable law will involve new rules. The question was put by a witness before a United States Senate Committee in 1981 (Kirby, 1983, p33):

When an electronic message is generated in country A, switched in country B and C, transits country E, F, G and H, processed in country I and J, stored in country K and involves entities residing in or operating in yet other countries, it is debatable whether existing choice of law and conflict of law doctrines are adequate. What law applies to data processing carried out by computer aboard a synchronous orbit satellite?

The answers, at the present, are just not there. Where there is a contract it may well specify the applicable law, but what force does a contract between the originator of an electronic transmission and the ultimate recipient have when the fault lies with one of the intermediaries who effect the transmission? Once again, a whole new set of legal rules and principles will have to develop, and will have to be accepted internationally.

## Criminal law

I have not touched on the many aspects of computer crime, the fraudulent movement of funds — whether by thieves obtaining unlawful access to a system or by criminals 'laundering' money by international transfers — or the theft, or criminal misuse of, information. Difficult to control in a national context, these activities are many times more so on the international scene. We can expect the development of an international, or at least transnational, criminal law in this field. One simple question, with no simple answer: is electronic data 'property' which may be stolen? A United States court answered in the negative in the Seidlitz case: the accused was acquitted of transporting stolen property across an interstate line (Bing, Forsberg and Nygaard, 1983, p.72). The problems of international control of illicit interference with a computer are illustrated in microcosm by the domestic situation in the USA. Accessing a computer without permission is illegal in some states but not in others. How then can, say, the Arizona prosecutor deal with a resident of another state who accessed an Arizona computer over long-distance telephone lines? The Federal Wiretap Act of 1968 covers only the tapping of oral communications (Kutten, 1985, p.7).

## Conclusion

There are no quick and simple answers to the legal questions arising from transborder data flows. Attempts to create an international legal consensus (which is still a long way from a code of international laws) will be hampered, first by the differences in national legal traditions and approaches, and second by the difficulty of achieving an integrated approach in an area which cuts across the various specialisms of legal experts who do not always understand each other's field. Differences of opinion on an international legal framework are often ascribed to ulterior motives or pigheadedness when they may really be the result of different national legal traditions (Ploman, 1982, p.551). There is at present no accepted coherent body of information law, at either national or international level. Approaches which were appropriate to the technology of the mid-century cannot be distorted or deformed to cope with the regulation of transborder data flows in the 1990s. The most fundamental presuppositions will have to be re-examined. The Declaration of Mexico of 1981, representing and reflecting the priorities of some 20 developing countries says:

the right to information, such as is recognized by the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and international treaties, has acquired, due to technological evolution, a scope which is qualitatively and quantitatively different from that which prevailed when they were adopted. The concept of the 'rights to information' needs to be reinterpreted in the light of changes due to informatics (cited by Garzon Clariana, 1985, p.104).

The author of the IIC report makes the following point with regard to a particular aspect of the problem:

In essence, neither individual nations nor the international community have been able thus far to adapt intellectual property laws to the different and demanding requirements of the information age (Kirsch, 1983, p.33).

I suggest that the questions of transborder data flows and law will demand totally new legal answers. The concepts which have slowly evolved over centuries of a paper-based information order will prove incapable of adaptation to the totally new situation of large-scale instantaneous data transfer in a form which leaves no physical record. We can expect the growth of a new discipline of informatics law, cutting across the current boundaries of tort, contract, intellectual property, negotiable instruments and so on, and even drawing some of its ideas from outside the traditional bounds of legal thinking. First of all it is necessary to view the situation in its totality. Novotny (1980, p.142) says:

The numerous national laws, international initiatives, reports from conferences, and the like, have generated much raw material but very little analysis.

Most attention so far has been focused on major policy matters of sovereignty and privacy but these are, in a way, side issues. Transborder data flows are concerned primarily with international commerce and finance and international intellectual property in its broadest sense: sovereignty and privacy issues are political aspects of more fundamental legal matters. It is not possible to halt all transborder data flows while the lawyers work out ideal global solutions. As always, pressing problems will demand hasty answers but it is important that the total view of a new approach to informatics law should not be submerged in ad hoc decisions: the trees must not be allowed to obscure the wood.
