# The Importance of Organizational Considerations for the Implementation of Information Technology

P. J. Lunt and I. Barclay, Department of Industrial Studies, University of Liverpool

Abstract: Many companies introducing information technology, in any form, can experience problems because the organizational aspects of introducing such technology have not received their due consideration.

The paper will firstly review the various aspects to be considered, and then discusses the results of research work undertaken in companies in the UK and Japan, to compare and contrast the differing approaches of these two countries to technology implementation.

## Introduction

Before starting to investigate the ‘organizational aspects’ of information technology, it is advisable to define what is meant by ‘information technology’. Definitions that usually spring to mind include data processing, office automation, etc. However, information technology can encompass a much wider field than these areas. One general definition is given below:

Information technology is that collection of machinery which enhances communication between people, between people and processes, between organizations and between nations. $^{1}$

If the subject is considered in the above context, it can be seen that the organizational implications and aspects of such technology can be far reaching, and as such deserve full consideration, together with the ‘technical’ and ‘financial’ factors.

## This paper is in three parts:

I. A discussion of the organizational aspects of the different stages of introducing information technology into a company, and how these need to be integrated with other relevant factors and activities.

II. An overview of the authors' research activities investigating the subject of the process of managing the introduction of new technology.

III. Suggestions as to lessons that can be learnt from the integration of the knowledge we have, and the methodologies used by companies which have been proved to be successful.

## The process of introducing new information Technology

The process of introducing any new technology can be simply outlined in three stages: the appraisal process; the implementation of the technology into current systems; and the operation of the technology (and the resulting effects on the company management and organization). We will look at each of these stages in turn.

## The appraisal process

Often, the initial stages of an appraisal system are conducted as separate entities, usually by separate departments. The basic aspects of an appraisal system can be categorized as financial, technological, and managerial, all of which need to be regarded both separately and in relation to each other. Traditionally in the UK, there has been an emphasis on appraisal systems concentrating on the 'financial' and 'technical' aspects which are the relatively short-term factors. This view was reflected in a survey by the authors to identify the key factors used in the conventional appraisal of advanced manufacturing technology. $^{2}$

In our work, senior managers and selected academics who were conducting research in this field were interviewed, and a list of key factors conventionally given as the reason for the introduction of advanced manufacturing technology was devised. The list is given below (not in order of priority):

★ product performance and/or quality

★ current technology and systems

★ economics, both external and internal

★ product type and quality

★ corporate strategy

★ fear of 'being left out'

★ desire to move forward

★ technical capability of employees.

This survey confirmed the authors' view that conventional systems are mainly based on short-term economic and technical evaluation of the technology, and tend not to consider the subsequent implementation, integration and operation of the new technology.

## Finance

Traditionally, this subject was evaluated using one of the many established financial techniques, such as payback, or discounted cash flow. However, there is a disadvantage in using these accounting methods for the introduction of today's advanced technologies, as they do not allow scope for including some of the ‘intangible’ benefits.

To reduce this problem, there are computer packages available now which, although they are based on traditional discounted cash flow techniques, also allow for some of the less 'tangible' factors to be included. An example of this is the 'IVAN' package developed by researchers at UMIST, $^{4}$ which includes an evaluation of the 'intangible' aspects of the appraisal, for example extra revenue that may result from a reduction in lead times. This necessitates the involvement of necessitates the involvement of engineers and operational managers in the financial decision-making process, and not just accountants.

This shows a reflection of one of the organizational aspects of introducing any technology, i.e. the need to integrate functional activities. Any newly introduced technology, especially information technology, will probably impact on several departments, and it is important for all the people who will be associated with the new technology to be familiar with it, and be assured that it will satisfy the needs of their respective functions as well as the whole company.

## Technology

Traditionally, when considering the technology to be introduced, the factors included in the appraisal were confined to the current and future needs of the departments involved, and the system available that best suited these requirements was identified and introduced into the company.

Again, due to the advent of advanced technologies and the need for companies to gain a competitive advantage, additional factors need to be considered. Rather than purchasing the best suited ‘available’ system, companies should be able to design a system that will exactly suit their present and future requirements, not only in terms of functions to be performed, but also taking into account the ‘technological affinity’ of the company employees.

The question needs to be raised as to whether or not the employees already possess the necessary technological skills to be able to design and operate the new technology efficiently and effectively. If not, can they be trained?; does the system designed or selected need to be adapted to suit the capabilities of the employees, or new employees introduced?

There have been many examples of companies experiencing problems due to the introduction of high technology systems which were expected to solve the majority of current problems. $^{5}$ These range from systems that are left unused because the employees are not sufficiently trained to operate them, to systems being used inefficiently. This problem is also increased if company employees feel threatened by the new technology, for example, the fear of job losses resulting from new equipment ‘taking over’ their jobs, especially for middle management. $^{6}$

## Management

There are many ‘managerial’ factors to be considered at the appraisal stage, including:

★ What training is required?

★ Will any restructuring by necessary?

★ How will the system be integrated into any current systems in operation?

★ What is the technological capability and affinity of the managers?

However, the major change in this area is the requirement to have managers that are ‘comfortable’ with engineering and technology concepts, i.e. an ‘engineering’ manager. The role of technology management has changed in function over the years, and these changes also need to be appreciated. The main change is due to the ‘cross functional’ nature of new technology, resulting in a change for technology managers from ‘function’ to ‘project’ managers.

For the introduction of information technology, significant problems can occur, particularly if such technology is being introduced for the first time. Information technology can benefit many organizations to some degree, and so extra care must be taken to ensure that the ‘correct’ system for a particular company is introduced. Due to the large number of ‘ready-made’ systems available, care must also be taken that a system that is too complex is not introduced.

Many writers have looked at the changing nature of management requirements. Parkinson and Avlonitis $^{7}$ summed up the requirements of today's 'engineering' managers as:

★ technically orientated and informed ★ having good internal and external communication

★ giving greater consideration to training policy

★ giving greater consideration to long-term strategy

★ taking higher levels of risk.

As well as having the required personal qualities, there must exist an organizational culture/climate that is receptive to change and to the introduction of new technology.

Companies may not automatically ‘possess’ this suitable environment. To use a phrase of the Department of Employment’s Work Research Unit, the ‘appropriate climate for change’ needs to be created within the company for the introduction of any new technology to have the greatest chance of success. Several authors have looked at this problem. One of the most comprehensive coverages of this cultural aspect was established by Gee and Tyler, $^{8}$ who developed the following list of guidelines from their comprehensive research activities:

★ establish corporate objectives

★ ensure all members understand all objectives

relevant to them

★ signal management expectations clearly

★ create a climate for innovation to achieve the management objectives

★ one person should be the focal point for responsibility

★ keep initial organization to a minimum

★ allow organizational growth only as needs require \* minimum interfaces between organizational levels and units

★ short communication lines

★ encourage informal discussions

★ reward success and understand failure

★ innovation means change: people, not structure, are the key.

## The implementation process

Given that the appraisal process has been conducted correctly and in sufficient depth, this process should follow smoothly. This then becomes a programme management exercise, and problems should be of a minor operational nature, as would be expected in any such programme. There should not be major dislocation problems as these should have been thought through and pre-empted.

## The operation of the technology

Although it is not practically possible to foresee all problems, the use of a comprehensive appraisal system can reduce or eliminate the majority of them. This fact can be seen more clearly when we discuss the different appraisal systems used in the U.K. and in Japan, and the effects of both systems.

## The research work

Here, a general description of the research methodologies used will be given, followed by some of the results relevant to the subject of this paper.

The area to be investigated was defined as ‘an investigation of the appraisal methods used to assess the evaluation, introduction and operation of new technology; in particular, the new management systems, knowledge and capabilities required to ensure that the newly installed technology operated to its full potential’. Based on the previous discussion, and in conjunction with a comprehensive literature review, the following set of hypotheses were defined:

1. In general, the decision to invest in new technology is made on an economic/technical basis.

2. Most companies pay little attention to the impact the new technology will have on the area of people and management.

3. For maximum success, evaluation, implementation and operational aspects should be included from the outset.

4. Methodologies can be developed that allow full consideration of all factors in an appraisal system.

Having defined the hypotheses, a research methodology was defined to evaluate and test each one.

One country in particular possesses an outstanding record in the field of manufacturing excellence — Japan. It was decided to investigate Japan's industry for the purposes of contrast and comparison to UK industry.

The following programme of work was developed: stage 1 — questionnaire survey of UK and Japanese companies; stage 2 — structured interviews with UK and Japanese companies; stage 3 — case studies; stage 4 — implementation of ideas to develop practical strategies to be used by companies.

The figures regarding the area of training and retraining again reflect the fact that the Japanese do not anticipate as many problems, and do not experience as many because they already have comprehensive training programmes in existence. ## Practical Implications

The Japanese have a long and comprehensive decision-making process for the introduction of new technology, which on average takes one year to complete. A separate appraisal process in undertaken for each new investment. Although this may be regarded as too long, resulting in missed opportunities, the overall effect does seem to be faster and smoother implementation, resulting in better productivity.

One of the advantages of this system is the attention given to the company workers during this process. Typically, the proposal for a new piece of technology will come from a member of middle management/shop foreman, who will construct the lengthy appraisal document. The document will contain detailed ‘financial’ information, details of the technology and the respective suppliers, and also full details on particular training programmes for respective people, with details of exactly who will be involved with the various stages of the project, and at what times.

The completed comprehensive document is then passed down to the shop floor for review by the workers who will be involved with the new technology. When all the workers are satisfied with the proposal (and have made any alterations if required), the document is then passed up to the next level of shop floor management for review. This process continues until the document has been agreed upon by everyone in the company who will be associated with the new technology. The net result is smoother and faster implementation and operation processes.

The importance of the early involvement of the workforce is now being recognized, as shown by replies from several senior managers who had just introduced a major information system. The comments from such managers to the question, 'What lessons have you learnt from the introduction of the new technology?' included:

'Operators respond well to new technology if they have had direct involvement in its introduction.' 'Financial objectives are relatively easy to set; technical issues can be resolved with a practical, careful approach; people/attitudes/perceptions create problems and constrain the rate of change.'

The same question was answered by a senior manager from a major Japanese company:

'It is necessary to understand the technology before introducing it; the differences in effects depend on how well you train the operators.'

These views were representative of the comments from most of the companies answering this question.

Another important aspect of the Japanese approach was shown by another reply from a Japanese manager: 'Projects which are arranged simply are feasible.' The approach taken is to use a step-by-step approach, which is simple to operate, and also allows time for the company to follow the stages of the implementation.

One of the main points to be understood is not just the fact that the Japanese have this comprehensive appraisal system, but the thinking and organization within the Japanese companies that has allowed this process to be developed. The fact that the Japanese do regard their workforce as a valuable asset is a vital factor for their success. The appraisal process has partly evolved because of the emphasis on the workforce, and their needs, not just their needs as perceived by managers. It is also for this reason that the Japanese have comprehensive training programmes within the companies, programmes which not only include technical courses, but also management and ‘self-enlightenment’ courses which continue throughout their career development. As one Japanese manager summarized, ‘People need to develop for the technology to be operated efficiently.’

To conclude, one of the main factors contributing to Japanese industrial success is due to their comprehensive appraisal system with its related benefits, both direct and indirect, but we would suggest that the real success in this area is due to the reasoning behind the appraisal system.
