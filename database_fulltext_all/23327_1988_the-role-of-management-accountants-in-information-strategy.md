# The Role of Management Accountants in Information Strategy

G Roland Kaye, School of Information Systems, University of East Anglia

Abstract: This paper is concerned with the emerging role of management accountants in the formulation, development and implementation of information strategies within organizations. The paper is both empirical in that it is based on survey data and theoretical as it postulates future developments.

The essence of the argument is that management accountants have adapted to information technology and have been active in exploiting data processing systems since their inception. This experience and knowledge of the technology alongside their managerial abilities form powerful tools to enable their participation in developing information strategies. The development of an information strategy enables the matching of technology, information content and managerial activity to achieving the overall business strategy in the face of uncertainty. This clearly follows the proposal of accounting process to manage environmental uncertainty reflecting Lorange and Scott-Morton (1974), Hedberg and Jonsson (1978), Hedberg et al. (1976) and Earl (1983).

## Introduction

This paper is concerned with the emerging role of management accountants in the formulation, development and implementation of information strategies within organizations. The paper is both empirical in that it is based on survey data and theoretical as it postulates future developments. The paper summarises the studies undertaken by Collier (1984), Kaye and Dixon (1987), Dixon (1988) and Collier and Kaye (1987), which were undertaken for the Chartered Institute of Management Accountants (CIMA).

This report draws on all these studies to try and plot not only how management accountants have been affected by information technology (IT), but also how they have taken part in the management of the introduction of information technology, and thereby developed a role in formulating the information strategy inside the company. This change in role for management accountants towards the management of change and the strategic exploitation of information technology offers new opportunities for management accountants.

## Accountants adapt

In the early 1980s UK Institutes of Accounting became concerned about the impact of information technology on the work of accountants. The professional bodies commissioned studies to be undertaken into the impact, four of which have been published by the respective institutes with some publicity and with claims that the reports show accountants adapting to the technology that will become commonplace in the 1990s. These reports are:

Bhaskar and Williams: The Impact of Microprocessors on the Small Accounting Practice (Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales (ICAEW) 1984).

Collier: The Impact of Information Technology on the Management Accountant (CIMA, 1984).

Carr: Information Technology and the Accountant (Chartered Association of Certified Accountants) (CACA) 1985).

Coopers and Lybrand: The Chartered Accountant in the Information Technology Age (ICAEW, 1985).

A comparison of these reports has been undertaken and is summarized in Kaye (1986).

Like most results reviewing the adoption of information technology, the studies show varying degrees of adaptation and various attitudes to the impact of information technology, and these results are not inconsistent. In order to provide an external comparison with the accounting profession, the recently published Kearney study (1985) provides a study of managements' adaptation to IT. In this study parallels were drawn between adaptation attitudes and business success with a classification into leaders and laggers in industrial performance.

This approach was not followed in any of these studies, but one may expect to find similar positions existing in the accountancy profession with early adaptors and late maturers to the technology. In the case of Carr the organizational studies precluded such an evaluations, while Collier did not attempt to measure it. Bhaskar and Williams tended to assume it. Perhaps these studies implied this hypothesis, but did not attempt to evaluate it. These static studies miss the possibility of different rates of adaptation and consequently the individuals and organizations will be at different positions on the IT lifecycle. This may result from the studies' over-concentration on the accountant rather than the relationship between the accountant and his environment.

## Categorization of accountants

The traditional view of accounting is usually simplified into the following categories:

— accountants in practice

— accountants in industry.

This can be viewed as an alarming simplification as accountants in practice provide a variety of services ranging from book-keeping and taxation to consultancy and brokerage, whilst their industrial counterparts are working for a spectrum of organizations — small and large, public and private, manufacturing and service. In addition, the industrial accountant may be undertaking work that a professional counterpart in practice can recognize as accounting or alternatively can be undertaking activities of a managerial nature which seem to have little relationship to the normal definitions of accounting. These conflicting views represent a starting point from which an explanation will be attempted as to how the activities of accountants will change as a result of adaptation to IT.

The American Association of Accountants defined the accounting objectives as:

‘to provide information for the following purposes:

i. to make decisions concerning the use of limited resources, including identification of crucial decision areas, and determination of objectives and goals,

ii. to effectively direct and control an organization's human and material resources,

iii. to maintain and report on the custodianship of resources,

iv. to facilitate social functions and control.' (Source: A Statement of Basic Accounting Theory 1966)

The American Institute of Certified Public Accountants, in APB statement 4, provides a contrast with the above view in that it states that ‘accounting is a service function’. This view of accounting as a service function is reflected in the definitions of accounting given by the UK institutions and strongly reflects the auditing and stewardship functions associated with the accountant in professional practice. These definitions fail to reflect the role of the accountant, the expectations of the users of accounting information and the associated management perceptions.

As the studies were based around the respective professional institutes, they have perhaps predictably produced different classifications of accountants, as follows:

Collier (CIMA): The accountant as a manager-employee working within organizations to provide information and advice for planning, control and decision making.

Carr (CACA): The accountant as a professional general practitioner worker in a mixture of locations providing accounting services.

Bhaskar and Williams (ICAEW): The accountant engaged in auditing, taxation and provision of accounting and consultancy skills to business.

Coopers and Lybrand (ICAEW): The accountant as a professional employed in practice or industry.

These differing classifications, which the marketer may describe as product differentiation, may be expected to generate the same conclusions if they are, in fact, classifying the same ‘product’. If, however, they are classifications or different ‘products’ and their markets, then it is reasonable to expect different conclusions. As discussed previously, the classification may be polarized into industry and practice, which have significantly different structures and relationships with their environment as shown in Figure 1. The findings for industry and practice accountants should not be compared directly as their role is significantly different in each situation.

We would suggest that, because of these different classifications of accountants we have different behaviour associated with them. First, accountants in professional practice have as their reference group services provided by fellow practitioners. They are not under pressure from their users in the same way that the employee-accountant is. They only discover their failure to satisfy users when those users make their demands elsewhere. On this basis we would suggest that the professional practice is generally much slower in adapting to change in information technology, with the exception of the larger professional practices with audits in large firms, who have been information technology users for some time. Such client firms have demanded appropriate service because of their customer power and have obtained if from the big practices who have sought to exploit their marketing advantage.

The employee-accountant is in a similar position, being at the beck and call of his employer/user-manager. The user-managers have expected the accountants to use all available technology to be effective and efficient in satisfying their needs, and hence accountants have been involved in information technology since Lyon's Electric Office (LEO) in the 1950s. It is not unusual to come across management accountants, therefore, who are active participants in data processing — indeed accountants and data processing managers are quite often one and the same person.

We would suggest, therefore, that the hypothesis could be propounded that if accounting is seen as a service function, then like all services, it must adapt to suit the needs of the users. In the case of the employee, the user's needs are paramount and clearly linked to his employment position, whilst those of the professional external accountant are subject to a delay mechanism resulting from the marketplace. This delay mechanism has meant that the professional practising accountant has been much slower in adapting to information technology unless he has been concerned with auditing major concerns or has been especially innovative. This would support the view of Bhaskar and Wiliams that the small practice is under threat, but not the 'big eight'.

A further problem existing for those who are late in adapting to IT is the learning curve. Early innovators will already be well up the learning curve and, in addition, will more rapidly adapt to new developments since they have confidence, experience and basic knowledge which furthers their learning ability. Latecomers have a lot to catch up, but at the same time should benefit from the pioneers' mistakes and thus avoid reinventing the wheel.

The one conflict we are left with is the view presented by Carr that employment of accountants will not change and yet their productivity will increase. This view does not seem feasible unless other management disciplines are going to contract or expand less rapidly. Given our referencing to the professional bodies, which seem to form a delay mechanism to change, it is highly unlikely that other management disciplines will not adapt to information technology at the same time as the accountants. Given this view then the only people who will suffer will be the lower accounting and clerical levels. Perhaps we may, therefore, expect the same organizational compression for the accounting functions as for the overall organization. If this is the case then trainee accountants and the lower-tier accounting bodies must be under threat. Alternatively, the view must be put forward that accountants themselves may not expect to enjoy the same employment opportunities with increased productivity.

Collectively, the reports generate a varied picture of current attitudes, usage and awareness, with all them indicating a trend towards the increased utilization of IT. Given that there is such a variation in the level of usage, the overall picture must indicate either varying rates of adaptation, with the associated consequences identified by Kearney, or a need for some rapid training and education of those who are currently behind. All of the reports tend towards this view, preferring to leave the individual to determine the consequences of failing to adapt. This maintains uncertainty and perhaps leads to the older member hoping to retire before the threat becomes real whilst the younger colleague may become more withdrawn, retiring into specific services or changing career. This view, which implies age-attributable differences, is not, however, supported by the studies, which indicate that adaptation is not age relted. More realistically, the view recognizes the alternative choices available to individuals under threat and these reflect their careers and working lifecycles.

## The alternative roles of accountants

Simon et al. (1954) classified accounting roles into: score keeping, attention directing, and problem solving. Hooper (1980) suggested that failure to maintain the traditional book-keeping role (score keeping) was easily perceived while the service roles (attention directing and problem solving) were less apparent. Newman et al. (1988) identified three task dimensions:

i. traditional financial score keeping vs problem solving orientation

ii. general responsibility vs specific problem for information solving provision

iii. economic evaluation vs long-term conceptual/strategic planning

However, without doubt accountants display certain personality traits (De Costa, 1971) and this is reflected in their learning modes (Kolb, 1985). These characteristics may be both a product of their socialization process and/or reflect the underlying personality.

The above examples represent just a few of the interpretations of what an accountant actually does. They also reflect the conflicting expectations of those who define the roles (Machin, 1977). The assumption upon which all of the studies have been based is that the subject of study is one and the same. This view is misleading and results in conflicting statements as to how the accountant is going to change. In reality, however, there is not just one accountant, but many, when considered on an individual basis. The use in this discussion of industry and practice represents a polarization of the variety of accountants which may be encountered.

The traditional view of the accountant is often distorted, which makes for an unclear starting position. The one certainty is that readers will have their own disparate views as to what an accountant is, and that these will form the basis for individual judgements of the impact of IT. Even those readers who are accountants may have a biased perception of the role of accountants and some benefit may be gained by asking non-accountant colleagues or clients for their views!

## Management accountants experience of IT

In the case of the Chartered Institute of Management Accountants four studies have been undertaken. The first by Collier (1984) described how the qualified management accountant was being affected. This study was replicated by Kaye and Dixon (1987) in their study of UK students and, subsequently, Dixon looked at students and members abroad (1988). However, while these studies are descriptive of accountants and their involvement with the hardware, the software and systems development, they gave little insight into how other managers perceived the accountants role in the management of the introduction of IT. This led to Collier and Kaye (1987) undertaking a study of the role of management accountants in the formulation of IT strategies. The Chartered Institute of Management Accountants specifically sought this information to assist it in developing a strategy towards information technology. A fifth study by Loughborough University is a longitudinal study which has yet to report.

Table 1 lists the studies undertaken to date on management accountants. However, it should be noted that only two of the studies (Collier and Kaye, Loughborough) used a wider definition than members of the Chartered Institute of Management Accountants in their sample, but all recognized that practising management accountants could consist of a wider population. CIMA has some 25,000 members and 40,000 students worldwide of which 78% are UK based.

Given that CIMA, with a worldwide membership, is still primarily a UK accounting body, we will seek to concentrate on the UK-based studies. In passing, the Dixon study has suffered a poor response rate which will only permit limited comment. At this stage the indications are of significantly varied exposure and experience of IT ranging from nil in certain under-developed or financially constrained situations, e.g., some African states, to relative sophistication in the Far East. Perhaps the warnings from the study are not to make generalised assumptions about overseas states of developments. Carr's (1987) study further highlights the varied experience.

## The role of management accountants in IT

In the Collier and Kaye (1987) study, opinions were sought on:

i. the role of accountants in the development, control, application, management and operation of computerized information systems

ii. the role played by accountants in training for information technology, and

iii. the expertise of the members of different accounting bodies.

Information was collected from six distinct groups using a simple questionnaire.

The most commonly occurring rankings were financial control (first), information provision (second) and advisory (third), with stewardship and decision taking deemed to be of less significance. Only personnel directors deviated from this ordering of the descriptions and the differences were minimal. The preponderence of emphasis on financial control with information provision clearly perceived as of less importance suggests that accountants' priorities lie with financial aspects rather than the more general flow of information within the organization.

## Identification of role for accountants

There was a unanimous agreement with respect to an increasing involvement in the application of IT to business. In the area of policy and resource control there were significant differences in that while all could see a role for accountants, the sample personnel directors, finance directors and CIMA members believed this was significant while managing directors and computer managers were less supportive. Further analysis of the areas of involvement helped clarify this contrast.

There is no doubting the overall opinion that accountants have a valuable role to play in the areas of security and control of information, preparation and dissemination of reports and decision support systems. Overall systems design is also earmarked as being of importance with only computer managers dissenting, while data collection and processing gains similar support. The management of activities of users is not seen by a clear majority as being an important role. This ties in with the answers to question 2 of the Collier and Kaye survey where information technology was seen as affecting all levels of management with the implication that the management of user activities will remain with the users.

Opinions were not wholly consistent with respect to any of the roles. In all instances members were most inclined to believe that a role existed. Elsewhere the outcome is less predictable with computer managers discarding a role in the determination of training policy and presentation, but acknowledging a design role.

It is probable that opinions are clouded by practice within individual firms in the short period over which information technology training has been separately recognizable.

## Expertise of different bodies

Respondents were asked whether any particular professional body might have a greater expertise in the field of information technology than another by identifying four areas: information technology policy, technical advice, systems design advice, and cost benefit assessment. As might be suspected, the majority of computer managers, personnel directors, managing directors and finance directors answered 'no' or 'uncertain' to the question. Indeed several included the apt comment that it depends upon the individual rather than the qualification.

Amongst members, especially in the areas of systems design advice and cost benefit analysis, there was a degree of partisan support for CIMA members. However, overall it must be concluded that no accounting body has convinced senior decision takers that it has a more expert membership in information technology than another.

## Summary

The Collier & Kaye research shows a remarkable consensus of opinion from the five groups surveyed. It gives a degree of confidence to the results, which might otherwise have been lacking given the difficulties in designing the questionnaire, problems in sample selection and the inevitably moderate response rates.

It would appear reasonable to conclude from the research:

i accountants are viewed, and see themselves, as being principally concerned with financial control. Information provision is universally agreed as being of secondary, but significant importance;

accountants are seen by other groups as being second in importance only to computer managers in having the ability to make an important contribution in an organization's response to computing and information technology. Finance directors and CIMA members both felt that accountants had slightly more important output in this area than even computer managers;

computing and information technology requires contributions from all levels of management;

accountants were deemed to have a significant role to play in determining and organization's information technology policy, designing and implementing the organization's computerized information system and selecting computer software, by not only finance directors and members, but also the other groups surveyed;

no accounting body is perceived by non-accountants as having a special expertise in information technology matters;

accountants are seen and see themselves as playing an increasingly important role in the field of information technology policy, the application of information technology to business and the control of information technology resources (computer managers marginally dissented for policy and resource control aspects);

vii accountants are not perceived and do not perceive themselves as being automatically involved in information technology training;

viii accountants see themselves and are viewed unanimously as having an important role in the organization's management of information;

ix accountants are agreed to have a valuable role to play in the information management areas of security and control of information, preparation and dissemination of reports, overall systems design, data collection and processing and decision support systems.

These results indicate clearly that senior decision makers view accountants as a profession with a key role to play in taking advantage of opportunities presented by information technology. Simultaneously members concur with this view. Therefore, it appears that the aspirations of members and the expectations of top management coincide. It only remains to be proved that accountants, in general, have the skills and abilities to fulfil this crucial role.

## Accounting origins of information strategies

It has been argued by various accounting theorists that certain prerequisites were required for the developing of accounting. Littleton (1933) suggested the following:

Economic Factors: property rights trade credit capital
Facilitators: art of writing arithmetic medium of exchange.

He indicated that they only came together in the 15th Century with the emergence of the Italian Banking and Trade; hence Luca Pacioli's thesis on Mathematics and Book-keeping of 1494. Likewise, we may argue that the Companies Act in 1848 gave vigour to the accounting profession with requirements for proper books and audits. Similarly the War Offices requirement for cost plus contracts in 1914–18 led to activity in cost-accounting which was refined to cost control in the subsequent depression (Banyard, 1985).

This same historical origin approach has been used by Wiezenbaum (1984) to suggest that modern commercial data processing owes its origins to organizational growth and complexity, increased numerical data processing and the availability of technology. LEO (1954) is a good example with the use of technology essentially developed for numerical processing of data for code breaking being applied to large-scale commercial activities previously served by battalions of Comptometer operators. Perhaps if this had not happened we would have seen the demise of large complex organizations due to slow seizure of its information systems.

While electronic data processing owes it origin to accounting and electronic mechanical technology, management accounting owes its origin to, amongst other things, accounting and management. The latter was an emerging force in the 1930s; they merged to form management accounting and fostered new functional disciplines like marketing. This latter discipline has perhaps the essential framework which enabled the establishment of corporate strategies while management accounting formed a facilitative framework to analyse organizational performance.

The integration of data processing into a central information role was achieved by undertaking not only the routine processing of data, but enabling management interrogation and reporting in the manner of management accounting reports. The use of information systems by management for planning, controlling and decision making led naturally to the use of information systems for planning, as in financial modelling (Naylor, 1979, Gryner and Wooler, 1978), controlling, as in continuous business forecasting and variance analysis, as well as decision support systems (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978). At the same time the increasing costs of information systems focused managements' attention on the need to use information systems strategically like other business resources. This may be seen as one origin of information strategies; however, it is not the sole one.

Clearly, in order for organizations to invest in information technology there must have been either a positive cost benefit, or the perceived non-quantified risk from not 'me too-ing' must have been sufficiently great to legitimize the investment decision. The climate of emerging management sciences and quantitative techniques certainly further encouraged the use of technology in the office and board rooms. However, there have been clear acts of faith and institutionalizing of these resources into our culture. The ever spiralling power to cost ratios only further encourage the replacement and expansion of existing facilities. Clearly, having established a place in the organization it has been in the interests of the data processing department manager to expand both resources and services.

While pragmatic reasons for the growth of information systems may be traced equally the growth of management sciences and the incorporation of behavioural as well as technological developments help further the maturing role of information systems. Perhaps they also appeared in a time when Western economies were looking for new opportunities to overcome their stagnation and exploit the increasing graduate population in management sciences.

Asch (1985) has argued that accountants should take on managerial roles, but to do this they need further broader managerial training. Holdsworth (1988) is quoted saying ‘Accountants in industry have represented a very good source of people for general management’, during a period when the British government is critical of management training (Butcher, 1986) and the Handy Report (1988) advocates a significant upgrade of managerial skills through training and education. It would appear, therefore, that the industrially based management accountants are well placed as a powerful management class, given their experience and role in financial control as well as information provision.

Accountants may also be described as custodians of tradition with their conservatism, stewardship role, standards, etc, as well as their techniques of cost benefit analysis, depreciation, accruals, etc. But accountants also introduce change through a system of authorization of budgets and projections.

Finally, we may view accountancy as the oldest business information system. This view can be both a straight jacket restricting innovation (Kaplan and Cooper, 1987) or a formal discipline to ensure logical incremental change.

If we now consider the accounting tradition in corporate strategy we will again perceive a significant influence. Anthony (1965) indicated the hierarchy of corporate planning, managerial control and operational management to explain control systems and illustrated this with examples from budgeting, standard costing. Subsequent linkages have been made by Earl (1983), Otley (1987). Seed (1980) and Donelly (1981) have emphasised on strategic management and the professional examinations of CIMA where students are expected to demonstrate knowledge and skill in strategic planning. The process of planning incorporates basic economic/accounting assumptions of profit maximization, an accounting measures role, as well as techniques for reporting — proforma profit and loss accounts, balance sheet and funds flow statement; selection — DCF and implementations and budgets. But perhaps the methods exploited by accountants reflect the logical incrementalism of business planning (Quinn, 1980).

## Conclusions

From the studies to date it would appear that accountants have adapted to IT at varying rates and at various levels. In addition, there are differences between the industrial- and professional-based accountant. At the same time, no professional body has any greater expertise in this area, but rather individuals show varying skills and knowledge. This is reflected both in industry, with the emergence of ‘systems accountants’, and the profession with ‘IS/IT consultants’.

However, this is perhaps a superficial finding, as clearly accountants are perceived to have a role in the development and implementation of IT and management accountants display significant experience and expertise in this area. In addition, management accountants have shown expertise in the development of corporate strategy, investment appraisal and generally are agreed to be concerned with a forward view.

The study by Collier and Kaye shows the clear role of computer managers in information strategies, but it cannot be their sole province as they, perhaps, do not have a wide enough perspective while at the same time they do have clear expertise in the technology.

The study also indicates the important role of other personnel in the formulation of information strategy, including the accountant. However, the variable experience and expertise of accountants, including management accountants, highlight a weakness in their training and subsequent practical experience. Clearly this weakness needs to be overcome both at the student level and the post-qualifying level, if accountants are to take a role in formulating information strategies (Kaye and Dixon, 1987).

The view drawn from these studies is the management accountants are well placed to be involved with the formulation of IT strategies, but currently their training and, in many cases, their experience is inadequate to fulfil that role solely. Clearly, the development of information strategies is an important managerial decision and requires participation at the highest levels and throughout many aspects of the company, but the management of this process is one which several managerial classes are qualified to undertake and perhaps the competition for this role will lead to a new status for members of professional bodies such as management accountants.
