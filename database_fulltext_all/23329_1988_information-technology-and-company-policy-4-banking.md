# Information Technology and Company Policy: 4. Banking

Henry Holloway, Consultant

## Introduction

This paper, which examines the use of information technology (IT) in the banking institutions of UK business, is the fourth in a series investigating the application of IT in different industry sectors. Other papers have looked at newspapers (Holloway, 1987a), electronic publishing (Holloway, 1987b) and the holiday and travel industry (Holloway, 1988). Throughout the paper, IT is taken to mean the whole range of computer facilities for the acquisition, storage, transmission, retrieval, processing and output of information.

The financial sector represents a large and rapidly growing sector of the UK economy. Moreover, legislation introduced in recent years has increased the complexity of this sector, as banks, building societies and insurance companies can now provide overlapping services in most areas. A complete description of IT and banking is further complicated by the use of similar systems and software not only in financial institutions such as the Stock Exchange, but for transactions in the travel and holiday industry and so this report will focus on the area known as retail, or High Street, banking.

## Deregulation

The increased use of IT is the most obvious change in banking over the past few years, but there have been other major changes in UK banking which date back to the 1960s. At that time overseas banks moved into Europe to take advantage of burgeoning Eurocurrency markets, many setting up offices in London. The 1970s saw deregulation set in motion commencing with the Competition and Credit Control Act (1971). This led to the abandonment of the interest rate cartel and genuine competition between domestic banks. Foreign banks, now numbering over 400, have also competed in certain market sectors.

Banks also face competition from other sources. This is partly due to deregulation; for example the

Building Societies Act (1986) has increased the range of services that building societies can offer in direct competition with banks. Another factor is the changed attitude to banking services in general. There is a tendency for retailers to operate their own credit cards and some large stores intend to offer card holders personal loans.

The banks' response to this changed market situation has been to expand the range of standard financial products and services available at every branch. Non-traditional banking activities have been added, such as stock market dealing, insurance broking, home loans and even estate agency functions. Some of these were offered in the past but with the bank operating as a go-between. Now the emphasis is on the branches providing a one-stop banking facility. Clearly, IT plays an important part in this as it provides the wherewithal to carry out dealings at the branch via terminals linked to networks.

## IT in banking

Banking has always been a labour-intensive activity and even today 60 per cent of staff are employed handling paper (Mills, 1988). IT's potential for increased efficiency was very attractive so that banking became one of the first industry sectors to adopt computers for commercial applications. Perhaps the first major step forward was the use of Magnetic Ink Character Recognition (MICR) in cheque handling which has been in use for nearly thirty years. Other developments have continued to be introduced as soon as they mature, for example the first cash dispenser in 1967.

The mid 1980s saw an increased acquisition of IT in banking. 1986 was a key year in the UK, containing as it did further deregulation and particularly the rush to automate in the City — the so-called 'Big Bang' — in time for the launch on October 27. This created pressure on the less technology-minded institutions to install computer systems as a defensive measure.

The high rate of take-up of new technology is largely technology-driven. Large system vendors play a key role in encouraging the move to computer control of transactions. However, it would be wrong to assume that IT in banking is solely the result of technology-push. Sowton (1986) writes about the importance of public image to financial institutions:

'being seen as a market leader will help to attract customers and technology has become something of a vote-catcher here. In the recent study by the consultants Arthur Andersen, 97 per cent of the 600 financial respondents believed that technology would be important in maintaining or increasing competitive advantage, and 98 per cent believed that technology leadership would help a bank's image and be an advantage in attracting new customers.'

Acquisition of IT equipment and expertise is a very costly business. Cane (1987), gives management consultant McKinsey and Company as his source for the figure of \$9000 million spent on IT by the 35 leading US commercial banks in 1986. Moreover, this figure is expected to grow at between 15 and 20 per cent per annum.

Figures for the UK are comparable. Ambitious investment banks or securities houses planning an international presence should have been spending at least £100 million a year on systems in 1987 according to Gordon Pepper, senior adviser to Midland Montagu. At this time Midland Bank's own recurrent systems expenditure was in the region of £300 million (Cane, 1987).

From the vendors' point of view, supplying computer equipment and support to banks is a very profitable business and naturally attracts the attention of many companies including, of course, the most powerful. In a survey of banking systems, Smith (1988) lists 35 companies which supply systems software packages at prices from £30,000 to over £300,000. Software maintenance costs from 10 to 15 per cent per annum. These packages run on the usual range of hardware; mainly IBM, NCR, Hewlett Packard, Unisys and DEC equipment.

Smith points out that the large number of packages available is a reflection of the move away from bespoke software to buying-in software packages. Users have made considerable savings by adopting this approach.

## Present technology

A key application of IT is to the automation of cheque and credit clearing. Automation of cheque clearing was achieved long ago using MICR but credit clearance is more recent, requiring more sophisticated equipment with the capability to read printed, typewritten and MICR characters. Even with today's high level of automation, manual intervention is still required.

The Bankers' Automated Clearing Service (BACS) was set up in 1971 by the four major UK High Street banks for transfer of funds between accounts held in the UK banking system. There are now more than 30,000 users. BACS handles direct debit, standing order and salary/wage payments.

1971 also saw the setting up of SWIFT, Society for Worldwide Inter-bank Financial Telecommunications, by a consortium of 68 banks to provide a private communication network for international payments and other messages. SWIFT is administered by SWIFT SC located in Brussels and there are two other administrative locations, in Amsterdam and in Culpepper, Virginia, USA. The network commenced operation in 1977 and there are now over 2,000 connecting points in more than 50 countries. The system makes use of a number of interface devices initially supplied by one of three approved minicomputer vendors. Recently, SWIFT has its own range of computer systems.

In 1978 the Committee of London and Scottish Banks set up CHAPS, the Clearing House Automated Payment System, for the transmission of high value, guaranteed, sterling payments for same-day settlement. Initially, it was expected to operate in a similar fashion to SWIFT and that a central clearing system like BACS would need to be installed. However, technology development eliminated the need for a centralized concept. In 1980, a new CHAPS system was formulated based on front-end processors at each settlement bank that would communicate via a gateway along the PacketSwitchstream Network operated by British Telecom. To ensure consistency, Tandem was chosen to supply each front-end minicomputer and gateway software came from Logica. CHAPS commenced live operation in February 1984. Since security is of paramount importance, encryption devices are incorporated for scambling and decoding messages.

The most visible evidence of the increased automation of banking is the presence in the High Street of the ubiquitous automated teller machine (ATM). A survey (Anon, 1988b) showed that UK banks and building societies were carrying out major branch automation programmes. Midland and Barclays had the greatest growth in the preceding year, 32 and 29 per cent respectively, but their installed bases were still some way behind NatWest, TSB and Lloyds. As at 30th June 1988, around 11,000 ATMs were installed. NatWest had the greatest number of these, 2376, followed by TSB (2011) and

Lloyds (1999). The leading suppliers of ATMs were NCR and IBM, other suppliers being Philips, Nixdorf and Inter Innovation.

Over the past few years there has been a rationalization of ATM networks. In 1988 two ATM networks, LINK, serving 25 building societies, Girobank and some of the smaller banks and Matrix, serving seven building societies, announced plans to merge. Later the same year, Halifax, the UK's largest building society, decided to join forces with this network. In 1983, Halifax became the first building society to introduce cash dispensers. LINK is comparatively new but growing rapidly since its start in 1986. The integration is expected to be completed in 1989 when its 3,400 ATMs will serve 10 million customers. The two largest networks are operated by groups of the clearing banks. Barclays, Lloyds and the Royal Bank of Scotland operate 4,400 machines for 13 million card holders, while NatWest and Midland operate 3,500 machines for 20 million card holders. The fourth largest network is operated by TSB with 1300 ATMs and 3.5 million cardholders. This is expected to join forces with the NatWest/Midland grouping (Ellis, 1988). At the end of 1987 there were around 28 million ATM cards in use, plus 13.1 million Visa and 11.4 million Access cards (Spink, 1988).

Electronic funds transfer at the point-of-sale (EFTPOS) has been mooted since the early 1970s. When fully implemented this would enable all goods to be paid for by computer controlled transfer of money from the customer's account to the vendor's account at the time of purchase. This would be achieved without the use of any paper, be it credit slip, cheque or bill. The main reason for the introduction of EFTPOS is a reduction in the cost of the transaction.

EFTPOS UK was set up in 1986 by the four leading clearing banks and ten other financial institutions to develop a common national EFTPOS system. Progress has been slow and leading banks have introduced their own schemes although the first of these lie in a halfway-house between cheque clearing and EFTPOS. In 1987, Barclays was the first in the field with its Connect card for automatic debit, followed by Lloyds using the same Visa system for its own card. Both schemes complete transactions in the same way as for credit card purchases. While not offering complete electronic processing, these transactions have been speeded up by the use of process data quickly (PDQ) terminals. At each transaction the card is passed through a slot in the PDQ machine which is linked to a computer for authorization and processing but there is still some paper work involved. The corresponding Access equipment is called ACCEPT (Access electronic payment terminal).

A group of banks, NatWest, Midland and the Royal Bank of Scotland, introduced their Switch debit card in 1988. This was claimed to be entirely electronic, removing much of the backroom work of other payment systems. The Switch cashless shopping system was welcomed by the Retail Consortium, representing major department stores, in contrast to its reception of Connect. Barclays was criticized for its initial charging method of percentage of sale (modified later) and for operating solely through the Visa system, thus forcing the retailer to accept the bank's terms to avoid losing the Visa licence. Switch is a new scheme and operates a fixed charge for each transaction at the same level as for cheques (Rodgers, 1988).

All the machine-readable cards issued by UK banks at present have data encoded on a magnetic strip. Typically, each card has a magnetic strip with three tracks; track one is read-only containing personal details (name and address), track two, also read-only, contains bank and account numbers for use on an on-line system, and track three is read/write containing track two information and other data permitting the card to be used for both on-line and off-line use.

Information is not updatable and the maximum capacity is typically 1,000 bits. UK banks have an enormous investment in this technology although it has been superseded by the smart card. Smart cards have one or more microchips embedded in them with a capacity of 8k bits or more. However, the key attribute of the smart card is not its memory size but its capability for updating. This is achieved by making part of the memory erasable programmable read-only memory (EPROM).

UK banks are obviously deterred from changing to using smart cards by the cost. Typically, a smart card costs around £2 and a terminal for reading and updating costs £1,000–2,000 (a magnetic strip reader can cost as little as £250). Of course, in addition, there is the capital investment in system development and networking associated with magnetic strip cards to be written off.

Other countries have smart cards in commercial use. France and Japan head the list with many of their banks issuing smart cards in preference to magnetic cards. Banks in Norway, Italy and the Netherlands have operated pilot smart card schemes. In the USA, Visa and Mastercard have run trials but this is an application of technology where the USA also lags in bringing the product to the marketplace. France had 3.6 million smart cards in circulation in 1988 with a forecasted figure of 20 million by 1990. Groupement des Cartes

Bancaire first issued smart cards in 1986 (Ody, 1988).

From the retailer's point of view, the smart card has advantages over the present card system. Ody (1988) quotes Damian Norton, executive director at Management Horizons (retail consultants), who gives two reasons why a retailer should favour the smart card:

‘it would eliminate the need for all that wiring and networking which is a major problem for retailers with conventional EFTPOS. Smart cards can also reduce fraud risks. Equally important, retailing today is all about knowing your customers, and card transactions are the ideal way of accumulating personal details about shoppers.’

Electronic data interchange (EDI) refers to the transmission of invoices and orders over telephone lines instead of paper documentation through the post. EDI has become increasingly commonplace in the UK in the past few years and in 1987 the first European cross-border pilot on EDI was set up. The importance of the cross-border aspect cannot be over-emphasized as the legal status of electronic information varies from country to country. Thus the pilot would test the legal as well as the technical and financial feasibility of trans-border EDI.

The value of EDI is shown by two sets of figures; UN estimates of the cost of shipping goods representing 7 per cent of their value and a claim that the EDI route could deliver a message for 6p compared with 69p via a paper-based route (Coates, 1986).

The increasing importance of EDI is illustrated by the spate of activity in this area; the UN recently approved a standard EDI message for commercial invoices, the UK's two largest independent EDI networks Tradanet (ICL and GE Information Services) and Edict (Istel) announced that they would interwork, and other organizations are moving into EDI. For example, Barclays set up an EDI system for two of its customers Peugot Talbot and Rists (part of the Lucas group) (Jones, 1989).

## Cutting IT costs

The enormous investment of banks in IT has been referred to above. Naturally, banks are looking at ways of reducing cost without impairing efficiency. One way is to reduce maintenance contracts by replacing separate contracts with different companies with one overall contract. Barclays is expecting its single maintenance contract with Olivetti to halve its maintenance costs for 150,000 computers originally maintained by 200 separate contracts (Jones, 1989).

Another area amenable to cost-cutting is the selection of systems. Obviously, there are savings to be made in finding the most cost-effective way of choosing a system for each bank. With so many parameters involved, however, this is a problem that is insoluble in the absolute sense; one only knows the right answer much later, when the system has been installed and in use for some time. An alternative approach to cost-cutting in the selection of systems has been adopted by the eight members of the Proteus Club; Midland Bank, Royal Insurance, Civil Aviation Authority, Central Electricity Board, IBM, Ministry of Defence, National Health Service and Peat Marwick McIntosh Consultants. With the support of the DTI, they have invested £365,000 in the development of an expert system which will help in the selection of the optimum IT hardware and software (Jones, 1989).

## Bank customers' opinion

IT has been introduced partly as technology-push and then as a defensive measure by banks fearing they would be left out in the cold by other financial institutions at the forefront of technological innovation. Bank customers have had technology thrust upon them without any choice, nor have they been able to see any advantage to them of new technology. In fact, the disadvantages of new technology, from the customer's point of view, vastly outweigh any advantages.

A survey of consumers carried out in January and February 1988, indicated that customers were worried about the temptation to overspend, the difficulty of keeping track of spending and the possibility of fraud and theft. Unfortunately for financial organizations, the 'phantom' debiting of accounts has taken place and this event is newsworthy. In some cases the customer may be at fault by disclosing the personal identification number (PIN) to other persons. Banks also make mistakes, for example a customer's account number being unwittingly allocated to another customer's card (Anon, 1988a).

There is also the worry of losing a card. In the case of credit card holders a solution is at hand in the form of insurance. However, despite the emergence of eight card and registration protection companies offering card loss insurance and other benefits at around £6, only 1.25 million out of 28.5 million card holders held protection in 1988 (Thomas, 1988).

From the bank's point of view, they need to check carefully on credit reference. IT can provide a solution to this problem. Infolink, a company set up by a traders' organization, the United Association for the Protection of Trade, supplies credit information to its members from its Unisys automated credit enquiry system (Leopold, 1987).

## Company profile — National Westminster Bank (NatWest)

National Westminster Bank was formed in 1968 as the merger of the District, National Provincial and the Westminster banks. At the time of the merger, there were 36 separate computers in eight centres serving 3,600 branches, and more off-putting, five-book-keeping systems. Eventually the computing function was consolidated into two interlinked centres located in London and the Midlands with one accounting system.

The centres use more than ten IBM mainframes. This quantity of computer power consumes a considerable amount of electricity and the primary source is its own power generation plant at the London computer centre. The Midlands site has a back-up power generation capability. Electricity can also be obtained from the local electricity boards. Fluctuations in supply are absorbed by battery systems which in the event of a power failure provide an intermediate supply until standby generators are brought into use. The building support systems — power, lighting, air conditioning, etc, providing instant warning of any malfunction.

Data is stored in the first instance on hard disks but back-up is provided by IBM3850 Mass Storage Systems and the higher capacity Masstor storage tape cartridges. NatWest, in common with other UK banks, is legally obliged to keep ten years of archival statement information. In the longer term, data is output on Datagraphix Computer Output on Microfilm (COM) machines and microfiche.

NatWest has used networks to link branches to one or other of its two computer centres since the mid to late 1970s. Over several years the original network has been replaced by a new digital integrated network (DIN). The different needs of voice and data dictated that the network would support a private telephone system using circuit switching and a private data communications network using packet switching. Phase 1 (voice) was completed in 1984. This comprises a trunk framework based on digital connections operating at 2 megabits and 13 switching centres. In phase 2 (data), three more switching centres were added and 32 64-kilobit channels (two of which are reserved for line control). The 30 available channels are allocated on a semi-permanent basis to either the voice or data networks. The sub-network architecture conforms to X25 protocol. The data channels also support security alarms.

Integral to this powerful network is branch interface equipment (BIE), intelligent distributed processors incorporating specially commissioned software, which link all branch equipment — ATMs, terminals, etc, plus alarm systems — to the network. One of the advantages of the BIE is the capacity to support non-intelligent devices. The network has a high level of back-up with reserve CPUs at computer centres and alternative trunk routes. The public switched network could also be used in an emergency.

The clearing operation of cheques and credits is serviced by IBM Cheque Reader/Sorters (MICR — 2,000 documents per minute) and REL Trace Reader/Sorters (OCR — 1,800 documents per minute). Although a high level of automation has been achieved there is still considerable manual intervention required.

System control is exercised from a central bridge running Job Entry System 3 (JES3) software. All jobs are initiated from the bridge and subsequent processing monitored from there. The clearing system has its own sub-bridge.

In addition to its considerable centralized mainframe computer facility, NatWest has turned to personal computers to cater for an increasing number of distributed applications. The problems arising from selecting equipment from an increasing range of PCs prompted the formation of an Information Centre. This is responsible for training, advice and product (hardware and software) evaluation, selection and acquisition. Advice is provided via a help desk for telephone queries, a user group and newsletters.

Computers also play a role in equipment control, monitoring the whereabouts and state of all equipment from typewriters to vehicles, and in transport and despatch.

NatWest's electronic cash management service for its corporate customers gives access to transaction and balance information, effects money transfers and provides treasury information such as foreign exchange and interest rates.

The bank is a member of the various consortia, both national and international, which use IT to ease the flow of money; BACS, SWIFT and CHAPS.

The bank was a founder member of the consortium of banks which set up the Joint Credit Card Company Limited (JCCC), which operates the Access credit card. JCCC maintains two computer centres using IBM and Amdahl mainframes communicating via microwave links. Initially, credit card transactions were entirely paper-based but an increasing amount of transactions are now processed in the store and recorded on magnetic tape or via the ACCEPT system.

NatWest's first ATMs, carrying the brand name Servicetill, were installed in London in 1975. Typically each ATM will handle 300 transactions and dispense £6,600 per day. The increasing availability and popularity of ATMs is reflected in the growth of Servicetill transactions. From 1984 to 1987 the number of transactions handled increased from around 65 million to nearly 123 million, an increase of around 89 per cent. In the same period cheque book, balance and statement requests increased by approximately 138, 187 and 97 per cent respectively. A reciprocal agreement between NatWest, Midland and TSB allows customers to use the other banks' ATMs for cash-only transactions. Servicetills are located outside the bank premises and are accessible 24 hours a day, seven days a week. A newer form of cash dispenser, the Rapid Cash Till, is located in bank premises and is accessible during banking hours. This dispenses cash only, in fixed amounts, delivery taking 10-15 seconds.

NatWest clearly has a major commitment to IT which at present is costing around £1 billion over a five-year period. Its network is among the largest and most advanced in Europe. Further developments involve the increased use of terminals around the office offering direct access to customer information without the need for paper.

## Conclusion

IT and banking have become familiar partners over the years. Initially, the IT presence was invisible but gradually it has become more and more obvious so that today more and more people are making us of electronic means of carrying out a variety of transactions.

The major questions are whether there are many benefits and if so who gains them. From a productivity viewpoint, it is difficult to establish whether IT has played a part in improvements in this area in UK banking. Coates (1986) indicates that productivity improved by 0.8 per cent per annum during 1960–65, while the corresponding figures for 1965–70 and 1970–75 were 1.7 and 4.5 per cent respectively. At first glance these figures appear to coincide with increased use of IT but other factors were involved, most importantly the more competitive nature of banking.

On the other hand, IT has supplied part of the means for banks to become competitive. Given this, it is unfair to also seek to justify on cost or productivity grounds the performance of IT. Referring to the part played by technology in the changing role of banks, Sir Timothy Bevan, former chairman of Barclays Bank says:

'Without the developments in this area, the credit card would not have gone far. Now networks of desk-top terminals are transforming communications, facilitating the pricing of mathematically-complex instruments, enabling transactions to be conducted worldwide.' (Bevan, 1987)

Thus IT is adding a new dimension to banking which certainly could not be achieved by other means. IT speeds up transactions, reduces the labour content and also the amount of paper needed for each transaction. This is clearly beneficial to banks although they have to invest in costly equipment to achieve these benefits. What of the bank customer? The corporate user also gains, particularly where CHAPS or EDI are brought into use, speeding cash flows and keeping accounts up to date. Benefits are less clear for the small business or individual user. Banks claim to offer newer facilities for this type of customer. It is difficult to see these as being of any great improvement on what went before and the part played by IT in them is not plain. The only real evidence of IT in banking to the everyday user is the cash dispensing ATM.

## Glossary

Accept NatWest, Midland and Royal Bank of Scotland's version of PDQ terminals.

ATM (automated teller machine) A terminal, essentially a screen and keypad, linked via network to the customer's own branch supplying information, dispensing cash and other functions.

BACS (bankers' automated clearing services)
The UK clearing house for processing transactions by computer.

CHAPS (clearing house automated payment system) An automated payment system initiated by the Committee of London and Scottish Banks for high value, guaranteed, sterling payments for same-day settlement.

EDI (electronic data interchange) A system for the exchange of invoices and orders by electronic means saving time on paper transactions.

EFTPOS (electronic funds transfer at the point-of-sale) A system enabling accounts to be debited electronically using a magnetically marked card which is inserted in a special terminal at the retail outlet and is linked to the system.

MICR (magnetic ink character recognition)
Special characters designed to be easily read by machine.

PDQ (process data quickly) Visa terminals which read magnetic cards and relay the information to the bank's computer for checking and processing.

PIN (personal identification number) A number given to cardholders as a security check.

Smart card Plastic card embedded with one or more microchips carrying 8k or more memory of user information.

SWIFT (Society for Worldwide Inter-bank Financial Telecommunications) A private communications network set up by a consortium of banks (then numbering 68) in 1971 to carry messages and payments data, with headquarters in Brussels.

## Biographical notes

Upon graduating in physics and mathematics at Durham University, Henry Holloway became a school teacher serving in Uganda and New Zealand as well as England. He returned from New Zealand and gained his MSc in Cybernetics from Chelsea College. He then worked on various research projects concerned with the effects of new technology in printing and publishing for Pira, the research association of the UK paper and board, printing and packaging industries. He gained a Ph.D from Aston University in 1983 for studies of the future role of the printing industry. Dr Holloway is a consultant in the area of printing and information technology.

A member of the AIT Committee and founder treasurer, he is Assistant Editor of the Journal of Information Technology. Dr Holloway has written a number of reports and is a regular contributor to journals and the trade press.
