# Measuring the Productivity of Technical Documentation\*

Paul Strassmann, Strassman, Inc., USA

## Definition of scope

There is an apocryphal tale about a twelfth-century bishop visiting the site where a cathedral was under construction. He asked workmen what they were doing. The first one answered, 'I am laying stones'. The second said, 'I am erecting walls'. The next one explained, 'I am building a cathedral'. The fourth one claimed, 'I am creating a place of worship'.

Had the bishop enquired about the productivity of cathedral-building, he would have received even more diverse replies. This is why, when asking how to measure the productivity of technical documentation, we can obtain completely disparate points of view, depending upon how we define technical documentation. $^{1}$ However, a recent STC survey showed that 44 per cent of its members worked within the computer and electronics industry. $^{2}$ Therefore, to give realistic insights into improving productivity this discussion is based on my experiences with documentation used by suppliers and users of computers.

## The documentation cycle

The productivity of technical documentation cannot be understood solely by an examination of its component activities, such as writing, editing, page make-up, printing or training. The process and end products of technical documentation are an integral part of the life-cycle of a product. A business plan for a new computer product is incomplete if it does not incorporate the various roles technical documentation must play. Thus, for the purpose of my analysis of productivity, I suggest that an eight-stage sequence (Figure 1) follows technical documentation.

## Stage 1: Plan

Before suppliers develop technical documentation they should first determine the readers' needs. For instance, in the case of computer maintenance documentation the supplier should ask questions such as:

\- How many service personnel need to learn about the product?

\- What is their skill level?

\- How much training should be included to bring their performance to expected levels?

\- Is the communication required for a single event, or over an extended time? $^{3}$

For maximum productivity, a documentation plan must be thought through with great care and recorded in a list of planning assumptions about objectives, measures of performance, and priorities. A well-documented technical communication plan is most valuable in the middle of a product's lifecycle, when management may be inclined towards altering operating policies without knowing how the different aspects of the communication activity are linked together.

## Stage 2: Design

Before one proceeds to write manuals, copy microfiche pictures, videotape instructional material, and schedule training classes, the technical communication process must be designed. Should the communication be self-explanatory or should it include training courses? Should customers receive on-line support for enquiries? Is it possible to assist service personnel with expert systems? Is it possible to reduce the skill levels needed to maintain the equipment by installing remote diagnostics directly into the product? Should the technical documentation be directed at a predetermined technical literacy level, or should one assume that technical understanding will have to be introduced as a matter of procedural routine not requiring many skills?

The design assumptions one makes during this stage have far-reaching effects not only on the cost of technical documentation but also on its overall effectiveness. All poor productivity originates with inadequate attention to planning during the design stage! During design one has the widest freedom either to make good choices or to mess things up so that even heroic implementation delivers only mediocre results.

Technical documentation involves a complex process of trade-offs among a wide range of possible design options of how to communicate technical knowledge. To achieve high productivity one should make these trade-offs before implementing a plan to produce technical manuals. I have found that greater productivity gains are possible by making better trade-offs in overall design of technical communications than by improving any of the details of how one produces documentation. Once a design concept is committed, especially if by default, the introduction of major changes is not only frustrating but usually unaffordable. Delivering a more productive design for a technical documentation program requires innovation in methodology. One of the purposes of this discourse is to suggest a method for evaluating that innovation in dollar terms.

## Stage 3: Document

The documentation stage covers the preparation of final copy in text, graphic, audio or video format. Because of an occupational bias in the editors and writers who dominate technical communication, this stage is frequently mistaken for the essence of the entire process. The document stage includes not only the costs of writing, artwork and editing, but also the costs of the acquisition of technical information from the experts.

Only 3 per cent of technical writers and technical trainers have formal education in computer science and only 9 per cent in engineering. $^{4}$ Thus, many technical communicators require large amounts of time to extricate information from its originators. This time is frequently identified on time sheets as ‘technical liaison’. It also suggests a requirement for education and training within the editorial organization before the communicators can begin the documentation process itself. The extent to which the costly experts devote their time to the preparation of technical information is important in judging the overall productivity of technical communication. The division of labour between design engineers, software programmers and technical writers becomes an issue when one is deciding how to organize technical communication most effectively.

I believe that the full cost of technical documentation ought to include not only the time engineers and programmers spend with writers, but also the time engineers and programmers spend in preparing formal engineering and software documentation needed to answer questions from the purchasing, legal, production planning, general management and audit functions.

## Stage 4: Produce

The production stage includes activities such as the printing of manuals, preparation of visual aids, recording of tapes and writing of software diagnostic explanations. The rapidly changing production technologies, such as laser printing, desktop publishing, demand printing, microfiche storage, optical disk recording, and satellite transmission call for decisions concerning what tools to invest in for further productivity improvement.

These new technologies offer impressive productivity gains when viewed in isolation. However, sophisticated computer workstations frequently turn out to be dysfunctional when they make co-operation between engineers, writers, illustrators, trainers and service personnel more difficult. One of the purposes of this article is to provide a methodology for evaluating a bewildering array of new means for productivity enhancement.

## Stage 5: Distribute

In the distribution stage, technical manuals are printed, bound and put into inventory. Audiotapes, videotapes, microfiche and other forms of documentation go through a similar process. Originals are revised and the distribution of the revisions tracked so that information gets into the right hands on a timely basis. The logistics of the distribution of technical information, whether in printed, graphic or electronically encoded form, have a significant influence on the format, costs and effectiveness of the entire technical documentation process.

## Stage 6: Train

The productivity of technical data depends ultimately on whether the users learn how to apply it effectively. Therefore, I include applicability and ease of use as critical indicators of a productive approach. A variety of factors relate this stage to results, including the length of initial training, the amount of retraining required, and the adverse effects on productivity from the loss of experienced personnel who require new hiring and additional training.

## Stage 7: Use

The ultimate justification of all technical documentation is in its practical use by paying customers or by service people who maintain and support revenue-producing equipment or software. Even in the public sector, such as in defence or in public security, the purpose of all technical documentation is to support the missions of the organization according to explicitly defined objectives.

The cost-effectiveness of applying technical documentation to satisfying practical needs or to meeting expected performance targets becomes the ultimate test of whether the investment in the preceding six stages is paying off.

## Stage 8: Value added

Meeting expected performance targets is a prerequisite for achievement of any kind of productivity. However, what is the value of exceeding expectations? In dealing with increasingly complex equipment or services, especially where software is involved, the quality of technical communication becomes a significant competitive advantage. Technical documentation that exceeds a competitor's capabilities contributes to improved market share, to reduced user costs, or to easier customer acceptance. Good technical manuals can also be revenue producers.

The most difficult problem in judging the productivity of technical documentation is usually found here, beyond the boundaries of the conventional view of what technical communication is all about. Without consideration of potential added value any discussion of productivity gains is incomplete. Therefore, this discussion will include the consideration of overall economic value.

## Summary of stages

The eight-stage approach summarized in Figure 1 structures technical communication in its broadest possible interpretation as the basis for understanding how to measure this activity and thereby improve it.

To do so, one ought to recognize the multiplicity of the variables that can cause technical documentation to be inferior. For instance, a mistaken estimate of the training levels during Stage 1 can have adverse effects on the value of a product during Stage 6. A skilful approach to cross-indexing of error diagnostics during Stage 3 can significantly improve the usefulness of manuals during Stage 8. A good method for handling the distribution of engineering upgrades during Stage 4 can improve the responsiveness to customer needs during Stage 8.

## Application

To place the discussion about stages into a realistic setting I explored the problem of providing service documentation support for an electromechanical office product to be introduced in 1988. $^{5}$ A comprehensive checklist of critical variables that could influence the productivity of technical communication through the eight stages resulted in more than 100 items. Theoretically, each item could have an effect on any other item, thus giving us an unmanageable complexity for computing any productivity index. This raised the questions: What is a workable method for coping with the consequences of a large number of interrelated variables? How can one handle the computational difficulties with perhaps as many as 100 variables as they fluctuate for as long as 10 years?

The following section explains some of the problems inherent in current attempts to quantify the productivity of technical documentation. Then the final section shows a new model for dealing with this over-supply of variable factors.

## Productivity assessment methods

## Current measurement problems

The current difficulties in justifying information technology investments are rooted in the conceptual limits of how we compute a meaningful measure of productivity. The traditional approach to productivity evaluation relies on the simplistic idea of comparing changes in the ratios of output (measured in terms of physical units) divided by input (also defined in terms of physical units). When managers of technical documentation are pressed to produce productivity comparisons to satisfy auditors, they will always go back to these simplistic output/input ratios and produce arithmetically accurate (but irrelevant) statistics. These ratios define productivity in terms such as costs per page, pages per day, lines of code per hour, words per month, students per instructor or illustrations per month. A narrowly functional view of each person's work frequently gives credence to the mistaken notion that these ratios are indicators of efficiency. But technical documentation is not like routine factory work.

Quality, creativity, insight, completeness and reliability shape every stage of work. Technical documentation cannot be viewed as a discrete activity that can be evaluated in isolation (as is the case with routine factory work). Neglect at any one stage can rapidly escalate costs at any or all subsequent stages, even though the computation of individual output/input productivity ratios may show gains.

Under a more complete, and hence more accurate, view of technical documentation, the evaluation of the productivity of the entire sequence of stages requires answering the question of how one evaluates interrelated efforts where quality and relevance are more important than the quantity of output.

The solution proposed in this paper is to quantify the consequences of management decisions to improve expected results from a technical documentation project. While the number of variables involved, and hence the computation difficulty, is imposing, current business modelling software packages make such analyses economically feasible.

## Evaluation criteria

Decision-making about the productivity of technical documentation systems involves evaluation of interrelated options. Some of these options are more attractive than others. Some of them are more risky than others. Some of them require more resources than others. The difficulty is that without the facts, even well-meaning and intelligent individuals submerged within a technical communication chain cannot tell whether they are making trade-offs involving an attractive expediency or a future damage. They do not have access to information that would display whether the productivity gains at one stage may diminish the performance at a subsequent stage.

A departmentalized approach to business organization magnifies the opportunity for mis-allocation of resources and for parochial optimization. For instance, engineers and software designers may conceive sophisticated solutions that customers understand only with great inconvenience. Technical writers may produce highly literate text that requires excessive amounts of training for the service people. $^{6}$ Graphic artists may hand-craft beautiful diagrams which cannot easily be updated. Printers may economize in printing pages which make intelligent filing of revisions difficult. Trainers may use tutorial techniques which are inconsistent with field maintenance practices. The service organization may re-assign inadequately trained individuals who then have to cope with contradictory technical instructions and with inconsistent documentation formats.

Task forces and inter-functional staff meetings are the most frequent means for reconciling such conflicting actions. The problem with these purely verbal convocations lies in their difficulty in reaching agreement about what is the measurable systems pay-off of their negotiated compromises. The complexity and interrelatedness of technical documentation requires an explicit method for displaying the most likely consequence of arguments.

The only way I know of to do that is to make it possible to compute the present value of anticipated cash flows which reflect the various choices among several revised directions. Productivity gain is then computed as the difference between the present value of an organization's current plan and the present value of an improved approach. In other words, there are only future productivity gains. (Past gains cannot be claimed except for purposes of a retrospective appraisal. $^{7}$ Past productivity achievements are worthy of discussion only for learning purposes.) In a technical sense, productivity of documentation is then defined as a measure of the improvement in the present value of future cash flows if one alters the methods of technical communication.

## Evaluation method

Given the complexity of technical communication and the numerical difficulty of computing the present worth of future expenses, the technique that will work in most cases is simulation. $^{8}$ Financial, investment, program and strategic planners routinely use this method. Proven simulation software generators are available for model construction using high-level planning languages such as LOTUS 1-2-3, IFPS and PRISM. An experienced analyst can construct and validate a complex model in a matter of hours. Once the structure of a model is known, a logical template becomes available. To reflect unusual conditions, subsequent users need only make modifications to the logic of the template. Data forms also can be generated by the model to allow direct entry of data by clerical personnel.

## Case study

## Model construction

For the purposes of this paper I constructed a comprehensive cost and value model of the eight-stage technical documentation process. $^{9}$ The simulation consists of 59 critical variables. I applied it to a case spanning the period from 1987 to 1999. Because of space limitation, the summary Figures in this paper only hint at how this technique has been put to use in evaluating the productivity of technical documentation. The actual simulation print-out displays all the variables, assumptions and volume projections along with expense forecasts for each year. It reveals the sensitivity of the summary pay-off to changes in assumptions.

## General characteristics of the case

An office equipment firm plans to launch a product in 1988. The placement of equipment builds up from 50,000 in 1988 to a peak installation count of 356,000 machines in 1994. All product enhancements cease after 1996 and only 23,095 machines remain in place at end-of-life in 1998, even though standard service is supplied until 2005.

Engineering design and production planning are completed in 1986. Documentation and development of training materials for the product launch will be done in 1987. Service and training documentation requires 470 profusely illustrated pages. In the period from 1988 to 1992 there will be a high documentation revision rate because of engineering changes and a change in training methods.

The cost of training escalates with time, and therefore the plan calls for changing service training methods after 1990. At that time the introduction of portable micro-diagnostic tools will permit switching service documentation from an expectation of higher skills to a lower skill level. After 1990, primary reliance is placed on following tightly defined step-by-step diagnostic procedures.

## Case results

Figure 2 summarizes the key results of the simulation. It records how Stage 3 and Stage 4 costs are concentrated in 1987 and how Distribution, Training, and Use build up to year 1994, after which they fall off towards the end of product life.

The 'Total' column gives an arithmetical sum of costs in each year. The column 'DCF' represents the 1987 valuation of the annual cash flows, at a 12 per cent interest rate. Because of discounting early activities, Documentation and Production will always have a relatively higher present worth than ongoing activities such as Training and Use.

Even though the ratios of the DCF for each stage will vary from product to product, the overwhelming cost influence of most technical documentation programs is in Training and Use rather than in Documentation and Production. For instance, the present cash value of Document plus Produce plus Distribute is less than 10 per cent of the values of Train plus Use. I have seen many cases where this ratio was much smaller — Document plus Produce plus Distribute is less than 0.1 per cent of customer Value! The conclusions to be drawn from such an analysis should be readily apparent:

\- In our case the traditional Document, Produce and Distribute stages should always be sub-servient to the competitive and revenue producing missions.

\- In the public sector, the Document, Produce and Distribute stages must always be subordinated to the value of the overall mission.

\- Product introduction delays, customer dissatisfaction, errors in the uses of the product, or matters of public safety should not be tolerated. The penalties for poor performance are very severe.

\- Expensive resources should be affordable to prevent an impairment in the capacity of the organization to create added value.

\- Less than optimal efficiency in technical documentation is tolerable as long as service quality to the customer is not compromised.

\- Technological innovation in technical documentation is desirable if there is a demonstrable improvement in customer value.

\- Technical innovation, such as computer technology, is less important if it results only in production cost reductions of technical documentation.

The trade-offs between the efforts invested in the Document, Produce, or Distribute stages and the Train or Use stages require tight managerial co-ordination. Excessive efforts at cost minimization will always tend to penalize activities which take place prior to the launch of a product because cash is expended before value is created. The fallacy of this view can be seen in model results which show that any sustained inefficiencies in Stages 6 and 7 can have a strong effect on profitability. Management must see to it that poorly conceived technical documentation does not jeopardize product success. $^{19}$

## Productivity measurement

## Principles

A cost model which projects the consequences of present decisions is the preferred tool to allow the computation of productivity gains. Such a model permits locking profitable changes into revised performance targets. Accordingly, productivity is always defined as planned gain realized against a base plan.

This method recognizes that it is not possible to define productivity in any absolute sense. Productivity is seen only as a relative phenomenon. One may compare (benchmark) productivity results against those of other organizations, but one must make sure that the scope of the measurement is comparable. Isolated measures, such as the cost per page or the text produced per worker day, are not only unreliable but also incomparable.

## Application of the model

The model shown in Figure 2 will be used to assess the productivity improvement potential of an idea of linking Stage 2 activities much closer with those of Stages 3 and 5.

We will consider a proposal for on-line integration of logic coding by software designers with text outlining used by authors of diagnostics. This calls for new software and hardware investments during Stage 3. Such an approach will make it possible to accelerate the introduction of changes currently contemplated to take place only after 1990.

Figure 3 shows the calculation method, which reveals a 21 per cent productivity gain. The discounted present cash value of the current case is compared with that of the proposed alternative. Several computer runs are necessary to reach agreement about the consequences of the new approach. The discussions about priorities and budget changes are aided by a model which displays otherwise hidden assumptions about the expected results.

## Summary

Computing the productivity of technical documentation requires an explicit definition of the relationships between the various stages of work. Many critical variables affect the total productivity performance. $^{20}$

The methodology for computing productivity is derived from tools now in common use by financial and strategic planners. Constructing a simulation model of the life cycle of a technical documentation project yields practical results. Since the life of a product can span decades, I apply the discounted cash flow method to make costs in different time periods comparable. By discounting, I recognize that future cash is always less valuable than current cash. Modelling the consequences of many options makes it possible to design a technical documentation program with a better understanding than is currently the case.

Technical writers, editors and trainers will have to learn such new skills as modelling to apply the techniques suggested in this paper if they wish to participate in the successful planning and management of complex technical documentation programs.
