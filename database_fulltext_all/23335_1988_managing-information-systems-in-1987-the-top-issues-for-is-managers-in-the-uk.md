# Managing Information Systems in 1987: the Top Issues for IS Managers in the UK

T. Parker and M. Idundun, London Business School

## Executive summary

The managers in charge of information systems (IS) in the United Kingdom's 100 largest companies are putting top priority on aligning their department's goals with those of the company as a whole. A survey carried out in the spring of 1987 shows that planning and developing information systems to support corporate strategy is their prime concern, followed by the traditional issue of productivity. The survey, sponsored by Index Group and carried out by postgraduate students at the London Business School, produced several other major findings:

1. While a wide variety of companies identify alignment of IS and corporate goals as a top priority, there is considerable variation in the degree to which alignment has actually been achieved.

2. In companies that have succeeded in aligning departmental and corporate goals — so-called 'leading' companies (FT100 only) — senior management is typically well educated in IS issues, and IS management is aware of broader business issues. In less successful companies, there is a credibility gap between IS and business managers.

3. Leading companies keep managers aware of IS issues and facilitate goal alignment through such means as planning cycles, seminars and meetings.

4. Leading companies are concentrated in industries where it is competitively important to be at the leading edge in IS.

5. Leading companies, having made large investments in IS hardware and personnel, are deeply concerned about productivity.

6. Current market forces have led many firms, and especially leading companies, to emphasize recruitment and training. They are looking for IS staff with business aptitude, to help bridge the gap between the IS department and the rest of the business.

7. In most manufacturing companies, the IS head feels that senior management needs to be better informed about IS issues and opportunities.

8. In companies where senior management is familiar with IS issues and goals are aligned, IS management concentrates on such issues as making the best use of data, reducing cost, linking up with customers, and exploiting end-user computing. In other companies, efforts to bridge the credibility gap between IS and the business consume a great deal of time and energy.

## Major findings and implications The UK rankings, 1987

Information systems managers were asked to rate the importance to their jobs of 23 IS issues by giving them a score of between 1 (not important) and 4 (very important). The issues were the same as those which featured in a Datamation magazine survey of 1986 (Herbert and Hartog, 1986) and the survey methodology is described in the Appendix. The issues and their average rankings are listed in Table 1.

From this table it will be seen that goal alignment was the top-ranked issue. Goal alignment is the concurrence of overall corporate goals as perceived by the management team with the goals of the IS department. The next three concerns — strategic systems, planning and the education of senior management — all support that primary objective.

Strategic systems are the manifestation of aligned goals — that is, systems that support the overall corporate objective.

IS planning increasingly has to do with allocating resources in accordance with the needs of the business. The works of Warren McFarlan (1983), Michael Porter (1985) and others have drawn attention to the potential competitive value of information technology. As a result, a wide range of managers are beginning to participate in IS planning, representing the skills and needs of many functional departments. This interchange is accomplished by such means as despatching IS representatives throughout the organization and forming steering committees.

Particularly in companies where top management is not highly aware of IS issues, education of senior management can be a very important part of the alignment process.

The fifth-ranked IS issue is productivity. This is a particularly pressing concern in companies that have made very large investments in information systems and wish to ensure the best possible return on that commitment of resources.

Three other high-ranked issues have to do with the traditional IS roles. Software development remains a concern. Recruitment and training have become particularly important in the current UK labour market, where skilled and experienced IS staff are in short supply. As information systems penetrate all parts of the organization and become interconnected, there is more and more to be gained from efficient data utilization (inputting data only once and obtaining appropriate output wherever it is needed).

The two other issues in the top ten group are data security and telecommunications technology. To protect corporate data from mishap, fraud, or competitive attack, and to guard against invasion of individuals' privacy, data security has long been an important part of the IS mandate. One reason for respondents' high ranking of this issue may be the special problems associated with the financial institutions' increasing use of IS. Finally, the merging of telecommunications and computing technology has made telecommunications a more urgent concern for IS departments.

Among the issues of less concern, end-user computing and office automation appear to be problems that largely have been solved. Surveys conducted in the United States (there was no directly comparable earlier study in the United Kingdom) show the ranking of end-user computing dropping from second place in 1984 to fourth in 1985 and twelfth in 1986, as IS managers brought this area under control. Both end-user computing and office automation are a greater concern for industry followers than for leaders in the UK.

Leading-edge technologies do not appear to be a dominant concern. One reason for the low ranking of computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM) is that manufacturing firms made up only a small part of the survey group. Some companies, however, reported that CIM was a high priority; many of these firms were leaders in their attitude towards IS. None of the respondents gave a high rating to expert systems and artificial intelligence (AI), but several were involved in limited research and prototyping in this area.

Although centralization was seldom listed as an important issue, the reality has been significant movement along the centralization/decentralization continuum. Many IS departments are currently devolving all or part of their function to divisional or departmental levels.

Finally, two factors may have contributed to the last-place ranking of external data. First, companies may not be fully aware of the opportunities for acquiring useful proprietary information from external services. Second, among companies that do acquire external data, the process may seem so straightforward that it is simply not an important management concern.

## Comparison with the US

Figure 1 compares the results of the UK survey with findings of the analogous study conducted by Datamation magazine in the US in 1986. The horizontal bars indicate the extent and direction of difference, in terms of issues' rank positions, between the two studies. For example, the UK respondents ranked strategic systems 19 places higher than their US counterparts.

The most striking observation is the broad similarity of the rankings. In both surveys strategic issues dominated, followed by productivity. Few issues changed position dramatically. The major exception — strategic systems — reflects a difference in interpretation of the term. US respondents understood ‘strategic systems’ as systems that would create an immediate and substantial competitive advantage. UK companies understood the term to denote systems that broadly support corporate objectives.

Other issues perceived as more important in the UK than in the US are recruitment and training (presumably because of the tighter labour market) and fourth-generation languages (perhaps partly because of the time that elapsed between the surveys, as these tools had become better known by the time of the UK study). High ratings for fourth-generation languages are associated with an emphasis on productivity, as they are used primarily to improve efficiency.

Among issues ranked lower in the UK survey than in the US, the difference was greatest for office automation. End-user computing appears to be less important in the UK as a whole than in the US, and particularly so among industry leaders. Perhaps partly because of the time that elapsed between surveys, UK respondents were less concerned with problems of integration (standards have continued to converge) and data utilization.

## The IS attitude grid

In addition to assessing the importance of IS issues, respondents were asked to rate their department's and their company's aggressiveness (pro-active versus passive) in using information technology. Figure 2 shows the distribution of respondents in a matrix defined by the intersection of these two dimensions.

One might expect the fit between departmental and corporate attitudes towards IS to be an important determinant of the problems the IS function faces within its organization. Of course, the concerns of many IS managers arise from the industry within which their companies operate, rather than organizational circumstances. But given the perceived importance of goal alignment in particular, the internal situation also matters. For example, if the IS department takes a very pro-active stance towards the use of information technology but the company as a whole does not, one might expect the education of senior management to be a major concern of the IS head. If top management and the IS head concur in a pro-active approach, then attention might shift to such issues as productivity.

Analysis confirmed that there were significant relationships between a company's position in the attitude grid (Figure 2) and the issues of concern to its IS manager. The quadrants of the matrix correspond to four characteristic IS roles in the organization:

\- Pioneer IS departments help their companies stay competitive by equipping an aggressive management team with systems that support corporate strategies.

\- Missionary departments recognize the opportunities that information technology offers for competitive advantage (or the dangers of not keeping up with aggressive competitors), but have difficulty in conveying their message to the corporate management team.

\- The clerks are IS departments that fulfil a largely routine and traditional data-processing role in companies that are equally content with a passive approach to information technology.

## The pioneers

In pioneer companies, both IS management and top management take a pro-active approach to information technology. Figure 3 shows the six top-ranked issues for these companies, comparing the pioneers' ranking of each issue with that of the survey group overall.

Pioneer IS managers recognize the importance of aligning their departments with the company's overall objectives. As one of these managers remarked, 'Information technology should not be an ego trip for technologists – it should move the business forward.' Conversely, these companies regard information technology as an investment, not just a cost, and are willing to fund new systems that provide demonstrable business benefits.

Goal alignment was the top priority for pioneers, as for every grid category; moreover, these companies had taken steps to ensure that alignment was actually achieved. Most of the pioneer IS managers had had other management experience. Through the planning cycle, regular meetings, and seminars with other managers, pioneer IS heads estimated that alignment, on average, was about three-quarters complete.

Two related top-ranked issues in this category were planning (the primary vehicle for ensuring that the IS department is functioning in support of the business) and strategic systems (the concrete outcome of planning and goal alignment).

Productivity has become an especially important concern for pioneers, who gave it a higher average rating than did the survey group overall. Operating in industries where information technology is competitively important, many of these companies have invested heavily in strategic systems. As one pioneer IS manager remarked, 'Now we want value for money.'

As hardware costs fall and labour markets tighten, increasing the relative cost of personnel, productivity becomes an even more important consideration. Pioneers are concerned about the quality of people they recruit as well as their costs. They want to find people with management potential; some of these companies are actively pursuing IS staff with business aptitude, who will be able to interact effectively with the business management team.

New UK legislation such as the Data Protection Act has heightened the importance of data security. This issue is of special concern to IS managers in financial institutions, where the value and volume of transactions can be very high. The fact that all such institutions in the survey were classified as pioneers has raised the importance of this issue within this group. Many pioneers were also concerned about preventing competitive access to their data.

Many of the pioneer companies operated in industries where IS has become crucial to competitiveness. (In fact, all the financial institutions and retailers surveyed fell into this quadrant of the grid.) In these industries, environmental pressures require the IS function to push ahead merely to maintain current competitive positioning; a complacent company could quickly fall behind. Pioneers in industries where innovation in information technology is not yet an established competitive tool may have greater scope to rewrite the rules of competition.

## The missionaries

Missionary companies have pro-active IS managers but passive top management teams. Their rankings of issues are shown in Figure 4. The top six issues for the missionaries were the same as for the survey group overall, with a small difference in ranking within the group.

Because of the need to drive their message home to an unreceptive management team, these IS managers listed education of senior management as their second most important issue. With one exception (a property company), all the missionaries were manufacturing companies. These respondents often complained that senior management was not familiar with IS issues and was reluctant to devote time to learning more about this area. As one missionary IS head commented, 'For the successful use of information technology, top management must not only understand what it is and what it can do, but what opportunities it can create.'

Recruitment and training were particularly important to missionary IS managers, who tended to feel that their current resources were inadequate and that the organization needed more staff members with a broader perspective. Many of these respondents noted that good training can impart confidence and motivation, as well as company-specific skills, to technical staff.

End-user computing and office automation (ranked 7th and 13th respectively and not shown in Figure 4) are more important for missionaries than for pioneers, who have already brought the personal computer revolution under control. Without the support of senior management, missionary IS departments have been less able to keep up with new applications of information technology.

As in the survey group overall, about two-thirds of the missionary IS heads had other business experience. These companies, however, tend to be governed largely by financial controls and to regard the IS department as a cost centre. There are few mechanisms for aligning the IS function with corporate goals. In these respects missionary companies differ markedly from the pioneers. Missionary IS department heads also tend to occupy a lower position than their pioneer counterparts in the management structure of the company. Missionary IS managers know that top management awareness and commitment are essential to exploit IS opportunities for competitive advantage. The communication gap between IS and top management is the most fundamental problem in these companies.

## The clerks

In clerk companies, passive attitudes toward information technology prevail both among top management and in the IS function itself. As shown in Figure 5, this group of IS managers shares many of the same concerns as other companies surveyed. They have an unusually strong interest in telecommunications, however.

IS managers in clerk companies feel that they are serving the needs of their business even if they are not particularly innovative in their use of information technology. One commented, 'My function is restricted to more or less routine transaction processing. There is no overall IS management that would include the company's other applications such as CIM and engineering research.' Others reported that they enjoyed a monopoly position, with little threat of competition, or that their industry was so 'low-tech' that there was little scope to be more aggressive with IS. Nevertheless this group is concerned about management education, to ensure that any opportunities that do arise are not overlooked.

Clerk respondents' special concern with telecommunications may be due in part to their role as managers of a central data processing function, responsible for gathering data from all parts of the organization. It may also reflect a more technical, less market-oriented perspective than is found among pioneers and missionaries. Only 20 per cent of these IS managers have had non-IS, business management experience.

Are the clerks correct in that more aggressive applications of IS could do little to benefit their businesses? No business is entirely immune from the threat of competition, and few industries are so low-tech that there is no room for IS-based improvement in product or process, particularly given the many new tools now becoming available. The clerks may be doing nicely at the moment, but they run the risk of being overtaken by change.

## The referee

In only one company did the IS department head describe himself as less aggressive than top management with regard to information technology. The issue ratings of this single referee, a high-tech company involved in telecommunications, are shown in Figure 6.

Because of the nature of its business, this company's senior management team is thoroughly familiar with the available IS opportunities. The IS department head faces the unusual problem of 'moderating the enthusiasm' of 'managers who know all the answers and want everything at once'. In this environment goal alignment is readily achieved, and the IS manager can focus on such issues as customer-distributor link-ups, data utilization, increasing the flexibility of existing systems, and exploiting the full potential of end-user computing.

## Conclusion

The broad picture, then, shows IS managers seeking to align IS with corporate strategy. Strategic systems and planning are an important part of this process, as is education of senior management in those companies where management is not abreast of IS developments.

The grid used in this analysis (see Figure 7) provides a framework for looking at those problems which arise out of the organizational context of the IS department. How close a given company plots to the upward diagonal on the grid serves as a proxy for the closeness of goal alignment between the IS department and the company as a whole. As such, this analysis probes those problems which are related to the alignment of goals - currently IS managers' greatest concern.

There is still a great deal of scope, however, for further work on some of the more technical issues. Why, for instance, are fourth-generation languages of relatively minor importance when they hold promise for increased productivity (which is rated 5th overall)? Why is acquisition and use of external data rated so low when IS managers purport to have such an external and strategic perspective? And finally, how have IS departments apparently brought end-user computing under control?

## Appendix

## Study background

This study was conducted by Tim Parker and Margarethe Idundun of the London Business School on behalf of Index Group, an information technology management consulting firm with offices in Cambridge, Massachusetts and in Los Angeles, London and Paris.

To provide a basis for comparisons with the United States and to gain some historical perspective, the study was modelled on one conducted by Datamation magazine in late 1986 among US Fortune 1000 companies. Analogous US studies had also been conducted in 1984 and 1985. (No comparable studies have been undertaken in the UK.)

## Study approach

Questionnaires were sent to the 100 largest UK companies as identified by the FT100. When possible, the enquiry was addressed to the head of IS for the company. (This information was taken from the Computer Users' Handbook, 1987.) In certain companies the respondent was the IS manager of a single division that operated as a separate business. In one case the respondent was a data-processing manager in a company that was involved in few IS activities and had no overall head of IS. His responses were typical of those classified as clerks.

Respondents were asked to rate each of the 23 issues listed in Table 1 on a scale of 1 to 4, depending on its importance to their job as head of IS. In addition, respondents were asked to explain why the top-ranked issues were important to them and to answer a few questions about their company, their department, and their own experience and qualifications.

Almost half (45 per cent) of the questionnaires were completed and returned. After preliminary analysis of the data, tentative conclusions were refined and validated through in-depth interviews with the single 'referee' respondent and two respondents from each of the other quadrants of the attitude grid (Figure 2). The study's conclusions, as outlined in this report, were presented to interested respondents on 27 April, 1987, at the Institute of Directors in London.
