# Effectiveness and Competition – Linking Business Strategy, Organizational Culture and the Use of Information Technology

Ewan Sutherland, Faculty of Social Sciences and Business Studies, Polytechnic of Central London, and Yves Morieux, Arthur D. Little Inc.

Abstract: The relationships between business strategy, organizational culture and the use of information technology are examined in order to see how overall organizational effectiveness and competitiveness are affected. The interference between these and the limits each imposes on the other are identified.

## Introduction

Managers are being bombarded with suggestions about how to make improvements to their firms in terms of competitiveness, effectiveness and efficiency. $^{1, 2, 3, 4, 5}$ Invariably these are offered in isolation from other such prescriptions and often in competition with them. Individually they may do good, but a multiplicity of approaches is likely to be less effective than a single all-embracing approach, if only because of the effects of interference. However, such an approach is not yet available and may not even be possible. Therefore, the best compromise is to consider the interaction, interference and interrelationships between different approaches and the limits imposed by these.

We have discussed elsewhere the interactions between organizational culture and the use of IT. $^{6}$ However, the rising tide of advice on coupling the use of IT with business strategy has increased the importance of other relationships and requires further and wider consideration.

We offer a guide to the relationships between three important areas (business strategy, organizational culture and the use of IT) which have been the subject of much of this type of advice.

## Themes

We have identified business strategy, organizational culture and the use of IT as some of the major areas where potentially conflicting advice is being offered on improving organizational performance.

## Business strategy

Strategy, at whatever level in a corporation it is applied, is management within some degrees of freedom: technological, economic, legal etc. The perspective from which these are identified and assessed varies according to the environment within which the strategy will be implemented. The environment may include clients, suppliers, competitors, shareholders, banks, governments etc. For example, Andrews $^{7}$ defines corporate strategy as:

... the pattern of decisions in a company that determines and reveals its objectives, purposes, or goals, produces the principal policies and plans for achieving those goals, and defines the range of business the company is to pursue, the kind of economic and human organisation it is or intends to be, and the nature of the economic and non-economic contribution it intends to make to its shareholders, employees, customers, and communities ...

At the level of the business unit, strategy will focus on the first part of his definition, relating to objectives and goals, ignoring the more general issues which will have been decided at corporate level.

There are two important points when considering business strategy: first to focus on actions by the organization and not on mere paper policies, and second to distinguish business strategy from the simple manipulation of products and markets.

## Organizational culture

Organizational culture is a set of emotional conditions, views of the past and standards of behaviour belonging collectively to an organization. The combination of these elements and the way in which members of the organization perceive them influence organizational processes.

In classical terms, organizational culture is seen as deep-set beliefs about the ways in which work should be organized, the ways in which authority should be exercised and the ways in which people should be rewarded and restrained. $^{8}$ Organizational climate usually refers to distinctive characteristics of an organization which influence the behaviour of the members. $^{9}$ Corporate culture is used in a much more pointed way, to identify single objectives – usually attention to customers – which should be given absolute priority and where senior executives should work to reinforce this. $^{10}$

By concentrating on the relationships between members of the organization (especially decision makers) and the influence these have on organizational procedures processes, it is possible to bring together these three approaches.

## The use of information technology

It is not primarily the inventory of IT equipment and the information systems which an organization happens to possess that matter; it is the use made of them.

In most medium and large organizations, transaction processing will have been fully automated for some years and there will be few potential benefits in the further automation of repetitious tasks. Therefore, the focus has moved to the use of IT to support business functions.

Many of the current applications lie in the areas of office information systems (OISs) and end-user computing (EUC) where difficulties arise from the involvement of staff who are neither IT specialists nor trained in the ways of using IT. Artificial intelligence and improved man-machine interfaces have helped, and promise more help, in providing systems which are less opaque and less difficult to use. An equally serious interface problem exists in ensuring adequate relationships between IT professionals and other staff.

Falling costs continue to make systems easier to cost-justify, and permit more managers to buy IT systems out of their own budgets. This frees managers from bureaucratic controls but often leaves them without adequate technical advice.

Integration of telecommunications, information systems and office information systems, e.g. unified voice and data networks, highlights the problems of divided control over IT and the need for an infrastructure.

## Relationships

Nothing in an organization can or does stand in isolation, although it is often easier to treat it as if it does. We suggest that it is now more useful to consider relationships between the three identified areas than to ignore them.

For as long as the use of IT was saving money it was not necessary to consider its potential role in competitive performance. While organizations were able to adapt to the changes in their environments there was little need to consider the organizational culture, let alone try to change its orientation. While products continued unchanged for years, business strategy was unimportant.

Today, it is no longer sensible to see business strategy, organizational culture and the use of IT in isolation. Instead they have to be seen as making a contribution to the overall performance of the organization. To take this view requires an understanding of the relationships between the areas we have identified.

## Business strategy and organizational culture

Certain aspects of the business strategy will, for better or for worse, be embedded in the organizational culture. The general attitudes of an organization towards its clients, suppliers, staff and to technology will be well known. If an organization is 'bureaucratic' then it is not going to give its clients the highest priority. Therefore, if a new strategy is considered which would require that absolute attention be given to clients' requirements, a bureaucratic culture is a very serious obstacle.

The organizational culture is a subset of the variables affecting the way in which the firm must work; it is both a blessing and a curse. If the organizational culture suits the business strategy, then it will reinforce the strategic direction. However, if the organizational culture is radically different from the business strategy, senior management are forced into the difficult task of changing the culture, or else they must identify a strategy which can be reconciled with the organizational culture.

The divestiture from American Telephone and Telegraph of the 'Baby Bells', and the denationalization of British Telecommunications, have required massive changes in organizational culture. Great leaps forward have been made in these firms, but organizational cultures are inherently slow to change.

Not only is it sensible for the various strategies of a firm to be aligned, in the sense of 'strategic fit'; it is also desirable that they be compatible with the organizational culture. $^{11}$

## Organizational culture and the use of information technology

The organizational culture influences the uptake of IT; it can be either resistant or welcoming and it will usually be selective in this (e.g. ready acceptance of flexible manufacturing systems with reluctance to have office information systems). It can range from over-enthusiastic and unquestioning 'IT mania' to the outright rejection and cynical dismissal which constitute 'IT phobia'.

The nature of the culture of the main organization and of the information systems department (they are not necessarily the same) will help to determine the manner in which IT is introduced to staff – in particular, the extent to which staff are allowed to participate in decision making and in the quality of the support provided to them.

The use of IT can change the organizational culture in several ways. Information systems will contain part of the existing culture, but will also contain alien elements caused by structures embedded in the software and omissions caused by misunderstanding by systems analysts. In particular, communications technologies open up new channels and new types of communications and in doing so change the culture.

Where the organizational culture fits well with the current and proposed uses of IT then there are usually high levels of appropriate use and rapid adoption of new systems. Where the fit is poor, problems of inappropriate use and slow adoption will arise.

## The use of IT and business strategy

There are three categories of linkage between IT and business strategy:

\- IT considered in implementation of business strategy;

\- IT considered in formulation of business strategy;

\- IT used to assist the formulation of business strategy.

These categories are neither rigid nor exclusive. There is also 'IT planning' which relates only to the strategy of the information systems function, where it is lacking any significant link to business strategy.

That the use of IT will allow improved internal operations is well known and well established. It is clear that the use of IT can, in extreme cases, change the rules of the business game by creating new products or by changing the means of distributing existing products. However, it is much easier to classify this than to indicate how it should be done.

It is in the creation of new markets, the alteration of entry barriers to existing markets and the modification of so-called natural boundaries between independent activities, that the greatest opportunities and dangers lie. $^{12}$ For example, in the production of newspapers the application of IT has dramatically cut the costs of establishing a new newspaper, although it has become obvious that being able to produce a newspaper cheaply is far from enough to ensure success, e.g. Today (UK). In banking and finance it has added to the forces for consolidation through the need to achieve larger networks with wider coverage for cash dispensers, treasury systems and dealer systems.

Whether or not the use of IT is considered in the formulation of the business strategy, it is desirable to use IT to support the business strategy. This is most commonly done through methodologies such as Rockart's which is based on critical success factors (CSFs). $^{13}$ Eastlake $^{14}$ and Curtice $^{15}$ have both devised similar methodologies which attempt to evaluate existing and proposed IT investments in terms of their contribution to the strategic objectives of the corporation or business unit.

A number of systems have been devised to help senior managers in their work of formulating business strategy — these fall into two principal groups: decision support systems (DSSs) and strategic information systems. DSSs allow the executive to experiment with a number of possibilities with a model reporting back on the likely outcome. Strategic information systems provide information about competitors and economic indicators.

The PIMS (Profit Impact of Marketing Strategy) database, run by the Strategic Policy Institute, is a particularly good example because it is both an external information system and a model. Over 2,000 companies provide information to the database and can test existing and possible strategies using a number of models with data pooled from all participants. $^{16}$

## Complexity and three-way links

By taking a single and comprehensive approach to the three areas, it is possible to identify a far-reaching change. Until now, organization theory has divided organizations into sub-units, each with its own culture. Similarly, the foundations of business strategy have lain in strategic segmentation, in which a firm's activities are seen to relate to independent battlefields.

However, the use of IT is changing the basis of these approaches. Interconnection and integration are eroding barriers between the sub-units which are differentiated by culture and strategy. The growing use of information systems which link different organizations (e.g. clients to suppliers through ICL's Tradanet) changes market and cultural barriers. The sharing of values and technology, brought about by progress in the use of IT, draws a new geography of organizations. The result is that both organizational culture and business strategy are in need of reappraisal.

Wyman $^{17}$ of A T & T has warned of ‘technological myopia’ where firms and managers fail to see the potential effect of applications of new technological developments on their businesses. He identifies the biggest barrier as being the business culture.

The CEO must believe — and make others believe — that the firm's fortunes hinge upon the successful application of technology.

However, it is not clear that his distinction between technological and marketing myopia is valid. It seems more likely that both are examples of strategic myopia, caused by too much concentration on making products or getting rid of them and too little on meeting the real needs of customers.

The growth in end-user computing has been instigated to help ensure that users get the facilities and the information they want. However, devolution of this sort is not immediately compatible with linking the use of IT to business strategy. There is a requirement for a framework to co-ordinate and control applications, together with support for users. There must be delegation of power and responsibility from the formal IS departments to informal IS units and individuals in functional and line management. This requires the community of potential users to be willing to accept these ideas and that the necessary support be provided. Overall control is required, to the extent that critical applications be given priority and that costs be contained. $^{18}$

## Conclusion

Any one of the three areas which we have identified is sufficiently complex to be studied in its own right and at considerable length. Consideration of the relationships between these areas might seem to be introducing unnecessary complexity. However, it is clear that the benefits of greater efficiency through the use of IT lie in the past and that future benefits must lie in the areas of effectiveness and competition.

To achieve such goals requires much more than straightforward automation of manual systems. The use of IT requires to be coupled to business strategy in a transparent and effective way. At the same time, control of areas of IT must be passed from the information systems department to operational departments. To achieve the changes requires a wide-ranging framework within which organizational culture is a major component.
