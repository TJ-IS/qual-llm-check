# The New Industrial Society and its Infrastructure

Ian Priban\*, BCI Research Ltd.

## Introduction

My purpose is (i) to provoke recognition of a rare opportunity for progress offered by a new infrastructure and (ii) to stimulate the pursuit of the sea of enterprises arising from it. The United Kingdom is reaching a point in history when with the increasing pace of technological development, many pieces of a bigger dynamic picture are falling into place and forming a new pattern. The recognition of this bigger picture, with shortening innovation lead-times, will enable a sea of emerging enterprises to contribute automatically to the creation of a new industrial society.

The day must come when [information] will be for everyone, as the waters of the rivers and the wind of the heavens. It should not merely be supplied, but lavished, that men may use it at their will, as the air they breathe.

Emile Zola used these words in 1901 to stimulate the far-sighted development of the electricity supply: giving the cleanliness, comfort and convenience of energy at the touch of a switch. Now I want to promote the development of the utility of the 21st century Information Society.

When giving my talk I invited members of the audience to tell us briefly about their particular interests, and these are summarized below:

\- How do we bring together dispersed information?

\- How does the individual, or a company, fit in as provider and user of information?

\- How do we improve the strategic use of information in our company?

\- What are the opportunities for small businesses as providers of information and value-added services?

I now invite the reader to define his interests and objectives before he reads on to recognize how they are facets of a new strategic perspective of opportunities.

## The opportunity

We coined the term 'Multiway Information Media' (MIM) to describe the infrastructure which has switched-star broad-band cable technology at its core and high definition video-interactivity as its functional feature. The other communications technologies such as satellite, cellular radio phone, local broadcasting and narrowcasting and Multi-point Microwave Distribution Service (MMDS) will be important links and feeders. With MIM, however, communication will be as different from one-way television as the telephone was to the telegraph. When the public recognizes this qualitative difference, it will know that any community without MIM will be as deprived as those communities felt themselves to be which, in the mid-19th century, were without a railway connection.

Like the roadways, waterways, railways and motorways in their time, MIM — or simply the 'multiways' — will accelerate the pace of progress, add value to trade and create wealth in the economy. It will link people within the global information economy. MIM will reduce congestion and wasteful physical transport of people and goods, because information in the form of electrons can be moved quickly and economically.

The multiways will change the way we:

\- identify needs and market our products
- travel and communicate
- develop and maintain health
- entertain and inform ourselves
- train ourselves
- live and use our time
- handle, spend and transfer money
- save and invest
- provide security
- value the things we do
- express our views about society
- pay our taxes
- research and develop, and exploit innovations
- optimize our freedom of action and use of resources
- regulate our activities
- influence each other
- organize government.

Imagine a map of the future economic landscape and project on to it the UK's unfolding of MIM infrastructure. Then assume conservative penetrations. Further, add 'intelligent homes' and 'intelligent commercial buildings' (automated factories and offices) that are linked by MIM. The map will make it clear that every aspect of life, as we know it, must change!

To recognize the new opportunities against a foreground of sweeping changes across UK society we must start from a new baseline. This baseline must show that Britain is poised to replay history. Three hundred years ago a fundamental shift in outlook opened new horizons, and new confidence spontaneously unleashed a surge of progress. Enterprising individuals who instinctively felt their actions to be desirable made a clean break with the old habits, simply by pursuing the new high-growth activities. Now MIM will link the fragmented pockets of innovation in offices, factories and homes to information and retailing services. It will become the highway of a dynamic information market and a new industrial society.

## Forces of change

To enable you to see the innovative leaps that can be made, a sound intellectual framework and a spirit of optimism are needed. To maximize one's chances of success that framework must define the new co-ordinates and the economic dividing line between new MIM infrastructure and the established media infrastructure which is becoming obsolete.

The UK has the building blocks (hardware, software and courseware), a relaxed regulatory attitude and a climate of growing expectations to give us a competitive edge. From this advantage, and against a world demand for learning the English language, British enterprise can create the infrastructure and global markets of the information age. $^{1}$ To succeed, we must target on the new technology, not the old. We must recognize that growth surges can emerge spontaneously triggered by a shift in outlook.

Britain led the first Industrial Revolution without competition for leadership with other countries. To succeed now it must match the pace of developments of the front runners in the world economy. Failure to do so will consign the laggard organizations of the UK to the scrapheap. The world has entered a second Industrial Revolution. Its driving forces are the perceived advantages from the application of information technology (IT), and new thinking, to virtually all other aspects of industry and life in general.

The signs of the second Industrial Revolution can be seen everywhere: in business (from manufacturing, distribution, retailing to the financial and other service industries), among professional users, and with consumers in the home. The disturbing fact is, however, that while the object of each of these developments is to manage resources and time, when viewed together they are fragmented. There is almost no general recognition in the UK of the tremendous opportunity for growth and complete economic renewal from linking the many fragmented developments by a national MIM infrastructure. As long as these initiatives are fragmented, they cannot generate the critical mass required for sustained economic growth.

Speculators are a driving force of progress. They create expectations and tangible reasons for optimism about the future. The UK could not have been the front runner in the first Industrial Revolution without them. One can speculate by cable. The betting shop and the stock market can be replaced by the TV key-pad in the home. But, before speculating, a sound intellectual foundation has to be built.

## Strategic vision

The UK has moved out of the phase in which the context of evolution was stable. In the stable phase a new technology was added to the existing system, and growth in each area of society proceeded by small incremental steps with little reference to changes in the other areas. We are now in the phase where structural change is simultaneous across the whole economy $^{2}$ and we can see that MIM will affect every aspect of society. It is essential therefore to be absolutely clear about the factors outside one's usual sphere of interest which will govern the success, or failure, of a venture.

Three forces are shaping society. Two are obvious: political and technological. The least obvious is conceptual: the quality of understanding (the constructs and mental infrastructure) needed to produce a picture of opportunities and advantages that the application of communications and information technology (new thinking) will give. Such a picture is the essential tool for organizing people and managing resources to pursue the identified opportunities. The lever to attain critical mass from innumerable fragmented initiatives is the MIM infrastructure. The trigger to new growth will be the main players' joint perception of the bigger opportunity, seen in terms of the shared frame of reference within which each player's vision is embedded.

From the perspective of economic development, the important feature is not the individual new products but the multiplier effect, produced by the convergence of diverse interests and technologies. Market making on such an extensive scale requires a diverse set of interdependent producers to work together to lead the way. When technological advance is fast, market research cannot say what is possible, or estimate demand and prices. There is a need for market-making by co-operation on an extensive scale, involving complementary enterprises with convergent interests. Creative jumps which will offer better choice and lower prices are best achieved by competition between groupings of co-operating enterprises. The traditional centralist bureaucratic mechanism (and piecemeal sector-by-sector intervention) is too slow for successful market-making on a global scale.

When cable was first promoted publicly in 1982-83, using descriptions such as 'the wired society', there seemed much promise but what was actually offered was largely an extension of existing TV driven by entertainment. The significance of interactivity and the sea of innovations which would be made possible were not recognized. Why pay again for cable when we already have TV? With such a prevailing attitude the case for all the possible new services was never made. As a result the fledgling cable industry is fragmented, relatively under-capitalized and its growth has been slow. The dominant structures of the BBC and Independent TV have been holding back the development of the new services which will take us into the 21st century. $^{3,4}$

The door was opened to the great opportunities arising from the multiways and the stimulation of new enterprise across UK society by the Telecommunications Act 1984 and the Cable and Broadcasting Act 1984. For instance, the Telecommunications Act says that the Secretary of State (Department of Trade and Industry) and the

Director-General of Telecommunications have a duty:

1. to promote the interests of consumers, purchasers and other users ... in respect of the prices charged for, and the quality and variety of, telecommunication services provided ...;

2. to maintain and promote effective competition between persons engaged in commercial activities connected with telecommunications in the United Kingdom;

3. to promote efficiency and economy on the part of such persons;

4. to promote research into and the development and use of new techniques by such persons.

There is a big difference between active, market-making promotion and passive promotion. This is especially true when the MIM market in general is poorly understood. $^{14}$ , $^{20}$ Enterprise not government or its agencies can spearhead such MIM innovation.

In 1986 the Peacock Committee opened the door to the great opportunity offered by the new infrastructure. Though its brief was to look at financing of the BBC, it went beyond that and began to lay intellectual cornerstones for UK telecommunications in the 21st century. The conclusion is that British broadcasting should move towards a sophisticated market system based on consumer sovereignty. Viewers (and listeners) are the best ultimate judges of their own interests, if they have true freedom of choice. It also concluded that there will always be a need for supplementing the consumer market by publicly financed public service programmes. Quality sponsorship by corporate and individual private patrons rather than state patronage, which will widen the choice of the educational or so-called public service programmes, should also be possible. It would open up the broadcasting market to the competitive production of quality programmes.

Interactive cable is beginning to offer the consumer a wide spectrum of choice which the BBC and ITV cannot do. Subscribers can select what they want at the time they want it. Cable will also change how advertisers promote themselves and their products. A viewer of so-called 'infomercials' $^{5}$ can control the products he wants to see, covering his range of interests. Such direct interaction builds positive promoter-viewer relationships which are specific to a viewer. It produces information that is far more useful to an advertiser than the present sampled approach. A viewer is less likely to feel that some advertising is an unnecessary expense and insult to the senses. The viewer switches off and this is recorded. Such statistics will show clearly how to reduce marketing (advertising and promotion) costs and obtain greater benefits.

## Innovators versus status quo

Historically the interplay between the forces of innovation and those digging in to maintain the status quo shows a recurring pattern, the so-called 'long wave' (Kondratieff cycle), of about 50 years' duration. $^{5,6}$ The end of each such wave is marked by a discontinuity, preceded by rising social and economic instability, and monetary disorder. $^{7}$ We happen to be at the end of such a long wave and the world economy is in a phase of fundamental dislocation. The pattern of unfolding events is in some ways ominously like that of the 1930s. Each crisis (e.g. the 1973 energy crisis, and the 1976 Sterling crisis from which we were rescued by North Sea oil revenue) gave rise to piecemeal adjustments, merely shelving, not resolving, the underlying problem of fundamental economic reform.

Growth surges are historically part of the changing pattern of events. They are the positive turning-points of the long wave. They have their roots in the advantages seen from the use of basic technologies and new infrastructures. They appear when the forces of innovation take off to outrun the vested interests which are associated with stagnant productivity and diminishing markets, restrictive practices and overmanned producer coalitions.

According to Mensch the Western world will stagnate until capital overpowers its aversion to risk-taking once it recognizes that (in the depression phase of the long wave) there is absolutely no more profit to be squeezed out of the old technologies. $^{5}$ Only then will entrepreneurs and investors pursue the new opportunities arising from available technologies.

The next long wave will be sourced by new basic inventions made during the wave now ending. About 90 per cent of all knowledge in the science has been generated in the last 30 years, the so-called 'technology explosion'. This development can be expected to continue, doubling again in the next 13 years. The UK's knowledge base, albeit fragmented, is enormous. To benefit from it and the coming wave of possibilities there must be full appreciation of the opportunities, and we must change our attitudes and institutions.

There is a still longer-term cycle of change. The UK's relative industrial decline was recognized as early as the 1850s. $^{8}$ But not one of the initiatives by successive governments was radical enough to breach the barriers obstructing innovation and halt decline. Methods successfully used in Germany (requiring organization from the top) were tried, but failed to work in UK conditions. The spirit of rivalry between different German States and regions (their banks, commerce, industry and civil servants) spurred innovation. Combined with their thoroughness in technical and commercial education, a spirit of competition constantly stimulated German innovation.

We can now break out from this longer-term cycle of decline! The problem facing innovators today is similar to that identified two centuries ago by the scientist Joseph Priestly, 1733-1804:

We are, as it were, laying gunpowder, grain by grain, under the old building of error and superstition, which with a single spark may hereafter inflame, so as to produce an instantaneous explosion, in consequence of which that edifice, the creation of which has been the work of ages, may be overturned in a moment and so effectively as that the same foundation can never be built upon again.

According to Arthur C. Clarke, to the technically uneducated any science and advanced technology is like magic. $^{17}$ There is an important role for the IT community, delivered, for instance, through the AIT: Self-education of members about the wider strategic implications of its many activities. In turn it can contribute to such education of the layman so that he does not need to see IT as magic, but as tools to be used with wisdom.

## Market-making structures

When the structures of the UK institutions are laid alongside those of Japan's big industry-finance-trade groupings (each representing broadly-based, powerful market-making interests), our relative disadvantage is apparent. Our interest groups are mostly sector-based, self-centred, producer lobbies which have demanded protective intervention for themselves, stunting the evolution of the free market. By not having constructive and far-sighted commercial relationships with groups from other sectors, they have opposed change. They have thereby not only failed to safeguard the larger national interest, but have undermined it. $^{9, 10}$ Japan's market-making structures and pace-setting mechanisms will be powerful factors governing the growth of the global MIM infrastructure and information markets, and the UK's balance of payments for electronics import-export trade.

The United States freed its telecommunications enterprise by breaking up its monopoly into seven smaller, competing units. Japan has followed. Pace-setting companies in North America and South-East Asia, and the UK's Cable and Wireless are now determining the global telecommunications scene.

Japan maintains its economic thrust in pace with quickly advancing technologies. Its big industryfinance-trade groupings compete with each other in a spirit of mutual understanding and shared purpose to capture world markets. They stimulate creativity and encourage innovative leaps. With four competing domestic telecommunications carriers, Japan is now poised to develop a dominant position globally. $^{11, 12, 13, 14}$

Nippon Telephone and Telegraph Corporation has an enormous investment programme in broad-band optical fibre to create an integrated network system (INS or BISDN), because of their great need for visual image transmission. They are therefore targeting beyond the integrated services digital network (ISDN) narrow-band stage, which British Telecom is installing to update its voice and data capacity. Japan's large global trading houses will be the principal beneficiaries, automating their services to side-step recession (controlled depression) by moving into information broking services. $^{12, 13}$

Japan was shocked into reforming and modernizing itself radically in 1868 after 265 years of feudal life and self-imposed isolation from foreign influence. It was stimulated by external factors: a demonstration of technical superiority by the US and the view of civil conflict undermining the sovereignty and independence of neighbouring China. It cut loose from its past to accept Westernization politically and technically, building on centuries of communal effort and values (the notion of loyalty to the team).

Initially Japanese industrial companies were state-owned, but in the 1880s the government started privatization of all (except defence-related) companies to stimulate competition. Such privatization enabled Japan to move quickly from a mature feudal system (with a well-organized nationwide commodity market) through a transition to a capitalist society. $^{9}$

The Japanese are thorough; everything that is happening in the world must be understood within their frame of reference, and their actions brought into conformity with a goal. Japan's Ministry for International Trade and Industry and its Think-Tanks (e.g. Nomura Research Institute and Mitsubishi Research Institute) are vision builders and pace-setting initiators, drawing attention to new opportunities at the frontiers of knowledge. Such opportunities are then fully defined in a pre-competitive phase of collaboration, and positioned on the economic landscape. A 'product concept' is then created, making tangible the opportunity in hand to everyone involved. It becomes the focus, drawing together commercial interests with complementary resources in large competing groups. As an organizing factor, it ensures that all facets of exploitation, from research to development, production process, selection of market niche, appreciation of user needs and behaviour, are fully understood and integrated into one innovation plan. By considering every aspect of market entry and establishing a common understanding, implementation is accelerated.

The Japanese are successful, not so much because they work harder than others but because they work together.

Foreigners in Britain see that the boundless individualism bordering on anarchy is a characteristic of the British. They also recognize that there have been spontaneous shifts in attitude to another extreme, coherent action when there is a commonly perceived immediate threat. Such shifts occurred, for instance, in the Second World War and the Falklands War. The recognition that external factors could undermine our sovereignty and economic independence, and that there is a rare opportunity for making tremendous progress, can now stimulate the British to cut loose from the past. I rely on numerous groups of creative individualists to now make such a move!

The British have the confidence to be different.
Sir John Harvey Jones said:

You squash individuality at your peril and that is what will defeat the Japanese. Their ability to harness big battalions was a good trick, but tomorrow will be a very different world with small groups linked to others, like a beehive.

He had such confidence and transformed ICI. In the mid-1930s Sir Henry Tizard had the confidence and willpower to develop the UK's radar radio infrastructure without which we could not have won the Battle of Britain. Only his nerve and persistence in standing up to the establishment secured final success. Now we must be dedicated to building a better future for ourselves by harnessing the latest infrastructure.

Attempts by previous governments to halt Britain's industrial decline, which began in the 1850s, have failed because they were not radical enough. In 1980 Mrs Thatcher started a radical reform process to break the UK's institutional deadlock. She has prepared the ground for change,

creating an enterprise culture and removing regulations. Now the British can move themselves into the 21st century with the initiatives coming from within society, from business and anywhere else within the community, rising upward and outward. They cannot be imposed from the top.

## Nature of change

Even in the 15th century, when the pace of change was slow, printing was carried in 30 years to key cities throughout Europe. This revolution was fuelled by human needs and technology. The citizen demanded to know what was going on in the world; printing, paper and ink technologies enabled publishers to produce and distribute information quickly. The laborious manual production of books by copyists was transformed into print-runs of 1,500 copies, in a fraction of the time. Commercial and educational information, broadsheets, maps, books and 'new world' atlases were published. Spelling, grammar, punctuation and the layout of text became standardized. These advances helped people to communicate, and overpower confusion, and revolutionized thinking.

Starting in 1830, the world's first railway network was built by speculators with private capital to span the country within 30 years. New understanding and ideas played an equally important part, but a largely neglected one. The development of the UK railway network was piecemeal in relation to American railroad development. The latter was a military-type operation where good logistic management using the telegraph and financial management (separation of capital and operational accounting) contributed to the high growth and profitability of the early US railroads. $^{15}$

Britain was the first country to enter (make) the industrial era and had the most dynamic economy at the start of the 19th century. $^{19}$ However, its industrial and financial interests began to move along divergent paths, and the pursuit of commercial and financial activities in world markets – rather than the production of wealth by UK manufacturing industry – became dominating influences in the national economy. Financial and trading interests became distanced from UK manufacturing industry and technology.

Specialization led to fragmentation. With these changes the financing of industrial research and development, and risky projects, which had been done by speculators and patrons, became a responsibility of government. There is now a deeply-rooted public belief that, regardless of the level of efficiency of the 'public innovation support mechanism', government must be responsible for funding such projects. Government cannot create wealth, but it must set the rules for the creation and distribution of wealth. It creates the climate in which the discovery process of the market will evolve at full throttle under its entrepreneurial steam.

The UK's industrial policy has been subject to short-term political influence. The historical division of economic activity into narrow interest sectors and a tripartite mechanism (government, industry and labour), with open channels for lobbying and rent-seeking, has enshrined the country's long-run problem of organized, producer-group pressure as an institution. Short-term political fashions have in the past too often set industrial strategy, rather than the national strategic (economic) needs determining the strategic policy of the economy.

The pace of change is accelerating. Without a vision as the new frame of reference we will remain confused and our expectations will be uncertain. However, we do have the technologies to understand what is going on in the world, to inform and re-educate all those who want to educate themselves and create and better world. Now personal computers (PCs) are helping anyone who tries to write and communicate faster, and to manage complex projects and respond quickly to change. The UK is a front runner in the PC user league. Marketing skills have accelerated the growth of PC users. In 1985 Amstrad created a new business and home PC market by offering users the PC they want at a low price including a good range of software – the 'Amstrad Effect'. The educational technology industry is also growing fast. The pace of advance, which is set by technology (hardware and software) is phenomenal.

Many businesses cannot operate efficiently without the electronic infrastructure. It may provide instant market intelligence or fast, low-cost completion of stock bargains. Or the point-of-sale terminal at a supermarket check-out offers direct debiting, cutting high overhead costs. The linking of such systems – 'islands of automation', intelligent systems in business and the professions, or consumers in the home – by the multiways will improve greatly their communication and the overall efficiency of their transactions. It will accelerate the speed of innovation, cut lead-times from research to sales and reduce the investment needed for taking an invention to commercial exploitation.

The principles governing the success of almost any of the individual developments associated with 'automation' apply equally across all sectors of the economy. It is crucial in order to benefit fully from the application of such principles and acknowledge their strategic value from the start of any new developments.

## Rising to the challenge

The UK is reaching a point in history when many developments and reforms will fall into place forming a new pattern. While there are reasons for concern about recession, we must also recognize that the fundamentals of the economy are being transformed. The 1987 Stock Market Crash is different from the 1929 Crash which signalled the Great Depression. Now we are in the midst of a great move towards a high-level information society of the 21st century. How individuals think and organize themselves is again undergoing a revolution. Now the crucial stimulus for progress is the ‘creative spark’ of the individuals, who are fired to do new things by taking advantage of new technology. This spark is there in everyone. We are poised to make a great advance and form a new industrial society.

The lever to raise the efficiency and creativity in all areas of the economy to those levels which already exist in good businesses is the Multiway Information Media. The strategic deployment of information technology, new know-how and thinking, to every aspect of life (business and the professions, industry and services, and consumers in the home) will give competitive advantage. Only the market can harness the enormous potential for growth by generating the critical mass required for economic lift-off. To balance supply (technology and infrastructure) and demand (creative market-making forces), we start with a vision of the full scope of opportunity which shows how the multiways will unleash electronic-based global growth by linking innumerable 'beehive initiatives', operating like a nervous system of the information society.

While the cable industry was seen as fragmented and under-capitalized in relation to the greater opportunity, the 1987 Cable Convention showed the new dimensions and thrust which are arising in this growing part of the communications industry. $^{18}$ Non-entertainment services, starting with voice telephony, are becoming important revenue generators. The BBC and ITV dominance in broadcasting is under challenge. $^{4}$ Do they offer real choice? No, they do not. There are some good programmes but the freedom of choice is very restricted. $^{3}$ How restricted viewers are, will not be obvious until there is a true alternative. The progress of cable enterprise so far has been slow in terms of the superficial expectations of the early 1980s. However, the consolidation of existing developments and totally new approaches to cable development and investment will soon change the quality and pattern of growth.

To break the slow growth impasse (associated with entertainment-driven financing of cable development) and move to faster growth, a new method is being used which integrates a range of revenue-generating products and services from the start of cable operation. National Telecable's Project Beany in Bolton is the first pilot project to use an integrated approach to residential cable development.

We have set up an MIM Think-Tank which also has an integrated approach to development. It starts with a common frame of reference for all members (including developers, operators, manufacturers, providers and a range of users). $^{2}$ They represent a group of forward-looking principals intending to make the markets of the global information economy. They aim to gain the advantage by coming to a quicker understanding of new ground rules and accelerating the convergence of their particular development across the entire field of MIM opportunity.

The most important agents for creating wealth and initiating any necessary changes for a better life are managers. We must rely on diverse management interests to co-operate and provide the intellectual leadership for national and commercial goal-oriented strategic thinking to produce far-reaching solutions and prosperity.

To move now from the old sector-based centralist-interventionist structures to market-making enterprise groupings with industry-finance-trade interests coming together, there must be an intellectual bridgehead. Several pace-setting (integrating) think-tanks are needed. By competing with each other they will boost the momentum as the transition from the old to the new is made. At the same time the structural reform of those UK institutions, such as broadcasting, education, health, science and the civil service, can be consolidated into numerous coherent processes using the multiways as the lever. The efficiency and creativity can thereby be raised to the levels already seen in good business.

Each such think-tank then develops a programme specific to its members' interests and those of the community (locality) of which they are a part. The members can then form the ventures and dynamic structures needed to compete, for instance, with the far-sighted industry-finance-trade groupings. Dynamic broadly-based groupings are a better way to exploit UK research. For instance, the work in the Alvey Programme co-operative projects, can be financed and exploited entirely in the market. There is no need to depend on slow centralist bureaucratic intervention as proposed by the Bide 86 IT Committee. $^{19}$ Such parallel competing (pace-setting and integrating) approaches can meet the UK's economic needs using the MIM infrastructure as the key.

## National Big-Bang: strategy at a stroke

In 1980 the UK saw a new political force emerging. It had already started to develop its intellectual roots and engender a positive attitude to change in 1974 under the leadership of Mrs Thatcher and Sir Keith Joseph in the Centre for Policy Studies.

The momentum of the new political force has been maintained in the face of established beliefs trying to maintain the status quo.

The ground for change has been prepared. A new enterprise culture is emerging and unnecessary regulations are being removed. The time has come for a new breed of entrepreneurs to move Britain into the 21st century. Such initiatives can come from business and anywhere else within the community.

A strategic vision of the highest level can produce an overlapping and co-ordinated set of policy decisions which will trigger such radical reform.

Incremental change is too slow. We need a National Big-Bang. We need it to stimulate UK industry, finance and trade interests to link with those from complementary sectors to form broadly-based groupings at a stroke. Such a move would start a contest and stimulate players to form such groupings to make innovative jumps. It would maximize the chances of progress covering the whole economy. It would be triggered by those entrepreneur barons who are far-sighted and almost ready to become the main players. They will share a common vision at the higher level of a new 'S-curve of opportunity', which includes reforms of institutions such as broadcasting, education, health, science, the civil service, and the legal and medical professions.

New expectations will then unleash demand for and investment in IT and the MIM infrastructure and enterprises, and in new know-how, particularly strategic knowledge and intelligence about the bigger opportunity. The front runners will both determine the course and set the pace of innovation. The challenge, therefore, is to move preemptively. The forces unleashed by a Big Bang can quickly pursue opportunities within the new field of potential across the whole economy. It would help to loosen the stranglehold of interventionist lobbies, and monopolistic and restrictive practices.

Only a political decision, based on a strategic vision at the highest level, can trigger such a Big-Bang. Mrs Thatcher can make such a move. What is needed is an event, similar to the City's Big-Bang but with better preparation. With it structural reforms of inefficient institutions would be consolidated into a multitude of coherent processes. With the multiways as the lever, these areas of the economy would be brought up to the levels of efficiency and creativity already seen in much of industry. Only an overlapping and co-ordinated set of policy decisions, governed by a strategic vision at the highest level, can make this happen.

However, a set of ground rules must be satisfied so that the transitional phase of radical institutional reform will be short, with minimum instability. This will ensure that the transition is effectively managed, leading to the new functions (such as listed earlier). $^{3, 16}$

We can learn from the City's Big-Bang. It set free a spirit of enthusiastic challenge, opened new horizons and unleashed enterprise. A far-sighted decision to reform the Stock Market started the process which imposed self-determined organizational changes on those wanting to be players, by bringing forward the use of communications and information technology and integrating it into their new organization (the electronic trading pit). There was a shake-out of those organizations that had not made the necessary changes on time. Self-regulation works in the City, where there is enlightened long-term self-interest and everyone understands and plays by the rules.

A National Big-Bang can draw together groupings of interests and resources from complementary sectors at a stroke. It is a quick way to unleash market forces to maximize progress across the whole economy. We can cut loose from past practices and benefit from the qualities of the British that normally appear only in crises. The time is right for the British to move themselves into the 21st century. Initiatives must come from business and anywhere else within the community, rising upward and outward. They cannot be imposed from the top.

A stimulus for a spontaneous shift in national outlook can be a political decision at the highest level, reactions to the budget or the market's recognition of a new regulatory framework freeing tremendous opportunities. Such a shift can come about with the recognition that diverse pieces of a complex problem have fallen into place as facets of an integral process. In turn, when such recognition is widespread, it can trigger the crucial Big-Bang which will start the New Industrial Society.

How many long-distance innovators will now make pre-emptive moves to exploit their ideas? How long will it take for a new perception of the political-economic circumstances to trigger a process leading to a crucial Big-Bang? How many readers will now go to their personal computer, or terminal, to use their expert system on political-economic and corporate, or personal, strategy to examine when and how best to make a preemptive move? Are you going to be among the first to make a move?

## Conclusion and summary

A new communications (and broadcasting) infrastructure, the Multiway Information Media is the lever of progress. Numerous developments are falling into place to form a new pattern showing a bigger, radically reforming process of society. It shows how fragmented initiatives, which are using IT and new thinking, are being drawn together. Taken together with institutional reforms of broadcasting, education, health, science, and the civil service, such initiatives are changing every aspect of life.

They are facets of a bigger process of reform. Incremental change is too slow. The British are moving themselves into the 21st century. Initiatives are now coming from within society, from business and within the community, rising upward and outward. They cannot be imposed from the top. We have a unique opportunity to raise the efficiency and creativity in all areas of the economy to those levels already existing in good business.

A National Big-Bang is needed to stimulate industry, finance and trade interests to link with those from complementary sectors to form broadly-based groupings. Strategic vision and vitality are necessary to harness the full scope of opportunity for tremendous growth and wealth creation within the market.

A spontaneous shift in national outlook – whether stimulated by a political decision at the highest level, by reactions to the budget or the market's recognition of a new regulatory framework which is beginning to free tremendous opportunities — can trigger the crucial Big-Bang and start the New Industrial Society.
