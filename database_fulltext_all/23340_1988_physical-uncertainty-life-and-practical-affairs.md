# Physical Uncertainty, Life and Practical Affairs

Gordon F. Scarrott,

## Introduction

At first sight there seems to be no connection between physical uncertainty and practical affairs. However, in the sense of intrinsic unpredictability in detail, uncertainty is fundamental in our present understanding of the physical world, in which living organisms exist and consequently should be included in any theoretical analysis. It should therefore be possible to trace a connection between the familiar intrinsic unpredictability in the behaviour of living organisms, including our fellows and ourselves, and the intrinsic unpredictability in the behaviour of small objects that has been recognized by physicists since Boltzmann and Heisenberg. This paper is an attempt to establish such a connection by consideration of the essential mechanisms of life and the relevance of physical uncertainty to their operation.

The resultant conjecture that some of the observed intrinsic unpredictability of organized human affairs is indeed derived from physical uncertainty is of great significance in practical affairs; in particular it offers a credible explanation of some of the difficulties encountered on the frontiers of evolving information engineering.

## Physical uncertainty

To the layman the concept of uncertainty is a glimpse of the obvious, unworthy to be raised to the status of a principle. To physicists it is a concession to reality that was slowly and painfully admitted to the list of conceptual foundations. When mathematical physics was first applied to the analysis and interpretation of astronomical observations, it was so successful that in the eighteenth century the universe at every scale was regarded as a machine whose operations could in principle be fully described by logical arguments enshrined in mathematics. Although it was obviously not practicable to know all the relevant details, in the eighteenth century such knowledge was assumed to be possible.

Now, 200 years later, physicists have reformulated the laws of nature so that this determinism of the behaviour of large objects is retained but the behaviour of a very small object is recognized to be intrinsically unpredictable. This recognition – that for a very small object precise prediction is impossible and not merely impracticable – was a bold conceptual step with many implications and it is of course now known as Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. Fundamental to the uncertainty principle is that events deemed to be 'uncertain' cannot even in principle be predicted by logical argument.

## Life

Although much progress has been made on disentangling the basic mechanisms of life, many mysteries remain so that it is not easy to identify in physical terms the essential common factor of living organisms. At a more abstract level, however, the essence of life is given away by the use of the word 'organism'. It is, of course, 'organization'. Every living organism can properly be regarded as an organized system (OS) of interdependent components whose physical activities complement one another to make a coherent whole.

Many of the components are themselves organized systems so that formally it is necessary to define an OS recursively: an OS is an interdependent assembly of OSs and/or 'elementary information devices'.

It must be recognized that elementary information devices in natural and artificial systems are not necessarily functionally identical. The definition then applies to any organized system, natural or artificial, and the recursion in the definition enables it to apply to organized systems at every scale, molecular systems within a cell, cells, vital organs, individuals, social groups, mixed artificial and natural systems, and so on right up to the whole biosphere.

To operate an OS the physical activities of each of its components must be controlled and, regardless of the detailed physical mechanisms by which the control signal is generated and obeyed, control necessitates power amplification to permit the control signal to operate at a lower power level than the action that is being controlled. Moreover, since energy is the ultimate scarce resource in nature, living organisms for the duration of their existence on earth have been subject to evolutionary pressure to achieve the precision of control appropriate to their mode of existence with minimum energy consumption in their control mechanisms. We may therefore conjecture that the elementary information devices in living organisms operate at as low a power level as possible. Thus the question arises: What determines the lowest power level at which an elementary information device in a living organism can be expected to operate satisfactorily?

## Physical uncertainty in a living control system

Control power cannot be arbitrarily small. In any conceivable information device each element of information is necessarily conveyed by transferring a parcel of energy to the receiver of the information, and the parcel must be large compared with energy that the receiver might by chance possess due to known physical mechanisms. The equipartition principle asserts that at a temperature of T degrees Kelvin every degree of freedom of a physical system has a statistically fluctuating energy with average value $\frac{1}{2}$ KT where K is Boltzmann's constant, and this of course applies to the receiver mechanism of a living information device regardless of how it operates in detail. $\frac{1}{2}$ KT is so small that the consequential power requirement for present artificial information technology is of only academic interest, but it would be expected that natural information devices have evolved to minimize signal energy and consequently to nudge the Boltzmann limit.

We do not know in detail how information devices in living organisms operate but we can be sure that they are based on molecular processes at an exceedingly low power level since, for example, some $10^{10}$ cells in the human brain consume only a few watts. Thus, regardless of the detailed physical/chemical mechanisms of information transfer in a living system we may expect that each element of information is represented by a parcel of energy whose magnitude is narrowly constrained by the laws of nature. If it were any smaller the control system would suffer from excessive random errors, and if it were any larger the natural selection process would have adopted an information technology with smaller energy consumption since economy in the use of energy has obvious survival value.

It is therefore to be expected that the behaviour of a typical living organism in response to changes in its environment is not totally determinate, but includes variations ultimately derived from physical uncertainty that cannot be explained or predicted by logical argument since they are caused by the actions of very small objects in the living control system. There is obvious support for this conjecture from the common observation that living organisms die with quite a small rise in temperature and it is very likely that the control mechanism fails first. However, the conjecture is not based on any experimental work and lacks quantitative verifiability since we do not yet know enough about the information handling mechanisms in living organisms.

## Some practical consequences

The question of the relevance of physical uncertainty in the mechanisms of life has been raised before, notably by Schroedinger, but it has generally been assumed that such matters are of little consequence in practical affairs. With the introduction of artificial information technology and ambitious endeavours to devise artificial aids to human judgement, however, it is now clear that explicit recognition of physical uncertainty in living organisms is of more than academic interest.

1. The living organism of most interest to mankind is, of course, mankind itself. We can all recognize that the behaviour of people including ourselves has a random component that Yorkshiremen have long recognized in the saying 'There's nowt so queer as folk'. Although we all recognize that human affairs are not totally predictable we also cherish the illusion that variations in human judgement and consequent behaviour can always be resolved by logical argument. If the conjectures in this paper are valid this illusion may have to be abandoned, and with it some of the ambitions widely discussed under the title 'Artificial Intelligence' since computers as at present understood are essentially logical argument machines.

2. For many years the evolution of artificial information technology has been driven by the imperatives to operate faster and use less energy per logical operation, and these objectives are still being pursued. Indeed some of the recent proposals have been directed to the conception of logical devices on a molecular scale with correspondingly low power consumption. Even the present generation of logical devices is sensitive to stray radioactivity so that the current drive to ever smaller devices will eventually lead to a technology that is error prone for basic physical reasons. This will necessitate an entirely new approach to system design that might be derived from a better understanding of living control systems since they have already survived prolonged testing in the fiercely competitive world of living organisms.

3. Many designers of artificial organized systems, steeped in the determinism of digital technology, consider physical uncertainty to be irrelevant to the practice of their trade. However, on account of the recursive definition of the concept of an organized system, a responsible designer should recognize that his system justifies its existence by its contribution to the coherent operation of a larger system that necessarily includes people, since the ultimate purpose of every information system is to serve people. Consequently the intrinsic uncertainty of people's desires infects the system designer's objectives even if his digital implementation techniques are fully determinate.

4. Evidently natural information systems in living organisms are quite distinct from artificial information systems such as computers. Natural systems are constructed from large numbers of slow, intrinsically unreliable elements, but they enable living organisms to achieve formidable feats of real-time control, e.g. walking on two legs. Natural information systems also provide a versatile pattern recognition facility that plays an essential role in the survival technique of all species, and in mankind provides a primitive judgement technique that is continually used in daily life. Our natural information system, however, is slow and prone to error when used to formulate and follow logical argument.

Artificial information systems, on the other hand, are constructed from fast logical elements that are assumed to be totally trustworthy. They can manipulate symbols to represent logical argument at a speed enormously higher than any natural information system, but they cannot compete with natural systems on pattern recognition tasks and offer only clumsy techniques for real-time control of physical action. Digital computers were, of course, conceived by the pioneers as logical argument machines so that it is not surprising that recent attempts to use their descendants to simulate human pattern recognition have run into difficulties. There are many requirements for pattern recognition machines and it would appear that meeting such requirements will necessitate a bold departure from established computer design practice explicitly directed to solve the pattern recognition problem. Indeed the need for such a bold departure has already been recognized by a few pioneers in the academic world. They have built pattern recognition systems that are not based on a conventional computer, but the performance of such systems is still far short of our human capability of identifying pattern signals in the presence of disturbing noise.

5. In the evolutionary sense primitive judgement is obviously much older than logical argument. Indeed it is not difficult to suggest how the basic techniques of logical argument could have grown out of primitive judgement in the course of the evolution of natural language by the addition to the vocabulary of idealized, easily recognizable abstract concepts. We all share a proper ambition to formulate logical arguments to support our primitive judgements in daily life. Since primitive judgement preceded logical argument in the evolution of our information handling skills, however, we cannot be sure that it is always possible to rationalize our own or anyone else's primitive judgement. Hence, although honest rationalization is sometimes possible, circumstances exist where no amount of computer power will enable human judgement to be predicted or simulated, and this proposition is essentially the same as the physicist's recognition that no amount of computing power will enable him to defy physical uncertainty.

## Conclusions

This paper covers only an introduction to an aspect of the foundations of information engineering that has received little attention. Moreover, the foundation of the argument is only a conjecture with no experimental support, so that the most basic conclusion is that a good case can be made for seeking supporting evidence, e.g.:

\- Is it possible to identify a common elementary information transfer process in living organisms?

\- How much energy is transferred for how much information?

\- Can we deduce the consequential intrinsic error rate?

\- Does it account for observed variability in the behaviour of the organism?

Even if some of these questions have already been answered, there is evidently a need to bring such information together and so contribute to the formulation of a science of information that is plainly needed to guide the deployment of microelectronic information technology.

If the basic conjecture survives - that control signals in living organisms are inevitably contaminated by noise - some other conclusions can be drawn:

1. No user's functional specification for an information system can be trusted unless it includes reference to the need for manageable adjustment of its function to follow evolving user needs;

2. Prospective users of artificial intelligence systems usually expect them to provide artificial judgement in the sense of faster, more trustworthy, or tireless judgement compared with natural human judgement. Mankind uses two quite distinct techniques for judgement — primitive judgement inherited from his remote ancestors, and logical argument, a comparatively recent addition to the catalogue of human skills. In principle it is possible to effect artificial judgement if the judgement in question can be resolved into logical argument, even if the argument is of such complexity that it cannot be followed by the unaided human mind, but no amount of computing power can simulate or predict primitive judgement on account of the intrinsic 'noise' in primitive judgement.

3. The matters that have been discussed are not only of concern to specialists; they are also relevant to practical men of affairs. For example the proposition that people are not deterministic machines is hardly news to anyone with experience of practical affairs. However, the suggestion that this common observation can also be derived from the operation of well established laws of nature is potentially significant since the laws of nature cannot be repealed.

Biographical notes

G.G. Scarrott worked from 1946 to 1953 at the Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge developing instruments for nuclear physics, culminating in the development, in collaboration with G.W. Hutchinson, of one of the first practicable pulse height analysers, for which the Physical Society's Duddell medal was awarded. Following this he joined Ferranti Computer Department and was appointed Chief Research Engineer in 1956. In 1967 he was appointed Manager of ICL Research and Advanced Development, which position he held until he retired from ICL in 1981. In 1980 he was elected a fellow of the Fellowship of Engineering. He has had published many articles and papers on subjects ranging from electronics for nuclear physics to the organization of design and development.
