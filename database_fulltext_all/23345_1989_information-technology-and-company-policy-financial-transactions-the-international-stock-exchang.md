# Information Technology and Company Policy: Financial Transactions — The International Stock Exchange

Henry Holloway, Consultant

## Introduction

This paper is complementary to that on information technology (IT) in the banking institutions of UK business (Holloway, 1988b) and is the fifth in a series investigating the application of IT in different industry sectors. Other papers have looked at newspapers (Holloway, 1987a), electronic publishing (Holloway, 1987b) and the holiday and travel industry (Holloway, 1988a).

Throughout the series information technology (IT) is taken to mean the whole range of computer facilities for the acquisition, storage, transmission, retrieval, processing and output of information.

Financial transactions are carried out not only in high street or retail banking but also in a wide range of organizations which deal in speciality money markets, futures, commodities, insurance and shares. Recently a great deal of attention has focused on the use of IT in the international stock market and consequently the International Stock Exchange is chosen as a company profile.

## Deregulation

As was the case with retail banking (Holloway, 1988b), other business areas involving financial transactions have been subjected to new legislation as well as an intense application of IT in recent years. Therefore, in order to assess the true effect of IT, the changes associated with this new legislation need to be considered in parallel with those following from the introduction of IT.

In retail banking the major effect of new legislation is to subject the high street banks to increasing competition from new traders in their markets: building societies and insurance companies. Similar effects are expected in other fiscal markets, but in the stock market no major new traders have emerged so far. The entry of, for example a leading building society such as Abbey National cannot be ruled out as a future development.

Governments are always changing legislation and affecting the world of finance, and the UK is no exception. However, fiscal changes of the past few years show a different pattern when compared to previous policy. There has been a movement away from short-term changes which have produced long-term distortions to supply the demand in financial markets. Recent changes are expected to make the UK financial sector more competitive by amending laws which gave an advantage to specific organizations, such as tax relief on life insurance. Other changes worth mentioning are the reduction in stamp duty and elimination of some of the restrictions on building societies.

One key piece of legislation is the Financial Services Act of 1986. This provides a framework for the regulation of financial institutions which entails a large measure of self-regulation for each specialized area. The Self-Regulatory Organizations (SROs) are under the charge of a Securities and Investment Board (SIB) appointed by the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI).

The International Stock Exchange is now an SRO within this framework. Its status could have been entirely different because prior to an agreement reached in 1983, the Stock Exchange was facing a long and costly law suit arising from the Restrictive Practices Act (Galletly and Ritchie, 1986, p22). The agreement between the Secretary of State for Trade and Industry and the Chairman of the Stock Exchange exempted the Exchange from restrictive practices legislation, provided fixed minimum commissions were abandoned and outside firms were allowed entry. It was this agreement that led the way to deregulation and the rush to automate in the City the 'Big Bang' of 1986.

## Company profile: The International Stock Exchange London

Stocks were originally traded in the coffee houses and walkways of the city near the site of the present building. The growth of the stock market led to the need for a permanent site and the first stock exchange building was opened in 1801. This lasted until it was replaced by a purpose-built tower, 26 storeys high, which opened for trading on 27 October 1986.

Shortly after this, on 12 November 1986, the members of the London Stock Exchange voted for a merger with the International Securities Regulatory Organization (ISRO) and the joint body became the International Stock Exchange (ISE).

The computer system introduced for the 1986 opening was determined largely by the time constraints for building it. As indicated above, deregulation (and the consequent need for automation) was initiated in 1983. This gave the Stock Exchange only three years to select, commission and install a system. In these circumstances, it was reasonable to build on the computer installations then in place. The best way of doing this was to model the system on the National Association of Securities Dealers Automatic Quotation (NASDAQ) system. Despite the name, NASDAQ had developed from a national system linking offices throughout the United States to an international one with terminals throughout the world, although predominately in Europe. NASDAQ is the third largest stock market in the world, after New York and Tokyo.

Topic, a videotex price and information system, had been in use since 1979. This utilized DEC VAX and Modcomp computers to provide 100,000 screen pages accessed from around 3,000 terminals. The decision was taken to build the Stock Exchange Automated Quotations System (SEAQ) on this base. More computers were added to provide sufficient support for up to 12,000 terminals while retaining the bank of 100,000 pages. SEAQ is not an automated dealing system; deals are placed over telephone lines.

SEAQ terminals display information on up to 3,500 stocks in three classifications; alpha, beta and gamma. Alpha stocks are the most actively traded, and information on business transacted has to be reported immediately by market-makers. Beta stocks are less active and information is not updated as rapidly as for alpha stocks. In both alpha and beta stocks the prices have to be firm. Gamma stocks are less active and the prices are indicative rather than firm.

A great deal of money and effort went into the preparation of SEAQ for launch on 27 October 1987. In the event the worst fear of dealers was realized when the system crashed before trading started. Topic was swamped with enquiries by those curious to see the new system. This initial failure was followed by several more. The first steps to curing the problem involved reducing the services offered by Topic and adding more computer power.

Another system was in use prior to 1986. Completely separate from SEAQ, Talisman (Transfer Accounting Lodgement for Investors, Stock Management for Principals) was introduced in 1979 after two years of development at a cost of £10m. This central settlement system now checks and validates as bargains are entered, facilitates settlement by moving stock against same-day funds and aids market surveillance with details of the client, time and price of each transaction.

Talisman, originally designed for 20,000 transactions per day, was successfully handling over 100,000 at the peak time during the market crash of 1987. Talisman began life as a central batch processing system with software written in PL/1 embodying some million lines of code. It has evolved through the integration of on-line techniques and relational daabases so that it can be seen as the core part of an on-line distributed settlement and depository service. This has been accomplished by a programme of enhancements encompassing some 25 major developments, all introduced without disrupting the day-to-day running of the market. Over £5m was spent between 1984 and 1986 to increase the capacity of Talisman. A further £3.5m in development was undertaken between 1986 and 1988 for Institutional Net Settlement (INS). A further £14m is scheduled to be spent in enhancing Talisman as part of the Taurus (Transfer and Automated Registration of Uncertificated Stock) system.

INS extended the availability of Talisman across the City, enabling institutional investors to settle with one payment all bargains transacted during the account with any number of counter-parties. (This is estimated to have eliminated some 28,000 cheques and 33,000 stock notes in each account.)

SEAQ was initiated as an information system with deals conducted over the telephone. This has inherent disadvantages and it was always intended to move to an automatic dealing system when feasible. On 13 February 1989, ISE launched its SEAQ Automated Execution Facility (SAEF) providing an automatic electronic trading link between brokers and market makers. A buy or sell order is made by tapping the appropriate key on a

SAEF terminal. SAEF transacts the order, and passes the information to SEAQ for reporting and to Talisman for checking.

ISE now has a wide range of computer-based services in addition to SEAQ and SAEF such as SEAQ International (similar to SEAQ but for major non-UK equities) and SEQUAL (a trade matching service for international equities). The ISE has put forward a plan to its members for integrating the separate services into one overall trading service. The outcome depends upon the agreement of the members but in any case there will be partial integration so that SEAQ and SEAQ International will combine to provide a common quotes processing service and SAEF will be expanded.

This is separate from the development of Taurus, which is now to be an enhancement and expansion of the Talisman system. The ISE also considered two other options for Taurus: a discrete central database holding all relevant data and a comprehensive communications network linking registrars. The first was ruled out on the grounds of expense and duplication of existing databases while the second, in effect a distributed database, would not fully satisfy the objectives of Taurus.

In the operation of Talisman during each sale titles are transferred from the registered holder to Sepon Ltd until payment is completed, when the registration is transferred to the new holder. This procedure protects the interests of the buyer at all times. This facility has been limited, so far, to Member Firm market makers and principal traders. Under Taurus it is proposed to extend this facility to Direct Account Operating Controllers (DAOCs).

The ISE has invested heavily in IT. In addition to the computer sytems described above, some £5m has been spent on a System X digital telephone exchange. This links regional stock exchanges at Glasgow, Liverpool, Manchester, Birmingham and Dublin with the London site. The ISE has a structured IT policy which is outlined in a policy document The Way Forward (Anon, 1989). This outlines the migration to an integration and enhancement of trading services in a series of steps phased over a three- to five-year period.

## Conclusion

National and international pressures to speed up security dealing forced the London Stock Exchange (now the ISE) to implement IT on a large scale. The Exchange was no stranger to computer systems and had been using them since the late 1970s, but the launch of SEAQ in 1986 indicated a bolder commitment to IT. Computers became more visible, not just providing a behind-the-scenes service.

Despite the breakdowns of the service which occurred from the very first day before dealing had commenced — and still occur from time to time — the system has been a success. However, it does not go far enough. There is a clear requirement for safely completed trade by electronic means. This entails further expense and the Exchange is to be complimented on pursuing this direction at a time when the crash of 1987 continues to have an effect. (Member companies are estimated to have suffered losses of between £500m and £750m in 1988.)

The greatest expense in dealing relates to the enormous time tied up in paper-based operations. Where computer systems are used, print-outs continue to be in demand either because of inadequacies of the systems or because people are more comfortable handling paper. There would be immediate benefits in removing the mountain of paper that clogs the system. This is shown by the estimated savings in cheques and stock notes achieved by Talisman.

As greater use is made of computers, the major fear is that the systems will fuel massive falls in the market. It has been suggested that the use of computer systems was a major factor in the Wall Street crash of October 1987. Indeed, there is no question that the faster rate of completion of deals provided by computers speeded events by a factor of ten. (The market fall in 1987 took one week compared to a three-month period in 1929 for a similar fall). However, the sinister question is: Did the computers cause the fall? Ince (1987) says:

What is causing concern to financial analysts is that all the prediction programs used in Wall Street and the City of London essentially use the same statistical theory. They will act in concert when they see the markets rise and fall. So as more and more companies use these programs, there is the danger of massive swings in the markets, particularly in the currency markets, which are eminently suited to computer analysis, and where some degree of volatility has been the norm for the last decade.

In order to prevent this, companies have been adopting the ‘expert systems’ approach to a new generation of predictive programs. These will offer traders a pool of advice as if coming from a team of experts. The advice will include options, explanations, forecasts and warnings of unexpected changes in market conditions. Taurus will incorporate this approach.

IT is used for financial transactions to speed up deals and to ensure accuracy. Where systems reduce or eliminate paper there are further advantages of time and cost savings.

## Biographical notes

Formerly a teacher serving in Uganda and New Zealand as well as England, Henry Holloway became involved in research in association with work for his MSc in Cybernetics from Chelsea College. He then worked on various research projects concerned with the effects of new technology in printing and publishing for Pira, the research association of the UK paper and board, printing and packaging industries. He was awarded his Doctorate by Aston University in 1983 for studies of the future role of the printing industry. Dr Holloway is a consultant in the area of printing and information technology.

He was a member of the AIT Committee from its inception to 1989 and founder treasurer, and has served as Assistant Editor of the Journal of Information Technology.

Dr Holloway has written a number of reports and is a regular contributor to journals and the trade press.
