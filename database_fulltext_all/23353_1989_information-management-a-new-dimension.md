# Information Management: A New Dimension

Ivan F. Jackson, Management Information Systems Group, Victoria University of Wellington

Abstract: The DP/MIS function has traditionally been concerned with the acquisition, manipulation and delivery of internally generated data. Typical reporting systems are unable to cope with a mixture of short term control information and broad strategic information, as well as information generated from external sources.

The move to a distributed environment has led to a need for the management of information flows, and specifically with the management of integrated data, often directed at the very top levels of management. There has been a recognition of the need for a formal information management programme and in response to this a new type of information professional is emerging – one trained specifically in the management of information as a corporate resource. This person may well have different skills from a systems analyst, possibly more oriented towards improving the efficiency of operational procedures. The information management profession must understand the technology, especially in the communications area, must be able to sell to and educate at various management levels and be able to formulate and implement a plan for increasing the effectiveness of the use of the information resource.

## Introduction

The traditional DP/MIS function has been concerned with the capture, manipulation and delivery of internally generated data. Much of the technology has centred around the mainframe. In the last several years there has been an explosion of computer power which, while initially uncoordinated, is now gradually being brought under centralised control. In the meantime, however, end users have established significant local databases. Under this new and relatively stable environment a new discipline is emerging – that of information management – primarily concerned with the delivery of information (both internal and external) to managers and executives.

This paper explores the concepts and driving forces behind the increased importance of information management. It looks at issues of responsibility for information management, and how a corporate information programme might be introduced. Finally, it examines the skills required of the growing number of information professionals working in the area.

## Information and the corporation

## Information, power and competition

Walter B. Wriston, a former Chairman & CEO of Citicorp has interesting things to say on the subject of information within the organisation (Financial Executive, 1987):

Timely information has always conferred power, both in the commercial and in the political marketplace. But as the availability and timeliness of information continues to increase some of the more traditional sources of power (e.g., natural resources) are declining.

'Today, management structures are being flattened and sharply reduced by those who really understand the impact of technology on business. The need for layers of management is being reduced everywhere, when information becomes available to more and more people at all levels at a faster rate.'

One of our leadership tasks for tomorrow is to design . . . data bases which are important to our own businesses . . . Anything that enhances the value of a company's data, and makes it more available to executives who lack computer skills in general, cannot help but improve the performance of our companies.'

'Corporations may have to develop a formal information strategy, just as they've developed a formal business strategy, or a formal financial strategy. As all successful companies are market driven, timely access to market information must not only be put in place, but it has to be linked to the internal MIS system.'

This extensive quote has been included as a kind of challenge. We are not talking about technology, nor even necessarily about the application of technology. Rather we are talking about remaining competitive in a difficult marketplace; and about new ways of managing organisations! A key issue is the provision of an effective information management policy.

## Improved service - the baseline for action

A recent European study reinforced the marketing oriented view expressed above (Tomlin, 1988). A survey of 300 IS executives in major European corporations listed ‘significant benefits expected from Information Systems (IS) by 1990’. Top benefits are:

• Giving better service to customers (64%)

\- Improving competitive position (55%)

\- Increasing managerial effectiveness (53%)

\- Increasing staff productivity (44%)

\- Improving the quality of products (42%)

Future priorities in IS development showed:

• Executive decision support (57%)

\- Replacing outdated systems (55%)

• Office automation (50%)

• Electronic trading links (39%)

• International telecommunications (32%)

What is interesting is that apart from the less obvious outdated systems replacement issue, all of the above priorities involve either improving the quality of data available to decision makers, or in moving that data around the firm or to or from other firms, in short information management! (Even the replacement issue is related to the unsuitability of current systems in delivering necessary information.)

## Information for managers

In the ‘good old days’ DP students were taught that the function of a data processing system was to ‘provide information for management’. We know now that the operational (or transaction processing) systems form the backbone of processing activity and the MIS adjunct provides, in summary or exception form, information about the state of the business. Associated with the MIS are the decision/management/executive support systems that provide a quick, and often transient, view of a business activity. In conjunction with the old view, we also learned about the ‘management triangle’ with information being ‘summarised’ as it progressed up the organisation (cf. Anthony, 1965).

This view is certainly not held by researchers at the University of Sheffield (Wilson, 1987). They found, in a study of 79 manufacturing companies, that ‘the neat (triangular) categorisation of information under such headings as ‘strategic planning’, ‘management control’ and ‘operational control’ turn out to be ‘fuzzier in the real world than in theory’. It was found that if one uses the ‘natural’ organisational divisions of production, sales, marketing, personnel and finance, it becomes evident that ‘different individuals pursuing different kinds of tasks within functional divisions, overlap quite significantly in their needs for information.’

In particular, the Sheffield research showed that both short-term, control information (e.g. financial information) and broad, strategic information (e.g. marketing) are regarded as important or very important by people in both strategic and operational roles. Interestingly enough, the only group to fit the stereotype was the financial group. The finding from the study was that people in all kind of roles needed all kinds of information and ‘although some clear relationships between role and information type appeared, in general the picture was much more diffuse than proponents of particular models had suggested’.

Another result emerged from the Sheffield research. The following information was rated as ‘very important’ by a majority of the respondents in the study:

Company general accounts

Stock levels in company

Delivery performance

Company targets

New product information.

To the researchers the message was clear: ‘information means internal information’. They concluded that ‘the management of externally generated information may well be of greater significance for R & D, but the day-to-day business of a company is seen to depend more upon the effective management of the internal information resource.’

Wilson postulates that it could be that the respondents were unaware of sources of information outside the firm. In a further study quoted by Wilson (Roberts, 1986), respondents, when presented with external information types only, selected the following information categories as being important (listed in order of decreasing importance):

Marketing

Products

Exporting

Finance

Competitors

Patents

Production

Health/safety

In almost half of the respondents, the need was for statistical information in those areas. Presumably, much of this information would be found in external on-line data bases.

## Information for CEOs

If the conclusions drawn by the Sheffield researchers are accurate with regard to managers, then it appears that we must differentiate these ‘ordinary’ managers from managers at the very top of the organisation. Such senior managers, according to the now classic study by Mintzberg (1973), ‘develop mental images – models of how an organisation and its environment functions . . . In effect, managers absorb the information around them in a series of mental models of the internal workings of their organisations, the behaviour of their subordinates, the trends in the organisation’s environment, the habits of associates, and so on. When choices must be made, these models can be used to test alternatives’.

Studies by Rockart and De Long (1988), of MIT, reinforce this view. They conducted an analysis of executives' comments on the attributes of ESSs (Executive Support Systems) deemed the most important. These are presented and discussed below.

Improved access to external data. Most executives interviewed consider environmental scanning a primary task of management, too important to delegate to subordinates. Great value is placed on filtering the data through their own mental models. Because the executives' cognitive maps are more complex and more sophisticated than those of subordinates they perceive information differently. The study goes on to note that access to external data bases not only provides standard information (e.g. news summaries and financial data as from Reuters) faster than printed matter, but it also makes available additional information that was previously too time-consuming to obtain.

New ways to combine data from multiple sources. This enables executives to enhance their grasp of their businesses by exploring cause and effect relationships. Resources typically include a substantial amount of public data on competitors and the industry. Product information from around the world may be consolidated.

Presentation of data in more meaningful formats. Flexible formats that combine text, numbers and graphics help executives spot trends that may not be apparent from tabular data alone. It appears that the presentation format has an important effect on how executives think about information.

Sophisticated analytic and modelling capabilities. Quite often this is done with help from analysts and typically involves enhanced spreadsheet analysis of financial implications.

The ability to identify and test assumptions. An ESS in this area enables an executive to question its own and employee assumptions. The assumptions are seen as the building blocks of mental models.

Off-hours data access. This is access to external data on demand, often during an executive's 'reflective' periods.

## A picture of information requirements for managers

(a) The aim of an information management system now increasingly involves improving the level of service provided to corporate users of the information.

(b) Information management includes moving information around the organisation as well as communicating with the outside.

(c) Internal information involves the integration of short-term control information with broader strategic information.

(d) It is possible that many managers are unaware of (automated) sources of information on matters of interest outside the organisation. This would require some marketing effort by the information providers.

(e) Many top level executives prefer to scan external sources of information. There is evidence, however, that they may need assistance in combining, analysing and presenting the information in more meaningful forms.

## Information management

## Political and cultural difficulties

Quite a number of observers feel that the emergence of powerful personal computers and workstations presages a devolution of processing away from the centralised MIS function. Initially it appears that many MIS departments viewed the personal computer as a 'toy'. Subsequently, as PC numbers increased, it became clear that not only had MIS lost control, but the proliferation of numerous brands of hardware and software was symptomatic of the road to chaos. In response to this, many companies established information centres whose tasks included acquisition guidelines and the provision of 'help' facilities in problem areas induced by the relative lack of skills among end users.

As the management of personal computers was gradually brought under control, a counter-movement emerged that seems very much driven by information management concerns. In particular, there is ample evidence that valuable data and information is being stored on the individual work-stations. Apart from obvious concerns about data security, there is a desire for the data made available to all those in the organisation who are entitled to see them. Data is seen to be a corporate resource whose value is enhanced by being shared. The technological vehicle whereby each piece of data can be related with any other, regardless of where they are sited, is the relational data base, and specifically the distributed (relational) data base management system.

It could be then, that the oft heard cry that the 'mainframe is dead', is premature. The MIS function will continue to use mainframes, even although end users use local workstations, some attached to multiuser local area networks (LANs). But, according to Burch (1988), the 'help' facility had been formalised in the form of the Information Centre, which increasingly has an autonomous role to play, rather than being a support function to MIS. There is indeed a self-promotion by the Information Centres to influential users and the executives who control the IS budget.

It is apparent from the above that the political dimension to information management can be a critical issue. This implies that any attempt to wrest control of overall corporate information management activities in a ‘quantum leap’, is doomed to failure. Yet information management means controlling and directing the flow of information between separate groups. Indications are that the opening up of communication channels induces a rather flat management structure with free passage of information moving in an apparently random fashion among interested parties. An organisation operating in such an environment will likely have a different culture than a traditional hierarchical one. The need to modify the corporate culture can also be an inhibiting factor in the information management stakes.

## Responsibility ultimately rests at the top

Given the politics of the situation, it is not sufficient to assign to the chief executive (the CEO) responsibility for overall information management. True, information might be, as Peter Drucker (1988) recently observed, 'data endowed with relevance and purpose' but there is still a 'selling' job to be done. The situation is not helped by textbooks and articles which continue to refer to information as a commodity. Most business people would think of a commodity as a particular good in which different lots would only be distinguishable one from the other on the basis of brand name or price, as with oil or video recorders. It would follow that most managers would feel they have little control over the nature of a commodity.

The above situation is certainly not true for information. The key marketing issue is that managers, through their actions, can have a very significant effect on both the nature of the information, and its value. For example, rapid processing affects the timeliness, and, presumably, value, of information. The competitive aspect may be introduced; Cooper (1988) observes that the value of information ‘increases when one’s competition does not have the same piece of data with which to draw similar conclusions’. It is probably best thought of as an economic resource, to be actively managed as finance, production capability, human resources and other business resources are managed. A particularly relevant quote comes from one of the US’s leading authorities on CIM (Computer Integrated Manufacturing), who, when asked whether he would delegate a decision on selecting an operating system for a CIM computer to somebody else replied ‘No. If you can’t understand the fundamentals of a decision, you’re a figurehead. You’re not the CEO.’ (Avishai, 1989).

Thus, while the MIS function may well retain the responsibility for the establishment and control of a corporate system it is the CEO who ultimately must provide the leadership for an information management thrust. In this respect, the function of the CEO might be likened to one who is committed to the introduction of a Quality Circle/Quality Assurance programme in a company. In that case, all employees must be made aware that the organisation as a whole, and, particularly top management, are totally committed to get the programme under way, and when started, to sustain it.

## Orderly introduction

In the material that follows, the author has made 'extensive use of two articles, one published in the UK (Vickers, 1985), the other in New Zealand (Cooper, 1988), and wishes to acknowledge these sources.

A number of steps are necessary for the orderly and productive introduction of an information policy.

Attract the attention of those in authority. This is where a person trained in management auditing can be very helpful. Jackson (1986) presents a number of factors which are likely to grab management attention:

\- Concern as to seemingly wasteful and uncoordinated expenditure on IT

● Problems revealed by independent (external) audits

\- User dissatisfaction

\- Concern over control (or lack of it) exerted over IS operations

\- Acquisition or merger (requiring the rationalisation of IS functions)

\- Poor communications between separate IS subunits

\- Worry about overall cost effectiveness of IS - Request for enhanced or upgraded equipment - Personnel problems, particularly high staff turnover

\- Substantial budget overruns.

Other factors, more specific to information management, have been suggested:

\- Inefficient storage and accessing of the organisation's technical information

\- Excessive volume of paper output and/or poorly designed reports

\- Departmental and personal filing systems in a sorry state.

Acceptance of the idea that information is a resource. While this notion has become reasonably widely accepted it is not directly obvious. Indeed, many managers, used to thinking of a resource in a more tangible form, would dispute this assertion. This, in turn, makes the task of selling the resource idea more difficult, and as a result a global approach to information management programme just that much harder. It is generally accepted, however, that the absence of market data and information on the competition can directly affect profitability. There might be other areas in the organisation where problems have occurred due to a scarcity of information. Perhaps these can be attacked first as a means of highlighting how a proper programme can help managers in their decision making.

Merely achieving an understanding that information is valuable will be insufficient in terms of making significant progress on implementing an information policy. Indeed, the University of Sheffield research showed that the idea of ‘opening an information store and the users will perceive the relevance of the system and flock to use the information’, may not be so easy. Further, ‘the market is there, it is up to the information manager, or independent information broker, to foster the market and meet its needs’. The ball thus lies fairly and squarely in the information managers’ court.

Responsibility for information management. In most organisations the responsibility for information management is completely dispersed, even more so since the introduction of PCs and the move to distributed processing. One could say, of course, that corporate information has always been dispersed – among librarians, DP personnel, engineers and records managers to name a few. But for a cogent policy on information management (especially the planning aspects) to be implemented, one person must be designated as being responsible. There should be no problem in the larger organisations. In the smaller ones, Vickers (1985) suggests that this function could be combined with other professional roles, e.g. production manager, company secretary, chief salesperson or the like.

Vickers goes on to note that while R & D might be the traditional home of technical information, marketing could, for instance, take on such a role if the firm is market driven. He observes that ‘accountants and their ilk are generally unreceptive to the concept of information management in the wider sense, and resent anyone messing about with their computers’.

Duties of the new managers will vary. Still, for information to be of value to the organisation there are certain well understood aspects that must be addressed. These include information integrity, the responsibility for ensuring that the holdings (i.e. data bases) are kept accurate, up to date and complete; confidentiality and privacy, which involves measures which must be taken to protect against deliberate or inadvertent disclosure, misuse or loss of information, and security, the various physical and administrative measures that are needed to safeguard the information holdings.

In order to achieve these features, it is likely that there would need to be a series of intensive short courses ‘for those who take on an information management role, but who lack training and experience in some aspects of the work’ (Vickers, 1985). No doubt also there will need to be a series of seminars for managers of all kinds to introduce them to the concepts of information management.

Technological awareness. Information management entails maintaining an awareness of new developments that can contribute to a better management of information flows. In fact, the introduction of any new technology requires a comprehensive education programme. It seems to the author to be a rule of thumb that the training costs associated with the introduction of a particular piece of software are usually at least equal to the purchase price of the software. Information management if usually much more complex than the introduction of a piece of software. The orderly introduction of an information management policy will not happen automatically in any organisation, and therefore must be a significant part of the organisation's IT strategic plan.

Information flows. Information management requires an understanding of the patterns of information flow within the organisation, and thus entails the use of systematic means of mapping and monitoring such flows.

In order to manage information we need to know where, and how, we can capture information (from both external and internal sources) the degree and rationale for adding value to it, and the best way to deliver it. In other words we need a picture of the actual and possible information flows in the organisation.

Coordination of expenditure. O'Leary (1987), on a slightly different slant, observes that the problem that burdens every information broker is that 'the industry suffers from the Carnegie syndrome (after Andrew Carnegie who endowed free public libraries in the United States and also in New Zealand), which is that information should be free, and therefore people don't put any value on it. Unfortunately, because a lot of information has been free in the past, executives are reluctant to put forward the expenditure necessary to put an information management programme 'on the road'.

Vickers (1985) has noted that the coordination of information management activity takes us into the realm of the management of hard cash and is therefore likely to encounter strong resistance. The issue of ‘ownership’ of the information can also induce problems in that it is hard to justify the additional extra processing that could be necessary to make functional information useful elsewhere in the organisation. The appointment of a senior person with corporate responsibility, could help with this problem.

## The emerging discipline

## The information audit

A gradual change in emphasis is under way in how information is viewed from within the organisation. Increasingly also, a different set of skills will be necessary to achieve an effective programme of management. Earlier, the possibility of performing a management audit was suggested. While this approach is usually used to assess the overall performance of a given management, there is no reason why the audit cannot be more directed towards the utilisation of information by management. When this approach is taken it is usually referred to as a information audit.

As a rule, an information audit is not a tool in general use by the IS profession although it is sometimes used in an information engineering environment. It is however one of a number of tools available to a person professionally trained in information

management (see below).

In Cooper's (1988) view, it would seem that accountability for the information held is an issue, in that ‘users (and user managers) should be accountable for the efficient exploitation of information resources entrusted to their care’. It would seem that this concept moves away from having a ‘corporate’ information manager, but is in line with the practical problems associated with the ‘ownership’ issue mentioned above. In this context, an information audit is used to determine whether information is used effectively, efficiently and economically; thus, for a given individual, the purpose is to identify:

(a) The information currently received (b) The format of that information (c) The type of information (e.g. financial, economic)

(d) The information gaps

(e) The flow of information

(f) The access to information

(g) The appropriate information technology.

The audit starts at the chief executive and works its way on down. Its aim is to be able to integrate both internal and external information resources into a single mode embracing data files, data bases, and the flows that comprise the knowledge base of the organisation. Cooper goes on to suggest that 'The information content must be integrated so that elements in any one database are available for appropriate combination and manipulation with data in another database, internal and external, text and numeric, hard and soft data. The information can be segmented and packaged, i.e. presented in such a way so that it is easy to pick your way through it: user-friendly interfaces and menus, intelligent gateways and front ends help enormously. The information can be filtered so that the user can selectively access specific relevant information.' In point of fact, the above sounds great, but might be difficult to implement in practice.

Another study (Browne, 1986), conducted in connection with defining a range of skills required by an information professional, highlighted the belief that effective information provision requires a marketing approach. This approach included, inter alia, that graduates of any course (on information management) should be entrepreneurial in their approach to work, e.g. in setting goals, seeking opportunities, etc. The marketing approach leads to a series of ‘focus questions’:

\- What opportunities are there for providing information?

\- What financial and other resources are available?

\- What strategies can be used?

\- How good are the products or services?

\- What impact does the product or service have?

(The reader may be interested to know that the study resulted in the establishment of Bachelor of Arts (Information Science) at Kuring-gai College of Advanced Education in 1986).

An information audit conducted by an ‘information professional’ would likely produce a different picture of the information resource than one conducted by an MIS professional. Peter Scott (1988), of the (New Zealand) National Library, describes three basic types of databases – full text, numeric and bibliographic:

\- Full text has its origins in the publishing industry and is basically as a ‘by-product’ of creating a document on a computer

\- A numeric database provides statistical and other numeric information of the kind found in statistical yearbooks; the main disadvantage being the degree of skill needed to use it

\- A bibliographic database provides references to essentially paper-based sources; in effect it is a computerised index. Typically lists of preferred subject heading and keywords are used in searching.

With reference to the latter, apparently New Zealand has quite a number of databases available that have not been computerised. Ironically they are sometimes available from overseas database providers. Scott gives as an example information of NZ patents available in an overseas data base called Inpadoc. Much of the information generated in NZ is not available locally on-line because:

\- The organisations that gathered it are not aware of the potential commercial value of the information

\- Data base development costs are high and the market is untested

\- It takes a fair degree of skill to organise the information so it can be useful.

## Integrated information systems

The issue of the skills required to achieve effective presentation is an important one, as a ‘quality’ information product implies that the information is available in the right form. The data must be organised so that retrieval is simple and effective. This could mean a fair measure of ‘intellectual processing’ of the information by skilled workers before it is even added to the database. To organise the information in this way may be far more expensive than the costs associated with the purchase of the database and in storing raw data in it.

In effect, in order to provide an adequate database product, what is needed is a merging of two skills – the information systems specialists who can bring together ‘raw’ information and the technological solutions, and the information analysts, information officers and librarians who, in Scott’s words, ‘add value to the raw information, analysing it, synthesizing and repackaging it into a form which can be quickly and easily assimilated.’

A good example of corporate librarians working in a cooperative way is given by Webb (1987) who is with Woodside Offshore Petroleum Pty Ltd., a company involved with the design, construction, and operation of offshore and onshore on the Australian N.W. Shelf Gas project:

Information Services is part of the Information and Computing Division and provides library, correspondence, control, document/records management, archive and micrographics services to all staff. Apart from traditional library services, services on offer include on-line searching, document delivery, indexing and progressing of all print and non-print material (including correspondence generated, received and held by the company), storage and indexing and showing of audio-visual material, management of trade catalogues and material on potential suppliers (keyed into the computerised sourcing system), standards, legislation and technical reports.

When office automation was introduced into the company, the services were extended to include the management of electronic information.

An interesting example of this approach, and one closer to home, is taking place in the Department of Health, NZ. The Manager Information Services (a librarian) is applying cataloguing/librarian techniques to the records section and also to building a Master Directory to enable Department of Health staff to trace the location of information – including databases at some time in the future.

When coupled with the move to a more distributed computing environment, a comprehensive and integrated information management approach will bring with it a freedom of choice to end-users. As noted by Brindley (1987):

In universities, academics will be able to return conceptually to their own private library – an electronic one accessed through their desktop workstations. Millions of dollars are being spent pursuing this concept at such universities as Brown, MIT and Carnegie-Mellon. In industry there is already a breakdown of the library monopoly, with a burgeoning private sector involvement in the profitable sectors of electronic information services. In a wider information management role we are talking about the organisation of a company's total intelligence system.

## Conclusion

In a significant deviation from traditional thinking, at least as reflected in many DP books on the subject, University of Sheffield researchers have found that managers have a requirement for both short term control information and broad strategic information. In addition there is a substantial, but often latent, demand for external information. A different perception of information requirements, coupled with a recognition of the importance of managing the flow of information within the organisation, has led to the emergence of the discipline of information management.

Within this discipline, new skills are required. Of particular importance is increasing top management awareness of the importance of information management. Closely coupled with this is the need to obtain their support in the introduction of a corporate-wide programme. Typically this facet must be done on an incremental basis with an associated intensive programme of education and training.

In implementing an information policy an information audit may well be appropriate to determine the nature and characteristics of organisational information use. This requires skills which may be different from those found in a traditional MIS environment. Increasingly, also, a new type of information professional is coming out of the education system – one trained specifically in information management.

## Biographical notes

Ivan E. Jackson is Professor of Management Information Systems at Victoria University of Wellington. Professor Jackson has been working in the computing area since 1961. He worked for a number of years in the United States and progressed from programming to large systems sales development with Burroughs Corporation and as a management consultant in Salt Lake City. He returned to New Zealand in 1972 and became Director, Systems Development, with the State Services Commission.
