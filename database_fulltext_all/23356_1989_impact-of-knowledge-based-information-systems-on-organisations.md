# Impact of Knowledge Based Information Systems on Organisations

Rodger Jamieson and Richard Szeto, The School of Information Systems, University of New South Wales

Abstract: This paper investigates the impact of knowledge based information systems (KBIS) on commercial organisations. A questionnaire and interview format was used to gather information from eleven commercial organisations developing KBIS. Three prime areas were considered, namely knowledge acquisition, knowledge representation, and KBIS development methodologies. For each of these areas, the normative position as expressed in the literature is presented and then compared to the survey findings. Problems involved in the development of KBIS are mentioned as well as developers' perceptions of the future directions for KBIS within their organisations. Additional information on each organisation is presented in an Appendix in order to provide a richer picture and background to the study. The main preliminary findings are that KBIS have made an impact on organisations in Australia who are willing to make a considerable investment of resources in this new venture. While most organisations are treating this technology as research and development, they believe that the technology will be absorbed into their mainstream information systems in the future. These systems are seen as providing a competitive edge to those organisations willing to make the investment.

## The need for KBIS research in an organisational environment

As knowledge based information systems (KBIS) or expert systems gain more interest and acceptance from the commercial sector there is a need to investigate their impact on the organisation's environment. More explicitly, how has the information systems environment changed to accommodate this new development, what problems and challenges are being faced by organisations when developing and implementing these systems, and finally what do the developers perceive as the future for KBIS.

Stefik et al. (1982), define expert systems as 'problem-solving programs that solve substantial problems generally conceded as being difficult and requiring expertise. They are called knowledge based because their performance depends critically on the use of facts and heuristics used by experts' (Stefik et al., 1982, p.135). For the purpose of this paper, the terms 'expert systems' and 'knowledge based systems' will be used interchangeably as expert systems are considered a sub-field of knowledge based systems (Waterman, 1986).

In a working paper prepared by the Australian Department of Industry, Technology and Commerce, Luckyj (1986) pointed out that one of the major problems in Australia is that most expert systems research and development is to be found in the academic sector. The working paper further emphasizes that there is a need to stimulate interaction between the academic and private sectors. This may be achieved by conducting research within the environment of private organisations developing expert systems. This may guide the academic sector's research on expert systems towards the interests of the commercial sector and at the same time practitioners may provide insight into the constraints of development within a commercial environment.

While expert systems have proved to be successful in a research setting, these successes may not be reflected commercially because of requirements and constraints of a commercial environment. If commercial interest in expert systems continues to grow, they will become an established part of the workplace and a function of the information systems environment. Bramer (1981) pointed out that 'if expert systems are ultimately to become widely used, it is important that they are not only useful in practice but soundly based in theory' (Bramer, 1981). To be 'soundly based in theory' and 'useful in practice', research will need to be conducted in an organisational setting to determine which theories are not only supported but are also practical. Importantly, as 'expert systems have until recently been experimental, we have not had to consider the need for long-term maintenance or the ability to be ‘friendly’ to a community of users who may not have a sophisticated knowledge of computers’ (Nau, 1983, p.83).

Thus there is a need to investigate both the status and success of development of KBIS in an organisational environment. This evaluation will help to fill the gap in the literature, indicating how real life requirements (experts, budget, and time) will or should influence the development process of expert systems, (Basden, 1983; Smith, 1984; Sviokla, 1986) and to characterize both the value of commercial expert systems and their development process. Specific social and technical research issues addressed in this paper are expressed in the following questions:

\- What is the overall impact of expert systems on organisations?

\- What is involved in the knowledge acquisition process?

\- What knowledge acquisition schemas are being used for ES development?

\- What methodologies are used to develop expert systems?

\- What expert system tools/shells are used for commercial expert systems developments?

\- What are the main problems associated with ES development?

\- What are the developers' perceptions of the future directions and impact of ES development within organisations?

The primary aim of this research is to compare the normative position expressed in the literature with the findings from commercial practice in three main areas (knowledge acquisition, knowledge representation and development methodology) by undertaking a cross-sectional analysis of 11 organisations. While the prime emphasis is on these three areas, additional information on each participating organisation is presented in an Appendix in order to provide a richer picture and background to the study.

## Research methods

The research methods used for this study were a questionnaire and interview format to gather information from participating organisations. Information was collected by graduate students from the University of New South Wales. There were no set criteria, such as size, industry, or technology-base, for targeting organisations to be involved in this research. Organisations with an interest in experts systems were contacted from a list of conference participants at the Sydney Expert System Conference. All organisations contacted were willing to participate in this study.

The questionnaire was designed to obtain a rich picture of an organisation's interest in KBIS and to provide insight into the current status of KBIS development within Australian organisations. It was important to examine a wide range of issues as the literature was sparse on the factors and management issues influencing the development of KBIS in Australia and overseas, as well as their progress and status. Some of the other issues considered in the questionnaire include:

\- Ideas for use of KBIS in the organisation

• Management of KBIS

• Management perception of KBIS

• Structure of the KBIS group

• Security of KBIS

\- Potential areas for application developments

\- Current and future perception of KBIS

\- Evaluation of expert systems

\- Marketing expert systems tools

The Appendix reports background considerations and information on some of these issues. The body of this paper presents survey findings on the three specific areas. This paper reports on the first stage of a longitudinal study which will enable monitoring over time the changing phenomenal factors of organisations developing and using expert systems. As expert systems in Australia are in their infancy, conducting research consisting of rigorous hypothesis testing was considered impractical. As most of the developments were still in their formative stages (most of the 11 organisations participating in the questionnaire classify their involvement as Research and Development projects), it was more appropriate to conduct a survey questionnaire and interview which enabled the capture of the widest range of information on commercial expert systems development. From this approach we trust that a significant amount of information on commercial development will be obtained and will contribute to the theory of commercial KBIS development. This initial research is a fact-finding mission to determine the current status of commercial expert systems development in Australian organisations.

## Knowledge acquisition

Knowledge acquisition is ‘the extraction and formulation of knowledge derived from extant sources, especially from experts’ (Hayes-Roth et al. 1983, p.40). According to Waterman (1986), and Barr and Feigenbaum (1982) knowledge acquisition is the primary bottleneck to the widespread availability and development of expert systems. The problems of extracting expertise from an expert ‘is characterised on the one side by an expert, unfamiliar with expert systems and unable to articulate what knowledge he has and how he uses it to solve problems; and on the other side by a knowledge engineer who may be totally ignorant about the domain of expertise’ (Kidd et al., 1985, p.92).

Time spent in acquiring information (knowledge) is mandatory to the success of an expert system project. This means that a considerable amount of time is required from the experts extracting their intrinsic expertise and heuristics that they apply in making important decisions. However, too much time spent in acquiring knowledge may have detrimental effects. Smith (1984) stressed that because knowledge engineers spent too much time in knowledge acquisition before starting development, they could not demonstrate in concrete terms their understanding of the knowledge and this slowed their progress. This argues for a prototype approach to KBIS development. Smith (1984) and Waterman (1986) suggest that one expert should be used to formulate the knowledge base, and later additional experts used to validate the system.

Methods of knowledge acquisition. Apart from the time spent, there are difficulties in extracting intrinsic knowledge and heuristics from the experts. Waterman (1986) referred to the difficulties faced by a domain expert in a domain as the 'knowledge engineering paradox', that is 'the more competent the domain experts become, the less able they are to describe the knowledge they use to solve problems!' (Waterman, 1986, p.154). In order to extract knowledge, various methods may be employed. Table 1 sets out some of these methods.

An alternative method for KBIS development and knowledge acquisition is presented by Milne (1985) who suggests that domain experts should develop the system themselves. He argues, why have the knowledge engineer learn all about the domain (refering to the process of knowledge acquisition) when it may be just as easy to train the domain experts in using expert system development tools to develop a system? He goes on further to say that 'in reality, the only way to build a good expert system is to have the domain expert build the system himself' (Milne, 1985, p.67). Currently the authors question this approach due to the limitations and complexities of available development tools and the dangers of user developed systems as expressed by Davis (1984).

Commercial activities in knowledge acquisition. The study revealed that the prime source of knowledge is derived from experts. Extraction of their expertise is done primarily through interviews and discussions. It is interesting to note that in three (27%) organisations personnel titled 'knowledge engineers' have the sole responsibility for acquiring knowledge. In the other 8 (73%) organisations, they are considered 'all-rounders', in that they carry out both the process of knowledge acquisition and coding the knowledge. Two possible reasons why the task of programming and acquiring knowledge are performed by the same person, are firstly that the organisations were just commencing expert systems activities and secondly in three out of these eight organisations, research was done by a single person. These individuals, while delegated with the initial responsibility for expert systems development, will assume managerial responsibilities for expert systems activities in their organisations in the future. So, while KBIS development is still under R & D, it was to their benefit to do both of these tasks to gain experience.

The most popular knowledge acquisition technique was ‘interviewing/discussions’, which was used by all of the organisations. The next most popular technique was use of literature to become familiar with the domain, supplemented by reviews of company documents and procedure manuals. Observation, case studies, and even intuition were mentioned as other techniques that were used for knowledge acquisition. One company attempted to use computer induction to help in this process, but this proved unsuccessful. However, Quinlan et al. (1986), experimented with the use of inductive inference techniques and reported success in formulating rules for aspects of the diagnosis of thyroid diseases. More research and practical application is required before this technique becomes accepted in the commercial domain.

Most of the companies identified problems in the area of knowledge acquisition, primarily centring around the experts. These problems include the difficulty in identifying which expert to consult, limited availability of experts as they are tied up in their normal organisational activities, getting experts to express their knowledge; and conflicts amongst experts on some aspects of their chosen domains.

In summary the majority of the organisations indicated that knowledge acquisition is a problematic area. Problems associated with acquiring knowledge from experts arise often because of the lack of commitment from experts, and from the fact there were no real working expert systems in their organisation to use as a model to inspire confidence in the knowledge engineer by the expert. Also, experts have great difficulty decomposing their knowledge adequately. Given these problems, the majority of the organisations don't see that it is yet possible for a domain expert, who lacks sufficient computer knowledge, to use commercial shells to develop their own applications. As a result knowledge acquisition will remain a problematic area when developing KBIS.

## Knowledge representation

Knowledge representation is defined as ‘a combination of data structures and interpretive procedures that, if used in the right way in a program, will lead to ‘knowledgeable’ behaviour’ (Barr et al., 1981, p. 143). Expert systems are able to perform some specific predetermined tasks of an expert due to the knowledge represented in a symbolic structure or format in the knowledge base. Without this knowledge base expert systems would not be able to ‘understand’ or ‘perform’ any of the problems they are supposed to solve in the domain. As such, the representation of knowledge in an expert system is vital to its ability to ‘understand’ and ‘reason’ about the domain.

Knowledge representation techniques. Table 3 provides brief details on five methods of knowledge representation, namely production systems, semantic networks, frames, formal logic (Assad et al., 1986) and scripts (Schank et al., 1977). Of the five methods, production systems seem the most viable in the commercial environment (Hayes-Roth, 1985).

One critical issue emerges from knowledge representation research, that is, how to find the most appropriate representation for given problems domains (Brachman et al., 1980). 'The critical problem is that no framework exists to guide the system builder in the selection of a knowledge representation technique for a particular application. Although AI researchers state that the correct selection of a knowledge representation technique for an application is a critical success factor, they have not been able to identify the selection criteria'. (Baldwin et al., 1986, p. 161). This is important as it has been found that some representation techniques are suitable for certain domains only (Davis et al., 1987).

The selection of a representation scheme for the expert's knowledge involves a compromise between what is natural and easy for the expert and what is natural and easy for the programmer. 'The choice of concepts and relations must certainly come from the expert, but the means of encoding them must be partly chosen by the programmer' (Buchanan, 1979, p. 418).

McCune (1984) observes that while expert systems shells are becoming increasingly available on the commercial market there is a need for commercial shells to offer a number of representation techniques. While shells may allow quick demonstration of a prototype system (Canning, 1985), they possess a major drawback in that the 'shell may impose on the application on alien knowledge representation formalism, too restrictive in its expressive power to accommodate all the essential qualities of the domain knowledge. This may make the updating of knowledge and following of program's line-of-reasoning difficult for the user' (Pollitzer et al., 1985, p.412).

While the above guidelines propose ideas on how to select a relevant technique, it may also help to consider other requirements for representational schemas:

\- accuracy of representation – it is important to select the form of representation which would most accurately represent what the expert knows. The representation chosen ‘must be able to capture the fullest possible range and power of the human expert’s knowledge’ (Kidd et al., 1985, p.92) in a given domain.

\- intelligibility of representation – for the user to interact competently and efficiently with the system, the knowledge representation and problem solving processes employed by the system must be readily intelligible to the user

(Kidd et al., 1985).

\- extendability – the format of the representation chosen must be flexible so that additional knowledge can be added or existing knowledge can be modified without forcing substantial revisions to the knowledge base (Buchanan, 1983).

\- simplicity – to achieve simplicity there is a need to represent knowledge as uniformly as possible. If the format of the representation is simple then it should be easy to modify the knowledge base (Buchanan et al., 1983).

\- explicitness – as the knowledge base is usually built incrementally there is a need to provide an easy method for inspecting and debugging the knowledge base (Buchanan et al., 1983).

Knowledge representation in the commercial sector. The choice of production rules by the majority of organisations 10 out of 11 (91%), lends support to the literature that it is one of the most popular techniques for KBIS development. Out of the organisations participating in the research, 8 (73%) organisations are using commercial shells which support production rules in addition to other types of techniques, while the remaining 3 (27%) organisations are using programming languages such as Prolog, C, Pascal, and OPS5. Within the three organisations using programming language, two organisations are currently using a production rule implementation for their applications with the remaining organisation using a network representation of logic and algebra.

With 8 (73%) organisations using commercial shells such as Personal Consultant, Guru and Expert Ease, there was an attempt to elicit whether the developers were influenced by the representation technique offered by the commercial shells. Responses to this line of questioning revealed that developers were influenced by the shells because a production rule implementation seemed to be easy to understand and modify, and adequate for their development purposes. There was a tendency with some of the inhouse application developers to evaluate and select the shells before carrying out a significant amount of knowledge acquisition work in the domain.

Developers were asked how they selected the representation techniques for a problem domain, that is, how do you know that production rules are suitable for representing knowledge in the domain. A common response to this question was that developers tried to model the knowledge as close to the experts' knowledge as possible. The companies did not point out any individual selection criteria for choosing the representation technique. Most companies evaluated the commercial shells according to the facilities the shells offered and the type of representation techniques they supported. On the basis of the facilities supported by the commercial shells, developers chose the shell and picked the representation technique supported by the commercial shell which seemed appropriate for the domain.

While the majority of the companies are finding production rules adequate, developers would like to see a wider choice of representation techniques offered by shells in the future. In their initial stages, inhouse developers believe that prototype systems developed using production rules are adequate for their purposes.

This area of knowledge representation is an important area of research in the field of artificial intelligence, because of the implications that it has on the ability to make ‘machines’ replicate characteristics associated with human intelligence. Knowledge representation techniques such as frames, first-order logic, semantic networks, production-rule systems and scripts offer the closest and most viable methods of replicating human intelligence in machines. Researchers have combined some of these representation techniques, for example frames and production rules, to increase the efficiency and strength of these techniques. Our research indicates that most in-house application developers are relying on techniques supported by commercial shells. Of the 3 outside developers, only company K (refer to Appendix) is using another technique for representing knowledge by the use of network representation employing logic and algebra. This organisation is a commercial KBIS tool developer, thus explaining their deviation from other firms who are following a more conventional KBIS development track.

## Development methodology

Project management of KBIS development is difficult to apply because of the iterative and evolutionary nature of its development and the difficulties obtaining complete functional specifications. Commercial organisations are endeavouring to develop and enhance their development methodologies to ensure successful developments and more importantly to ensure that resources expended are effectively managed and reported. The concept of middle-out evolutionary design seems a possible choice for a KBIS development methodology as it enables development of KBIS without a formal functional specification. As KBIS are usually constructed to solve ill-structured problems, traditional programming methodologies are inappropriate for KBIS development, being more suited to well-structured problems (Sviokla, 1986).

Expert systems development methodology. Smith's (1984) experience and self assessment in developing a commercial expert system (Dipmeter Advisor) provides some insight into rudimentary methodology. Smith (1984) began by demonstrating the feasibility of the expert system by gathering knowledge on the problem domain and selecting a set of tools to implement the system. This was followed by gradual expansion of the domain knowledge using the selected tools. Rapid proto-typing was the strategy chosen to demonstrate the feasibility, given the uncertainty as to what the system would accomplish. However, at one stage during development, the initial tools became in-adequate to allow for a expansion of the knowledge base. This required a new selection of tools and tool refinement which would allow resumption of work on expanding the knowledge base. Smith's next stage was the demonstration of utility and performance of the system. That is, would the system solve problems that are useful and interesting, and whether it could efficiently perform in the field without overutilising available computing resources. A further stage was the evaluation of utility and performance of the system. Whether the system correctly solved problems for which it was developed and whether its knowledge base was complete.

To enable the KBIS to become operational, the design must be evolutionary as the problem domain is investigated, understood and codified. Sheils (1983) suggests that this is more of a design problem rather than an implementation problem. For the KBIS to remain effective will require continual enhancement to reflect real world changes in knowledge and the application of expertise.

The literature on KBIS development methodologies is not extensive. The most widely quoted development methodology is from Hayes-Roth et al. (1983) who define five steps of identification, conceptualisation, formalisation, implementation and testing. These steps are considered interdependent and serial in nature. An iterative process is applied which allows the return to any earlier step. This methodology has been supplemented (Lock Lee, 1986) with an additional two earlier steps of technical and commercial feasibility. The step of commercial feasibility is difficult to quantify. The cost and benefits estimators suggested by Lock Lee are subjective but may add rigour and insights to the feasibility of expert systems development. These additional steps may be considered as a refinement to problem identification. Alternatively, these two steps could be considered necessary but outside the scope of the development methodology.

Comparison of these three methodologies reveal similarities, which is not too remarkable as Lock Lee's methodology is based on Hayes-Roth's. Furthermore Lock Lee considers his methodology as preliminary and is expecting it to evolve as more KBIS are completed using it. The distinguishing part of Lock Lee's methodology is that consideration is given to the area of commercial feasibility. Although Canning's methodology may appear rather complete, not much detail is provided in his article as to what constitutes each stage. His methodology seems to provide major stages of the design of an expert system right down to the integration of the expert system with other systems.

Prototyping as suggested in Canning's methodology is also employed in other expert systems development methodologies, for example Harmon and King (1985). While the prototyping approach seems to be applicable for developing expert systems there is a problem in that while 'it provides a good start toward system development, it does not offer clear guide-lines on how to produce a well-engineered commercial product' (Smith, 1984, p.68; Sheil, 1983). While this may be correct, one benefit of using prototyping is that it may show its potential usefulness before committing a large amount of resources to the task (Rees, 1985, p.74). Sagalowicz (1984) states that an additional benefit of using prototyping is that it allows the knowledge engineer or developer to gain experience, test the suitability of the technical approach taken, and to demonstrate KBIS feasibility. For comprehensive details on KBIS methodologies and KBIS validation refer Jamieson and Ching, 1988 (a & b).

Methodologies in participating organisations. Perhaps, not surprisingly, the organisations participating in the survey classified their KBIS projects as research and development. This may be attributed to the lack of experience, techniques and past performance to aid KBIS development. Also, as pointed out by a knowledge engineer from an organisation which commenced expert systems in the late 1970s, a research environment in an organisation provides a good work environment as less management pressures are imposed. Tax incentives may also foster KBIS development as R & D. With the introduction of Section 73B of the Australian Income Tax Assessment Act it maybe possible for a company's projects classified as R & D, to incur expenditure which may attract a deduction of up to 150 percent depending on whether a company's R & D project falls within the criteria established in section 73B.

Six (55%) of the organisations are developing expert systems for their in-house application (organisations B, C, E, F, H & I in the Appendix). Noticeably, these six organisations commenced expert systems activities between 1984 and 1986. Out of these six organisations, three (50%) (organisations B, E & I) have developed their own methodology. Organisation B, pointed out that with the aid of their development methodology they are able to produce expert systems in a short period of time, within 3 to 6 months. They also perceive that expert systems will lose their appeal in Australia due to ineffective development methodologies. Organisation E emphasised that their methodology was not followed strictly but ‘followed in spirit’ as it was a preliminary methodology which will be refined over time with the insight of experience. The remaining organisation (organisation I) developed its own methodology based on a synthesis of available literature, experience, and drawing from a traditional information processing environment. The other 50% (organisations C, F & H) of in-house application developers emphasised that while no in-house development methodology is currently in use, they will be formulating their own methodology from experience, literature, consulting universities or recognised consultants in expert systems development, and through attending expert systems conferences and seminars.

The methodologies reviewed from the literature seem to lack consideration of the activity of project selection, by prioritising (ranking) the potential applications to be considered within a commercial organisation. The methodologies assume that an application exists and the justification will be carried out at the feasibility stage. However, in the commercial environment, organisations identify a number of potential areas for expert systems development then rank them accordingly to predetermined criteria in order to find applications considered vital to the organisation. Each participating organisation identified more than three potential areas for expert systems applications. Given constraints of limited human, financial and time resources, organisations pointed out some of the criteria they consider when deciding which application should receive the highest development priority. Criteria mentioned include: areas of high staff turnover, where experts are hard to find or require considerable amounts of time to train, where competitive advantage or the retention of expertise in the firm is desired. The activity of prioritising is vital at the initial stage of commercial KBIS development. Critical Success Factors (CSFs) may be used as one of the means to identify and prioritise potential applications.

## Other observations

Most of the companies budgeted a considerable amount of finance for the research and development of expert systems. As this research was conducted when most of the organisations were still in their initial stage of evaluating the feasibility of expert systems and identifying potential areas for expert systems applications, we were not able to examine the impact of expert systems usage on its intended users. Thus the research focused on issues revolving around the three prime areas of knowledge acquisition, knowledge representation, and development methodology. An interesting comment by one of the firms was that even if a machine became expert, its 'behaviour' in solving problems will never be fully accepted by users, even though the user will tolerate the expert's advice in solving the problem. Other observations from the research seem to indicate the need for criteria for selecting and developing expert systems. Suggested criteria ranged from issues covering the organisation's need, to the financial viability of the expert system project, and were different amongst the firms.

However at the time of the research most of the organisations were just ‘testing the water’ and to see whether there is a need and a place for expert systems in their organisation. Of the six in-house application developers (organisations B, C, E, F, H & I), most started by developing small scale prototypes of their applications using commercial shells. The most popular choice of a commercial shell was Personal Consultant Plus with four (50%) out of the eight organisations using commercial shells (refer to Table 6).

While an attempt was made to find out why different types of shells were purchased (one organisation had 4 types of shells), it was found that no criteria were established prior to the purchase of the shells but rather the purchase was made in order to ensure that the company could evaluate shell capability, not for the initial purpose of development. This indicates a need for organisations entering the KBIS field to develop adequate selection, criteria by literature review, attendance at seminars, or/and to consult with experienced developers, on identification and review of potential KBIS applications prior to purchasing a shell.

Problems experienced by the organisations during KBIS development and implementation are set out more fully in the Appendix and include the difficulty of identifying experts, their time availability, and their ability to express how they solve problems. Lack of adequate development tools, management problems, integration with existing systems, and maintenance of the knowledge base were some of the other problems to emerge. These are also echoed in most of the literature surveyed.

## Future directions

Issues dealing with the future direction of KBIS were elicited from the participating organisations by interviews in order to indicate potential areas for future research and to gauge where organisations believe KBIS will change as they become more prominent in an organisational environment.

Most organisations believe that KBIS will become a part of the information systems environment. The majority of the participating organisations see KBIS activities increasing and that personnel in their KBIS groups will increase.

In the future KBIS will be integrated with other different types of KBIS and information systems with the ability to access databases. This access to the corporate database certainly offers a greater advantage if the KBIS is on a distributed network. The creation of a distributed environment for KBIS seems to be a major consideration of some organisations as this will create a competitive edge by ensuring that they can offer their services to all branches in situations where expertise is scarce.

A major problem in developing expert systems at the time of the survey was that there was no recognised expert system language or tool to support the commercial mainframe environment and which linked in with commercial data bases. This is important as most organisations see that it is from a mainframe environment that an efficient distributed environment of expert systems will emerge. Languages such as Prolog are currently not efficient for a distributed environment. Organisation F, who maintains over 30 branches in the N.S.W. region have given much consideration on the issue of a distributed network and are preparing to implement their expert system in a traditional programming language once it is up and running. Other products, such as IBM's ESE (expert systems development environment), are now available for commercial mainframe environments.

## Conclusion

The impact of KBIS on Australian organisations is far reaching in that the eight in-house developers invested significant resources to investigate the feasibility of KBIS. Organisations see KBIS as an integral part of their future information systems portfolio. These KBIS developments are made possible in the commercial environment because of the availability of expert systems shells which can be run on existing microcomputers. In house developers see the use of shells as offering the most feasible means of developing prototype systems in order for the expert and potential users to observe the potential of KBIS to the organisation. The use of commercial microcomputer expert system shells are used more by in house developers as they allow quick development of a prototype without expending vast amount of resources. Third party contractors are using programming languages such as Pascal, C, Prolog and OPS5 to build expert systems shells and delivery systems for their clients.

The literature indicates that knowledge acquisition is a major bottleneck for expert systems development. This view is supported in the commercial environment as the majority of the problems indicated by the organisations point out that obtaining knowledge for the knowledge base is a difficult and problematic area centring around the experts and their knowledge. This problem will continue and should spur development of tools to aid the knowledge acquisition phase.

With the use of commercial expert systems shells, representing the knowledge is not a major problem. There seem to be an indication that users of such shells sub-consciously elicited or structure the knowledge into one of the representation schema offered by the shell, which tend primarily to be production rule systems.

The development methodologies used by organisations are a derivative and refinement of the methodologies expressed in the literature, especially the methodology offered by Hayes-Roth (1984). This methodology provides the basic guidelines for commercial firms when developing their own methodologies.

Although not specifically detailed in this paper, grouping organisations within their particular industry, for example, banking, insurance, revealed that firms within the same industry identified similar domain areas for potential expert systems development. This would indicate these areas are considered vital for those firms within that industry. Also this tends to indicate that these firms are entering into expert systems from a genuine desire to explore the area and its potential benefits to the organisation.

The first stage of this research study reveals that expert systems have impacted on the organisational environment. That is, organisations have considered expert systems a viable technology and have spent time in research and development to ensure that the potential benefits of this technology will accrue to their organisation. Most of the organisations identified several potential areas for development and if these developments are successful then not only will expert systems have an impact but give the organisation a competitive advantage. Further research of a longitudinal nature will continue to examine the organisational impact of expert systems, and will include user perspectives.
