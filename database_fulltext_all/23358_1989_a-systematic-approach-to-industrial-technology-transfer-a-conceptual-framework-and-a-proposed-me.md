# A Systematic Approach to Industrial Technology Transfer: A Conceptual Framework and a Proposed Methodology

Larry D. Gaultney, Miroslaw J. Skibniewski and Gavriel Salvendy, Purdue University, Indiana, USA

Abstract: This paper presents a new concept of modelling successful transfers of technological innovations across the boundaries of traditionally defined areas of industry. A summary review of work in the related topics is conducted. The proposed model of technological transfer includes a provision for the development of a new taxonomy of work tasks targeted for technology applications. The model also incorporates a matrix framework for representing a match between the generic components of work tasks, typical engineering projects, generic components of technology, and specific self-contained technologies. A validation procedure for application within an industry targeted for technology transfer is outlined.

## Introduction

Billions of dollars are spent each year by governments and private industry to develop new technologies. Although many of these new discoveries are applied immediately in the niche for which they were intended, the transfer to other disciplines or groups of people of the knowledge associated with the technology is often painfully slow. It is safe to assume that the value of technical knowledge that has already been developed but not transferred is easily many times the billions of dollars that were required to first develop the knowledge. A method for evaluating, comparing and connecting new technological knowledge with the technological needs of other disciplines would greatly enhance the diffusion of the technology into society. Furthermore, a sound systematic approach to this problem would have the effect of stimulating new ideas and enhancing creativity in all disciplines because of the acceleration of the knowledge front to researchers.

The development of a sound systematic methodology for technology transfer would provide at least three important contributions to the field of production research. These include solutions to the following problems:

\- Some segments of the manufacturing industry are significantly more technologically advanced than others, yet the transfer of technology to the less advanced sector has been painfully slow.

\- Advanced technologies which have been developed and effectively utilized in office technology, space programmes, and the service industry have been transferred at a slow pace to the production segment of industry.

\- Many technologies which have been developed explicitly for the manufacturing industries and which have the potential for use in other segments of industry have either not been transferred or the time lag for transfer was extremely long.

The limitations inherent in the above three transfers can be greatly reduced by developing conceptually-based, systematic and structured methodologies for technology transfer; thus larger segments of our society could benefit from technological advances; this would make the use of these technological innovations less costly per application than currently is the case.

## Background

## Critical factors in technology transfer

The concept of innovation and utilization of new technologies and subsequent transfer of those technologies is addressed by Toulmin (1969). The science-technology-utilization topology for technology transfer proposes that developments in all three of the key elements of the model, namely science, technology and utilization, develop in parallel in a type of symbiotic relationship. He further states that although advances in one area do not guarantee advances in one of the other areas, the likelihood of knowledge transfer is enhanced if all three areas are advancing vigorously. Specific technological breakthroughs in one discipline may not be immediately applicable in other areas but may prove to be a fruitful source of innovation in a parallel technology.

Conditions needed for successful transfer of technology are that the innovation be:

\- technically possible

• economically feasible

\- socially acceptable

• environmentally sound

• administratively manageable

• politically acceptable.

If any of the first three is not met, it is highly unlikely that the transfer of technology will occur at all. If the last three are not met, the technology may be adopted to some extent initially but it is unlikely that it will be adopted permanently.

The progression from a technical idea to a marketable technology with well defined applications is regarded as one of the most complex industrial activities. It involves R&D, design, production engineering, quality assurance, manufacturing, marketing, finance, senior management, sales, and other units of an industrial organization (Morrison, 1983). Very large financial commitments and key industrial strategies are usually at stake. Technology transfer is a central element in this activity. Five factors are critical to achieving a successful transfer:

1. technology transfer is a human-dependent process — special objectives of groups interested and involved in the transfer must be met;

2. the capability of the receiver to respond to the new technology is fundamental to the success of the transfer, regardless of the competence of the provider;

3. an intermediary between the provider and the receiver, specially sensitive to the needs of the receiver can be crucial to a successful transfer;

4. a strong, well-defined market is a prime contributing factor in successful transfers;

5. if truly desired, the receiver will eventually acquire the technology regardless of the competence or attitude of the provider.

An attempt to explain the incentives behind technology transfer from a business perspective is made by Lett and Salsgiver (1983). Technology transfer as a commercial transaction is examined. The fundamental underlying considerations of a technology seller, satisfactory financial remuneration and minimal complexity in the transfer process, are considered. Likewise, the sometimes conflicting goals of the buyer are considered. The only successful technology transfer relationships are those in which both sides gain substantial advantages. The remuneration received by the seller must meet his objectives in terms of amount, form of compensation, and relationship to the value of the technology received. The seller must obtain satisfactory gain while not being placed in an unfair competitive situation or in a situation where valuable rights might be lost.

## Technology transfer management

The influx of new technologies is causing problems of survival for many companies, large and small. A methodology called Master Planning for Modernization (MPM) has been developed to address this problem of survival in a positive, systematic manner (Shpiner, 1983). It provides for the determination of the most effective future ‘to-be’ configurations for the company, the modelling of the present ‘as-is’ operation, and identification and implementation of selected modernization projects. In this way, the necessary changes for survival are managed ahead of time instead of forcing emergency short-term reaction responses.

Master Planning is divided into three sequential phases, with specifically identified decision points included within the phases, to measure progress and predict success probabilities.

1. The Phase I activities begin with a structured factory analysis to match technologies to the modernization opportunities. Conceptual designs are then mapped back into the factory model with cost-benefit analysis. The end results are translated into potential candidate modernization projects characterized by significant parameters such as cost, return on investment, implementation time, and other factors necessary to support corporate selection and approval.

2. In Phase II, the improvement projects are carried into detail development. Cost-benefit demonstration projects are selected for implementation planning.

3. In Phase III, the projects are implemented and costs validated. Feedback into the next modernization cycle is utilized to improve the process.

Within each phase there are functions which are performed:

\- 'Master Change' function: transforms the long-range, strategic, and business plans and goals into criteria for assessing technology. It is the function managing the overall modernization activity.

\- 'Perform Systems Analysis' function: uses systems analysis techniques to integrate with the first function and perform the structured analysis to identify improvement potentials and, ultimately, define projects.

\- 'Provide Technology Transition Support' function: identifies and prepares for implementation of selected technologies.

The above three functions occur in all three phases, but the detailed execution of the integration between them differs within each phase. In Phase I, this integration is directed towards identification and characterization of pertinent areas of technology; in Phase II, emphasis is placed on the customized development of specific pertinent technology; Phase III provides for the implementation and installation of such technology.

## Long-range technology planning in decentralized firms

Decentralized organizational structures, often carried to a low level, are commonly used in the electronics industry and, under a different form, in construction. These forms have proved to be efficient in a business sense with the existing production technologies. They pose special problems, however, when resources such as innovative technology need to be shared among a number of quasi-independent elements (Fayer and Kaplan, 1983). Major issues that should be addressed are the balance between short-term and long-term investment priorities, the identification of technology needs, assessment of the candidate sources of technology to fulfil these needs, coping with uncertainties and risks of technology development and fluctuating market demand, and consolidating the diverse needs and approaches of company units and individual decision makers involved.

The result of such a planning process can be the identification of the core technologies which will form the base for the long-term growth of the overall organization. Phased, multi-year R&D programmes can be established to capitalize on their potential. In parallel with these core programmes, each organizational element can sponsor its own, more specialized R&D programs to support specific short- and medium-term goals.

## The current state of technology transfer

## Technology transfer from government laboratories to private industry

Large government R&D programmes generate a vast amount of technology that could be used by private industry to advance its technological progress. The realization of this opportunity requires the following measures of co-operation between private industry and the national laboratories (Olken, 1972):

\- to develop a commercial approach in the engineers of private industry by requiring national laboratories to document technology development produced in each laboratory;

\- to prevent the loss of technology through failure to document at the technology development sites;

\- to give private industry access to the laboratories' new technology by making development documentation available to the public.

In addition, two other measures of national laboratory/private industry co-operation can be helpful in the long term:

\- to make provisions for private industry to efficiently retrieve the design and production information to make the transfer of selected technology development quick, easy and low-cost;

\- to require, as part of each R&D contract, that the technology produced to achieve the research results, as well as the results, be written up in reports that will be effective in enabling private industry to adapt that technology.

## Technology transfer in agriculture

Technology transfer has been a topic of great interest in the agricultural sector during the last three decades. Transfer of mechanization, crop cultures, methods and processes from developed countries to less developed nations has proved to be successful.

A common method of agrotechnology transfer is by 'diffusion' of information from one country or researcher to another. This 'diffusion model' was adopted as the foundation for technical assistance after the end of the second World War and led to an extension bias in aid programs in the 1950s (Hayami and Ruttan, 1971). Often this diffusion of knowledge resulted from travel and information exchanges undertaken for other purposes. According to Hayami and Ruttan, the three stages of technology transfer are:

1. material transfer stage;

2. design transfer stage;

3. capacity transfer stage.

According to Silva and Uehara (1985), the three requirements for successful transfer of agrotechnology are:

\- a basis for transfer;

\- a common language for communicating information for transfer;

\- a source of innovation for transfer.

A hierarchy of minimum data sets for technology transfer in agriculture was proposed by Nix (1983). Each of three levels of data sets aimed at a balanced monitoring of whole systems but with successive upgrading of the frequency, precision and accuracy of measurement. At each level, the emphasis is on the minimum data set that is required for explanation of system performance and subsequent prediction at that level.

\- Level 0: data needed are the absolute minimum required for sample analysis of empirical models; a weekly time step is adequate.

\- Level 1: data provides a basis for development and testing of process-based models; a daily time step is needed.

\- Level 2: data collected provide a basis for more explicit representation of component process.

## Technology transfer in construction

Construction is a large sector of industry in the United States with an annual work volume of about \$300 billion, yet it is also one of the least advanced industries in the use of advanced technology in the performance of work processes. One reason for this lack of progress is that the construction organizational structure has been particularly ill-suited to facilitate a meaningful work process automation. Unlike batch manufacturing, where the work object is mobile through the production facility and work tools can be stationary, the work object in construction is stationary, of large dimensions, and constantly changing as work progresses. Tools are mobile, whether hand-held or mechanized, operated by humans working on a stationary, massive and constantly changing object. Also, construction processes are usually performed in dusty and noisy environments, preventing the use of fragile, high-precision and sensitive devices.

Another probable reason for this lack of application of recent manufacturing automation achievements is traditional, craft-oriented structure of construction labour. Work rules and fixed crew sizes have made it more difficult to introduce new methods and concepts for solving severe productivity and efficiency problems. This has frequently led to stagnation and to decreased quality and productivity rates experienced by both abroad and domestic, has been rapidly increasing and damaging the US construction industry.

One method used for dealing with the difficulties associated with the ill-structured work environment was to remove a number of construction operations from the building erection site into an off-site, well structured environment of a prefabrication plant. This solution, attempted in the US in the 1970s with help from the federal government, largely failed. Apart from the concerns mentioned earlier, one reason for that failure was the fact that for large-scale, non-residential construction projects pre-fabrication did not prove cost effective. Another reason was concern that the adoption of industrialized building methods would result in monotony of non-customized buildings and structures, with uninspiring interiors and exteriors, and an overall shabby appearance.

Another contributing factor is the labour-related limitation of the process of manual work at off-site prefabrication plants followed by on-site structure assembly and erection. For example, it has been difficult to obtain the desired construction output volume and satisfactory quality levels. The primary reason for this volume-quality mismatch is that structural components of a building are produced in prefabrication plants according to a given set of quality standards realistic in a factory environment. Subsequently, the on-site erection work performed on components produced off the site actually decreases the construction work quality assumed by designers.

The past efforts to industrialize construction in the US were undertaken at the time when industrial automation technology was at its infancy. Engineering and economic analyses of prefabrication processes and systems were lacking. Traditionally, construction has been very resistant to technical innovation as described earlier. On the other hand, some construction operations have been noted historically for their arduous, repetitive nature, with relatively little dynamic decision making on the part of a human labourer. Hence, the work involved in these tasks is rather unattractive for humans. In some cases, the work site also poses a significant health hazard to a human involved. Some industrial nations suffer from a shortage of unskilled construction labour, and this trend will probably continue into the future. As a result, the real cost of construction labour has been increasing steadily and significantly over the past two decades and will be likely to continue to increase in the near future. These facts, together with the rapid advancement in other areas of the industry, indicate a promising potential for advanced technology transfer to construction.

## Technology transfer modeling

A probabilistic approach to the prediction of transfer of technology was proposed by the participants in the MIT Conference on the Human Factor in the Transfer of Technology [Gruber & Marquis 1969]. This model provides a sequential series of probabilities that must be considered to determine whether technical information will be transferred into a new use. The model may be depicted as:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{P} (\text { transfer }) = f & [ \mathrm{P} (\text { idea }), \mathrm{P} (\text { research }), \mathrm{P} (\text { development }), \\ & \mathrm{P} (\text { production }), \mathrm{P} (\text { innovation }), \mathrm{P} (\text { diffusion }) ] \end{array}
$$

The two critical links with the outside world, at the point of entry into the model and at the point of marketing the technology, are considerably more difficult to predict than the other steps.

McClelland (1969) discusses a functional model of technology transfer which attempts to combine variables to predict the magnitude but not the direction of technology transfer. The independent variables are often inter-related and therefore must be considered simultaneously in arriving at a solution. The model is represented in the following form:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text { Magnitude   of   technology   transfer } = f & (\text { source,   nature   of   item   to } \\ & \text { transferred,   structure   of } \\ & \text { channels   for   transfer, } \\ & \text { potential   recipients }) \end{array}
$$

Friedman (1983) describes a catastrophe theory model for R&D, technology transfer, and technological innovation. Technological innovation is represented as a function of both R&D and of technology transfer. In terms of catastrophe theory, R&D and technology transfer are control parameters and the rate of technological innovation is the state variable. Predictions based on the cusp-catastrophe are made with regard to the limits within which technology transfer and R&D can be substituted for each other within frames of reference of various types and sizes. A continuous interaction between technology transfer and R&D can result in a large, discontinuous increase in the rate of technological innovation within a particular field of endeavour, although the normal course of events would preclude the appearance of such discontinuous behaviour. Management can benefit from catastrophe models by maintaining a technology transfer to R&D ratio within a range which secures a steady level of innovation throughout the entire planning horizon.

## The need for transfer structure

The approaches and interpretations of technology transfer processes presented above do not provide a means for the systematic and scientific identification of new technologies targeted for transfer and implementation in areas of industry. Also, work tasks and activities in the areas potentially receptive to transferred technologies cannot be systematically selected with the existing approaches and procedures.

There is a need to provide a structured methodology to facilitate a better understanding of the mechanics of technology transfer. Such a methodology should carry the transfer process from the initial concept through the redesign of the traditional work system to accommodate the new technology. Also, the implementation phase during the initial period should be carefully designed to assure a fail-proof process. Such a methodology has the potential to streamline the transfer effort and helps to avoid the loss of valuable financial resources on unfeasible transfer attempts.

The structure of the technology transfer method should attempt to reflect a transfer strategy implemented by an expert professional with a consistent experience of conducting successful technology transfers between industries. The relevant information on individual components of the strategy can be collected directly from the top experts in the field.

## Objectives

A model for advanced technology transfer is presented below which provides a structured method for introducing pertinent knowledge into disciplines where it was not developed. The model serves two functions:

1. to identify the need for transfer of technology;

2. to aid in the decision of what technology to transfer.

Each of these functions is accomplished by an appropriate method which provides structured technical data assessment of targeted needs, as well as guidelines regarding the technology implementation procedure. The model consists of a database, and knowledge-handling strategy which:

1. assesses the relevant advanced technologies available outside a given area;

2. provides a match between the advanced technologies and the needs of the area of interest;

3. utilizes a decision support system to help to implement the new technology in the new area of application.

## Procedure

The first objective of this methodology is to develop a new taxonomy of tasks which combines work tasks that exhibit similar operational attributes (e.g. eye-hand co-ordination for humans, vision-manipulation co-ordination for advanced machines). The new taxonomy considers the following three dimensions:

1. type of operational task (according to currently used classifications);

2. human and machine performance components required;

3. available specific technologies.

The initial step in technology transfer, that of assessing and categorizing the available knowledge, is modelled in a four-dimensional matrix framework (see Table 1). The matrix is graphically represented in Figure 1.

The second matrix contains data which connect the generic components of technology with the generic components of tasks. This correspondence will allow comparison of methods across industry bounds and will suggest more efficient or less complicated ways of performing the same work.

The third matrix contains the keystone of the technology-task match and relates all other previous factors to specific technologies which exist. These technologies will often have capabilities to perform several generic tasks, or may be limited to one task. At this stage, no inference is made as to the suitability of a given technology for a specific application; only the potential capability of the technology to perform the given tasks is established.

Most technologically disadvantaged industries are characterized by the lack of task performance-oriented standardization of work. Most of the existing standards are work-item oriented. A new taxonomy of work tasks across all possible areas of these industries is required which groups and structures the tasks for the purpose of their advanced machine performance. This new taxonomy enables easier assessment of work innovation potential when the opportunity arises.

Example attributes in the new taxonomy include eye-hand co-ordination for humans, vision-manipulation co-ordination for advanced machines, repetitiveness of work, strain on human labourer, and cumbersome human-machine interaction. The new taxonomy considers three dimensions of classification:

1. type of task (according to currently used classifications in a given industry);

2. human and machine performance attributes required;

3. available technologies outside the given industry.

The second objective is to design a decision tool to implement the technology transfer for a given job. To accomplish this, a generic language defining in a consistent way the tasks, the methods and the objects relevant to the considered transfer is required. Data regarding the matching of tasks with available technologies, as obtained in the first methodological objective, are utilized.

The structure of the language takes advantage of object-oriented programming to define objects, and a semantic network to link tasks and methods.

Each task is operated by, with, or on an object. Tasks which can follow or supersede other tasks have 'hooks' to link with possible neighbours. Tasks without mutual 'hooks' are not allowed together in a sequence. A sequence of tasks which accomplishes a desired final goal is called a 'job'. Each task is divided into sub-tasks (i.e. generic components of tasks), which more fully define the composition and the action of the tasks.

Under each subtask there is a list of methods. A method is potentially capable of performing more than one sub-task and is therefore independent of sub-tasks except for explicitly defined operational relationships. Methods of sub-task performance are obtained from all possible areas of industry without regard to current industry association or affiliation.

Each method carries with it a list of attributes. Attributes consist of highly specific details which serve as limiting factors in the selection and matching of existing technologies to needs. Data associated with attributes may be either quantitative or qualitative, although values for new technologies may be unknown. Multiple values or ranges of values will also be allowed when appropriate.

An example representation of the language structure is presented in Figure 2.

The knowledge base developed for each industry in the above-described manner is applicable for inclusion into an expert system. The object-oriented approach is useful when programming in a language such as LISP. Future additions to the knowledge base would also be simplified because of the independence of the knowledge base from the inference engine.

## Validation

The proposed methodology must be validated within each industry targeted for technology transfer. A possible scenario could include the following steps:

1. Two major task categories in the target industry are identified as good candidates for the infusion of advanced technology.

2. A fixed number of individuals from a company representing the target industry are selected. The selection is based on each individual's responsibility for infusion of advanced technology within his or her company.

3. The individuals identified in 2 above are divided randomly into three groups. One group forms the control group and derives the solutions for technology transfer for both tasks using conventional methods. The second group utilizes the methodology described in this paper in order to attain a technology transfer solution. The third group evaluates the effectiveness of technology transfer using psychometric methods.

4. All three groups are required to complete a psychometrically based questionnaire which provides information about likes, dislikes and difficulties experienced in using a specific method for technology transfer.

5. Time required to attain a solution is recorded in each method.

6. Based on 3, 4 and 5 above, a composite effectiveness score for the control and experimental method are derived.

7. Based on 6 above, a person with the highest and lowest effectiveness score from each group is selected for participation in the further evaluation study. For this purpose, a third task category is selected as in 1 above. The four persons conduct the technology transfer task using the same method as they did in 3 above. During the performance of the function a protocol is taken of their thought process. This analysed information is used for two purposes as follows: (a) to compare the structure and effectiveness of the decision processes used in the two methods, and (b) to provide a rational basis for the development of an expert system for the tested experimental method.

## Conclusion

This paper presents the concept of a structured approach to the process of technology transfer between industries, providing this cumbersome and frequently erratic process with a consistent methodology. This not only facilitates more expedient transfer but also streamlines the transfer effort into the most promising areas and activities in the industry. Thus, the resources normally spent on unsuccessful transfer attempts can be better focused on more certain outcomes, thereby encouraging more activity in the practical application of new technical ideas. The obvious beneficiaries of such outcome will be the least technologically advanced industries.
