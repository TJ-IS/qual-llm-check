# Information Technology Management into the 1990s: A Position Paper\*

Clive Holtham, Bull HN Professor of Information Management, City University Business School, London, UK

Abstract: Information technology poses many challenges both to general management and to the IT industry itself. After extrapolating some of the technological trends that could have significant impacts on commercial and public sector IT in the 1990s, the paper reviews four major challenges. These are the need to develop IT strategies, both nationally and for individual organisations; the need to create alliances; the need to avoid misalliances; mechanisms for converting ideas into action. A considerable emphasis is placed on the need to understand the managerial environment within which IT is used, and to see IT as only one element within wider organisational systems.

## Context

The nineteenth century saw many fundamental innovations in technology. Arguably the twentieth century has had proportionately fewer. However computing stands out as a major and genuinely twentieth century innovation and it is at the heart of the massive growth in information technologies generally.

This paper examines some of the specific issues likely to be faced in IT and for organisations using it in the 1990s. There will continue to be major growth in the use of IT. There will continue to be huge investments in R & D in fundamental and applied IT. But both the suppliers and users of IT need to begin to address more explicitly some of the negative aspects of IT. A noticeable development in the 1980s – certainly in the UK – has been a sense of disappointment or even disillusion with IT, with it perhaps being treated as a necessary evil in some quarters.

One of the most striking features of a recent study into the attitudes of managers in Britain, France, West Germany and Italy towards information technology was the much greater level of dissatisfaction in Britain with the use of IT in business (R. Tomlin, 1989).

## Challenges

This paper reviews challenges. It is addressed to the IT industry, including hardware and software suppliers, consultants, the managers of IT in companies and other organisations, as well as general managers. The challenges identified are prescriptive, including areas that in the author's view should be given close attention. But they do not represent a fully comprehensive world view of the 1990s, rather a selection of key points.

Any observer of the IT industry cannot fail to notice some of the exciting and even glamorous features it exhibits. There are many people of the highest intellect and business calibre in IT. The industry tends to be stronger in marketing than many others, and also to be more willing to invest in R & D, and in training, than many other industries. It is a highly competitive and visibly international industry. Its glamour is often best reflected in new product launches and announcements – there are few other areas of non-media business that use dry ice to enable both products (and even industry personalities) to make a dramatic appearance.

Yet the industry faces many problems. The term vapourware – announcing products a considerable period before they physically exist (and before it is even known if they will exist) by no means reflects a small scale aspect of the industry. There are extreme problems in interconnecting equipment and systems provided by different suppliers. Many systems are still too complex to learn and to use efficiently.

This paper does not intend to focus solely on the problems facing IT suppliers or to suggest that they are in fact unduly or substantially responsible for disillusion amongst the users of IT. But there can be little doubt that the raising of expectations (from a variety of sources including the media, management literature generally and even academics) about what IT can achieve, and the failure for all these expectations to be realised, are probably the single largest cause of disillusion.

The author is essentially an optimist about the potential IT has to improve organisational performance. But he believes that it is particularly incumbent on those who publicly advocate the use of IT to scrutinise closely the reality of the use of IT and to recognise that not all is well in the supply and use of IT. There is of course much successful IT. But there is also too much unsuccessful and only partly successful IT.

Most of us involved in the IT industry derive our views in part from our personal experience and it is essential to clarify at least how we believe they have influenced our current views.

Experiences as a user, as a manager of IT and latterly as a researcher and teacher of IT have given a particular perspective on the IT industry which cannot be in any sense representative. But it has left a number of deep impressions including exhilaration at actually seeing dozens of staff using a system successfully, unaware of the effort and midnight oil that went into it. At the other extreme is the frustration when a fixed disc drive fails 40 minutes before an already delayed payroll run and the service engineer brings a spare with the correct part number only to find it was actually the wrong drive, it having been mislabelled.

## Problems

My particular icon for the current state of the IT industry is the pound sign. Vast effort has been expended in the UK on trying to get pound signs printed out. However there is no doubt that the local area network is challenging hard as a source of incompatibility, unexpected occurrences and down-right frustration. The failure to print the £ sign is first of all a sign of the internationalism of the IT industry. Much software is American, hardware from the Far East (regardless of the country of assembly) and printers are predominantly Japanese. Printers do have pound signs in their character sets but they are not always easy to get at, and particular software packages cannot be guaranteed to do so.

The failure to print the pound sign is also a symbol of the lack of standards which is all too prevalent in the industry. The personal computer is supposed to represent a more standard approach, and it is (compared to mainframes and minis) but even it suffers from major incompatibility, minor incompatibility and misleading compatibility (for example, the RS232 interface).

It is perhaps hardly surprising that in areas where IT is presumed to be critical (major commercial data processing) there is still so much orientation towards single suppliers, particularly because dp managers then find it easier to pinpoint the responsibility for problem solving.

Many of those involved in the supply and use of IT are preoccupied with the technology itself, including its software. This is of course the root of the industry, the source of much of the innovation and excitement and the most physically obvious part of most systems.

It is interesting to contrast the contribution of people to the use of IT. These are often under-rated and even at times key groups of people are accidentally or deliberately ignored. They are the source of much of the creativity that leads from the inert technology and code of computing to bring it alive and make it of greater or lesser use to business and the users. They are physically the least obvious part of any system.

This paper identifies four major challenges, namely

• Developing strategies

\- Creating alliances

\- Avoiding misalliances

\- Converting ideas into action.

It can be seen that these are strongly managerial in nature and this is no accident. It is argued at times that the technology in information technology is far less significant than the information and managerial aspects. In one sense it is, but ultimately it is the technology that distinguishes IT from manual methods of processing information. It is therefore neither possible nor desirable to divorce the management issues in IT from the actual technology itself, and in particular from the developments and innovations in the technology.

## Technologies of the 1990s

One of the most discussed issues in relation to management education in information technology is precisely how much knowledge general managers need of the actual information technology itself in order to be able to manage their organisation's use of IT. At the one extreme it is clearly not essential for managers to be able to program computers (except perhaps when the 'computer' is an advanced featurephone), but at the other extreme it is very doubtful that managers can escape at least a basic understanding of both hardware and software.

In looking ahead to the challenges facing IT management in the 1990s, an important aspect is some forecasting of the technological trends of that period. This is a notoriously difficult area. Looking back at the forecasts made for the 1980s it is easy to be wise after the event. It is exceedingly difficult to forecast outright innovations; an easier route is to examine technology that is already in the development or even implementation stage and which is likely to have a significant impact, even if other (but unpredictable) areas could become even more dominant. Seven particular areas are considered below:

\- Parallel processing

• Network proliferation

\- Workstations

\- Optical storage

• ISDN

\- IT standards

\- Human-computer interface.

Parallel processing is currently confined largely to scientific and technical areas, but it can only be a matter of time before commercial uses materialise for the sheer raw processing power involved. The only current applications tend to be for complex specialist financial calculations.

The 1980s has seen the rapid growth of a variety of networks alongside the traditional 'star' communications configurations of traditional minis and mainframes. The 1990s is likely to see the variety of networks expand before it reduces, but by the end of the 1990s the free standing micro is likely to be an unusual sight outside small businesses and the home.

As with parallel processing, the high powered workstation has grown out of a scientific and engineering base, but it is almost certain to become commonplace in commercial applications where power computing is needed either as a single user machine or as a server on a network. There is considerable scope for the debate where the high-power PC finishes and the lower-end workstation begins, but whatever these doubts a workstation is likely to have:

\- many megabytes of RAM

\- very large hard disks (300–500 Mb not being uncommon)

\- UNIX or derivative as operating system.

The largest single order for workstations in the UK has recently been placed by a bank to be used for commercial purposes. This perhaps demonstrates the start of the movement towards the use of the workstation as a replacement for PCs, where great processing power is needed for high-end users.

One of the major areas of development in storage is optical storage. There are already commercial systems which offer disk platters with several gigabytes of storage, arrangeable in jukeboxes of up to 270 disks, giving massive total on-line storage capacity. The innovative NeXT computer, designed by Steve Jobs after he left Apple, comes with a re-writable optical disk as standard; the magnetic disk is an optional extra. The relatively cheap storage offered by optical techniques will have a particular impact on the storage and processing of images, and of voice. Anyone who has tried to store detailed images captured from a scanner will know that it is commonplace for a single photograph to take up 1 megabyte or more of storage. This can be reduced by compression techniques but compared to standard floppy disks of no more than 1.4 Mb or business PCs with 40 Mb hard disks, clearly common commercial magnetic devices are not yet economically ready to handle image. Traditional image storage such as microfiche has not yet been threatened by magnetic storage – indeed it plays a key role as archival backup. But it can only be a matter of time before images, which have such a powerful role in our day to day lives and in the mass media, will become an important part of commercial IT. For example, database packages increasingly have the capability to handle image alongside text data.

In the telecommunications field, the innovation likely to make the greatest commercial impact will be ISDN (Integrated Services Digital Network). As the public phone systems become fully digital in the 1990s, our domestic voice handsets will be replaced by combined voice and data terminals. Our single domestic line will be replaced by a multiple channel giving for example a voice line, a 64K data line and a back channel for signalling etc. This will enable a whole range of information services to be developed for both domestic and commercial use. Some of these services, such as home shopping, many already be available but are unlikely to take off with the relatively low quality of display available, for example, through traditional teletext.

One of the areas that has always been a subject of controversy in information technology is international standards. The strong proprietary flavour of most mainframe sales has not as yet been outweighed by the greater standardisation which has followed from the growth of the personal computer. An industry that depends so greatly on innovation will always have products that challenge standards, but for many users a price is paid, particularly financially, for an overemphasis on proprietary sources. The 1990s will see a much greater emphasis on standards, above all by the users of information technology, whose procurement choices are likely to influence IT suppliers far more than standards bodies or similar initiatives. This can already be seen in the minicomputer area, where there is a marked move from proprietary operating systems to UNIX.

The final area of technological development for the 1990s relates to the human-computer interface. Anyone who has to look at or use more than a handful of software packages is aware of the non-standard approach which software designers take, except in relation to the Apple microcomputer where the guidelines developed by the hardware supplier have been followed fairly consistently by software houses. Some of the worst interfaces can be found on mainframes, where I have recently seen 'modern' accounting packages of quite horrendous complexity, without sensible on-line help, and very sensitive, for example, to the cursor being in exactly the right position when the RETURN key is hit. Another example would be an electronic mail system that needs at least 6 introductory screens to be used (in conjunction with the manual for the occasional user) before the first mail screen is reached. There is a great deal of research being carried out into the human-computer interface, for example through European Community and Department of Trade and Industry funded projects. However there can be few grounds for optimism about the speed with which the results of that research can be disseminated throughout the IT and user communities and, more significantly, actually acted upon. It may well take much of the decade before major improvements in the human-computer interface are available to most commercial users of IT.

It is not only technology that shapes the context of the 1990s. Another important area is the people who will be developing and using the information technology. A very great deal of emphasis has been placed on the fall in the number of school leavers – over 20% in the 16–19 age group between 1987 and 1995, for example. Surprisingly less emphasis has been placed on the massive growth in the 45–54 age group – just short of 20% over the 1987–1995 period. It is also the case that over 70% of the workforce of the year 2000 are in the 1989 workforce. It is clear from these two facts alone that there needs to be a greater recognition that information technology in the 1990s will very largely be developed and implemented by people currently in the workforce. So the training and development of this group to make more effective use of IT should be of primary importance. Many people in the current 35–44 age group and indeed even in the current 25–34 age group already feel that they have ‘missed out’ on IT because they did not learn about it in school, college or as part of their apprenticeship of professional education. Enhancing the IT skills of the current workforce at all age levels needs to be given a high priority by employers, training boards and professional bodies.

There has also been considerable publicity recently about the falling proportion of women in systems analysis jobs over the last 20 years, as well as the need to provide returners to work after childcare (largely women) with skills such as IT skills that are now prerequisites in many commercial areas of work. There are many deep issues surrounding equal opportunities in IT work specifically, and in work using IT generally. These issues will require greater review and examination by the IT industry and IT users, if both groups are to be sure that they are recruiting and retaining the best staff.

## any means the exclusive preserve of IT firms.

As an industry that is both modern and which has enjoyed so much growth over the last 30 years, it may be less well placed than some other industries to be aware of the radical changes it may itself face in the next decade or two. The hardware arm of the industry has always been its flagship if not its raison d'etre. Hardware can be said to encompass communications, networks, systems software and servicing also. The hardware role is being subjected to two major trends (or threats, depending on perspective). The first is the globalisation of product design and manufacture. Even the humble personal computer, badged with its supplier's name, may well have its components sourced from half a dozen or more different countries. This is not to say that there is a free international market; different countries and governments use a whole host of methods to favour particular national or national-based suppliers.

The second trend is the movement away from proprietary hardware, where there is an emphasis on the purchaser of IT being locked-in to some extent to particular suppliers' operating systems, hardware and software, towards both standards based computing and even commodity computing, where the product is standard and the name on the box counts for little or nothing. It is this trend that poses the greatest changes for the IT industry as it has developed in the last thirty years, particularly in relation to mini and mainframe computers. The microcomputer market has developed in a different way and although there is a continuing effort to promote proprietary approaches, key elements of the pc market have already reached commodity status.

Hardware-based companies are already accelerating their pace of diversification into the other four roles as a method of insulation against the pressures of standards and commodity-based competition.

The applications role has been focussed on explicitly by software houses but it is one where hardware-based companies have always had a role whether by directly producing applications software or indirectly through supporting and jointly marketing the products of third parties whether software houses, value added resellers or even their own innovative customers. Much applications software is essentially bespoke in nature and this has amongst other things been more of a limiting factor on the internationalisation of software packages, though it is noticeable how there is a steady growth in packages that are explicitly designed to be multinational in nature.

There has been a great deal of advice to users of IT to concentrate their efforts on adapting to a suitable software package rather than designing a one-off bespoke system. Users continue to ignore this advice in large numbers and in the process increase the opportunities for a wide variety of bespoke applications software suppliers. Even where a standard database package is used, there is still a need for someone to design and implement the applications system. Since the use of IT is ultimately applications driven, this is likely to continue as a major area of growth.

Training has formed an integral part of the IT industry if only initially in terms of the technical skills needed to enable users to operate computer equipment and to develop systems on it. Education and training form a continuum and there is therefore some logic to grouping them together. If training is concerned primarily with the acquisition of specific usable skills, then education is concerned with the broader body of knowledge and awareness about IT, systems and their management.

The cost of training is rarely fully provided for in IT budgets, either for small or large projects. The biggest single cost of training is in the staff time needed to be released for training. It is one of the ironies of microcomputing that it is now common for the direct cost of training (e.g. courses) in a new package to exceed the actual cost of the software, and for the indirect costs (learning the package in the work environment, including trial and error) considerably to exceed the package purchase price. Despite the likelihood of an inverse correlation between formal tuition and learning at work (the greater the formal training, the less needed at work), there is still too great a reluctance to invest in formal training.

However, IT suppliers are recognising that training is an important service to be sold alongside hardware and software; in some cases formal training is a prerequisite to any use of the hardware or software, so effectively increases the purchase cost. Education and broader awareness are likely areas of future growth. Some IT suppliers are now even offering general management education courses as part of their portfolio. The general training and education area (as opposed to product-specific courses) is highly competitive with many organisations having grown up or focussed in recent years on particular segments of the IT course and conference market.

The fourth role in the IT industry is consultancy. This may be provided by an IT supplier of either hardware or software, by the IT specialists in a general management consultancy or by firms who wholly specialise in IT. Large organisations also have their own consultancy services of various types. Consultancy is important to the users of IT for several reasons. Firstly, some types of project only need developing in a particular organisation infrequently, so there is value in bringing in specialist skills from outside. Secondly, it may not be possible to recruit certain skills in-house, so consultancy is seen as a method of acquiring those skills. Thirdly, consultants can provide an independent external viewpoint even where in-house skills exist. Fourthly, consultants are used for political reasons. This is most particularly either to give external support to an internal proposal that faces barriers and objections, or to review and possibly revise or reject in-house proposals that have not fully found favour internally.

The scope for IT suppliers, whether or hardware or software, to develop in the consultancy area will tend to be in the first two of these areas. The scope for entering the third and fourth areas is affected by the degree to which there is, or is perceived to be, a conflict of interest.

The fifth role is that of systems integration. Whilst this is by no means a new role, it is one that is developing rapidly. In one sense the traditional hardware supplier has always performed a systems integration role. They may well not have manufactured or even assembled all the equipment sold under their brand name, but they designed the integration of a range of equipment into coherent systems.

The same applied to networks and communications, and to the support for and promotion of third party applications software. It may well be in the 1990s that at least some hardware suppliers get as much business from systems integration activities, with not all hardware, communications and software being their own proprietary products, as from direct conventional sales.

This will become an extremely competitive area. The role of hardware suppliers has already been identified. Consultants will identify readily with it. Since most developments are applications driven, so too will software houses. And of course the role of in-house data processing departments is already changing to encompass the systems integration role also.

## Strategies

The whole area of IT strategy is a very popular one, particularly in the American IT management literature, as well as in articles and courses in the UK. In relation to the challenges of the 1990s, two particular areas stand out as of particular significance, namely whether there is a need for a national IT strategy, and the need for IT strategies for individual organisations.

A major debate on the need for a national IT strategy was sparked by the all-party Select Committee on Science and Technology's report in 1988 (Select Committee, 1988) which was highly critical of what it perceived as a lack of a national strategy. It made 52 recommendations for action, which is perhaps not the most focussed approach to bring about change. The response to the report (Department of Trade and Industry, 1989) rejected almost all the key recommendations of the Select Committee. To many observers, it appeared that in contrast to most of its major economic competitors, the British IT strategy is, in essence, to have no IT strategy beyond an emphasis on the market yielding appropriate solutions and some very specifically focussed interventions.

## National IT strategy

There are five main reasons why there is a need for a UK IT strategy beyond the market-minimalist level:

• The global challenge

\- Innovation

\- Standards

• Government procurement

\- Investment in skills and awareness.

The first relates to the global challenge faced. The UK already has a massive net trade deficit in information technology. If all main competitors were also to adopt the market-minimalist approach, there would be more of a level playing field. But in the next decade the prospects of this happening appear remote, except as a result of the European single market. Both the USA and Japan have a variety of explicit or, perhaps much more powerfully and subtly, implicit methods of reducing the free import and sale of foreign IT products. Much of the USA's IT strategy is formed through its defense research and procurement programmes. Like Japan it also has a massive and somewhat introverted home market. Japan has both a variety of restrictive procurement practices as well as a close explicit strategic planning relationship between industry and government. Even its language and script could be said to assist a restrictive approach.

Centrally planned initiatives will not always succeed. The attempt by Japan to lead or impose a microcomputer standard internationally via MSX was a signal failure. But there is equally little evidence that a market-minimalist approach will actually propel the UK into a more successful position in global markets given that the various playing fields involved are typically anything but level.

The second reason the UK needs an IT strategy relates to the nature of the innovation process. The argument from the DTI is that the market is best placed to promote innovation but that the UK is in any case a major contributor to European-wide programmes of fundamental and pre-competitive research.

This is a comprehensible and internally consistent position, and it is possible for commercial success to be based on good marketing and trading skills rather than any form of innovation, as with the Amstrad personal computers. But it seems unlikely that a country that is low in its rate of innovation will be able to develop products and technologies as quickly as those who actually have the first-hand knowledge. It can be argued that even where the UK has innovated, it has been its lack of commercial acumen that has failed to achieve the ultimate benefits; the sale of the INMOS transputer company to a French IT firm being only the latest example of this situation.

The innovative capabilities of the United Kingdom have been present throughout the last 200 years. They are not, at present, being fully harnessed towards making the IT industry in particular fully competitive in a global context. It is easy for those of us involved in education and research to say that a root cause is the lack of investment in these areas, but there can be little doubt that even if this is not the single most significant area of weaknesses, it must be amongst the top two or three such areas.

A third reason why a national IT strategy is needed relates to standards. It is a central theme of this paper that IT standards will become much more significant in the 1990s than they have been to date. It can certainly be argued that if they will develop anyway, there is no need for major initiatives in this area, beyond the relatively modest information and awareness campaigns currently under way.

However, a counter-argument to this is that standards will in the 1990s, if not now in all respects, reduce costs for users and increase the portability of software for software suppliers. This will in particular apply internationally, so the essence of this counter-argument is that software houses in a country which is well advanced in procurement based on standards can gain competitive advantage over software houses based in countries where standards are less prevalent. This is because by focussing on supplying to standards early on, they will be well placed when the use of standards start to grow in the currently proprietary-dominated markets.

A fourth reason for a national IT strategy is that in most countries government and the wider public sector are enormous purchasers of IT, and this in itself can have a major effect on the IT industry of a country. The Government's response to the Select Committee rejected the idea of the kind of single tender favouritism of the 1960s and 1970s public procurement policy.

But a review of government procurement for the 1990s does not have to rely on this type of policy. A much more appropriate route is that recommended by the Select Committee, namely that government should ensure that its procurement policies are procedurally efficient, thus not excluding developing IT firms from this country. It should also ensure the use of IT standards is mandatory.

One of the strongest advocates of IT standards in any part of the economy has in fact been the Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency (CCTA) which is central government's strategic and advisory arm for IT. But in certain key areas its advice on standards is only advice, and the Select Committee wished to strengthen this.

The situation on IT standards in public sector procurement is, however, in Europe, gradually passing out of the hands of individual national governments. The European Commission has been particularly active in this field, and it has now made the use of international standards in communications (OSI) mandatory for the great majority of public sector procurement. From the work that is now proceeding at Brussels it can be expected that further European Community legislation will follow in due course. This legislation is not being passed to fulfil desirable IT objectives. It is an active part of the movement towards a single European market. The Commission sees the European public sector as a whole as a massive procurement base, much of which is currently being spent on non-European proprietary suppliers. An advantage of a European public procurement policy based on international IT standards is that it does not discriminate at all against foreign suppliers (unless they are unable to supply non-proprietary systems) so there is no feather-bedding of community suppliers.

The final reason why there is a need for a national IT strategy is that one of the responsibilities of Government, even in a market-minimalist environment, is to influence or ensure the nation is investing adequately for the future as a whole. There are known areas where market based organisations are unwilling or unable to invest sufficiently to meet national objectives, education being the most clear-cut example. Most industries are dependent on the skills of their producers and the awareness of the users of the goods and services they produce. But this is acutely so for a high technology information based industry such as the IT industry. If Government does not ensure sufficient investment in education in the UK, there is, under current tax regimes and cultural history, little likelihood of either individuals or businesses fully making up the difference.

## IT strategies in organisations

The other important aspect of IT strategy is the need for organisations to have IT strategies. Most do not. There is a wide variety of approaches to generating an IT strategy, and this is not the place to analyse actually how to do so, but rather to indicate why IT strategies are important now, and likely to become even more so in the future. Four main features of an IT strategy can be summarised as:

\- Vision

• Competitive advantage

• Preparing for opportunities

\- Implementation capability.

It is essential that the expenditure on IT is related to the strategic direction of an organisation. Only a minority of organisations have clearly articulated business strategies. But regardless of whether there is a formal business strategy, every effort needs to be made to:

\- Capture the essence of the vision that is driving the organisation forward.

\- Harness this to the direction of IT in the organisation.

Much of the literature on IT strategy uses the language of competitive advantage. In its crudest form there is almost an equation between higher spend on IT and improved competitive advantage. The conclusion of the Kearney report (Kearney Management Consultants, 1984) – that there was a marked correlation between spend on IT and company success – is often misquoted. It is, for example, perfectly possible that it is not the IT which is a cause of success but rather that it reflects a well managed organisation that is able to make good use of IT and therefore confident of investing in it (P. Strassman, 1985).

One of the consequences of rapid technological change is that it provides new business opportunities achievable through technology. Even where a particular technology is inappropriate or not cost-effective at a given point in time, it may well be necessary for businesses to track its development. A strategic approach to IT can take this on board.

However, one of the biggest problems in any kind of strategy whether it relates to IT, or the organisation generally, is in ensuring that:

\- The strategy takes account of the capabilities of the organisation and its suppliers to physically implement the direction sought.

\- The method of developing the strategy has maximised the likelihood of adequate support within the organisation for that strategy.

'Selling' a strategy is not something that can be left as a final stage or almost as an afterthought, particularly not in an environment where there is greater departmental and middle managerial understanding of IT, where there are more technological options, and where IT can affect or even lead the fundamentals of the business. The very process of developing the strategy must take on board a wide range of inputs and can rarely any more be carried out on a top-down basis.

To re-summarise, the reasons why organisations need to develop IT strategies, are:

(1) To harness IT to organisational vision

(2) To provide one input to creating competitive advantage

(3) To improve the potential for utilising technological innovation for business opportunities (4) To ensure IT plans are realistic from an implementation viewpoint, and that there is commitment within the organisation to implementing these plans.

In some cases developing an IT strategy is seen from below as a process of form filling; what it should be about is providing direction and motivation and to achieve this needs a blending of both managerial and technological expertise.

## Alliances

The tendency of some of the IT strategy literature to emphasise competitive advantage has just been touched upon. It can be argued however that: 'Understanding how to use IT for competitive advantage requires an understanding of how to build and maintain alliances'.

There are five types of alliance that will be discussed here:

\- International

• Business and education

\- Users

\- Competitors

\- Suppliers and customers.

## International alliances

International alliances are particularly important to IT suppliers, which really need to have a global strategy, particularly in relation to hardware and components. But it is also the case that IT, particularly in relation to data and voice communications, is also altering the nature of global businesses. In several industries, including shipping, finance, and air, the ability to transmit and receive information virtually instantaneously throughout the world is steadily transforming those industries.

In the case of the securities industry and air transport, new technological capabilities have been paralleled by increased deregulation in many countries, increasing the likelihood of global and genuinely transnational businesses in these areas. So it is increasingly possible that developments in information technology may be having direct impacts on the way international business is carried out.

But the complexity of trade and commerce on a global scale means that few parties are likely to be able to proceed far without creating alliances. In the case of airline booking systems, most such systems now involve federations of airlines – the investment involved being too great for any one operator. In the telecommunications industry the reality or prospect of deregulation has broadened PTT perspectives beyond their own national boundaries. It is fascinating to examine the consortia bidding for major contracts such as the telepoint franchises in the UK. The Phonepoint consortium is British Telecom (49%) together with France Telecom,

STC and Nynex (USA).

Many hardware suppliers based in the USA or Europe have developed strategic alliances with Japanese firms, particularly to gain access to specific technologies or products which the firms concerned would be unable to develop themselves economically.

## European strategies

The creation of the European single market is aimed to commence in full in 1993. (It is interesting that in the UK the publicity for the single market has focussed on the year 1992, which is the last year of the 'old' system!)

This will have an impact on all industries, but will particularly affect the IT industry, both within and outside Europe. The European Community has a net deficit in its trade with ‘non-Europe’ in IT products generally and there is no doubt that one of the aims of the single market is to make the European Community increase its share of its own market, not least in areas as strategically important as IT.

One of the effects of greater economic harmonisation will be to increase the likelihood of two types of application development:

\- The greater use of pan-European systems by individual companies and their subsidiaries and affiliates.

\- The development of software packages that will be marketed much more easily across European national boundaries than is currently the case.

Economic harmonisation will be promoted by legislative changes and the removal of nationalistic bureaucratic barriers to trade. However, this will remove only some of the obstacles to applications development across national boundaries. The issue of different national cultures still remains of significance. There is considerable scope for research into both the specification of multinational applications, and also the cultural issues relating to development teams and the use of such systems.

## Alliances between business and education

Improved education and training is of great importance both for the suppliers and users of IT. The education system primarily meets educational needs though parts of it are more training-orientated. A high technology society and particularly an information-oriented society needs excellent alliances between IT suppliers and users, and educational institutions. There are four aspects of these alliances

\- Research

\- Development

• Education and training

\- Sponsorship.

At the higher education level, one of the basic features of education is research. Fundamental research has been at the root of most of the information technology innovations since the Second World War. But the higher education sector should not just be concerned with producing high quality researchers and research. It also has an important, but less well recognised, role to play in the development of new products and services. The university or college can and should act as a testbed for hardware and software products under development, offering informed users an analytical approach but also a climate in which innovation, experimentation (and things going not quite right) are in general well understood concepts. Research and development provide excellent opportunities for collaboration and alliances between business and education.

One of the issues affecting education for and in IT is just what sort of academic subject areas should be contributing to research and teaching of information technology and information technology management. The roots of IT are in technology and perhaps to a lesser extent in information systems analysis and design. These are important inputs for those concerned with the supply of IT but even suppliers are concerned with many issues relating to the use of IT that relate much more to general business management, organisational behaviour and strategic management. And for the users of IT this type of issue should be more significant than the technological areas.

In practice information technology management is researched and taught in a wide variety of environments, some rooted heavily in technology, others in business studies, with yet others having for example information science or cybernetics at their core. Whilst there is probably no need as yet for the development of an explicit discipline of 'information technology management', the question of what constitutes a discipline is of more than theoretical interest. In developing courses, allocating resources and measuring performance, decisions are taken based on a variety of third party judgements about subject areas and their importance. If IT management is primarily researched and taught as part of a wider subject area, or by academic staff who are essentially focussed on another area, this may not accelerate its perceived impact and importance.

There is no suggestion here that IT management should become a narrow introverted area. It can be strongly argued that it should always be taught by a multidisciplinary team involving a wide range of perspectives from information engineering fundamentals through to organisational sociology and even the philosophy of knowledge.

The time is also ripe to re-evaluate the relationship between the teaching of IT and of quantitative methods. Modern software packages such as spreadsheets with statistical and graphical features are making it easier and easier to manipulate and present numeric data. But there is a very grave danger that this will be done with an inadequate understanding in particular of the statistical fundamentals and of the intuitive understanding which are needed to communicate numeric data reliably and effectively. Modern software can process data faster, and superficially more attractively without actually leading to better communication or understanding, and perhaps even worsening it.

A similar argument can be applied to information science. This discipline has developed a breadth of understanding about the storage and retrieval of data of both a structured and unstructured nature. With the growth of both high speed communications and massive storage, it is essential that this understanding is widely disseminated to those designing and using information systems.

It can be seen that the teaching of IT and its management should itself be based on an alliance between the teachers of various subjects. But there is probably a need to develop a co-ordinating and catalytic group of IT management specialists who can draw on such a range of skills rather than try to replicate them in a new discipline.

The final area where a closer alliance between business and education is needed relates to sponsorship. There are very few people at any level of the higher education system who would believe that their institutions were adequately funded for the standards expected of them now, and more particularly for the future. This particularly effects subject areas that are subject to continuous change (such as information technology and its management) where there needs to be constant investment in updating both equipment software and teaching material.

There are no clear medium-term prospects for adequate funding from government-based sources. It is therefore essential for higher education to collaborate with IT suppliers and users in relationship to the sponsorship of research, courses and students. This relationship need not be one-sided; progressive firms can see the advantages of close contact with research, management development and the highly educated workforce of the future.

## Alliances between users

The traditional IT model was a hierarchical centralised one, both for technological reasons (hardware and software were expensive and had to be kept fully utilised) and because the management style of the 1950s to the 1970s still tended towards ‘traditional’ organisational models.

It was a coincidence that the widespread development of more decentralised models of management was paralleled by the development of decentralised/distributed/localised data processing.

It is no longer necessary to consider the users of IT as second-rate citizens wholly dependent on centralised hardware and support. Indeed it is necessary to consider a number of areas in which alliances can and should be created between users:

\- Networking

\- Communications

\- Learning

\- Teleworking.

The most obvious area is in relation to the local area network. The outstanding feature of the personal computer for most of its users was that it was totally under the control of the user, who was unaffected by down-time, maintenance breaks, communications failures or poor response times. The user felt in total control. However, there are only certain types of application where users in most organisations are wholly independent of each other. A local area network is a basic method of interconnecting users. But the interconnection also creates interdependence. The user is no longer an independent island. Printers are shared with others – and there may be a queue for use. File servers have to be shared – and two people may want to access the same record at once. There can be heavy traffic of data over at least part of the physical network. And then there is the network software residing in RAM, leading the user to discover that they no longer have enough memory to run both their wordprocessor and their favourite terminate and stay resident (TSR) utilities.

These are not criticisms of local area networks – they are inevitable features of them. But what they bring home to the new user of a LAN is that they have to learn (or re-learn) how to co-exist with other users. They have to learn what is polite and neighbourly behaviour on the network. Fortunately most LANs are relatively restricted to one or a few offices so users actually know and can often see their network neighbours. Effective use of LANs requires better understanding by users of each other's impact and even needs. This can be a very positive alliance but it will not happen by accident. Introducing a LAN is not just a technological exercise of adding cards to PCs and wiring up the office. It is essential to manage the change and highlight the dependence and interdependence issues in particular.

The growth of communications systems also highlights the effects of interdependence and the scope for alliances between users. Electronic mail systems are often stunted in the development phase by both too small or unfocussed a user base, and by users who as a consequence see little value in sending messages or, particularly, looking at their electronic in-trays. It is not enough to carry out training – it is probably even more critical to have a focussed group of initial users with a need to communicate electronically.

One of the areas of alliance between users that is inadequately discussed is the scope for learning between users directly. In any large organisation the wheels of learning the features of software packages will be constantly re-invented, and this despite all the efforts of IT training and support departments: Partly this is due to the reluctance of some users to ask for help from colleagues, and also to the negative effect that interruptions have on an office. It would be undesirable for general staff to turn into IT software support staff, but there is almost certainly scope for a slightly more structured approach to cooperation and alliances between users in the learning process.

One change in the nature of work in the 1990s is the move towards teleworking – people working for a greater proportion of the time at home and relying on advanced voice and data communications technology to interact with their colleagues. The pace of actual development of teleworking seems at times to be rather slower than the growth of publications about the subject. Teleworking is inhibited at present by lack of advanced features in telecommunications. It seems that the features found in ISDN are much more likely to promote teleworking than the present PSTN-based services. There also needs to be cultural changes within many organisations if teleworkers are not to be seen as a marginalised peripheral part of the workforce as opposed to part of the core workforce who simply happens to be carrying out their tasks in a different location. Whatever the inhibitors to teleworking, there is no doubt that it places a much greater emphasis on the need for colleagues to work harder on relating efficiently to each other, since they can no longer rely on the convenience of physical proximity to do so.

The networked organisation of the 1990s offers the prospects of greatly enhanced sideways communications between a whole variety of users of IT. The alliances created and facilitated between users by

IT can in principle improve organisational effectiveness. However they cannot guarantee to do so. Indeed without positive effort in relation to creating the right climate for change, and in relation to implementing the change, they are unlikely to yield their full benefits.

## Alliances between competitors

The up-beat literature on using IT to gain competitive advantage has already been referred to. But one of the striking features of IT is the way in which it actually creates a need for otherwise hard competitors to collaborate with each other. There are other domains of business where such collaboration is needed, but the breadth of impact of collaboration is arguably distinctive, including:

• Technology collaboration

\- Standards

\- Electronic data interchange (EDI).

The clearest area of collaboration within the IT industry itself is over basic technological alliances. The cost of fundamental research and development can be so great that there are very few single firms who can either financially afford basic research, or who would wish to tie up their scarce research personnel into particular avenues. Alliances may even lead into mergers such as the creation of GPT by merging GEC's and Plessey's telephone interests.

Within the IT industry still, one of the most remarkable sights of 1988 came from the launch of the Open Software Foundation (OSF), with top executives of IBM and Digital – such an aggressive pair of competitors – sitting alongside other competitors on a stage in Geneva to announce their collaboration on developing new Unix standards. The fact that this alliance was ostensibly aimed at a perceived AT & T and Sun Microsystems attempt to narrow control of Unix is probably less significant than the fact that by mid-1989 all major IT suppliers had publicly committed to one of only two forms of Unix for the early 1990s.

For microcomputers two groups were formed to create the dominant PC-bus of the 1990s. One group coalesced around the IBM MCA bus, the other led by Compaq, around the proposed EISA bus. Although on Unix Hewlett Packard was alongside IBM in the OSF, on the PC-bus HP was in the EISA consortium. And on the bus front several suppliers were ensuring that whichever group they were nominally in, they were actually backing both standards as an insurance policy.

Turning to the users of IT, the most noticeable types of alliance tend to relate to joint commitment to extremely expensive systems and, in particular, communications systems and networks. It is often not appreciated by the general public how the sheer cost of setting up and maintaining networks of bank cash machines (automatic teller machines or ATMs), has led to the major banks and building societies sharing these networks and their costs.

The development of electronic data interchange (EDI) also places a premium on cooperation – the larger the network the greater the benefit to all participants. As in other aspects of standards, there may well be competing and perhaps incompatible networks, such as in the motor trade.

## Alliances between IT suppliers and customers

In most market-driven industries suppliers aim to have a good understanding of customer needs, and to maintain close links with the potential customer and post-sale with the actual customer. In this sense the IT industry has few distinguishing features.

However, there are reasons why some of the features of IT mean that the question of relationships between suppliers and customers takes on certain special features, including an emphasis on alliances.

Pace of technological change. Customers need to put effort into keeping abreast of developments in the industry and its products, because they can so quickly become technologically, if not physically, obsolete.

Lack of specificity of user requirement. From the largest mainframes to the smallest microcomputers, IT is still being procured at least in part because of a fairly general belief that it will improve organisational effectiveness, and without full analyses of user requirements. This places a much heavier burden on the relationship between customer and supplier, since the latter may often have to anticipate the second-order problems arising from the initial lack of clarity.

Requirements change as IT is used. Not only is there a lack of clarity as to user requirements but even where there is specificity, requirements often seem to change in the light of experience of actually using systems. Some of this arises from technical constraints. A user discovers that particular usage of an on-line system leads to unacceptably long response times. Experience shows that a report writer on a package either takes 24 hours to run, or halts all other jobs in the system while it is processed.

There are also the changes in requirements that derive from the way people actually relate to systems in practice.

Potential impact of technology on the organisation. Some organisations are so dependent on IT for customer- or safety-critical activities that their IT must be run at very high levels of service. For most types of IT this involves a continuing close relationship with the supplier.

There are at least two ways in which the relationship between IT suppliers and their customers takes on characteristics of an alliance.

## The quality goal

Quality is a managerial theme that has become much discussed in the 1980s and there is every likelihood that this will continue into the 1990s. IT has a potentially important role to play in improving 'internal' quality of production of goods and services and also of the 'external' quality as perceived by customers.

If an organisation has explicit goals of high quality, then it needs to develop relationships with all its suppliers. The classical examples of quality management through suppliers, such as Japanese car manufacturers, tend in practice to hinge on standard setting and tighter control over the supplier: the manufacturer in other words being able to influence the product directly. In the IT world this type of relationship is possible on bespoke projects but is less likely to apply to the bulk of hardware and package software purchases. In these cases the customer has to take the product as supplied, in most cases being able to choose between suppliers.

If many customers of IT develop managerial philosophies of quality management in the 1990s, then there will be direct or indirect pressure on IT suppliers:

\- To be aware of that philosophy and its implications

\- To amend their products and even their own product development processes to reflect the philosophy explicitly.

It is not being suggested that IT suppliers are turning out poor quality hardware and software products. It is rather that they are in general, and perhaps of historic necessity, orientated towards a whole variety of managerial philosophies or not at all. If the 1990s sees the common emergence of quality management, then the IT industry needs to collaborate more closely with its customers to develop appropriate products.

Quality-orientated products will not necessarily reflect leading edge technology. Reliable software packages which are easy to install, learn and use may be much more relevant to quality management than packages full of functionality and complexity.

It may be more important that a lay user of IT can ensure pound signs are printed out reliably, than that the package offers a byzantine series of printing options. The IT industry has often associated 'quality' with 'technical innovation' rather than 'simplicity', hence the need to build a closer alliance with customers to understand closely their management philosophies.

## Strategic planning

If IT is likely to make a much greater contribution to achieving the strategic goals of nations and of organisations, then the IT industry needs to understand these strategic goals. But the process is not all one-way. Organisations need to be aware of technological opportunities and constraints, developments and alternatives. So particularly where IT is critical to an organisation's success, that organisation may well have to involve IT suppliers in their own strategic planning either directly or via some structure integration of 'IT futures' thinking into the strategic planning process.

There is already the development of information planning methodologies that begin to integrate IT much more fundamentally into business planning, for example Information Systems Planning from James Martin Associates, and this type of approach is likely to become much more commonly used in IT-dependent areas in the 1990s.

## Misalliances

The preceding section has argued that the concept of an alliance has an important role to play in developing the most effective use of IT within organisations. But there is another side to the coin. The inappropriate or badly managed use of IT can decrease organisational effectiveness.

Examining the whole range of material written about IT, there is substantially more produced about the success of, and through, IT than about failures. There are various reasons for this:

(1) Much of what is written is directly or indirectly marketing and sales material of IT suppliers. One would not normally expect any industry to dwell on problems and failures.

(2) Users of IT, again quite naturally, tend to prefer to talk about their successful use of IT.

(3) At the interface between academic research and practitioners, the latter tend to be most interested in how they can improve their use of IT and/or become more successful through IT. This means that even research which is equally about success and failure may tend to be presented in terms of success stories.

None of this is particularly unique to IT management. It is a feature of much of the current literature and debate about management generally.

There are a variety of channels through which poor use of IT is made known. Firstly, there is the 'grapevine' of users who may well be prepared to discuss their problems on a one-to-one basis, rather than in front of a large conference. Secondly there is the press, particularly the IT press. They perform an extremely valuable, though by no means popular, function in identifying problem areas. Thirdly, for public bodies the audit and review process may well publicise problem areas; the National Audit Office's reports doing so in relation to central government and the National Health Service. Fourthly, there are the professional IT publications and courses, where users, consultants and others may touch on problem areas. And finally there is academic research. Some academics have built up a specialist understanding of the problems of designing and implementing IT systems (Willcocks and Mason, 1988).

Whether enough effort is being devoted to this topic, even via a variety of channels, is doubtful. While there is plenty of ‘ownership’ and resourcing of solutions to IT-related problems, there is perhaps insufficient independent analysis of the root cause of the problems.

This paper summarises three specific problem areas as 'IT misalliances'. This is primarily to provide some symmetry with the concept of alliances used earlier. The three types of misalliance are:

\- IT and business

\- IT and managers

\- IT and users.

The phrase ‘misalliance’ is being used as a shorthand for problems caused by IT and difficulties occurring during the process of designing and implementing IT. One area not covered here but which could be analysed in more depth is the extent to which the physical structure of the IT industry is based on appropriate (or misappropriate) alliances – vertically between hardware, software and services suppliers and horizontally between different market areas.

## Misalliances: IT and business

It is very difficult to analyse situations where a strategic mismatch between IT and the business has led to the decline or extinction of a company. As indicated earlier in relation to the Kearney study, it may well be that 'poor management' is a common cause of company failure and that this is reflected in poor IT management rather than IT problems being the root cause. At this stage it may be appropriate to touch on some anecdotal situations which may be symbolic of wider problems.

In the mid 1980s I visited the Imperial Group Headquarters to view their Chief Executive Information System, an innovative precursor of today's Executive Information Systems. I was very impressed but was struck by the extent to which parts of the system I saw were looking inwards at the company, and also looking backwards at historic data. Not very long afterwards the Imperial Group was subject to a predatory takeover, which was successful, and the Group was broken up. I have often wondered (with the immense benefit of hindsight) whether or not the Chief Executive Information System was, strategically, actually tracking all the right variables.

In 1986–7 I cut out from ‘Accountancy Age’ magazine an article on an investment company that was making creative use of IT for clients’ fund management, both for administration and in its use of highly sophisticated and complex mathematical models of financial markets. This company later became famous or indeed notorious for allegedly having defrauded its private clients. The company was Barlow Clowes. I have often wondered since how far the hi-tech image was genuinely leading to efficiency, how far it was itself a marketing ploy, and how far it was used for more doubtful motives.

The actions of Eddie Shah have led almost directly to the reform of operations of the British newspaper industry. New technology was central to this. But although highly innovative, Shah himself is widely reported as having been someone who was greatly absorbed in the process of innovation and particularly in the technology, at the expense of some of the wider business issues. Neither of his two major innovations were successful initially, and the technology played an important role in this. The great beneficiaries of Shah's innovations and failures were the other newspaper companies, who paid extremely close attention to his failures, and learnt the appropriate lessons.

The final anecdotal example relates to George Davies, the former head of the NEXT Group. It was much publicised that Davies had a VDU in his bedroom, no doubt to emphasise his management style and close contact with a company that he had done so much to develop. But eventually he was ousted after a degree of internecine conflict within the Board of Directors. There is no question of an obsession with the information from the VDU, but rather like the Imperial Group example, at the end of the day conventional IT of the type mostly used in business in the 1980s has often been of little or no relevance to either personal or organisational survival.

## Misalliances: IT and managers

There is a greater body of evidence on how there is often an uneasy relationship, or even misalliance, between IT and managers. One of the major problem areas in the UK is the extent to which managers perceive their expectations from IT systems as not having been fulfilled. As indicated at the beginning of this paper, the actual reality may be different, but perceptions are what dominate most. The immediate causes of this expectation gap range from over-zealous promotion by the suppliers of IT to wholly unrealistic expectations of IT (often in relative ignorance) by the managers.

A second problem area was identified in a major Department of Trade and Industry Study into software quality (Price Waterhouse, 1988). Managers often feel that it is more difficult to forecast the ultimate costs of IT systems, than in other budget areas. This can assist in undermining confidence in IT more generally.

Thirdly, the design of most IT systems is essentially close-ended. A specification is designed. This often meets current perceptions of what is needed from a system. But the managerial world is anything but close-ended, especially in certain fast moving business environments, and in a climate where company takeovers or internal restructurings are either commonplace or widely believed to be so. Much of the effort in developing systems, by being close-ended, closes off options for the future. Much more attention needs to be paid to how future information needs which may be unknown (or even unknowable) can be addressed. This will almost inevitably be at the expense of currently perceived needs. To bring about this reorientation from relatively close-ended systems needs considerable effort to increase the awareness of both general managers and IT specialists. Both groups tend to be more comfortable with defining the present and with specifics than with the future and the intangible.

The fourth type of potential misalliance between IT and managers relates to the area of risk. IT introduces new types of risks, both for organisations and for their individual managers. IT can also eliminate or reduce risks, particularly through increasing the information available on which to make decisions, e.g. through a credit scoring system. Organisational risk is increased through significant dependence on IT especially in environments where instant backup recovery is impossible. I recall early one Christmas Eve going into a catalogue goods shop with a long queue of customers, all observing the frantic efforts of the manageress to get the branch minicomputer into action. The manual system which was the backup was so slow that many of the potential customers simply took their business elsewhere.

In relation to personal risks, managers involved in designing, implementing and operating IT systems will frequently be all too aware of the implications of delays and failures not only directly, but also indirectly such as where a poor system adversely impacts on staff morale. Yet many general managers involved with IT have never had any training at all in its implications. In order to drive a car, which can admittedly kill other people, formal tuition and testing is needed. It has often struck me that managers are frequently let loose on the development of computer systems with potentially massive implications and risks for them and their organisations without even the smallest element of training, much less testing of their skill.

The mismatch between managerial expectations of, and capabilities in, IT should be a matter receiving considerably greater attention in both the academic and practitioner worlds.

## Misalliances: IT and users

It is not only managers who may be troubled by the development and use of IT systems. There is also a whole area of potential misalliance between IT and its direct users. There is an increasing amount of evidence in this area. Willcocks and Mason (1987) usefully summarise some key findings.

Some of the most common problems can be categorised as follows:

\- Failure to consult direct users during the design process

\- Creation or maintenance of a poor physical environment, compared to what is needed for effective and safe use of IT

\- The total system in which the IT is embedded is often weak; an IT system may be developed that largely replicates the previous manual system, and where the parts that remain manual have not been re-designed

\- The inadequacy of training at all levels has been touched on several times already in this paper. Inadequate training and awareness seems at times to be so deeply embedded as to be the norm, that when an effective training programme is seen, it is all the more noticeable.

## The IT industry in context

The IT industry is not a closed system. IT firms have diversified into non-IT areas. Firms partly in IT may have expanded or contracted their IT operations. But by and large the evolution of the industry has been predicted along the assumption that there will be technological convergence between data processing, telecommunications and office automation. This still remains a prospect. Some IT industry restructuring has moved along these lines such as the STC–ICL merger. Yet in practical terms there are still very few products that actually integrate these three components. In particular there appears to be continuing difficulties in producing products that fully capitalise and create synergy between computing and telecommunications.

Whatever the pace of this first phase of IT convergence, there is nonetheless an even more significant phase of convergence. Some of the technological developments alluded to at the beginning of this paper including optical storage and transmission mean that a much greater blurring between the various forms of published media are likely. This is already happening in small ways:

\- Satellite systems carrying both broadcast and private data

\- Public television incorporating text-based information services (Ceefax and Oracle in the UK)

\- An increasing number of publications also being available electronically even, as in the case of the Financial Times, being available slightly earlier electronically than in print form

\- Broadcasting companies such as the BBC publishing computer-based interactive videodisk courseware

\- Compact disk technology which first had mass use in the music business, following on with data processing orientated CD-ROM systems.

It is unlikely that the publishing industry will ever become fully electronic – the sheer convenience, portability and lack of user-complexity of newspapers, magazines and books will be difficult to emulate electronically even in the 1990s. However it is possible to conceive of a series of events that could alter the balance between print and electronic publishing:

\- Ecological concern about forests leading to cost and other problems in the supply of paper

● Dramatic breakthrough in display technology - Some enormous leaps in the scope and nature of text retrieval software.

The convergence of print and electronic publishing has already slowly begun. The impact of IT developments on broadcasting and entertainment is still embryonic:

• the growth of IT/computer games

● electronic/video-based dating services

\- use of CD for both leisure and business.

Some of the most promising areas are likely to emerge in the area of images, both still and moving, natural and simulated. The development of hypermedia software and hardware already permits the close linkage of text, graphics, video and voice technologies. Low cost systems are now available to allow video images (for example from a security camera, TV or video) as a window in a PC which is otherwise carrying out ‘ordinary’ data processing work.

In terms of the structure of the IT industry, it is by no means inconceivable that it could become a subset of the publishing, broadcasting and entertainment industries; perhaps a more likely scenario than the reverse.

## Commodity computing

The early history of computing saw immense variation between hardware suppliers without, initially, even common programming languages. During each decade of computing some elements became standardised either by explicit standard-setting efforts, via major customer pressure (such as from the US Department of Defense), or through de facto standards arising out of market domination or leadership.

A prospect now much discussed for the 1990s is the extent to which some parts of computing will reach the status of a commodity, where the products produced are essentially standardised, and customer choice is not constrained by proprietary considerations which lock them in to a particular supplier or group of suppliers.

Although it is possible to point to parallel areas where a product has become a standard commodity (e.g. the cassette tape standard), it is still possible to observe that in other areas there appears to be a great increase in product differentiation and the proprietary approach. There appears to have been a great growth in, for example, the number of brands of flour prompted in part by greater customer awareness of the differences in taste or origin which they believe are either noticeable or of importance.

One of the great tensions is between a standard (and essentially historic) approach to IT and one that is based on innovation and risk taking, but which almost by definition is difficult to make consistent with standards.

The Apple and the Macintosh computers would never have appeared had their inventors not decided to both reject and ignore first the CP/M and latterly the MS/DOS operating system standards.

In the hardware area, the IBM XT, AT or PS/2 compatible computer market has come closest to commodity status, but the degree of compatibility of:

\- Processor chips and speeds

\- Screen graphics devices

\- Expansion cards

\- Keyboards

\- Versions of the DOS operating system

\- Disk formats

is very varied.

The very flexibility of the PC open architecture has made it vulnerable to slight variations and ‘improvements’ that reduce the prospects of standardisation.

At the minicomputer and high powered workstation level, Unix is proceeding to develop as the main standard. The split between Unix International and the Open Software Foundation is in the medium-term less significant than the commitment of both these groups to the POSIX standard and, recently, to X/Open.

In relation to networks and communications, there has been a longer history of standards, prompted not least by the concerns of PTTs to be able to have standard voice communication, both within a country, and between otherwise incompatible systems to enable international phone calls. The development of Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) standards under the aegis of the ISO has still not led to their mass introduction; there is still a massive proprietary emphasis particularly in mainframe and mini-computer communications and networking.

Turning to software, there are three particular areas where the development of standardisation is proceeding with reasonable speed, although whether there is a realistic likelihood of commodity status being reached is more doubtful.

The question of graphical user interfaces (GUIs) has sprung into prominence in the late 1980s due to widespread dissatisfaction with the almost anarchic lack of standardisation of screen layouts (and associated keyboard or input methods). The PC-software world noted in particular the success that Apple Corporation had had in imposing its recommended user interface standards on software houses, so that the user did not have to re-learn the interface with each new package acquired.

It is perhaps ironic that the great influence on both the Apple and most of the other PC and Unix GUIs was the Xerox Star, developed for R & D purposes in the 1970s. Its use of windows, icons, mouse and pointer (WIMP) has developed into the conventional wisdom. Attempts to utilise other types of interface such as touch screen, unusual keyboards, voice input and output have as yet been limited to specialised situations such as dealing rooms or the handicapped. Much of the reason for the lack of successful innovation lies in the physical limitation of screen displays, both in terms of size, quality and pixel-density. Most probably the major innovation in GUI for the 1990s will not be based on technologies such as Cathode-Ray Tube displays and well worn devices such as the mouse. They will hinge instead on flat screen technology, enabling huge screens to be hung on the wall or to take up a whole desktop. A whole desktop screen could also be a touch sensitive and fully configurable keyboard. This could permit many 'pages' and 'writing devices' to be visible and usable simultaneously. In other words, the real breakthrough is likely to come when the computer interface can be as good as that currently open to the person who uses his or her desk with several books or papers in view at once, writing onto several documents, also visible together, in succession.

The second area of software where standardisation is developing is in the development environment for applications. The ability to apply a structured query language (SQL) in a limited but standard way is one example. Another is the emphasis placed by producers of both relational databases and fourth generation languages in porting them onto a wide variety of hardware platforms, including the major proprietary ones. It is possible that one route to standardisation in the software area will be from organisations specifying the 4GL/Relational Database they intend to support across a wide variety of hardware.

A similar approach is emerging in relation to applications software. Major suppliers e.g., of general ledger packages have always made efforts to port their packages onto at least the most popular hardware platforms in a given country. A newer trend is the movement towards moving particular packages across from PCs to minis and mainframes. An example of this is with Word Perfect. At present the latest version of the software is only available on PCs, with the Unix version drawing on the older release of the package.

The major proprietary approach is that being set by IBM through its Systems Application Architecture (SAA). IBM became successful in networking by creating a de facto standard in Systems Network Architecture (SNA). Many other suppliers developed at least an SNA option because they needed to do so to enable their equipment to be attached to IBM's. It appears less likely that SAA will have the same effect – as yet neither software houses or even mainstream IBM users have made total commitments. It could well be that if X/Open is successful in developing a common applications environment for Unix, this could also in time influence other types of computing.

## Conclusion – converting ideas into action

No matter what developments take place in technology and no matter what changes take place in organisational and competitive structures, a central problem to be faced is how actually to convert worthwhile ideas about the use of IT into effective action.

This is, of course, the central managerial task whether for IT or non-IT matters. But it is acutely highlighted by IT-led change. It would be presumptuous to suggest that such a complex task can be solved by approaches summarised in a few sentences. It is perhaps therefore best to conclude this paper with a few personal observations.

Much has been made both elsewhere and here of the issue of IT strategy. There is always a great danger in the development of strategies being intellectually absorbing exercises, divorced from organisational reality. Staff at middle and junior levels of an organisation commonly feel that 'strategic' matters are matters only of concern to top management. Yet successful IT strategy and successful IT implementation need to be owned not only by top and middle managers, but by individual people throughout the organisation at all levels.

In researching the use of IT in organisations, there often appears to be a clear distinction between those organisations where IT is treated as functional and narrow in scope, and typically not well understood, and those where it is being broadly applied and is widely understood. Creating the organisational culture where IT can be effectively used is a major managerial challenge. It will normally be created as part of a wider organisational change and improvement. But this type of culture can be developed, though not perhaps so actively promoted, from the middle and even bottom up. The problem for many organisations is that they may well not have time for the middle and bottom up approaches to work through the system, hence the recurring need to engage and interest top managers in IT.

The challenges of IT and its management in the 1990s are at least as difficult as those faced in previous decades. Technology will certainly advance, but it is only through the application of sound management that the technological opportunities will be harnessed to an organisation's advantage.
