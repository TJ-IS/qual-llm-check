# Information Systems Planning and Alignment: A Progress Assessment

Ivan F. Jackson, Victoria University of Wellington

Abstract: This paper examines the ways in which to conduct planning for information services (IS) so that both computing and business problems may be addressed in an integrated fashion. It traces the evolution of IS planning from a more reactive mode, with a steering committee deciding on future directions, to a value-added approach where incremental components of a total IS product are offered to user managers for consideration. This helps overcome problems with the strategic planning process itself which is both incremental and cyclical. The organisation often focuses on a direction, only to change as the economic environment or market conditions change. The IS function consequently must move away from plans set in concrete in spite of implementation difficulties associated with that approach. The problem is one of aligning the IS plan with an often mobile corporate plan. In order to respond to this situation, there has been a move towards a marketing of IS services, so organisations can avail themselves of IS opportunities that are within the capabilities of the IS function to deliver.

## Introduction

By way of introduction it is convenient to divide Information Systems (IS) planning into three categories: operational planning, management control and strategic planning. This grouping follows the traditional management breakdown:

(a) Operational planning. This may be viewed as being characterised by project-oriented developments with relatively short time horizons and a virtual hands-off attitude by top management. Planning for these systems has typically been a bottom-up endeavour designed to fulfill a specific need.

(b) Management control. Expansion of IS planning emphasised the importance of top management input. Business objectives were incorporated into the overall plan with the ‘emphasis on ensuring information (resource) availability and the efficiency and effectiveness of the systems producing this information’ Hufnagel (1987). Hufnagel gives as examples IBM’s Business Systems Planning (BSP) and Critical Success Factors.

(c) Strategic planning. A wide range of viewpoints have been presented in the literature. A typical approach has been proposed by IBM (1983), in which an architectural structure is derived from the business plan. In turn the IS plan is derived from this architecture:

(i) Business strategic planning: based on defining the IS mission based on enterprise mission and objectives, defining business processes and associated information needs.

(ii) Architecture definition: a technology architecture is derived from a combination of an application architecture and a ‘data architecture’.

(iii) IS strategic planning and control: alternate implementations are assessed on the basis of business justification and risk; strategic objectives are prioritised and approval obtained. Subsequently the strategic plan is controlled against the tactical performance.

In recent times there has been a very heavy emphasis in the literature on the use of IS as a competitive weapon, a trend spurred on by the Michael Porter books (1980, 1985). Indeed there is no shortage of planning tools. An IBM share document (1985) lists 25 tools covering the three categories listed above; the Hufnagel article lists eleven.

The aforementioned tools, however, only partially address the problem ‘of how to conduct planning for computing that is directly linked to the business planning of the enterprise . . . and linked in such a way as to address the many associated problems in computing and business’ (McConkey, 1983). The remainder of this paper will therefore address this issue, and in particular explore how the IS strategy can be linked with the business strategy.

## Planning and strategy

Planning, as generally understood, can be thought of as extending the past and the present into the future. By contrast, strategic planning concentrates on defining goals and then on deciding on how to get to those goals from the present. Implicit in this is the presence of an organisational component.

A simplistic list of the basic questions that need to be asked would include:

\- Where is the organisation now?

\- Where does it want to be in the future?

\- What is the most appropriate way of getting there?

A much more realistic approach has been given by Rowe et al. (1985):

(1) What kind of business should the company be in and what should its performance objectives be?

(2) How should the firm pursue its objectives in each field of endeavour specified in the economic mission?

(3) How should the events and activities required to achieve competitive strategy be programmed?

(4) When and to what extent should the master plan be changed?

Corporate planning thus differs from conventional planning in that the straight application of a model does not apply. Usually, if nothing else, the planning process is both iterative and cyclic, moving into the domain of scenarios, perceptions of which are successively refined as assumptions take on a clearer form.

Most companies involved in strategic planning would follow the process depicted in Table 1.

It should be emphasised with respect to point (1) above, that there should be an emphasis on opportunities, rather than strengths. A firm with this approach would not make the classic marketing mistake of, say, producing the same or similar goods because that is what it has done for years (i.e. playing to its strengths, perhaps in spite of market changes). Rather it would involve itself in determining opportunities and acquiring the capabilities to move in direction indicated by them.

Implicit in the above, also, is that once a situation audit has been performed, actual strategies will be derived from the corporate mission statement. If this does not reflect the reality, or the overall sense of purpose for the organisation then participants in the planning process will be working from a fuzzy base. In a sense the determination of the mission is the No. 1 strategy.

McConkey (1983) argues that at the outset an organisation does not really determine its purpose or mission; the customer makes this determination. His view is that an effective mission statement always proceeds from the outside (the customer and the environment) to the inside (the management's response to the customers' needs and wants). All of the above points apply to an IS group. Just as with the organisation as a whole, it is the IS customers (i.e. its internal users) who must inevitably determine the raison d'être for the information systems function.

The above suggests that the IS department must develop a marketing orientation to its planning. There will be an emphasis on assessing opportunities rather than selling. The IS planner will thus seek a degree of alignment with the organisational plan. In practice, this will most likely require that the most emphasis be placed on what is typically the one particular strategic area the firm is concentrating on at any given time. (Tregoe and Zimmerman (1980) refer to this concentration of effort as the driving force behind the firm's strategic operations.) In IS literature the concentration is usually referred to as the thrust of the company.

## Practical problem with alignment

Some of the difficulties in alignment were explored by Lederer and Mendelow (1986), who observed that the making of formal plans was not sufficient. The plans must be implemented. To assess what is involved in this, the authors interviewed IS managers at the top, middle and operating levels. They were specifically interested in identifying and ranking the difficulties encountered in planning for information systems. A shortened version of the results is presented below:

(1) IS Staff at the Top Management Level (Strategic planning??) (Director, MIS; VP, etc.)

(i) Knowing top management's objectives. This includes the corporation strategic objectives and the business plan.

(ii) Determining resources (personnel, skills, hardware, etc.) to assign to projects.

(iii) Flexibility of the planning cycle, including the need to realise that planning is a continuous process.

(iv) The need to coordinate MIS priorities with the business plan.

(v) Getting top management to understand that there is a strategic role for MIS.

(2) IS Staff at the Middle Management Level (Management control??) (Manager, Computer Services, etc.)

(i) Adopting a methodology for planning (i.e. IBM's BSP).

(ii) Integration of the business strategic plan with the IS plan.

(iii) Getting deined statements of needs or objectives.

(3) IS Staff at the Operating Management Level (Operational planning??) (Systems Supervisor; Project Manager)

(i) Having to understand the perceived needs of the user.

(ii) The impact of the market (i.e. business conditions) which force immediate reaction by the IS function.

(iii) Change, and the effect of technology, user and workforce variables on the IS function.

The above list is particularly interesting because, with the exception of Item 1(ii), and possibly of 3(ii) and (iii), all the difficult areas are concerned with the integration of the IS plan with the corporate plan. Communication between the two groups, is one aspect of this difficulty; translation into resources and priorities is another. The situation is not helped by the apparent fuzziness of the corporate plan, and as will be seen, the cyclical and iterative nature of the planning process. With heavy capital commitments likely, the IS manager is understandably looking for a degree of firmness that is possible not there.

The situation is further complicated because for all too many organisations, the actual strategic planning process has been not all that successful. Some of the reasons for this are discussed in the next section.

## Problems in the corporate strategy area

Over the years a number of criticisms have been levelled at the strategic planning process. These will be examined in an information systems context:

1. Unable to cope with rapid change. The business environment can change so rapidly that strategic planning (here read long term planning) becomes irrelevant.

Comment: This depends on what the strategy was! If the strategy was to pare costs to the bone, then the company could well have been able to better weather a very rapid change in the environment (e.g. the 1987 crash). A strategy could also be in place to respond to shifts in technology. This is a strategy for change rather than a static strategy and as such must be incorporated in any IS plan.

2. Too narrow a focus. All too often a management's focus is on specific issues rather than on a central strategic theme that allows for integration on the organisation's resources in a systematic manner. Thus strategic concepts rarely focus with sufficient depth on the basis functions and operating skills that are the core of the organisation's business strengths.

Comment: The organisation's products and services, how they are produced, marketed, priced and delivered; the organisation's relationships with its suppliers, customers, clients and indeed its market, with its bankers and with the rest of the industry, must all be considered in formulating a corporate strategy.

A failure to look at the breadth of resources is likely to be accompanied by a lack of understanding of the interrelationships that can exist among them. This has strategic implications for planning an MIS as more often than not it is these very interrelationships that provided the justification for the MIS in the first place. Further, objectives will have little meaning for an organisation if they cannot be shared and so help weld discrete components of the organisation together.

3. Misapplied concepts. This is very tied in with a lack of adequate information. Because, strategic planning is future-oriented by definition, an ill-defined base can lead to the selection of inappropriate methods.

Comment: When key pieces of information are needed, they are often unavailable or limited in their reliability. Information on competitors may be highly subjective. Executives may be unable to effectively sift through a large quantity of information and so discard valuable items along with less valuable ones. The lack of an adequate planning base, plus the dynamic economic environment jeopardised chances of success. Planners thus are perceived to be remote from the real world and lose credibility.

## 4. Gap between strategy and capabilities. New strategies must take advantage of the historical strengths of the organisation.

Comment: A new strategy may, of its very nature, changes the way a firm does business. It should not be assumed that the present management will automatically be able to adjust to the new circumstances. Thus the imposition of a complex technology on an unsophisticated organisation will increase the likelihood of failure. Put another way, an integral part of an IS strategy must be to match the plan with the organisational capabilities.

5. Inappropriate management and structure. This is very much tied in with 4. above. Change, and particularly strategic change, invariably causes disruption. Management response must be either to leave the organisation unchanged, or to reorganise. (It is not coincidental that organisational reviews often recommend a restructuring.) In other words, a 'strategy' often results in a change in structure. The problem, however, is as Ansoff notes that 'Managers lack (the) skills and knowledge for performing in the new roles defined by the structure; the historical information system no longer serves the needs of the new roles ... and ... the historical management system (is) ineffective in solving problems posed by the new strategy'.

Comment: The signal then is that technological change must involve the management of the new work environment, including a redefinition of management roles and an examination of associated accountability.

## 6. Poor implementation

Comment: Poor implementation often comes about because the operational managers do not share the same perspectives (or even the same culture) as the planners. Except where a whole new area is to be initiated, implementation usually involves superimposing the plan over an existing operation or functional area. Unless the planners and the operational managers can get in synch, it is unlikely that the line managers will be able to/willing to achieve effective implementation.

From an information viewpoint, a failure to provide reporting on a differential basis may mean that results of new activities are buried in a host of interacting factors. If planners fail to observe the operational reality they run the risk of seeing only the results that they were looking for. It must be concluded therefore that any planning activity must be carried on with those operational managers concerned with implementation.

A recent research article by Lederer and Sethi (1988) identified a number of potential causal factors affecting planning for and implementing information systems. These are:

\- Organisations with less sophistication in business planning had more severe problems than more sophisticated organisations.

\- Organisations with less participation by the IS department in business planning had more severe problems than organisation with greater participation.

\- Organisations where the top IS executive reported to a controller (i.e. financial executive) had more severe problems than organisations where the top IS executive reported to a general manager or at a similar level.

\- Organisations where top management initiated the study had more severe problems than organisations where IS management initiated it.

Comment: This will no doubt come as a surprise to many readers. One can assume that the IS departments would have been operating in a reactive mode for this situation to occur. The TOP model approach, presented towards the end of this paper, goes a long way towards alleviating this problem.

\- Strategic information planning studies with a division of function as their scope had more severe problems than studies with the entire enterprise as their scope.

\- When a planning study failed to specify a planning horizon (i.e. a schedule is drawn up and followed), problems were more severe than when it did specify a planning horizon.

From the discussion above it is apparent that there are serious problems with conventional approaches to IS planning. These may be summarised as:

\- Strategic planning, of its very nature, is future oriented. In association with this there must be an emphasis on seeking out opportunities rather than necessarily moving from current strengths.

\- A move to the use of IS as a competitive weapon means that IS managers must be in a position to sense the direction of the organisation. It has been noted that the IS mission statement must be derived from its customers, the users and potential users of IS services. A failure of IS to accurately assess reality could mean that the IS planning activity will operate from a fuzzy base.

\- There are communication difficulties in assessing and understanding top management objectives. This is undoubtedly due, in part at least, because the corporate plan is self-evolving on an iterative/cyclic basis. Most of the IS literature assumes a corporate plan to be a fixed entity. Any IS planning approach must be dynamic in the sense that it responds to variations in the corporate plan.

\- Further difficulties may be induced because of possible basic deficiencies in the planning approach as used by the organisation. The potential gap between the strategy and capabilities, and the existence of an inappropriate management structure must be of concern to IS managers as these factors will clearly impinge on the risk elements inherent in any implementation process.

\- The IS function must position itself with regard to its capabilities in offering services to the rest of the organisation.

## Planning for alignment

Goodhue et al. (1988) in a major study of data management efforts (read IS planning build around data driven approaches), found that there is no single dominant approach to the management of an organisation's data. Rather, firms have adopted multiple approaches, 'all of which need to be considered by any organisation striving to leverage data as a valuable business resource'. Indeed, the report notes that there is very much a limited general knowledge of firms' experience with existing approaches to data management and that effective data management efforts fit no single clear pattern.

The report goes on to note that to many people, 'planning for data resource management is synonymous with a large scale strategic data planning and modelling effort'. To some extent this perspective is unfortunate as the implication is that there either is no need, or it is not possible, to plan for smaller incremental-type IS activities. The study, however, did note a number of business objectives behind the planning process. These should be borne in mind when considering the various planning approaches discussed and presented below. The particular points found in the Goodhue study were:

\- Most successful data management (DM) processes observed were aimed at solving a clear and specific business problem or exploiting an opportunity.

\- A major objective for DM action is to better coordinate operational activities, either within or across specific functions or business units.

\- A desire for greater organisational flexibility was also observed. This was to allow either an internal restructuring of the organisation or a refocussing due to changes in the environment.

\- The dominant objective was to improve information for senior and middle managers and key staff. Thus, when management wants to analyse changing market trends or more closely monitor profitability for instance, it was found the information consumers wanted two things: improved access to data and improved data quality.

In discussing the evolution of IS planning, the reader should bear the above factors in mind. It is quite apparent that new skills are involved in developing and implementing an effective planning approach. In particular, a move towards organisational level IS planning must involve a move away from the process-oriented traditional methods.

## Top-Down IS planning

This is the traditional approach to IS planning, with an EDP Steering Committee meeting on a more or less regular basis. Typical discussion centers around the performance of existing (and especially newer) systems. Priority determination is based on perceived corporate need and are usually based on submissions by individual functional managers. This approach, illustrated in Figure 1, is very much alive and well in New Zealand.

The degree of alignment that is achieved using this approach is problematical. Because the steering committee is seen to determine policy, this approach is generally understood inherently to encompass what the textbooks refer to as top management involvement. Except in smaller firms, however, top management usually delegates the task of chairing committee meetings. The chairperson, in turn, attempts to keep order in an organisational bunfight as individual managers compete for priorities and scarce resources. With so many benefits of a modern information system being in the intangible category, it is unlikely that committee members will be able to devote enough time to adequately assess each candidate system.

If the committee is actually a policy committee (i.e. composed of the top level managers) then it is likely that their thrust will be mainly in developing a corporate information plan and statement of intent. If however the committee operates merely as a steering committee, then it is likely that developments will tend to be more application oriented and as a result the IS will be cast in a reactive role.

The steering committee may operate within the framework of a capacity plan (i.e. hardware projections), but is unlikely to operate within an organisational plan. Also, with application-oriented developments there is always the danger that the IS thrust is not really geared towards the overall operation of the firm. With IS effectiveness low (that is individual applications contribute little to mission achievement), it follows that the degree of alignment is also low.

## Business process strategic planning

This approach is widely promulgated in IBM literature (see for example IBM, 1983). It offers a break from the traditional policy committee in that, rather than being oriented towards a specific user, it results in a development approach oriented to the business strategy. It can be assumed, therefore that the degree of alignment is high.

Process-oriented planning is essentially a top-down approach that assumes that a corporate plan already exists. The corporate plan is broken down into functional areas and a determination is made of the information requirements needed by each functional area. An inventory is made of the existing applications in each functional area so that deficiencies can be highlighted.

To a large extent, business process strategic planning is one of the earlier attempts at providing a data driven methodology. In this environment, the applications can be built around data classes (or clusters), representing business objectives, performance objectives, and data for management responsibilities and decision-making. When the entire business enterprise is examined in this manner, a data architecture can be defined: see Figure 2.

The problem, as it happens, is that it is quite difficult to develop the data architecture. Part of the problem is that although the data are expressed in matrix form (i.e. data against application, function, etc.), the data are essentially linear, as compared to structured data (as, for instance, in the Information Engineering method – see below). The trouble with this is that it takes a great deal of skill to transform the unstructured data into database form. Further, it is not at all apparent as to how a data architecture can be transformed into an environment for IS planning and control.

## Information Engineering: a data management approach

Information Engineering (IE) could be considered an advance over BSP in that the process is very much top-down. What this means in practice is that the process can be stopped at any time while still retaining a useful product.

As with BSP, IE can be considered a strategic data driven approach. The key difference, to this author at least, is that the process starts off at the information level rather than the data level. To explain the import of this one needs to recall the difference between data and information. Most computer people will be aware that data are the raw material of an information processing system and are usually of little value in isolation. A unit of information on the other hand is generally much more valuable. This value can result from the input of the costs associated with massaging the data through a processing system or because of the inherent nature of the information content (i.e. its surprise value, the opportunities presented from its acquisition – e.g. marketing or financial opportunities, crisis or problem information, etc.).

The expression data analysis, as used in development methodologies, implies a bottom-up approach. However, when used, and the data elements associated with a system or potential system are listed, the number often becomes very large indeed. Further there is a tendency to treat the individual elements as all being roughly equal, that is the actual process of defining the data does not impart a sense of priority or importance to them. This is not true for information. Any manager would be able to list particularly important information items and would also be able to specify information that needs to be broken down in some way or which needs to be supplemented or recast. A high degree of alignment is thus an inherent part of the process.

A representation of the information engineering approach is shown in Figure 3. The business is first broken down into strategic business areas, for instance in a bank, commercial lending would be relationships are identified. As with the BSP approach, data elements may be clustered into logical sets. In an ideal situation an individual manager will be given management responsibility over the functional activity and associated cluster. The cluster also forms the basic data set around which a specific information processing subsystem (hardware/software) may be implemented.

## Enterprise-wide information management

The approaches discussed above follow an essentially evolutionary sequence, the aim being to overcome alignment problems and integrate the IS plan with the corporate direction. However, as noted earlier, an IBM study (Parker and Benson, 1985) found that there was a major gap in the understanding of the how of linking the business planning of the enterprise to the computing plan. In the study it was suggested that an approach called Enterprise-Wide Information Management (EwIM) be used. In EwIM a cyclical planning approach is used – see Figure 5.

![](/api/attachments/6PW4AEG2/fulltext/images/13258139cfccefdb6913f3b72add386b873264e438db06b70ae8cfe6b7e0939f.jpg)  
Figure 5. EwIM planning relationships (Parker and Benson, 1985)

The idea behind EwIM is that the planning relationships are on-going as well as being incremental. Business planning drives the business organisation which drives the technology planning that is needed to support it. Technology planning produces opportunities for the future use of technology in the enterprise. Completing the circular relationship, the strategic use of that technology will influence business strategies and plans.

The key to the above framework is that the organisation has developed its technological capability before the technology is used as part of the business strategy. Thus an organisation moves from a technology scanning mode to the piloting or testing of a particular technology which is likely to be appropriate to the organisation.

In following this approach, the IS function will have adopted a proactive role in the diffusion of information technology. It will also be in a position to install a particular technology as the strategic demands of the organisation require it.

There are complications to the above however. The realities of the economic world and the inhibitors to change in the organisation combine to create forces that can dictate much of the planning process. These include:

\- The economic environment (e.g. post-crash conditions have resulted in a reluctance to invest in new technology).

\- Technology changes in that a given technology can very rapidly become obsolete or alternatively, new technologies can very rapidly become economic (e.g. facsimile and some communications technologies).

\- Organisational inflexibilities (e.g. the lack of a support infrastructure can inhibit future growth).

A successful planner must be in a position to address such inhibiting factors as well as being able to develop a planning process which is acceptable to the organisation.

## Product-oriented information systems planning

It should be clear from the above that alignment is a critical issue in the IS planning process. No doubt its importance played an implicit part in the evolution of the various development approaches. It is also apparent that the planning process itself is cyclical, and that IS planners should be cognisant of this.

In such an environment it is probably going to be very difficult, if not impossible, to develop an IS plan which will remain stable under all conditions. In a proactive environment IS management could well follow the means-ways-ends model suggested by Hayes (1985), in which the means are the capabilities or resources necessary to implement a strategy; the strategy itself represents the ways of achieving corporate objectives and the ends are the objectives themselves. Basically, this implies a positioning by the IS function.

Implicit in the above suggestion is the idea of an incremental and dynamic approach to planning. It is not reactive, however, in that substantial effort is expended on developing the means, through a coordinated technology transfer programme, of providing the necessary alignment as the overall corporate plan is adjusted and modified.

It is not proposed that all systems development activities would be treated in like manner. For some time now, it has been recognised that certain components of the corporate information system perform backbone capabilities essential to the efficient running of the organisation. Normally corporate financial and operational systems fall into this category. The planning approach, therefore, must be capable of addressing these core systems as well as the incremental improvements and add-ons associated with these systems as well as standalone, ad hoc systems often implemented in a prototyping and/or 4GL mode.

Quite clearly the dual approach is required. In this regard the approach proposed by Ansoff (1984) can be applied. He proposes that strategic planning be split into two parts – operations planning and innovations or future developments planning. The first is the responsibility of operations management and is concerned with the profit making activities of the company – i.e. operating programs and budgets. The second is more concerned with issue management and is directed towards the profit potential or developments within the organisation. The dual management approach recognises that operations planning, or backbone systems planning, is different from the more dynamic strategic planning for future developments. (It is of course understood that the backbone systems are absolutely strategic in their own right but the massive investment in operational procedures and training means that they cannot be easily adjusted without substantial effort.)

Jackson and Wong in an unpublished paper (1989) propose a method which addresses the future developments component of Ansoff's duality as well as addressing the issues raised specifically in this section and generally in this paper. This approach rests on the TOtal Product (TOP) model as a vehicle for IS planned alignment. The model is based on the total product concept first introduced by Levitt (1983).

The total product concept provides a perspective for viewing the various aspects of a product from a consumer viewpoint. In our case, the IS function is clearly the product supplier, the product is the various computer and information services that can be offered and the consumers are the internal customers or users of these services. The idea is that ‘consumers attach value to products in proportion to the perceived ability of those products to help solve their problems. Hence a product has meaning only from the viewpoint of the user’. The concept is illustrated in Figure 6.

In the total product model the core or generic product is a basic item or service being offered. In an informations systems environment this could be a basic ledger system. Very few customers would purchase this basic product but would rather have an expectation of a minimal service requirement. This is the expected product. This degree of expectation is often expressed when organisations go to tender for a software product. The augmented product is tied in with the ability of a supplier to differentiate between its particular product and those of the competition. Thus, a supplier may increase the desirability of a product by adding value to the product. The last component or outer shell is the potential product which is very much related to the vision of where the customer (or in this case the consumer of computer related services) want to be.

What is being proposed is that the IS function moves more to a marketing orientation in their planning for computer services. This marketing approach can be used either in the design (or purchase) of a new backbone product or in planning for a new strategic product. The planning process of the services oriented IS function will thus include an element of consumer research or planning for the plan. In other words the planning orientation seeks to define the total product as a base platform for the definition of its future services. The IS function then builds up its capabilities to be able to deliver elements of service within the TOP model framework, prior to requests being made.

With the capabilities in place as a result of the above process, the IS function will then be in a position to negotiate the provision of value added services to its internal clients. Because the whole scheme is retained within the TOP framework requests for further services will, in all probability, not come as a surprise and be well within the pre-established capabilities.

The advantage of the TOP model approach is that a high degree of alignment and responsiveness can be achieved. The IS function operates very much in a marketing mode and decisions by customers to purchase augmented capabilities can be negotiated on the basis of the value added to the client activity as compared to the cost of the actual service. There is thus a marked movement away from the traditional difficulties associated with the cost justification of new services where many of the benefits are intangible and thus extremely difficult to quantify. In simple terms customer will be offered a particular service element and they, with an understanding of their own operation, will be able to decide whether or not the additional service is worth purchasing.

## Conclusion

Information systems planning remains one of the critical and most difficult issues facing the IS executive. Historically, the planning process has addressed individual business processes. In a limited way this has been quite adequate up until the point that the organisation wished to assess the effectiveness of its IS services from a corporate viewpoint. In a move to be responsive the IS industry has emphasized the requirement for alignment of the IS plan with the corporate plan. Unfortunately, this might be likened to shooting at a moving target, in that a dynamic organisation, in responding to a changing environment, must continually adjust its own plans.

It is probably generally accepted that data driven approaches to both information systems development and planning constitute an effective tool. Increasingly, through the use of information engineering, coupled with an enterprise-wide view, plans can be developed which provide some degree of flexibility as organisations combat the competitive forces in the market place. Planning for implementation can also be enhanced through the use of the TOP model which looks at the provision of information services on a value added basis which at the same time the overall corporate focus is kept in perspective.

Ivan F. Jackson is Professor of Management Information Systems at Victoria University of Wellington. He also acts as a management consultant with PA Management Consultants.
