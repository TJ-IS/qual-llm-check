# Internal Auditors and the Prevention and Detection of Computer Fraud

Claire Marston, Newcastle Business School
Rob Dixon, Newcastle Business School
Paul Collier, Exeter University

Abstract: This study examines the involvement and attitudes of internal auditors to the prevention and detection of computer fraud. This approach differs from previous research which has concentrated on learning from frauds which have occurred. The main enquiry was by means of a questionnaire sent to members of the Institute of Internal Auditors. Verification and additional information was forthcoming by visiting some respondents.

Almost a fifth of internal audit departments reported that they had no specific responsibility for either prevention or detection of computer fraud. It was clear that where responsibility was acknowledged, it is generally on an informal basis or is self imposed.

Internal auditors reported that most reliance was placed on computer assisted tools and manual techniques like input/output reconciliation for detection of computer fraud. Few of the organisations surveyed had any laid down guidelines what to do in the case of a fraud discovery. Where guidelines did exist they called for dismissal and prosecution. In smaller firms, external auditors have a larger role in the prevention and detection of computer fraud than in larger firms.

Opinion on the prevention and detection of computer fraud included the view that as network systems become more common, so detection and prevention will become more difficult. In addition it was claimed that management did not appreciate the level of the threat. Internal auditors feel that they have a role to play, but highlighted the fact that there is a shortage of staff with the requisite skills.

## Introduction

Recent research on computer fraud in the UK has concentrated on the circumstances surrounding known computer frauds and has attempted to point out the lessons which should be learned. The main source of information has been the government surveys conducted in 1981, 1984 and 1987, first by the Audit Inspectorate but latterly by the Audit Commission. The pattern of these studies, as summarised in the Audit Commission (1987) is for an increase in the number of incidents and the average loss incurred (1981 67 incidents – £905,149 lost, 1984 77 indicents – £1,133,487 lost and 1987 118 incidents – £2,561,351 lost). Even more dramatic is the change found in BIS Applied Systems (1987) which reported the average loss per incident up from £31,000 in 1983 to £262,000. Abroad, studies like American Bar Association (1984) in the US; and the Chisholm Institute of Technology (1984) in Australia present a similar picture. Despite these results the Audit Commission survey also found that 92% of the 1,200 respondents had not experienced a computer crime in the past three years. This result suggests that although computer fraud is a growing problem: it is not yet widespread. Although this may not be the case in specific business sectors. For example, a police survey (Financial Times 26 September 1989) pointed to £477m involved in cases of fraud or attempted fraud, often relating to computer systems, in the Square Mile. However, commentators suggest that the threat of computer fraud will grow (for example the Audit Commission (1987) observed that “opportunities for misuse continue to increase in line with technological advance”).

This survey, in contrast to the studies discussed above, focuses not on reported incidents, but seeks to explore where responsibility is vested within firms for computer fraud prevention and detection and the role and opinions of internal auditors in counteracting this threat.

Many definitions of computer fraud exist. These range from any dishonesty taking place in a computer environment to specific technical adjustments to hardware or software. For the purposes of this study, the definition that was used by the Audit Commission in the 1987 survey has been applied. The concern of this study is therefore, 'any fraudulent behaviour connected with computerisation by which someone intends to gain a dishonest advantage'.

## Methodology and objectives

The specific objectives of the research were to: (i) identify were the responsibility for computer fraud prevention and detection resided within the organisation;

(ii) examine the role of the internal audit department in corporate efforts to prevent and detect computer fraud; and

(iii) discover the opinions of internal auditors on level of risk and threat of computer fraud in various areas.

The emphasis on internal auditors arises because they are uniquely placed to provide information for three reasons:

(i) the requirement of auditor independence precludes them from operating or otherwise being involved in the systems through which computer fraud might be committed;

(ii) internal auditors are responsible for examining and evaluating the adequacy and effectiveness of the organisation's system of internal control. Internal controls are the means by which organisations counter the threat of internal fraud; and

(iii) internal audit departments as well as existing in the vast majority of large and medium sized organisations are also often present in smaller firms and therefore a survey targeted at internal auditors would cover a representative cross-section of firms.

The research approach was to develop and field test a questionnaire, which was sent to three hundred members of a population defined as members of the Institute of Internal Auditors and Chartered Institute of Management Accountants, who were working as internal auditors (a population at 31

January 1988 of 2631). 184 usable responses were received – a response rate of 61%. The responses were validated by contact through visits or by phone to 32 respondents. The standard tests failed to detect any non-response bias.

To establish the breadth of coverage of the responses the respondents were analysed into groups by reference to either annual turnover or total budget, dependent upon which was applicable. Table 1 gives an indication of the range of organisations surveyed.

Further, the responses were analysed between public and private sector. Of the 184 respondents 125 (68%) were in the private sector and 59 (32%) were in the public domain. These analyses suggest that the responses are representative of organisations in the UK and there the results should give a broad indication of current practice amongst internal auditors and within firms.

## Responsibility for Prevention and Detection

Table 2 shows that respondents considered that specific responsibility for countering computer fraud is not consistently attributed within organisations. Fifty seven (31%) indicated that responsibility was spread between three or more of the categories given; ninety four (51%) limited responsibility to one or two categories and the remaining 18% reported that no specific responsibility was placed on any person or function within the organisation for the prevention or detection of computer fraud.

Nevertheless, the responses showed that internal auditors are most commonly responsible for most aspects of computer fraud prevention and detection (see Table 2). Although internal audit departments in many firms may shoulder the responsibility for both prevention and detection, such responsibility is assumed in an informal, unstructured way, rather than being part of the documented department function (see Table 3).

This lack of overall and specific responsibility causes some disquiet. There is an apparent lack of supervision in these matters on the part of senior management. Given this state of affairs the apparent absence of line management responsibility adds to this concern. It is accepted that security responsibilities rest with senior and line managers, and it is therefore up to them to introduce an adequate system of internal control.

When Le Grand (1986) points out that the prevention and detection of computer fraud, it is clear that precautions need to be taken at various stages in the process. With a new systems design prevention needs to be considered so that the software and hardware may be designed in such a way as to prevent particular types of fraud. Similarly for detection, program patches may be inserted to help detect a fraud once it has been perpetrated. This is true of all stages of the computerisation process. Also of considerable importance is the monitoring of existing systems. Advantage may be taken of an amendment to an existing system to carry out an audit function to discover ways of improving detection and prevention techniques. Rather than waiting for such a period of amendment, there could also be times of specific reviews, when new measures of prevention and detection may be added. As demonstrated by Table 4 nearly all those taking part in the study claimed that staff considered computer fraud specifically at various stages in the system's life cycle, and are involved in all the stages shown. It is worth noting, however, that there is relatively low involvement in the amendment of existing systems, in spite of the fact that involvement at this stage would be a useful tool in attempting to prevent fraud.

Audit departments in some organisations would appear to have taken responsibility for some detection. SIAS 3 indicates, however, that although internal auditors should be aware of the possibilities of detecting fraud, and subsequently recommend controls to make perpetration more difficult, it is not their primary purpose.

The responsibilities resting with the audit department are clearly undertaken informally rather than as a specific function of the department. In circumstances where responsibility has been accepted by the internal audit department, most (nearly two thirds) cases do not have such responsibilities laid down as departmental objectives or job descriptions.

The emphasis on input controls tallies with the results of the Audit Commission (1987) and the Commission of the European Community (1984), both of which found that in the majority of cases reviewed fraudsters merely prepared false documents for input to the computer or entered false data.

From the viewpoint of fraud prevention the low significance given to personnel matters is of concern. Authorities like Krauss and MacGahan (1979), the Institute of Chartered Accountants (1987) and Comer (1985) stress that fraud is committed by people and therefore the selection and retention of trustworthy staff is a key countermeasure. This point was illustrated by the case in the 1987 Audit Commission report of a person, who falsified educational and professional qualifications to gain the post of accountant, defrauding the firm of £1,125,655. Basic personnel checks should have detected that he was not a qualified accountant. Another question on controls over staff operations in high risk areas, (controls such as compulsory holidays, prevention of working out notice) produced a similar response to the effect that these were deemed to be a low priority. This reinforces the conclusion that the risk to an organisation from the staff is not appreciated.

## Detection techniques

The most common detection technique is the use of interrogation software. Others in common use are installation review and input and output reconciliation. The use of other techniques is fairly evenly balanced as shown in Table 6.

Whilst these figures may represent the use of particular methods of detection, they do not demonstrate the variety of techniques that are available. In addition, there may be relative shortcomings associated with individual software packages that allow some fraud to slip through. Due to the shortage of accurate figures measuring the degree of computer fraud successfully perpetrated, it is essential to bear in mind the difficulties in assessing the effectiveness of using only one or two techniques of detection. At this point it is worth noting that even in the absence of reliable official statistics on how widespread computer fraud is, it is generally agreed that computer fraud poses a significant threat in many organisations.

On the occasion of a positive detection of computer fraud, it seems that the majority of organisations do not follow any specific guidelines as to what action to take. From the results of the study, it is clear that larger organisations are more likely to have guidelines than smaller ones. Also, private sector organisations are less likely to have formal guidelines that public sector ones.

However, where guidelines do exist (in only 30% of the sample in the study), it is most common for action to be taken on detection of a computer fraud. In 78% of cases the response was to prosecute the perpetrator. Recommended action also included dismissal. This is interesting in that it is often said that prosecutions do not occur in such cases due to bad publicity. However, the figures extracted from this study agree with those of the Audit Commission in 1987, which states that ‘contrary to popular belief, those caught abusing the system were penalised in some way’.

## Main areas of risk

Having considered means of prevention and detection under the control of internal auditors, it is interesting to see how this section of the organisation perceives the main areas of risk. As is demonstrated by Table 7 the internal auditors who took part in this study, felt that the main risk was from three principal areas: real-time and on-line processing, automated payments and mini-computers under user control. Also worthy of note is that the use of microcomputers represents a risk.

The view that batch processing is less risky than online and real time processing as demonstrated above, agrees with the findings of the Audit Commission (1987) which showed 3.4 incidents involving on-line systems for each one associated with batch processing.

As stated earlier, in spite of the lack of official statistics concerning the level of computer fraud, it is generally thought that it poses a significant threat to organisations. The internal auditors who took part in this study considered that all except one of the areas listed in Table 8 constitute a threat. The fact that the Audit Commission survey of 1987 found that input fraud was seen to pose the greatest threat is supported by the figures in Table 8. The areas representing the greatest threat may be said to require a lower level of skill to commit fraud. For example the incorrect entry of data requires less 'know how' than an insertion of a program patch or the alteration of, for example, part of a software audit package.

## Response

How should organisations respond to the findings of this study, and others like it? To begin with, it must be accepted that as information technology advances, and has greater impact on more and more sections of society and industry, organisations will become more vulnerable to computer related fraud. Bologna's (1984) statement that 'while the technology of computer systems was exploding, the security of EDP continued to lag. Today the gap between EDP technology and security is bigger than at any point since 1954' is perhaps even more true now. Therefore management needs to accept that there is a threat, assess the dangers within its organisation, and then respond.

The following measures would be prudent:

(a) Education: the relevant staff, who will be concerned with the prevention and detection of computer fraud need to be educated in their role.

(b) Responsibility: once an organisation has adequately trained staff, responsibility for prevention and detection of computer fraud should be allocated to them. Responsibility may cross different areas within an organisation. If this is so it may be necessary to appoint a member of staff with overall responsibility. This measure should assist in co-ordination between the different sections, as well as assuring compliance with an overall corporate response.

(c) Implementation: action is required in response to the threat of a fraud. Controls need to be put in place, and careful detection measures must be put in motion. Incidents of fraud need to be identified and recorded because any control mechanism in business needs to be cost effective. The cost of implementation has to be measured against estimated future losses due to fraud. The choice of controls will tend to be specific to a particular installation, but controls that are likely to be included are: security in physical access to computers, (i.e. to ensure that only authorised staff have access to the machines), the use of passwords (which should be changed regularly), software security, communications networks (again to prevent unauthorised access to data which is vulnerable in the transmission stage), personnel management and systems development.

## Conclusions

There was a number of significant findings as a result of the study.

(1) Organisations appear to have failed to address the subject of concentrating responsibility for the prevention and detection of computer fraud. Where responsibility does exist within an organisation, it tends to be spread widely with the danger that the approach is haphazard and uncoordinated.

(2) In cases where responsibility for detection and prevention has been allocated the internal audit department is the most likely holder. In most cases, however, this is undertaken on an informal basis without formal documentation. This would appear to indicate that organisations do not consider computer fraud to be a high priority matter.

(3) Most internal audit departments had some interest in and consideration of computer fraud at the installation stage of the system. At later stages of the system's life cycle, however, such as at an amendment of an existing system, there was a relatively low involvement of the internal audit department. The significance of this omission is that, without close scrutiny from the audit department or adequate development/maintenance controls, there may be an opportunity to insert a fraudulent routine.

(4) Organisations adopt a variety of computer fraud prevention techniques. Of particular importance were segregation of duties and the use of passwords. Due to the apparent lack of appreciation of rigorous personnel selection procedures as a defensive measure against computer fraud, such measures are regarded as being of low to moderate importance.

(5) Organisations adopt a variety of computer fraud detection techniques, with particular emphasis on input and administrative controls.

(6) In the majority of organisations in the survey, there was no clear or specific guidelines for action when a computer fraud was detected. In those organisations where there are guidelines to follow, the most common action is prosecution and dismissal.

(7) In assessing the risk of computer fraud, internal auditors consider that most areas of data processing represent a moderate risk. Perceived as being a much higher risk is the use of real-time processing and mini computers under user control – both areas which are spreading with new technology.

(8) The perceived level of threat arising from different methods of computer fraud perpetration was found to vary. Input fraud is seen as presenting the greatest threat. Other methods, requiring greater knowledge or 'know how' are seen a posing less of a problem. These other methods are therefore seen as being only of a moderate threat.

## Summary

Management must take the threat of computer fraud seriously and instigate a strategy for its prevention and detection. The planned approach would include; the implementation of preventative and detective procedures; and monitoring of the situation to ensure that new exposures are identified and covered.

There is also scope for further research. At present research into admitted computer fraud shows that fundamental controls are not being adhered to and that the level of skill is low (see, for example, the Audit Commission reports). Areas that could now profitably be covered include:

(a) the attitudes and contribution of other significant groups to the prevention and detection of computer fraud – line managers, computer managers, and financial staff;

(b) an examination of the countermeasures adopted by firms, which reportedly do not suffer from computer fraud;

(c) an analysis to identify the various factors present in firms which do experience computer fraud as against those who do not;

(d) the extent to which security procedures built in to existing systems are used;

(e) how cost benefit analysis techniques may be implemented to prevent and detect computer fraud.

At present computer fraud has a low profile. In the future, technological advances and better trained criminals will make it a significant problem unless firms react and adopt countermeasures against this threat.
