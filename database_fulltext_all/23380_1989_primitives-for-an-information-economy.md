# Primitives for an Information Economy\*

John Lindsay, Kingston Polytechnic, School of Information Systems Design

## Introduction

I thought it would be suitable to start off by introducing you to a tabloid journal that you might not all have come across, called Capital Gay. In the last year, something very interesting has happened on pages 18 and 19. These are adverts for telephone dating services. One of them, according to a very small study, has generated $1 \frac{1}{2}$ million telephone calls since it started. Their capital costs consist of putting the advert in Capital Gay. There is a difference between peak-time charges of 38p a minute and off-peak charges of 25p a minute. The only difference, I can imagine, is that the 13p in those differences has gone to British Telecom. Those of you who are nasty and competitive will be pleased to know that not a single one of them uses 'you know who's' telephone lines. The only other thing they have to do is to employ somebody called Arthur, who doesn't appear to be a computer but who, if you use a few words which he can recognize, beeps in and disconnects you because either 'Nanny Telecom', or somebody outside British Telecom in 'Nanny Government', decided the last thing that was to happen in Britain was that people were to use naughty words over the telephone because after all it's illegal to use naughty words through the mail.

Those of you who have an international perspective on telecommunications will know that if you do a classic MBA study of Prestel and Minitel, Minitel has been vastly successful. You might also know that the bulk of Minitel's trade appears to be in the 'ladies and men of unseemly and improper virtue' who use on-line code at 300 baud. Can you imagine trying to type it all in, in order to form salubrious relationships late at night? There must be lessons in here somewhere or other about the nature of information, and the potential market in which British Telecom and its staff have a capacity to generate a successful niche and add to their commodity what I'm told is called 'value'.

## Information primitives

At this point we will end MBA-speak and go into what we consider to be really the fundamentals of information. Because it seems to me that if we are looking at the sort of market that information is moving into in the foreseeable future, I make four quite distinct market niches. I'm sure they're only a historic accident and won't last, but I think they're useful in terms of having some history and perspective on what we are talking about. I divide them up into information, networks, processors and services.

## Information

The first one — information — is really historically the province of people like librarians and publishers, from the days of Asurbaniphal organizing the Lands, Registration and Taxation system of Assyria on tablets of clay — little clay marks, organized in a particular order, capable of being retrieved and so on and so forth. There has been a tradition in the organization of information and the function of an information market, in the past predominantly the function of librarians and publishers probably, with the possible exception of railway timetables and telephone directories.

But there's an idea there of an information of which we have some sort of knowledge. The basic idea of information is that you have messages; that messages contain data, and that data acts as evidence on the basis of which people test hypotheses; on the basis of those hypotheses they take decisions, the consequences of which are actions; and the consequence of those actions can be measured, on the basis of which measurement the information can be valued.

So information evidence, the basis of which is the testing of hypotheses, the consequence of which is the taking of decisions, and the consequence of taking those decisions is action, has a measurable impact upon the world which results in the hypotheses either being validated or devalued.

We have traditionally recognized the market within which that exercise takes place by saying that every message has an author and a reader. The reader, and the consequence of taking those decisions and those actions, in turn becomes an author who initiates a new series of actions which in turn has a new reader. We recognize, if we want to bring that slightly more down-to-earth, that basically every message has a producer, that every message has a consumer, and that the process of getting the message from the producer to the consumer is the process of communication which takes place in a concrete medium.

For the first several thousand years, it was the voice to the ear through the air; for a couple of thousand years it was clay tablets and bits of rolled up grass and the odd skin of the poor sheep or enemy who happened to be lying around the place; for a few hundred years, it has involved printing presses. And for the last 20 or 30 years it's involved really the third substantial revolution in information which I'll come to later on.

At the more abstract level behind this concept of producer and consumer engaging in communication we have an intention: the author has an intention in producing this message and the reader (the consumer) has an intention of consuming the message — not necessarily clearly stated, not necessarily conscious, it might not have been written down on tablets of clay beforehand, but there is some sort of intention there. And this communication takes place within a context. It is the context of the production and consumption of the message which is creating the context of meaning of the message.

In order for this meaning to occur, there has to be a grammar and there has to be a vocabulary; there has to be a set of rules by which communication can occur and there has to be a lexicon; there has to be a set of data elements which are capable of being translated. And these in turn have to function within a market because every activity of production and consumption involves human labour, and because they function in a market there has to be some mechanism of pricing. The consequence of their functioning in a market and having some concept of pricing is that inevitably the production and consumption involve some process of intellectual property. The author and the consumer consider themselves to have some rights over their products and the activities of their consumptions. Under some circumstances these activities occur in privacy, in secrecy, or there is some intellectual property, there is some different pricing mechanism according to the mechanism of being able to make access to this information conceivable or to be able to protect the information from being accessed.

Those are some rather basic primitives that we can say are generic to this concept called information which makes it behave in the market rather differently from most other commodities that we have to concern ourselves with. So, out of those primitives, we are going to produce some ideas about the way in which the information economy might actually be seen to function.

## Networks

Now let us move on to this idea of networks; they really are, in a sense, a little more complex, because as I said before, this convergence of computing and telecommunications really is the interesting thing we have to be bothered with. It has changed the relationship of space and time as far as information is concerned. But if we go back to Asurbaniphal and his tablets of clay, the process of the author producing the document was fixed in that moment of time and the relationship between the author and the reader was contained and limited by that single document which then, because it was a tablet of clay, couldn't actually be changed. If it was a papyrus document it was rather better: you could make a palimpsest out of it. You could wipe out the old message and put a new message on it, but the relationship of space and time of production and consumption was separated by the medium. Go to the days of Gutenberg and the discovery of movable type, and you then separate this relationship of space and time, because now, the author can produce a document which can be read in many different places by many different people and each of them can change that document although they have no mechanism of communicating the change in the document either back to the original author or to all the other readers. In fact, they're unlikely even to know the network of readers who are concerned with consuming the document.

As soon as we get to the convergence of telecommunications and computing we are into the third information revolution, because not only can we almost simultaneously engage in the production and consumption of information, but also it is possible for the author to know the set of all possible readers. It is possible for those readers to communicate with that author, and it is possible for all those readers to become authors on the same document with one another. So we have fundamentally changed the nature of the concept of the communication of information because of bringing together computers and telecommunications.

We have, however, a problem. In these networks, it appears that telephone engineers have their own history, they have their own traditions, they have their own ideas and are optimizing the capacity of networks whereas, for example, broadcast engineers actually have a completely different idea: maximizing the proportion of the market captured by a single message relative to that single transmission is the key dynamic. We have computer scientists who don't actually understand the tradition, either of telephone engineers or of broadcast engineers, but who actually have completely different understandings about the amount of traffic on their processors, the degradation of their processors, access to storage capacity, etc. And then we have editors, we have people who are interested in bringing packages of information on to the market who have come from a tradition of journalism and so on. None of these four groups really has any tradition of understanding what the others are on about.

Underlying this problem of the convergence of telecommunications and computing is that we have sets of networks. The first basis of the networks is that you have one-to-one communication. The second process is that you have one-to-many communication where the 'many' are known to the author. We might call that narrowcasting. Then we have one-to-many communication where the recipients of the message are unknown to the author. We might call that broadcasting. Then we have many-to-one communication but that's only an instance of lots and lots of one-to-one relationships, and we have many-to-many communications but those are only in instances of either one-to-one or one-to-many communications.

All of these four different types of communication in turn actually have different economics built into them. They have different pricing mechanisms that we need to concern ourselves with. But the fundamental point, no matter what form of the network that we are looking at, is that we start off by saying that it has input devices and output devices: these might be telephones, they might be faxes, they might be workstations, they might be broadcasting stations and television sets. The fundamental principle is the same — we have input devices and output devices. Then we have a transmission mechanism, we have a cabling system of some sort or other — either a real cabling system in terms of being proper wire and optical fibre, or a virtual cabling system, taking place as a result of radio transmission of one form or another — and we have some mechanism of switches. So the primitives of any of these networks consist simply of input/output devices, they consist of transmission channels and they consist of switches.

If we look at the concept of the network in this way, we see that we have British Telecom with a tradition of copper wires, telephones and exchanges. We have Cable and Wireless with, I think, much the same tradition. We have British Rail with a network of railway lines, of signals and of railway stations where the initiator of the message then has to make the journey to the railway station, but we are still engaging in the same sort of principle of a network. We have the Post Office, which has the mechanism of addressing the idea of a system of addresses where space is organized in order for a communication to take place. And then there are post boxes, and sorting offices, but the network functions in the same way. We have the BBC, ITV with transmitters, television stations, radio, television sets, wavelengths and so on. And we have something like, for example, the CEGB which has a network of copper wires linked eventually to almost every home in the country. I think it is still true that telephones have entered something like only 80 per cent of the potential domestic market in Britain, whereas electricity has very close to 100 per cent penetration. And I'm sure we can think of lots of others.

So there are a great number of different companies; there are many different organizations and people who are involved in this process of putting in place networks whereby communication can take place between the authors and the readers of a variety of messages. The issues that have come up over the last few years are probably those of privatization, of liberalization of the market, and of deregulation. And I'm sure you are all so steeped in those issues that I don't need to touch on them. So the network, then, means that we really need to broaden our ideas about who is capable of providing the sort of network that might be interesting, if we accept that my argument — about the nature of the commodity called information — is as it was when I outlined it at the beginning.

## Processors

The matter of processors is a relatively more simple issue to deal with because I think we understand that more clearly. It's basically boxes, wires and chips. It goes more traditionally with manufacturing as we understand it. We understand something about vertical markets. We understand something about market niches. We know whether we are trying to produce the chips and mine the copper. We know whether we are interested in taking one ton of copper in at one end of the factory, breaking it down into thousands upon thousands of tiny things called chips and then building it up again in order to produce boxes at the other end that somehow or other we can sell. I think we know something about the functioning of that market; I think we know something about investment; we know something about production; we know something about amortization rates; we know something about depreciation and devaluation. We probably understand this process a little more clearly. I'm not quite so clear why it is that when everybody seems to be involved in the GEC deal, I've not actually seen any reference to British Telecom yet, but I'm sure there is a very good reason. The fact that from today AT&T is involved in the deal, I'd have thought, might just have changed the rules that you thought were in play last night.

## Services

The last issue is that of services, and that seems to involve, in the first instance, the area that we might understand most clearly: the production of software as a mechanism whereby the processors are actually turned into processors where they become capable of doing things. A company that comes to mind immediately is, for example, Logica.

We then move on to the area of the consultancies, and the ones that immediately come to mind here are the Arthurs. Why so many are called Arthur, I don't know. But clearly there is a major industry there and historically the bulk of them — Arthur Little, Arthur Young, Arthur Anderson etc. — were predominantly accountancy and auditing companies. They have severe problems because their accountancy and auditing wings are not producing a profit, not producing a rate of return in comparison to their management consultancy wings. Now that's a contradiction, that's a problem for them but it does mean that they're having to move into expanding markets, and so we find that fairly major parts of software development are actually being undertaken by these consultancy companies.

I would add to that, of course, the question of education and libraries, because if we are going to be talking about an information economy, it seems to me that the issue of education is going to be a fairly major one because these primitives that I'm talking about are going to require the capacity of children to understand the world in a way rather different from how they have in the past.

One of the research projects I did in the early 1970s was to study what children in a secondary school in East London actually needed to know to become urban literate and how that related to what they actually got from their education system. In fact I was fairly horrified to discover that 40 per cent of the

16-year-olds had never actually been on the underground and that of the 14-year-olds, if you drew a line between Dalston Junction and the school, 90 per cent of them had not been more than half a mile outside that line. We then went on and discovered that in fact substantial numbers of them couldn't use a telephone directory and that a large proportion of them didn't know how to use an A-Z. (For those of you who are not London-based this must sound very provincial and detailed, but in London things like A-Zs are actually extremely important.) If you can't use an A-Z, my argument would be that you are functionally illiterate. Even if you drive a company Volvo, in fact you are functionally illiterate. So my argument here is that education is another area of this service and, of course, I've also got to mention here the process of designing information systems because that seems to me to be the process of what's linking these things together.

So, what I'm suggesting is that this combination of information networks, processors and systems is producing for us a new sort of information economy in which a company like British Telecom is really going to have to attempt to produce some sort of position about what it's going to try to do in that market.

## Intelligent cities

I've read the Butler Cox Report on European Telecommunications Markets until 1992. I've read several of your strategy documents and I must admit that I'm not any more clear now than I was before I read them about precisely what sort of area it is we will be going for. In order to try to elaborate this, I want to draw a little on some work that I was involved in, in the city of Kawasaki in Japan, which used to make motor-bikes, but because of the 'nasty little yellow chappies' (who, as far as they are concerned, are the Koreans), they don't make ships any more, they don't make motorcars any more, they don't make motor-bikes any more, they don't make steel any more, they don't make aluminium any more. All that fantastic industrial development that took place after the 1950s has been wiped out. The city government of Kawasaki opened up (and this was a fascinating experiment) a competition for information strategies for the city of Kawasaki. I was at the conference at which the result was announced, and it's not an accident that the competition was won by an architect, because I don't know that we have any tradition of opening up information strategies to competition in the way in which architects do it. The conclusion might be that either you don't end up with the Lloyds building or you do end up with the Lloyds building, but I think the process of competition actually creates interesting ideas. And the idea that came out of this

Kawasaki city study, was the idea of 'the intelligent city'.

The intelligent city seems to me to be a manageable idea because people understand urban spaces in some sense as not only being spatially delimited but also as being complex in a way in which urban dwellers don't understand rural communities to be complex. I think it's a partitioning of the problem purely for the point of tonight's discussion. One could, with no difficulty, talk about an 'intelligent universe', but let's limit ourselves for the moment to an idea of an intelligent city. The metaphor which I use to try and get this across, is that the motorway is fundamentally concerned with the movement of people and things, and a possible alternative to the movement of people and things is the movement of information. Or you could be moving information about the movement of people and things.

In the generic form, however, we can introduce another set of primitives: really the measurements that we have to deal with are, first, the relationships of space and time. Second, every activity of communication has an origin and a destination; every event in this communication has decomposition — it came from being something which it is not any more, rather like that ton of copper that enters the processor factory and that ceases to be a ton of copper in the process of becoming a few million chips, and it's in a process of recomposition when it comes out at the other end as something else. Those of you skilled in some of the traditions of information systems design will recognize that that's not dissimilar to the idea of entity life histories.

## Motorway metaphors and information networks

If what's going on in the motorway metaphor really is at the primitive level, we can say that the bits of metaphor that we have to deal with are, at the most elementary level, things like roads: we have things like vehicles, we have things like signals. Other things we have are instances of journeys which have destinations, we have intentions, we have intelligence, but those actually really involve people who we normally call drivers.

This metaphor is that the motorway has to be built. That involves construction and materials. We have another part of this metaphor which involves the motorway manufacturing industry. Another part of the metaphor involved petroleum and service stations, the petroleum companies. Another part of it involves the police, the Automobile Association, ambulance services. Another part of it involves the insurance industry. Another part of it involves driving lessons, licences and the MOT. Another part of it, for those of you who know the M25, involves traffic jams, which involves the idea of transportation planning which involves the idea of regulation and the State. So here we have a little bit of a metaphor for a motorway system with its cementing net, but with all its major components that involve market opportunities for each of the different players involved in some process or other in production and consumption.

## Receivers and transmitters

We then have to go on and ask what the motorway metaphor looks like when we move it into the field of information and networks. And I think we have to say first of all that there are receivers and transmitters. We have the idea of receivers and transmitters, telephones, television, faxes, workstations, and that all these people here (you'll remember from my introduction on the question of information) are all conscious active agents, all authors and readers, all producers of information and consumers of information. Their activities all have consciousness and determinancy about them, and they can be at home, they can be at work, they can be on the move — they're at it any time and in many places. And we are concerned with these receivers and transmitters with their manufacturing and sale. Predominantly (at the moment) we are talking about telephones, televisions, faxes, workstations and so on. What we will be talking about in ten years time is less easy to see. Now that seems to me to be as clear a market as we can talk about — the process of motorway construction, of motorway manufacturing.

## Channels

We are concerned also with the question of channels. The first layer of these channels seems to be the traditional hard wiring, copper wires and probably optical fibres. This is a mechanism of communication which seems to me to be fairly clear. Second, there is probably any place where there's line of sight, anywhere that you can have microwave transmission. Third, there's anywhere that you could position a satellite. So here are three quite clear manufacturing opportunities to go back to the receiver and transmission. The channels are, first of all, manufacturing opportunities: the optical fibres have to be manufactured, the transmitters and receivers have to be manufactured, the satellites have to be launched, and so on.

## Switches

The third question is that of switches. The switches are what is going to link together these different categories of channel, and the switches have to basically understand, first, where something is going and second, the best route and chart. So these are the primitives: that every act, every author, every reader, every act of communication, is basically a packet that has to start off with an idea of where it's going and has to have a mechanism of understanding the best route by which it's going to get there, for which there is some charge. Where these things are going to be really is a matter of sublime irrelevance. You can park your satellite in any available parking space, either in a geostationary orbit or a sun synchronous sort. Your line of sight can be from any building to any building provided you can get a line of sight, or from every hill to any hill.

If you look at channels in this way, the idea of copper wires and optical fibres as being the real barrier to entry that British Telecom has been able to put in place historically becomes a bit weak, because it might well be that all somebody has to do is be able to lease (rather as happened at Kingston College of Further Education) a parking space on the tallest building in the area and position a mechanism there within line of sight of anywhere in the region. (Certainly I read yesterday that video transmission over 15 kilometres on line of sight is now absolutely feasible and in practice.)

Given that everything I read tells me that shunting video transmission down copper wires is a major impediment to the movement of video data it might be that all sorts of people could come into this competition. I mentioned earlier on that the ones that occurred to me were organizations like British Rail and the Post Office. I don't see why it couldn't be any property developing company. We already know that the major banks are making more money out of the leasing and management of the spaces above their branches than they make from managing the accounts inside their branches. So the capacity to do all sorts of things with odd bits of real estate, if you can put the right consortium together, is considerable.

## Protocols

We have to remember that what we have here, within this concept of the information intelligent city, is this idea of receivers and transmitters, of channels, of switches. The fourth component is the area that I'm calling protocols, and the protocols that we grasp and understand already, are the idea, for example, of addresses. We already have the idea that the Post Office actually couldn't function if we didn't have in place a regulated and systematic notation whereby people's physical and spatially located activity can be accounted in a way which can be understood by people with virtually no training at all. In fact, the innovation of the Post Office over the last 15 years or so (the idea of the post code) actually produces for us a digitized map data set whereby any particular event can be spatially located down to the level of hardly more than a few square metres. The post code at its lowest level of disaggregation, at the moment, probably involves something like seven to ten mailing addresses. We also know that the entire post system involving 23 million addresses is available on a CD ROM. Because the capacity to put this address system in place and then organize, either at the level of the post code as a digitized map data set boundary defined standardized polygon, or, moving on from there to some more informal form of addressing (like, for example, street names and street numbers and so on) the bulk of the protocols for address forms are in place already. There is a little blurring at the edges in terms of how you actually structure and define the orders of the digitized map data sets and there are certainly some rather clever little games about how you handle the boundary spaces involving things such as quad tree analysis, but that's really simply in order to reduce the drain on the processing capacity, of being able to handle very large chunks of data.

However, not only do you have that capacity in place, but you also have the capacity from the minimum data set of your lowest possible spatial boundary to be able to aggregate upwards in order that at one level you end up with Mundocart, where you have an entire map of the world which either can operate at a global level or, with the click of the jolly old proverbial mouse, can actually bounce down to any disaggregated level you like. Now the Mundocart, if I remember rightly, has only digitized 2 million or 3 million spaces, so it's only really the major United States cities that are handled in this sort of way. If you click over into India, it's a sort of big blob with Hyderabad and Ahmedabad and Delhi and not very much else. But it's clear that the capacity is there. We already know that the Australian census is going to be published in 1991 on CD ROM(?). We know that the United States with their Tiger program are going to integrate the capacity to handle the whole of the US census linked into an integrated digitized map data set which is going to be capable of generating data disaggregated to the level of either an enumeration district or a post code. And if we are talking about those sorts of data set which are predominantly concerned with polygons, it's not very difficult to imagine (I know there is one major British Telecom research project in this area) and to then say that nodes and channels within these networks are, in turn, simply where your telephone cables are lying, or where the water board has its pipes, or where the gas board has its pipes, and so on.

So it seems to me that we need protocols that deal with the addressing facilities. We need protocols that are involved in the mapping facilities in order that the spatial and temporal relationship of information systems that I've been talking about can be put in place, and we need protocols about how the information is actually going to be communicated through this information city. It ought to be a matter of absolutely sublime indifference to the author or the reader how the data gets from point A to point B. That should be a matter which is outside their concern. What they require, is to know where it's to go, to know when it's going to arrive there and to dispatch it. And it seems to me that there's a whole layer of activities that should be put in place in order to be able to satisfy these two requirements.

I consider that another of these protocols (as I've said already) ought to be in education and training, because it seems to me that if we are capable of thinking about this motorway network, we are capable of seeing the idea of an intelligent city that has these sorts of things to it — vehicles, signalling systems, a motorway construction, a police force, a driver/vehicle licensing centre etc. Then we are going to need similar education and training if an entire population is going to be able to understand and take advantage of this market in the intelligent city and the price of moving any goods or person from point A to point B.

In other words the pricing in the distribution and exchange part of the model becomes so cheap, that we fundamentally change the relationship between the cost of production and the cost of consumption for any particular commodity that we might be involved in. This has the potential, in turn, to considerably change our ideas about the process of marketing. In fact, our ideas at the moment of high street retailers, of home banking, of smart cards and so on have a capacity to be quite fundamentally changed if we can grasp the way that this information city is going to work.

How are parts of this going to develop? Quite clearly, not by any individual with a Masterplan. We are not going to have some Department of Transport person coming along and saying that what is needed is the M25 but because nobody wants it, it will only be built to a third of the required capacity; and because they, in turn, completely get wrong the fact that if you intervene in a transportation system then actually you don't simply redistribute the balance of distribution of transport, you actually change the pricing of transport. Therefore, the consequence is that by improving your transport system, you make it cheaper to move a person or a thing from point A to point B and you therefore change the entire absorption and consumption of that network as a whole; if you change the functioning of an information network, it is far from clear that those sorts of consequences will result. In fact it would appear that, provided we are absolutely certain what the technical issues are — in terms of the capacity of the switches and the capacities of the channels — then we could actually change the costs of transportation of goods, services and people in ways that we really haven't grasped so far.

## How will it develop?

So, how are parts of this going to develop? Workstations: we have a good deal of those already. People are now using their Macintosh environment in a way which is really quite different, although others are still using MSDOS on those dreadful little machines manufactured by a company which is unmentionable, in order to really totally cripple their lives. I don't know whether any of you saw the programme recently on television, 'The Goddess and the Computer', about the way in which water management systems were used in Indonesia with an environment that people could grapple with immediately. The information might be wrong, the politics might be wrong, but people were able to grasp the idea of modelling extremely quickly and were able to test their received models of the world, on the basis of their thousand years of activity, against the models that had been produced to analyse the developments of complexity by increase in the population flow.

So it seems to me that, in the foreseeable future, that sort of environment is going to be one where a very large number of people can actually engage in manipulating information; thereafter, we'll get into talking to the little machines, but that's probably not going to be for some years yet. So, for the foreseeable future, my guess is that something like the Macintosh environment, at the price at which Alan Sugar can get the things into the shops, will give us the mechanism where workstations really become about as disposable as most other hi-fi equipment.

Possibly another part of this will come through ISDN. I think it's perfectly conceivable to build with core and spine wiring through involving optical fibre, some sort of network, large parts of which are in place already. How it's going to develop is less clear, but it seems to me that there might in turn be quite a large number of cores and spines that have some sort of interfaces to one another. And then we have the mechanism of being able to communicate amongst them.

A third part which might be interesting is the area involved around Centrex. It might be that a capacity is there by really putting intelligence into switches so that it doesn't actually matter whether your network nodes are on the roofs of buildings and the buildings are wired. There's already quite a substantial literature in intelligent buildings and the capacity is, then, for a Centrex-type intelligent exchange sited on a building or in a building or going from buildings to another building which is called an exchange and which is dedicated to that purpose. It might be that these buildings begin to work like token rings; that they simply have enough intelligence built into them; that they receive a bundle of messages and simply forward them by the optimal route to their destination. But using the idea of being able to put intelligence into switches in order that switches can be gathered into exchanges which can be spatially located almost anywhere, but as a consequence of the intelligence contained in the switches a message can be communicated from any point to any point — possibly independently of hard wiring or alternatively by a variation in pricing according to investment capital, development capital, or rate of return on capital employed, you can go through a process whereby you can optimize the utilization of your networks in the most convenient way.

Another part that clearly is going to be required is the idea of regulation. I'm always amused by a famous quote by a British Telecom Director, who said, 'what we require is competition and co-operation'. I heard another version of this at the Financial Times Conference on electronic financial services, where one of the bankers stood up and said, 'what we want is competition in the high street and we want co-operation in the back rooms'. What the banker didn't understand is that when he talks about co-operation in the back rooms, what he means is people fighting with one another over who's going to end up setting standards, or control significant chunks of the market.

There is another side to the incomprehensibility of the two of them — a classic example of the unspeakable in pursuit of the inedible: the telecommunication and computing people were saying that what they require is the ability to compete for markets over standards, but what they require is a stable money supply. What they didn't understand, of course, is that what the bankers regard as competition is being able to fight over money supply and over the values of currencies and the values of prices. So here we had an example where two really mutually incomprehensive groups of people were talking to one another about how these networks are not going to come into place.

Part of what we can see also, though, is the development of value added data services. They started in a large part with libraries and librarianship, Dialogue, ESA, IRS, Pergamon etc., concerned with access to very large bibliographic data services. It wasn't long after that companies began to trade in financial information and out of things like Reuters and then Seac and Topic and so on. You have to trade in financial services, which took rather a battering in October 1987 and still haven't recovered; what this has, I think, indicated is however, there are substantial other niches where trading in this information creates a potential — I'm sure you already know about things like Trader Net, and you're probably involved in Swift and so on. I've certainly seen one of your documents which identified half a dozen market niches where you thought that value added data services was where you ought to be in place. So bits of this information city are in place already, rather like (if you remember the film) 'The Life of Brian': what did the Romans ever give us? Gave us the aqueduct. But apart from the aqueduct what did the Romans ever give us? Well, they gave us housing. Yes, well apart from housing what have the Romans ever given us? They gave us security and banks and so on and so forth. So we've got bits of it in place, but it's clear that we don't yet have anything that people can take hold of with the metaphor, with the clarity of the idea as when we talk about a motorway system. And if somebody or a company is looking at where they are being located, they can't grasp their market opportunities with a clarity that can enable them to say, there's the Automobile Association, or there's the driver/vehicle licensing centre or there's Austin's (actually Austin Rover is a very bad example, despite the fact that Graham Day has just got a knighthood out of it), because who could say what market Austin Rover is into. So, in turn, there are clearly major barriers even having identified that we have bits of it in place already.

## Barriers

The first barrier is really something which is structural to the nature of capital itself. It's a bit like the Spirit of Free Enterprise sailing out of Zeebrugge with its bow doors wide open. There's an enormous amount of water, an enormous amount of capital, slushing around inside this tanker, but companies are not prepared to invest substantially because the rates of return on capital employed are not high enough.

There is also the issue of pricing and charging, much of which I've covered already.

Then there is what I call the ‘proof problem’ in information systems design, which relates the organic composition of capital to pricing and charging. Although we have an enormous amount of detail in things like structured systems, data analysis, databased sizing etc., we are much weaker in having any idea about how to relate an information strategy to the actual design of that strategy — the design of the architecture that’s going to implement that strategy. And we are much weaker in being able to evaluate investment in information systems design and investment in information technology in order to justify that investment against other possible types of investment, or investment in other areas of markets.

There is also the issue of ‘Chinese walls’ which seems to be a necessary concomitant of this idea of competition. In several of the functioning city finance houses, they’re not Chinese walls just in terms of people putting up boundaries that can’t be crossed, but they’ve actually built physical walls in order to stop two departments talking to each other. I think that, as the system becomes more complex, we are going to have increasing impediments to the free movement of information in networks, not simply because of the matter of pricing and charging but because of the question of privacy and secrecy that I have already mentioned. Because information functions as a commodity in a way different from other commodities, there are a whole series of issues built in there that are going to increase problems.

Lastly, I think another barrier is going to be the absence of generic protocols, whereby we actually don't have the addressing system and we don't have the mapping system for this new information city. And so it's going to be difficult for information to successfully communicate from one place to another.

## What is to be done?

From a definition of what I understand the nature of information to be, leading on to the nature of what I consider networks to be (the role of processors and the role of services), I've developed an idea of a metaphor in the motorway from which we can grasp the idea of the information city. I have indicated what I considered to be the opportunities, those partially in place already, and went on to indicate what the barriers are. It would be unfair if I didn't end up by commenting very briefly on some of the things that I think a group like yours could be doing. I would put the bulk of my resources into cracking this question of intelligent switches. It's this mechanism of building up the intelligence whether they're in individual switchboards, in exchanges, in parallel processors or in neural networks; whether they involve expert systems, databases, or hypertext; none of this is clear yet — they might involve large data stores on CD ROM, interactive optical discs and so on.

As yet, I am far from clear about the architecture of these intelligent switches, but if I were to allocate my resources I would say that the people who are going to control the major chunk of the market that this information city, this intelligent city, can handle are the people who handle the switches. The idea that came out in Kawasaki was that they are going to install intelligent workstations as commonly as they install letter boxes and street lights (and a marvellous thing that's apparent, which you don't get in Britain, are little coin dispensers that, for a few yen, throw out at you litres of beer and litres of whisky — and I've never seen one of them vandalised). The intelligent workstation is going to be as common as the intelligent whisky dispenser. The key is going to be the switches that optimize the routing of any of those messages from their authors to their readers.

Those intelligent switches are going to then require the second area that I think needs working on, which is basically the building up of protocol libraries. Going back to another old metaphor that people grasp, the idea of encyclopaedias and dictionaries, it was an amazing shift in the 16th century to suddenly have this idea of putting information into alpha-numeric order because previously all information had been organized systematically. The idea of alphabetical order was an enormous step forward, but it then meant that you could balance alpha-numeric order and thematic order to be able to put intelligence into an information system — that's how a dictionary and an encyclopaedia emerged differently from things before. Dictionaries and encyclopaedias mean we are going to have things like lexicons and bestiaries. Basically, we need to have maps of how these protocols are going to fit together. If we have the lexicons, the bestiaries, the maps, then we must have the grammars, the rules, the protocols and the methods, whereby these different structures fit together. Once we have the grammars, we then need to have the catalogues and the classification schemes in order to be able to make sense of this information, of this intelligent city. And then we need to have the directories whereby we can navigate our way around this intelligence. It seems to me that it's the production of these protocols that are going to be a pre-condition to the creation of the capacity of people to navigate their way through these intelligent cities.

Finally, I'd like you to join me in helping to fight the Nannies.

## Biographical notes

John Lindsay is currently Principal Lecturer in the School of Information Systems at Kingston Polytechnic working on information strategies, and part-time lecturer at University College London in the Development Planning Unit. His particular interest is in the role of information systems as a contribution to the management and development of big cities in Third World countries. Recent work has involved visits to India, Japan and Chile.
