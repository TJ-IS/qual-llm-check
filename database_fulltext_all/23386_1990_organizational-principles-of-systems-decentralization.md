# Organizational principles of systems decentralization

C. JAMES BACON

University of Canterbury, Christchurch, New Zealand

Abstract: A study aimed at a determination of the organizational principles involved in systems decentralization (SD). It is based on a survey of 14 American and British companies selected for their significant investments in and mature experience of decentralized systems. There were eight principles underlying the policy and practice of SD in these companies, and the inference is that these principles appear to work. That is, considering their commonality of use and the depth of experience in the companies concerned, these principles appear to be optimizing the potential gains achievable through SD, while minimizing the potential losses. The implication is that, if these organizational principles can be effectively implemented in other organizations, they may facilitate success in systems decentralization, as well as the evolution toward distributed systems. Viewed as a whole, the principles appear to complement each other, such that they may be viewed as a conceptual model, wherein each depends upon at least one other for its effectiveness.

## Introduction

Systems decentralization (SD) represents a fundamental change in an organization's systems environment. It also represents both an opportunity and a challenge for many companies in today's competitive business arena. It has been the subject of numerous articles, some focusing on departmental computing (Serlin, 1985; Hobuss, 1989), and some on user development of computer applications (Rivard and Huff, 1988). Now the stage has been reached where some companies are coming to a level of mature experience in the implementation and management of decentralized systems.

The subject of SD is defined as follows:

Systems decentralization is the policy and practice of enabling the individual business units in an organization to have delegated responsibility for and control over the initiation and development of respective systems projects, and all or part of their own data processing facilities. It represents a devolution of the traditionally centralized information systems and technology (IST) function.

In broad terms the subject of SD might be divided into two areas:

(1) hardware and software, and

(2) organization and management.

With respect to organization and management, it would seem that there ought to be key principles that might be brought together in achieving the basic aim of harnessing the potential benefits of SD while minimizing the potential losses thereof. The potential benefits involve an optimization of the discretionary powers of business unit management in leveraging IST resources for enhanced revenue. The potential losses involve an erosion of corporate/central coordination and control with consequential increases in cost (Boynton and Zmud, 1987).

In an effort to uncover and develop the organizational principles concerned, the senior information executives and certain other colleagues in some 35 major companies in the US and the UK were interviewed. An unstructured interview format was used so as to optimize the pursuit of any potentially fruitful line of enquiry. Of the 35, eight American and six British companies were found to have large investments in decentralized systems and judged to have a significant level of experience and maturity therein. For example, one of the companies has more than 20,000 microcomputers/PCs installed, while another has had decentralized systems in operation for some 20 years. Therefore, these 14 companies were selected for closer observation and scrutiny. The expectation was that the experience and maturity of these companies in decentralizing the IST function might be drawn upon in developing relevant organizational principles.

The assumption in observing the selected companies more closely was that, where certain organizational principles appeared to be commonly employed, after significant experience of SD by the 14 companies surveyed and selected, then these principles might be considered for general use in optimizing the process of systems decentralization. The implicit measure of success is that the principles are being used by all or most of these companies, and this would seem to indicate a level of agreement on that which has been found to work in decentralizing the IST function. This might be considered an implicit ‘macro’ view of success, compared with a direct ‘micro’ view of success obtained in user satisfaction surveys (Rivard and Huff, 1988).

The business sectors represented in the selected companies was as follows: electrical goods, banking, stockbroking, chemicals, industrial and scientific equipment, clothing distribution, shipping and transport, pharmaceuticals, leisure and entertainment, packaging, and food processing.

Of the companies not selected, some have strongly centralized organization structures, and embrace a policy of centralized processing and systems development. These are mostly retailing organizations, with standardized systems and management operations in each store. The implicit alignment, in these organisations, of centralized IST with a centralized organization structure, is consistent with a number of studies demonstrating this alignment relationship (Ahituv, Neumann, and Zviran, 1989). Comparison with such companies was useful in observing the decentralized systems environment in the selected companies.

## From decentralized to distributed systems

Ultimately, SD may lead to distributed systems in an organization, wherein a full computer network provides for connected computers in various parts of the organization to process given application systems concurrently, and interchange data as required. This may represent a natural growth (Huff, Munro and Martin, 1988), and such growth might be graphically represented as shown in Fig. 1.

However, to achieve a distributed systems environment it appears that an organization may need to evolve through the preceding stages of SD because of the learning, organizational, and attitudinal changes required (Huff, Munro and Marin, 1988). Success

in SD may pave the way for success in distributed systems.

## The push towards systems decentralization

The push towards SD has accelerated over the last few years due to the basic factors of enabling technology and increasing competition and change. But there is a third basic factor that has always been there since the first mainframe business computer was installed. This is the inherent communication gap between a centralized information resource and its disparate users.

This communication gap may be cancelled out as a potential problem where:

(1) users participate effectively in and are committed to systems development,

(2) user managers rather than IST managers plan and decide the systems development priorities, (3) IST specialists have good communication and behavioural skills, and

(4) IST specialists are oriented to the business of the user (Barki, 1989; Synnot and Gruber, 1982).

However, where these requirements have not been met the inherent communication gap has lead to:

\- a perceived lack of response from the centralized information systems and technology (IST) function, and a consequent demand for user control over and independence in the use of IST

\- a generally sub-optimal sensitivity to and understanding of user business needs by the centralized IST function, and a consequent demand for IST specialists to be directly responsible to user management

\- a dedication and loyalty to central computing's technical needs on the part of IST specialists, and a consequent demand to move this dedication and loyalty to the respective business units.

A second basic factor in the push towards SD is the enabling technology and decreasing cost thereof. Powerful microprocessors and desktop microcomputers (alias personal computers), user-oriented fourth generation languages (some migrated down from mainframe environments), graphics-oriented software, powerful systems software, improved communication facilities, and a wide array of 'off the shelf' application systems are all part of this enabling technology that continues to make systems decentralisation more and more technically feasible.

The third basic factor in the push towards SD, that of increasing competition and change, has lead to the following general effects:

\- the need for a ‘more market’ orientation, and the consequent requirement to place the discretionary use of IST resources closer to the market, in the respective business units

\- the need for greater flexibility and quicker development in supporting new and enhanced business products, and the consequent requirement to place information systems and technology resources at the locus of decision-making relative to these products

\- an accelerating demand for systems application development versus a limited supply of human resources to meet that demand, and a consequent burgeoning backlog and source of user dissatisfaction

\- a move toward and ‘re-discovery’ of decentralized management responsibility and authority, and the creation of profit-responsible business units in the corporate organization, pushing decision-making down the organization, and the consequent requirement to align the IST function with this decentralized management responsibility and authority.

These are the basic factors and the effects thereof in the push towards SD, but the potential gains in terms of greater flexibility, end-user control, and greater proximity of IST to the marketplace, etc., may be offset by potential losses arising from SD.

## Potential losses in systems decentralization

One of the organizations interviewed in the survey had developed a model showing that SD could not be justified on the basis of comparative total cost with centralized operations. Everything was shown to be of greater aggregate cost in the model, including hardware, software, people, administration, and communications.

Similarly, another organization saw increased costs in moving to decentralized systems, via an overall loss in economies of scale. In a sense, this tends to complement Grosch's Law of decreasing costs with increasing size of computing facilities. The apparent increase in overall costs may also need to be a factor in research on the average cost per MIPS (Mendelson, 1987).

Another company is tending towards some recentralization for economic reasons, after being a pioneer in SD, finding that centralized systems operations and development provides clear savings. This same company also aims for improved security and control, and enhanced cross-over selling, through consolidated operations and an integrated corporate-wide data base.

Each of the companies cited continue to support and facilitate SD and end user computing, but this is with the realization that SD is an investment that should produce a return in enhanced leverage of IST resources; and then only through the use of certain organizational principles.

It has been projected that microcomputers will ultimately replace the central mainframe (Eardley-Wilmot, 1990). However, based on the experience of the companies concerned, it seems more likely that they will grow to be used in a complementary role.

Apart from the apparently increased costs in SD, there is a common concern in the potential loss of control and coordination. This can result in duplicated development, redundant purchases, a loss of synergy, insecure operations, poor development discipline, incompatibilities in hardware, software, and information architecture, an erosion of data integrity, and a loss of the 'big picture' strategic view. That many companies have fallen victim to at least some of these losses is evident from occasional reports in the financial and systems media (Moad and Carlyle, 1988).

The basic goal in systems decentralization is therefore to optimize the potential gains described while minimizing the potential losses. This is where the experience of the 14 selected companies, each of which have very significant investments in SD, might be drawn upon in developing organizational principles relevant to SD.

## Summary of the principles

The organizational principles that appear to be common to the policies and practice of SD in the companies surveyed and selected are as follows:

(1) SD is supported and given impetus at the corporate level as a positive direction for the organization

(2) responsibility and authority for information systems and technology (IST) functions and activities is specifically delegated and assigned to the management of the respective business units of the organization

(3) SD is coordinated and controlled in a formalized manner

(4) there is a top-level coordinating committee structure, representing both corporate and business unit management, which provides for the development and integration of overall IST strategy and policy throughout the organization

(5) there are certain IST functions and activities that remain centralized at the corporate level: vendor relations, corporate consulting, education, communications network management, data administration, IST strategy and policy support, disaster recovery, financial applications systems, and payroll/personnel systems

(6) compliance with agreed systems standards is strictly audited throughout the organization

(7) the SD decision is made on a business unit by business unit, function by function, and system by system basis

(8) the corporate/central IST function influences the respective business unit systems environments by persuasion and example rather than coercion.

These organizational principles are common to the 14 companies that were selected for their level of investment and experience in SD. The expectation is, therefore, that the pursuit of these principles in general may facilitate and optimize SD, and the growth towards distributed systems.

## The principles in detail

## Top-down impetus and support

Perhaps the most fundamental principle in decentralizing the information systems and technology (IST) function is that:

Systems decentralization is supported and given impetus at the corporate level as a positive direction for the organization.

This is not to say that SD necessarily originates at the corporate level; in many cases it does not. But it is corporate-level support of SD, which may occur after end-users are already initiating and developing their own systems, that is fundamental to its optimal implementation.

The typical comment from the companies surveyed and selected was that SD is being pursued as corporate policy in order to complement and align with the organizational policy of decentralized decision-making, and the delegation of responsibility and authority to respective business units. As one corporate manager put it: 'Decisions are being pushed down to the janitor'. Implicitly therefore, there is alignment of a decentralized IST function with a decentralized organization structure, which supports a number of studies showing this relationship (Ahituv, Neumann and Zviran, 1989). In this connection, Peter Drucker's prediction made in 1955 is noteworthy, wherein he wrote: 'The new [information systems] technology will demand the utmost in decentralization, in flexibility, and in management autonomy' (Drucker, 1955).

In turn, the re-discovery of decentralization and 'flat' organizations a la Drucker represents an alignment with today's environment of accelerating competition and change which calls for a corporate culture of enterprise, initiative, and flexibility. Managers therefore need a large measure of control over the information tools necessary to do their jobs in this environment. This, at least, is the view of the companies surveyed.

With this view, and notwithstanding any previous 'laissez-faire' attitude towards SD, the companies concerned seem to have evolved to a 'controlled growth' strategy, wherein SD is being rapidly developed but in a carefully controlled environment, (Huff, Munro and Martin, 1988). This strategy means that there is an investment in the resources needed to support SD, which includes full internal consulting support, education and training, user-oriented software, job-oriented documentation, and the promotion of user groups.

The principle of top-down impetus and support in SD may not be surprising to information systems veterans, who have long argued that top-management commitment and support is essential to the success of any systems policy, and IST in general.

The principle also supports the notion of 'DP-push' in SD, as being more related to user satisfaction and therefore SD success (by this criterion), than 'user-pull' (Rivard and Huff, 1988). However, 'DP-push' is not necessarily something that is initiated by the IST function itself. Nor does it seem to be essential for central IST to believe in SD as an initial requirement for its success. Rather, IST takes its cue from top-level corporate management in promoting and 'pushing' systems decentralization.

## Delegated responsibility for IST

The second organizational principle common to the companies surveyed is that:

Responsibility and authority for information systems and technology (IST) functions and activities is specifically delegated and assigned to the management of the respective business units of the organization.

This principle is in conformity with the management dictum of equating responsibility with authority. That is, where a manager or department has authority or control over a resource there must be the assignment of equivalent responsibility, and vice versa.

In some organizations systems decentralization is a fait accompli, but the requisite assignment and acceptance of specified/formalized authority and responsibility may be missing. In the organizations surveyed, this was found to be an imperative ingredient. Senior corporate and information systems management in the organizations surveyed stressed the autonomy of business units with respect to the initiation and development of respective application systems. However, the implicit authority is invariably coupled with a responsibility for implementing and complying with agreed standards.

## Formalized planning and control

## The third principle is that:

Systems decentralization is coordinated and controlled in a formalized manner.

By ‘formalized’ it is meant that planning and reporting tends to supported by and complementary to specific written policies and plans, designated reporting structures, and agreed guidelines.

In the companies surveyed, there is a general requirement for the respective business units to present an annual plan, showing that their IST resources are aligned with agreed corporate aims and objectives. Complementary to this annual planning process, there is an annual corporate-wide inventory of systems applications and equipment. Budgetary planning and control is also required, in conjunction with the annual planning process, and although the form and degree varies, this too is a formalized process.

The implicit aim of SD is to optimize the discretionary powers of business unit management in leveraging IST resources and at the same time optimize corporate/central coordination and control (Boynton and Zmud, 1987). To accomplish this, it would seem that formalized coordination and control is essential. At least, this seems to be the experience of the companies concerned. Although informal communication and reporting is also very important in SD, formalized responsibilities, guidelines and reporting provide the assurance that corporate/overall goals and requirements are being met. The principle is that, if there is to be effective delegation of a responsibility or function, then there needs to be strong reporting for the purpose of feed-back within the management process.

Despite their formalized nature, these structures provide for optimal discretion in the use of IST in the respective business units. The premise is that guidelines gives freedom. Where there are none, a manager may not be clear as to what to do or what not to do. And the tendency may be not to do. This may be due to fear of ‘over-stepping the mark’, or having to work in a ‘twilight of continuous uncertainty’ (Brown and Jaques, 1969). Where such guidelines are in operation, the manager knows the requirements and the limits, and is free to exercise the fullest level of energy and discretion within them.

In SD the challenge is to control without stifling (Moad, 1989). As stressed by several of the organizations surveyed and selected, the authoritarian approach is to be avoided. The consensus is that the consensus approach works best, and agreed rather than dictated directives are to be preferred. But real consensus does not just 'happen'; it has to be planned and facilitated, and that is what these organizations seem to be doing. Thus, the conclusion is that not only does the formalized nature of planning and control optimize the fulfilment of corporate requirements, it also optimizes the level of discretion and consensus, and with it the business unit's leverage of IST.

## Top level coordination committees

It is apparent that most companies with significant IST investment have senior management steering committees to guide and monitor the direction of their information systems. Such committees complement and tend to be associated with formalized planning and control, and successful systems environments in general (Doll and Torkzadeh, 1987).

In the companies surveyed, a coordinating committee structure plays a pivotal role in the formalized planning and control required in SD, thus the principle:

There is a top-level coordinating committee structure, representing both corporate and business unit management, which provides for the development and integration of overall IST strategy and policy throughout the organization.

If steering committees have been found to facilitate planning and control in organizations in general, they would seem to be imperative in successfully coordinating SD (Withington, 1980; Van Rensselaer, 1985). These committees also seem to be more involved, with more to do, than is the case where SD is not operative.

In the organizations selected for their investment and experience in SD there is usually, in addition to a top-level strategy and policy committee, a top-level sub-committee responsible for standards development and compliance. There may also be an operations sub-committee, responsible for effecting SD strategy and policies. Alternatively, similar functions to these sub-committees might be undertaken by user groups.

In most of the companies surveyed and selected, each business unit also has its own IST committees, which complement and dovetail with the corporate committees.

Lastly, in each of the companies concerned, the central IST function reports to the top-level corporate committee, and sits on and supports the corporate sub-committees and/or user groups.

## Corporate retention of key functions and activities

Regardless of the degree of IST decentralization, there are always some functions and activities that need to be retained as a corporate/central IST responsibility. In the organizations surveyed, these tend to be as indicated in the following principle:

There are certain IST functions and activities that remain centralized at the corporate level: vendor relations, corporate consulting, education, communications network management, data administration, IST strategy and policy support, disaster recovery, financial applications systems, and payroll/personnel systems.

The immediate reason generally given for the corporate retention of vendor relations is the favourable pricing obtainable through corporate ‘blanket’ contracts and aggregate purchasing. Corporate retention also ensures uniformity and compatibility in hardware, software and communications architecture, and minimizes duplicate evaluation. It has also been found that corporate handling of vendor relations provides greater leverage vis-à-vis vendors, facilitates consistent review of capital commitments, and minimizes risk and exposure in contract negotiations (Taylor and Tucker, 1989).

Corporate consulting is retained by the corporate IST group for two basic reasons: to provide the support and advice needed by the various business units in making SD successful, and to exercise an influence and maintain a presence on behalf of the corporate strategic view.

Education and training in the use of development methodology, high-level (4GL) languages, CASE tools, standards, and other systems skills by the central IST group is a central resource available to and supporting end user development. It is retained or controlled as a centralized function largely because it is a specialist function, but it also facilitates corporate control through the presentation of consistent information. Complementary to this control is the influence of corporate in assuring a high standard of education and training. However, it is a shared resource that is promoted by corporate but generally used by business units at their discretion.

Communications network management is retained by corporate IST since it is a function that can only be satisfactorily exercised by a central group. The organization's communications network, global, shared and local, is something that needs to be viewed and planned as a whole, for reasons of architecture and interfacing standards. However, it is the 'backbone' of the network that needs to be managed centrally; intra-department communications might remain within the province of the business unit. Network management also needs to be centralized for 'bottom-line' reasons; it has been shown that significant cost savings are obtainable through the centralized management of voice and data communications (Kerr, 1988).

Data administration may be retained centrally to the extent that corporate reporting needs from respective business units need to be compatible, and to the degree that there is data interchange between business units. To the extent that this is not the case, data administration may be carried out at the business unit level, but with a coordinating function at the corporate level.

The basic concern expressed in connection with data administration by the companies surveyed is that of data security and integrity, and it seems to be a growing concern. One large company is retreating to some extent from its long-established decentralized systems environment due to this concern. Another large company has implemented a policy favouring the file-server/workstation approach, as opposed to microcomputer/PC LANs, since end-users will not have to exercise the necessary disciplines relative to data security, as they do with disk-removable micros.

Overall IST strategy and policy support is retained by the central IST group as an essential and direct resource for corporate senior management in optimizing the leverage of IST resources. Central IST therefore plays a pivotal role in seeking to optimize the congruence between IST direction and corporate goals.

Disaster recovery is generally promoted and planned by the corporate IST group in the companies surveyed because it is a specialist skill, and it is an area critical to corporate needs. The latter applies where any loss of business unit data and/or systems could significantly affect the corporate ‘bottom-line’.

Finance, Payroll, and Personnel are generally handled by the central IST development group

because:

(1) the same system and similar data is used by each business unit,

(2) the government and audit requirements pertinent to these applications systems need to be met in a controlled and uniform manner,

(3) it is through these systems that reporting by respective business units is integrated and

(4) such systems are generally critical in providing requisite corporate control.

There may, in addition, be other systems with these characteristics that are the responsibility of the central IST group.

In retaining the key functions and activities indicated, the 14 companies surveyed and selected tend to align with the concept of federalism (Boynton and Zmud, 1987). According to the United States Constitution, the Federal (central) government can only exercise certain powers as specified. Any remaining powers of government are left to the respective States. Thus, the States have the residual powers that have not been specifically allocated to the Federal Government. In the corporate context, this means that business units are encouraged to exercise the fullest level of discretion and imagination in their use of IST resources, and any function or activity, as long as it has not been specifically reserved at the corporate level.

The federal concept also provides for the central government, i.e. corporate management, to exercise those powers, or IST functions and activities, that cannot feasibly or economically be undertaken by the respective business units.

In the case of the United States Constitution, the division of powers is not altogether clear-cut, because it also says that where the Federal Government has a legitimate right in a given area, it takes precedence over the States. There is also the question of interpretation of the Constitution as a ‘flexible and living’ document. Nonetheless, it is a useful concept in the demarcation of centralized/decentralized functions and activities, in the freedom it gives to business units in using IST, and in the basic rationale it provides for the exercise of centralized powers.

## Strict auditing of standards compliance

In the companies selected for their investment and experience in SD, there is the principle that:

Compliance with agreed systems standards is strictly audited throughout the organization.

The degree of strictness varies among the companies concerned, but in one company standards are policed so seriously that any significant deviation is likely to cause dismissal of the manager responsible. In another company, a monthly standards compliance report is required from each business unit by corporate management. In another, compliance with systems standards is written as a specific responsibility into managerial job descriptions, and is included in the annual review process. Also, in most of the companies, a senior-management corporate standards committee, representing corporate and business unit management, oversees the development and agreement of systems standards.

The areas with which standards in these companies are concerned may include: communications network interface, data formats, data storage and operations housekeeping, disaster recovery, development methodology, documentation, and testing. In most cases the Internal Audit function has a role in monitoring these standards.

## Decentralization on a case by case basis

In spite of the first principle of a top-down impetus to SD, the companies surveyed and selected do not necessarily promote and support SD across the organization as whole, nor without first satisfying certain criteria:

The SD decision is made on a business unit by business unit, function by function, and system by system basis.

The most important criterion in systems centralization/decentralization in the companies concerned is the economic one. Some systems and functions clearly come cheaper when they remain centralized.

A second important criterion in the companies surveyed is that of ‘critical mass’. A business unit must be big enough to sustain its own IST function. There may also be a qualitative criterion as to the ‘readiness’ of the business unit in terms of its systems experience skills, and its general awareness of the disciplines required.

Other criteria found to be relevant to the SD decision are: the level of impact of a given system on the organization, the functional stage of systems development and the skills required therein, and the degree of specialization required (Klein, 1985).

## Influence by persuasion and example

A corporate behavioural aim stressed repeatedly in the organizations surveyed is the avoidance of authoritarianism:

The corporate/central IST function influences the respective business unit systems environments by persuasion and example, rather than coercion.

Being behavioural and informal in nature, the principle may not be an easy one to apply, and it may appear contrary to the principle of strict auditing of compliance standards as well as that of formalized planning and control. However, formalized planning and control and the development of agreed standards are generally pursued in a ‘team’ spirit, with the full involvement of corporate and business unit management. Any sense of authoritarianism by corporate management therefore tends to be avoided.

Viewed from a distance, it is evident that the decentralized systems environment calls for the development of a team culture. The corporate aim in the organizations surveyed is to foster relationships and provide a platform for dialogue and consensus, in which the central IST function has a key role to play. A team culture might be promoted through such mechanisms as user groups, corporate information systems conferences, and the use of corporate consultants.

In the organizations surveyed, corporate consultants from the central IST group play a pivotal role in exercising an influence on behalf of corporate management. They are the means by which corporate IST is 'out there', with end users, acting to minimize the potential losses in decentralized systems, while optimizing the leverage of decentralized IST resources. But corporate consultants are only able to carry out their influencing role to the extent that they are seen by the respective business units as providing a useful and supportive service. To this end, corporate IST consultants play an important part in achieving success with user-developed applications. The more useful and helpful they are, the more influence they have, and at the same time the more impact they have in helping end users to succeed.

It is the job of corporate IST consultants to facilitate the use of development software and software tools, provide coaching in methodology and standards, and give guidance in getting the most out of systems resources. There is therefore a lot expected of them. They must be people-oriented, able to teach, understand the application of tools, be aware of corporate strategy and information architecture, and be available when needed. However, their services in the companies surveyed are never imposed, but used on request, whenever it is in the interests of a business unit to use them.

A further aspect of informal influence by the corporate central IST function in the organizations surveyed is that the corporate IST manager has a clear role as ‘first among equals’ vis-à-vis IST managers in the respective business units. Although these managers may be hired by or on the recommendation of the corporate IST manager, they report to business unit management. It is then the job of the corporate IST manager to foster relationships with the business unit IST managers, and to lead by example.

The principle of top-down impetus and support is the driving factor in successful SD, and this impetus and support is given structure and implementation by top-level coordinating committees and delegated responsibility for IST. In turn, it is the top-level coordinating committees that enable and support formalized planning and control, and delegated responsibility for IST is the complement of decentralization on a case by case basis.

Strict auditing of standards compliance is complementary to and dependent upon formalized planning and control, and it is the consequence of delegated responsibility for IST through decentralization on a case by case basis.

At the centre of the model, providing the nexus that knits the other principles together and promoting the team culture, is influence by persuasion and example. Since the nature of its relationship with other principles is an informal one, this is shown as a dotted line.

In sum, the model represents a ‘gestalt’ view of the principles operating as a whole in and upon the organization. It illustrates the need for each and every one of the principles concerned, as success in the use of one depends on one or more of the others.

## Concluding remarks

The eight principles uncovered and described are the result of observation and classification in the 14 selected organizations, and build upon previous research in or related to the areas of SD. The principles may represent nothing more than common sense and good management. In other words, they may seem obvious, as is often the case after the fact, and there is no apology for this.

A basic aim of the study has been to draw a number of strands together and develop a cohesive approach in an area of change that seems to be of fundamental importance to the systems environment of the 1990s. However, there may be a limitation in applying the principles in that the companies selected were all major companies. Therefore, the principles and the conclusions drawn would need to be applied with discretion, and may not apply equally to smaller companies.

Part of the underlying motivation for embarking on the study was the informal observation in consulting work, over a number of years, that SD is not being optimized in many companies, and that the problem seems to be one of organization, rather than technology. Given this observation, the a priori conclusion is that the effective implementation of the principles described may facilitate a measure of success in SD, and in distributed systems. As always, however, more work needs to be done.
