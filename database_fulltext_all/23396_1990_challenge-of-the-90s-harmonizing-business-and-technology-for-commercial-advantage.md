# Challenge of the 90s: harmonizing business and technology for commercial advantage

GARRY E. HUNT

Business Development Director, I.T. Partners Ltd, Reading, UK

Abstract: Every business executive in every industry throughout the world is facing increasing and unrelenting competition. During the past decade this growing competition has brought with it the associated threat of survival of so many enterprises at a time when we are also experiencing the effects of a new industrial revolution created by the continuing and rapid advancements in information technology.

In this paper we present the argument that the key to corporate success is associated with the development of the effective management of IT. The expenditures involved are now so large that they must be the concern of the Chief Executive Officer of the company. But, as we demonstrate, this is not the current situation in many organizations. The challenge of the '90s will be to integrate in an effective manner, the corporate business strategy with the increasing use of IT. In many cases, it will require a new style of management, but for those organizations who do not make this change, their corporate prosperity may be at stake.

This paper sets out my personal view on this major issue, which was presented on the 23 April 1990, as the British Computer Society 1990 Jubilee lecture and sponsored by Computer Weekly.

## Introduction

Every business executive in every industry throughout the world is facing increasing and unrelenting competition. During the past decade, this growing competition has brought with it the associated threat of survival of so many enterprises. The causes of this significant increase in competition are many and involve the globalization of markets, deregulation and the ability to maintain business effectiveness through the exploitation of technology, in particular that created by the information technology revolution. But regardless of these causes, one fact is clear; management teams have been forced to sharpen their decision making skills, take difficult and sometimes painful actions and broaden their approaches to competing effectively. But are they really in control of their businesses?

In the last few years there have been a number of breathtaking changes in the state of corporate and national economies reflected in the responses of stock exchanges around the world. During October and November 1987 the stock exchanges reacted to the rapidly falling share values of more than 60%. But this has not been a unique event as we have recently seen with the significant downturns on Wall Street and also in Tokyo with the sudden drops in the Nikkei index producing severe implications for the Japanese and world economies. In any year there will be several events outside the framework of market forces which can affect the financial situation of any company. In the UK, businesses are trying to maintain their competitive edge against the additional burden of high interest rates which are creating major problems for all organizations, large or small. Consequently as organizations develop their corporate strategies for the next few years, they need an extremely efficient operation, in particular in their use and management of IT, in order to remain competitive at all times in the future, when their markets and corporate prosperity can be adversely affected by sudden, unexpected and external economic issues beyond their control.

The world of business is changing admit a time of a new industrial revolution, namely that of information technology, (see for example Arthur Anderson/Sunday Times, 1988). Computers have become cheaper, more powerful and more convenient to use over the last few decades. The advances in digital transmission have been the key to telecommunications networks which now enable data, voice and images to be communicated from one machine to another and therefore become part of the global corporate infrastructure. Space transmissions have actually shrunk the effective size of the globe and opened up the opportunities for intercontinental businesses. Fibre optic technology is providing a further increase in band width and improving the economics of these networks. Major advances in the technologies of compact storage have now advanced from a storage density 30,000 characters per square centimetre in 1968 to more than 3 million characters in the early 1990's. With the continuing scientific and computer and software and storage advancements, we do have the technology to support the current commercial challenges. But are businesses sufficiently mature in their management skills to exploit these IT systems in order to maintain their competitive edge?

Consider the following example of an international retail business and the way in which the globalization of IT substantially enhances the competitive edge of the business. We assume that the headquarters of the company is in Columbus, Ohio. From the point of sale computers, daily reports on the sales trends and associated information flows in to the headquarters. To restock, the company sends orders by satellite to plants in the US, Hong Kong, South Korea and Sri Lanka. The goods are sent back to Columbus from Hong Kong aboard a chartered Boeing 747-SP that makes four flights a week. At the highly automated distribution centre in Columbus, apparel is sorted, priced and prepared for shipment, all within 48 hours. By truck and plane the goods are transported to the company's 3200 stores throughout the US. As a result of this sequence of events, the apparel goes on sale within 60 days of receipt of the order. Compare this approach with the more traditional schemes still in use where it takes 6 months or more for companies to place their orders for goods

For a company to operate in this way requires a carefully developed IT support infrastructure which integrates all the management, sales, manufacturing, and accounting operations. Boardroom management is not as efficient as many believe. Currently 80% of a meeting time is taken up with getting the information leaving only 20% available for decision making. These figures need to be reversed. The introduction of EIS/MIS would facilitate management organization, scheduling, tracking of business performance, reminds of deadlines and warns when a project falls behind. Paul Lego, CEO of Westinghouse has been recently converted to this way of operating and as he says “. . .this hasn’t changed the way I manage, but I feel more comfortable with the decisions I have made . . .”

The corporate IT systems, exploiting the LAN/WAN technologies, provides the electronic mail network to link the work group with remote operations, in other parts of the building, in other branches, subsidiaries and overseas.

This system then connects into an information system to handle all data relevant to the business; document transfer, factory/inventory details, sales, warehouse/distribution details, cash flow, financial news, market reports and it is all available instantly throughout the organization. Therefore, an immediate consequence of the integration of IT into a company will be the breakdown of the traditional pyramid structure. Peter Drucker stated “... whole layers of management neither make decisions nor lead. Instead their main, if not only function is to serve as relays, human boosters for the faint unfocused signals, which pass for communication in the traditional pre-IT organization.” We have seen dramatic reductions in middle management in IBM, AT&T, BT and BP in the recent past and this may be the trend in the next decade.

## Business needs, trends and barriers to success

A computer system is not a piece of magic that will immediately guarantee success when introduced into the company's operation. There must be a systematic approach to the implementation and operation of computer based facilities for each business application. Any aspect of the system; hardware, software, maintenance, security, staffing, management etc., if not carefully planned, prepared and supported, can produce unexpected project costs and bring failure to an otherwise well planned system.

There are however, a number of basic business needs that must be supported when using IT in corporate activities:

\- operational efficiency

\- improved quality

A number of studies have confirmed that extensive scope exists for further development of software engineering methodologies and tools. One such investigation, commissioned by the Department of Trade and Industry and carried out by PA Consulting Group, Computers and Telecommunications (1985), studied the extent and effect of using software engineering tools in the commercial sectors. The study showed that at that time the major activity was associated with software construction, with considerable use of software methodologies in all areas except quality assurance and system specification. The responses received from the 60 organizations consulted at that time, indicated that:

\- 66% of their projects had overrun their timescales

\- 58% had exceeded their budgets - 45% indicated the development task were difficult.

There is little to establish that these figures have changed with the passage of time.

\- maintain control

\- creative growth

• good investment performance

\- data security.

It is interesting to note that this is currently the number one issue for more than 41% of companies recently polled, compared with hostile takovers (35%) and fraud (16%).

Amid these requirements from the commercial sectors, the IT trends are toward such issues as;

\- business led use of IT

• value for money from IT

\- open systems

\- standards

\- choice of solutions

\- third party supply

If we already have the technology, then why are we not using it in an effective manner? That relatively simple question masks a multitude of problems which are the core of the matter that faces UK plc and indeed the majority of the leading nations today. It can however, be reduced to a number of basic issues which are the barriers to corporate success.

\- lack of business strategy related to the use and exploitation of IT.

For many organizations, the Board sees IT issues as isolated software matters, responding to user demands and not integrated with the corporate business strategy with a supporting investment programme.

\- lack of available resources

\- lack of (large) project experience

\- lack of commitment from the top.

We do need to overcome these matters if we are to make real progress in the next few years.

## Demands on resources for IT/software development

In Britain alone software production is consuming more than £3 billion worth of resources, in Europe the figure is £16 billion and worldwide it is estimated to be a massive £75 billion. With a predicted growth in 1995 to £192 billion, consuming 24 million man-years of resource annually, we need to be both efficient and automated in many of the tasks and greatly reduce the current problems of projects failing to be finished on time, within budget and possessing unexpected operational problems when they are first implemented.

The modern approach to many of these software problems, is to use the methodical, structured and disciplined attitudes of an engineer in these software tasks. This approach has given rise to the subject we now call Software engineering, see for example Hunt (1988) and Simons (1987). The tools are themselves pieces of software, designed to carry out the specific task in a simple and reliable manner. The introduction of software engineering concepts and tools offers the user the potential benefits of:

\- higher software reliability and reduced maintenance costs

\- better control of costs, project management and time scales

\- application requirements fully achieved

\- improving the total quality management of the project and within the company's overall activities.

There are some suggestions of the likely cost justifications of using automated tools from the earlier study by PA Consulting Group, Computers and Telecommunications (1985) who suggested that the benefit to UK industry could be:

\- £260 million savings from the use of improved management tools,

\- £100 million from the use of specification design and generation tools.

This total potential saving of £360 million is equivalent in cost terms, to the employment of about 24,000 software engineering staff. Similarly, the introduction of automated tools will release staff from maintenance and enhancement tasks. If this amounted to, say a reduction from 20% to 15%, the estimated benefits could be as much as £57 million, the equivalent of some 4000 software engineering staff. Furthermore, using the right software engineering tools could reduce the development effort by 25%, while project time could be cut by a further 10% (Rippon 1987).

If UK commercial companies wish to be competitive in the world markets, then they must make the fullest use of all aspects of software engineering, CASE tools and expert systems which are already beginning to make an impact upon a number of market sectors.

## The problem at the top

The key problem in the corporate exploitation of IT lies in the organizational responsibilities of many companies. Chief Executives, in general, remain unskilled in computer applications. A recent survey in the US (Personal Computing April 1989) showed that only $21\%$ of the CEO's of the top 500 companies actually used a computer in their daily work. While $59\%$ indicated that it improved their personal productivity and $35\%$ found it provided better access to information, $64\%$ still found these machines difficult to use. 29% complained that the technology continues to change too rapidly. This must be an important message to the manufacturers.

In the UK the picture is worrying. In 1988, a survey showed that 64% of top managers in financial institutions had received no training in IT and only 10% of directors recognised the value of knowing anything about computers. We must improve upon this situation if UK companies are to survive.

The costs of IT can be large enough to be significant at the corporate level and be a factor in the CEO's mind as they try to fit the corporate objectives within the constraints imposed by finance, politics and the stockmarket. A major bank may spend as much as £1 billion over a five year period. For many the business benefits are not immediately apparent, so that CEO's and board directors are frequently reluctant to simply accept the recommendations of their computer literate managers.

But is there a potential limit to the growth in IT as a consequence of the reduced availability of the key resource, namely people? We cannot escape the demographic trends. There are some 300,000 skilled people in the UK working on software related tasks and the professional part of this figure is growing at 8% per annum. But with a 30% decline in the 18 year old population during the period of 1983 to 1995 the resource pool is drying up or at least not growing with the speed to match the need. This once more points to a need for more automated IT systems and software products, where the increasing complexity of systems as they support a greater range of tasks may then be effectively transparent to the end user, so that a wider range of people can confidently and effectively use IT systems for business tasks.

There is still a further issue of why we still travel to a central office, fight the crowded trains and congested roads every day. Is this the pattern for the future? I hope not.

A recent economic and social benefit analysis study of tele-working carried out by the Henley Forecasting Centre (1990) found that through using existing IT systems 7 million full time staff could do a significant amount of their office work at home. In addition, 1.6 million part time staff were in a similar position, together with a further 1.3 million self employed people. By 1995 10.5 million people could be 'home working' which could improve their quality of life, productivity, efficiency and allow an effective integration of women in the business work force. The problem for large companies like ICI and BP may then be the retention of their talented staff.

## Challenge of the '90s

As we enter the 1990s there is no doubt that the existing technological capabilities in computing, and communications are shrinking the information gap which exists between the information required by an organization in an ideal situation and the information easily and practically available. Therefore, if UK plc is to prosper in the increasingly competitive global business arena, then it does require the education of executives into the value of IT and effective marriage between business and technology so that companies can maintain their competitive advantage. Top management must aspire to what can be done through IT, bringing their understanding of the business issues and their imagination to the situation of conceiving business strategies. For companies to succeed, the challenge of the 1990s will be the effective marriage between business and IT technology. But above all, top management must be in charge and that includes being in charge of IT too.
