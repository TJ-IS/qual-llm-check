# Teaching information systems to the visually handicapped — a case history

A. ROBERTS and T. G. GOUGH

School of Computer Studies University of Leeds.

Abstract: This paper outlines a number of issues to be addressed in teaching information systems to visually handicapped students. It is a case history in that it draws on the authors' experience of teaching a multiply handicapped student over a three year period. Its particular emphasis is on the need for early preparation, in advance of the student's arrival and then throughout the teaching process. It points to the type of support facilities required. Although all the main headings would need to be considered in the teaching of any subject to visually handicapped students there are several that have added importance in the teaching of information systems. The paper is an attempt to share experience rather than make a case for tackling the issues raised in one particular way.

## Introduction

Although, at last, much is being written particularly in the computing press about the development of Information Technology designed with the needs of disabled individuals in mind (Mattison, 1984) and systems are being developed for school use in the education of handicapped children (Durre et al., 1984 and Schweiklardt, 1985b), almost nothing has been written of relevance to teaching Information Systems to visually handicapped students in Higher Education. In general there is a surprising lack of information although the Bardsoft and Bardtec databases supplied by Newcastle Polytechnic might go somewhat towards supplying information; help is gleaned by the verbal sharing of information and experience in a piecemeal fashion.

For this reason this paper will concentrate on the authors' experience of teaching a multiply handicapped student on a three-year BSc honours scheme in Data Processing and on the lessons learned so that others may be enabled to share and adapt this experience to their own situations. Visually handicapped, used in the title of this paper is a term currently used interchangeably with sight-impaired and covers the complete range of visual problems, saying nothing of the level of disability. Alternatively, there is the term severely visually handicapped which often, but not invariably means blind, i.e. with no useful sight. It is, therefore, wise to avoid presuming anything about the level of disability before meeting the student.

## Recruitment

At the University of Leeds there is an Office of the

Adviser to the Physically Disabled and the Adviser is involved in an advisory capacity as part of the student recruitment process. The final decision as to whether or not to make the student an offer of a place remains with the individual department or Admissions Tutor. Prior to the Admissions Tutor making a decision the handicapped candidate is invited to visit the University and spend time with the Adviser to the Disabled and with staff of the Accommodation Office to assess whether he or she is likely to cope with the physical environment of the University. The need to adapt accommodation and potential problems of access are determined and an initial assessment of possible solutions is made. Responsibility for these issues lies outside the academic department, although the department may have to be flexible in various areas, perhaps by making available keys to allow the handicapped student to use routes through the building normally denied to other students, for example, to avoid flights of stairs or revolving doors, or by allowing guide dogs in lecture theatres and laboratories. Responsibility for assessing academic support needs is primarily that of the Department or Admissions Tutor in collaboration with the student and the student's current place of education.

This initial assessment of both physical and academic needs is very important and must be made before a place is offered as it is irresponsible to take on a student and then be unable to offer the level of support required. The support must be in place by the time the student arrives as thereafter time is at a premium. The level of academic support will depend on the nature and extent of visual handicap and on the individual's preferences. The extent of the visual handicap will vary and may include tunnel vision or dyslexia where the main source of advice on requirements will be the student and his or her current school or college. Limited sight might require large print or large character display, with or without audio. There are several systems available which meet these requirements and are realistically priced, though rarely within the individual's pocket, for example, Vantage and Vista.

If the prospective student has no useful sight then he or she may wish to make the choice between audio and braille. A student who prefers braille may have to accept audio occasionally, as it is normally simpler to record onto a cassette at short notice than to produce braille at short notice, for example, overnight. Audio will include notes or textbooks on cassette or speech output from a computer. Braille can be hard i.e. long lasting or soft i.e. transient, or an Opticon can be used, (see also the section on Support).

## Course selection

The Data Processing scheme at Leeds contains compulsory and optional components and, in addition, requires the student to take an ancillary course in each of the first two years. There are a range of ancillary courses available to Data Processing students and restrictions on choice are few. However, for a visually handicapped student there may well be a conflict between student preference and what is possible. The members of staff in the Department providing the ancillary course have to be prepared to take on the extra work involved. At Leeds, the Admissions Tutor for Data Processing agreed with the student (and the providing department) on her choice of ancillary course six months in advance i.e. before A levels for her first year ancillary and before her first year examinations for her second year ancillary. This rather risky strategy would have been entirely successful except for a misunderstanding of the term ‘severely visually handicapped’ in the discussions of the second year ancillary. The provider department had experience of teaching a student with some residual sight and none of teaching someone with no sight. It took some time to overcome the difficulties that resulted from this misunderstanding.

If a compulsory component is inappropriate, this has to be determined early and a substitute component agreed (and perhaps formally approved by a courses committee). In the second year of the Data Processing scheme at Leeds, the Computer Graphics component is compulsory and the intention was to agree a substitute course with the student during her first year. In the event, the student, who had a strong visual sense, decided that she would like to do Computer Graphics and the lecturer responsible for the course agreed, though suggesting and making some modifications to the lecture material and the coursework. All parties were later to agree that the outcome was unsatisfactory and an alternative should have been sought.

However, this unsatisfactory outcome raises a significant issue for the teaching of information systems to the visually handicapped. The use of computer graphics both for the presentation of information to management and to support (or provide) communication between systems developers and users of information systems continues to increase. This underlines the need to address the problem of providing adequate access to computer graphics for the visually handicapped, if they are to take as active a part in the design and use of information systems as their sighted counterparts. Work has already been done in the general area of tactile representation of graphical output, (Schweikhardt, 1985a, 1985b, Shimizu, 1986 and Turk, 1983). The use of the braille font on the Apple Macintosh and a stereocopier, noted later in this paper in connection with the preparation of diagrams, represents no more than a small step towards addressing this issue.

## Support

Two types of support are available and required: people and equipment.

## People

At Leeds people-centred support came from a colleague at Bradford University who provided essential advice during the recruitment cycle and whenever requested thereafter. Another useful contact was The Research Centre for the Education of the Visually Handicapped in Birmingham. Support from the RNIB was somewhat variable, though very good in the provision of examination papers in braille. There is a tape reading service on-site at the University funded, jointly, by the RNIB and the University, and this was an excellent service which was rather under-used as our student preferred braille to tape as a rule. There were some difficulties with taping technical material. The University Computing Service provided a limited service on conversion of text to braille using volunteers. Help in producing handouts and some textbooks was forthcoming from very supportive staff in our local reading machine service at Leeds Central Library, and, initially, high quality diagrams were produced at Wakefield Prison and other diagrams of an acceptable standard were prepared by hand in the School of Computer Studies. However, even with this level of local assistance, it would have been impossible to maintain the quality of provision to our student had not the School agreed to invest in a part-time teaching assistant to support lecturing staff and our blind student. This will not be necessary for every visually handicapped or even severely visually handicapped student but was found to be necessary for our multiply handicapped student in a rapidly changing area like Information Systems.

As any other student, the blind student was supported by colleagues and took an active part in practical work, both on an individual and group basis. However, additional moral support was required on careers prospects, on coping with the workload and with being under extra pressure. There was considerable extra pressure on student colleagues as well.

## Equipment

The general term braille is about as informative as the term visually handicapped. Braille can be hard, usually embossed on a thick paper, or soft, normally electronically produced by temporarily raising a series of metal pins. Hard braille is long lasting, while soft braille lasts only until the next item of information is required and, dependent on the equipment, is produced in units of 20, 40 or 80 characters.

There are two grades of braille, Grade 1, which gives a character by character representation in braille of sighted text, and Grade 2 or contracted braille, where one braille character can represent several sighted characters or a whole word. There is also computer braille, similar to Grade 1, but including all the available ascii characters, mathematical braille covering all mathematical notation, and many others.

Leeds University lent the student a Versabraille, a very versatile machine, producing soft braille and capable of storing information on tape or, in the case of the more modern machines, on disk. The machine can be set to produce different versions of braille, for example, English braille, American braille, or computer braille. This machine was extensively used for note-taking during lectures and, to a lesser extent, as a computer terminal.

The School of Computer Studies purchased a Braillo printer to link to the School's VAX network. The student brought with her a BBC workstation with a speech synthesiser, and a Perkins (which was used in the examinations, primarily, and as a back-up to the Versabraille for lecture notes). Locally in Leeds the student (and staff) had access to a reading machine for conversion of text to braille and to a stereocopier for producing tactile diagrams. Raised diagrams produced by hand were labelled using a braille labeller.

## Learning environment

Teaching visually handicapped students involves additional effort from lecturing staff, whether the component being taken is compulsory or optional. Lecturers need to be made aware of the extra demands to be made them, for example, in converting handouts to non-visual form, in the early identification of texts (or parts of texts) to which reference will be essential and of which conversion to non-visual form will be necessary, in changing lecturing style to include the reading aloud of the contents of any OHP foils and in the use of diagrammatic material. The fact that Information Systems is a fast moving discipline makes it harder to cope with these additional requirements.

Thus, teaching the visually handicapped may involve significant changes to the working pattern of many lecturers who prepare the night before (or even the hour before)! The student may find it helpful if some members of staff are prepared to give individual tutorials on a regular or ad hoc basis, particularly if the material is of a highly visual nature, for example, tree structures.

Great care has to be given to the setting of suitable coursework. There are many questions to be addressed. Will the blind student be able to tackle it alone? Debugging long programming assignments is very difficult, producing diagrammatic material, even for a student who can make use of diagrams, is virtually impossible without help. If the student is going to work in a group, is there some aspect of the assignment where they can 'pull their weight'? We were rather late in spotting our student's developing keyboard shyness. Because it was obviously easier for others to use the keyboard other members of the group tended to do so, with the blind student's full agreement, and the keyboard skills the student had on joining us atrophied, thus depriving the student of a potentially valuable future skill.

If great care has to be given to the setting of suitable coursework, even greater consideration has to be given to the choice of a suitable project. In their final year our students are expected to do a project of some 14–15 weeks duration. These projects may be in Computer Science, Data Processing or Operational Research. Some are purely internal to the School while others may involve outside collaboration. Several generalizations are possible. Firstly, the more severe the handicap the more supervision and support will be required and this should be borne in mind when the supervisor's load is being decided. Secondly, an internal project will make it easier for the student than relying on external contacts. A handicapped student will have more trouble than an able-bodied student in making up lost time if external contacts prove unreliable. Thirdly, a blind student will have extra problems, in debugging computer programs, with calculations (mathematical braille is very long and unwieldy), with diagrams, and with literature searches. Even reading round the subject will prove difficult and very time consuming. Fourthly, although the student may object, its is to everyone's advantage to play to the student's strengths rather than to his or her interests (at this point it is as well to be prepared to be accused of discrimination by a determined student!).

## Preparation of teaching materials

There are four aspects to the preparation of teaching materials and the willing co-operation of lecturers is essential if materials are to be ready in a non-sighted form when they are required. Some texts are available on tape but those that are readily available (from the RNIB) may be rather dated. There is a long lead time for the creation of new tapes. Conversion of texts via a reading machine was often found to be impracticable due to changing fonts, layout of tables, and machine sensitivity to text quality, for example. It is possible to obtain from some authors (or publishers) machine readable copy which can then be converted to braille, although formatting commands may have to be altered. However, unless a convertible version is already available, it can take several months from the selection of a text to the availability of a braille version, despite the best efforts of all involved, (Cox, 1984). Some publishers are more helpful than others. It may be necessary to encourage lecturers to select parts of texts (rather than the whole) for conversion and to be sure of the level of need. The question, 'Is this material essential, nice to have or of only peripheral interest?' is not rhetorical.

Coursework briefs were prepared via the reading machine if the text was of good quality or converted to braille via the VAX and the Braillo printer otherwise. Similar procedures were used for handouts, though on occasion, tape was used. As with texts and OHP foils, lecturers may have to adapt their use of handouts in lectures to accommodate the non-sighted student.

Initially, diagrams were prepared by hand in the School or at Wakefield Prison. The acquisition of a braille font for use on the Apple Macintosh greatly facilitated the in-house production of good quality tactile diagrams. This font was first developed for use in geographical applications (Jansen and Jansen, 1986). This produced flat laser printed pictures (Figure 1a) which were then photocopied onto a special capsule paper (Andrews, 1985) and raised in a stereo photocopier belonging to the Northern Regional Association for the Blind, (Figure 1b is the sighted version of Figure 1a). Figures 2, 3 and 4 show the location of the braille output from sighted text prior to printing, and Figures 5, 6 and 7 show the translation of braille back to printed text. Some blind students want tactile diagrams but the level of complexity is something to be watched. Diagrams can also be described on tape which requires a highly skilled reader but may be necessary if the diagram is essential to the understanding of the associated text.

Teaching material has to be available early and some editing of sighted copy is unavoidable. Again the rate of change in Information Systems has a significant impact on preparation.

## Examinations

Examination papers to be taken by the non-sighted student have to be prepared much earlier than is usual to allow for the brailling process. From the examiners' point of view setting the paper raises similar questions about technique as does the use of handouts and OHP foils in lectures. It is important to avoid putting the non-sighted candidate at a disadvantage with the avoidable use of diagrams or tables or by selecting a style of question which restricts the candidate's choice. These issues have to be put to examiners prior to the construction of the papers and checked in the assessment process.

One third extra time was allowed for the completion of each paper by our student and this time was absolutely essential in the case of papers with a large mathematical content. The examination took place in a separate area to avoid distracting other examinees. Papers were read through by the examiner to enable the student to check that the braille version and the sighted version matched. It is desirable to allow the student to be examined in the medium he or she prefers. Some institutions will supply an ammanuensis. Alternatives are audio tape, although staff may have reservations about marking from tape, and hard or soft braille, using a Perkins or a Versabraille.

If the examination script is completed in braille, as our student preferred, it is necessary to get the braille transcribed. This poses a number of problems. Transcription from braille to sighted text requires considerable skill and can be time consuming, whereas time is short for marking examination scripts. Asking the student to dictate to tape the brailled script places extra demands on the student and the marker. Using a workstation for the typing of the answers requires the provision of a facility for reading through on completion of the script and for the correction of errors in the translation process from braille to sighted script.

Consideration must be given to the timetabling of the examinations. The papers need to be early enough in the exam cycle to allow for any necessary translation time if this is to be done manually (which may be necessary for anything involving mathematics, as translation programs capable of handling mathematics do not exist). Our multiply handicapped student was physically incapable of sitting more than one examination in a day, a problem exacerbated by the prolonged duration of each examination. Every effort was, in fact, made to have at least a day between each examination which the student was taking.

The choice of examination setting and answering mechanisms may involve examiners in extra work and the examining body in extra expense, if the nonsighted student is to be enabled to cope.

## Additional handicaps

Our student at Leeds was confined to a wheelchair and required 24 hour support. Her additional handicaps affected both the domestic arrangements and her academic work. Community 'Service Volunteers provided 24 hour domestic support and the University adapted accommodation for the student and provided additional accommodation for the Volunteers. Wheelchair access affected the choice of lecture theatres and constrained the location of tutorials and classes. Keys normally available only to staff were issued to the student to facilitate access. Additional demands were placed on tutorial staff to supervise the domestic arrangements.

Our student had limited reach and limited physical strength. She could not handle large sheets of embossed paper easily and using a keyboard for more than short periods was very tiring. She had hospital appointments and, if a blind student misses a lecture, the cost to the student is probably double that for a sighted student, as someone else's notes not only have to be read, they also have to be converted to braille or tape.

## Conclusion

The expertise acquired in teaching information systems to a blind student is clearly re-usable both in Leeds and in responding to requests for help from elsewhere. Whether the non-sighted materials are reusable is not so obvious, as even minor changes may involve starting again and different blind students will have different preferences in material presentation.

If teaching a severely visually handicapped student is to be successful then forward planning is essential. It was difficult, at least initially, to locate expertise and source material despite help from elsewhere. A lot of reliance seems to be placed on informal contacts.

It is very important to have local contacts. As can be seen from this paper, we made heavy use of local contacts throughout the student's three years. It is important that your contacts can and are willing to help, as most institutions who work with the blind are, rightly, geared to help the majority of the visually handicapped, a category which does not include those in Higher Education. Also, many local groups are staffed by volunteers, so while they would be willing to help they cannot provide a service to the required timescale.

It is very easy to underestimate the time and resources required to provide appropriate support for both staff and student(s), even with considerable help and advice from those with prior experience. However, as it is probably clear from the tenor of this paper, the most important resource is goodwill. The willing cooperation of colleagues at many levels in the School, in the University and elsewhere, was crucial to our efforts to provide an appropriate learning environment for our multipally handicapped student.

## Biographical Notes

Ann Roberts is a Project Officer in the School of Computer Studies at the University of Leeds. She has worked in various projects for the University of Leeds over a twelve-year period. At present Ann is involved in a CEC CTS2 project on Conformance Testing, developing test software for the Computer Graphics interface. Ann was closely involved in the educational support of the multiply handicapped student and acted as liaison officer between the School and all

outside supportive bodies.

Tom Gough is a Senior Lecturer in the School of Computer Studies at the University of Leeds. He joined the School in 1978 after spending fifteen years in management services in ICI. He was recruited to establish the School's Undergraduate Degree schemes in data processing and is presently Course Tutor for the School's MSc in Office Automation and Information Systems. His teaching and research interests are in information systems, in their development and evaluation, in the people dimension of such systems. He has a particular interest in office systems. Tom works as a consultant for ULIS (University of Leeds Industrial Services Limited), mainly with first-time or inexperienced users of computers. Tom was the Admissions Tutor responsible for recruiting the student referred to in the paper and was her personal tutor throughout her time at Leeds.
