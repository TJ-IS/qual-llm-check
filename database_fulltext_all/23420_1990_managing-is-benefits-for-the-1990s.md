# Managing IS benefits for the 1990s

DAVID J. SILK

Director of Studies, The Senior Course, Greenlands, Henley

Abstract: This paper analyses the situation and suggests a way ahead. It is in three parts. The first part reviews the situation at the start of the 1990s. It identifies the growing concern of managers about justifying IT investment, and the methods which they currently use in their organizations to do so. The second part discusses the challenge facing managers, at the general level and in the field of Information Management. A major challenge is for IT/IS to be viewed within the context of strategic business thinking, the principles of which are described in this section. The third part of the paper outlines the response of managers and IT professionals in this area in the 1990s. A benefit-level matrix is described which can be used as a tool for both analysis and synthesis within the business context. Finally, there is some practical guidance about managing benefits alongside other aspects of IT projects.

## Introduction

The generic benefits of Information Technology and Systems (IT/IS) are efficiency, effectiveness and strategic advantage (or competitive edge). In theory, we can assess these benefits in terms of the usual financial measures of business performance. In practice, this is difficult, partly due to the growing importance of the soft benefits of IT/IS, and partly to a faster changing business environment. Despite these difficulties, the general level of IT investment is rising, but in the 1990s we shall clearly need better tools to assess and monitor the benefits of IT/IS.

## The situation we have reached

It is now a management truism that modern IT/IS is too important to be left to IT professionals. IT/IS has to be seen within the context of general management: concerned with the future direction of the enterprise as a whole. For an effective dialogue between general managers and IT professionals we must be clear how the management of information fits into that wider context.

## Fundamentals of Information Management

Let us start with fundamentals. Managers direct resources to achieve business results. Those resources include people, money, material, energy, time and information. All of these, except information, can be measured. Information is closely bound up with the control of the other resources. It is intangible and its value is subjective, yet it is vital to the successful running of the enterprise. Modern IT/IS can dramatically improve the collection, storage, processing and communication of the information; it presents an important opportunity. Information Management can therefore be defined as the informed response of the general manager to that opportunity. IT professionals must now see the issues of investment in, and management of, IT in the same way as general managers: as just one resource area contributing to the running of a successful business.

Because IT/IS is competing with other resource areas, we must be clear about the benefits. The three generic benefits of IT/IS (each corresponding to a so-called era of IT usage) are often defined as:

(1) Efficiency: doing the same job better and thus saving other resources.

(2) Effectiveness: doing a better job and thus enabling other resources to achieve better business results.

(3) Strategic advantage (or competitive edge): bettering the business, by exploiting the ways in which IT/IS can support or drive strategic business change.

It is clear from these definitions that the benefit depends on the management context in which the technology operates. These three generic benefits became prominent in the 1960s, 1970s and 1980s respectively. Today, most of the big savings of efficiency systems have already been made, and strategic use of IT/IS is still a controversial area. Informal surveys of managers suggest that only 10 – 15% of companies are currently implementing efficiency and strategic systems; they are more concerned with effectiveness systems.

## Current Issues in IM

A survey of general managers by Silk (1989) identified five major issues of Information Management which were of concern to them in the period 1987–88:

(1) The impact of IS on the organization;

(2) The need for an information strategy linked to business strategy;

(3) Maintaining the security of electronic data;

(4) Managing the IS function;

(5) Justifying IS investment.

The issues are listed in descending order of the frequency cited. A further survey (Silk, 1990), showed that during 1989 these five issues were still the most important. Number one was still the most frequently cited, but Number five seemed to have become more prominent compared with the remaining three. This indicates a high, and possibly increasing, level of concern amongst general managers about justifying investment in IT/IS.

It follows that investment as an act of faith is no longer good enough. The important question for general managers is – what is the expected impact on the bottom line of the business (whether in the short or longer term)? For a nonprofit-seeking enterprise, it is the impact on cost or quality of service which matters. Managers will rightly demand a clearer answer to that question, and will check that the benefits are in fact delivered.

## Justifying IS

Quantifying the benefits of IT/IS is the core problem of justifying IT investment. The precise measures to be used will depend on the nature of the business, but the three generic benefits suggest corresponding financial measures:

(1) Efficiency systems to be judged by cost savings;

(2) Effectiveness systems to be judged by Return on Assets (RoA) for other resources (such as people and material);

(3) Strategic (competitive edge) systems to be judged by growth (of revenue or profit).

Recent research in the US by Weill and Olson (1989) supports these three generic benefits and the corresponding types of business measure. The problem, of course, is that as the emphasis has moved from efficiency systems in the 1960s to strategic systems in the 1980s it has become more difficult to make sound financial estimates. Figure 1 illustrates this problem.

The hard benefits of efficiency systems can be quantified without great difficulty, and a clear financial business case made for investment. This was appropriate for the large mainframe systems of the 1960s which did make real savings of other resources such as people. At the other extreme, strategic (edge) systems have often been justified by acts of faith, because the benefits are soft, or too uncertain, and confidence in financial figures is therefore low. This can produce a polarization within an enterprise between those who are uncomfortable with anything other than a quantified financial case, and those who believe that the wider opportunity of IT/IS will only be achieved with some acts of faith, stemming from strategic vision. A minority (often including accountants) believe that if you think long and hard enough benefits can always be expressed in financial terms. Few would disagree that the business case should be as sharp and quantified as possible. It is therefore useful to identify some milestones in sharpening up the business case. The lower part of Figure 1 does this and suggests a total of seven types of justification which might be used for IT/IS projects:

(0) Must-do. This is a special case, not shown on the diagram. It relates to an investment which is unavoidable – one required by legislative change, for example, or which is essential to remain as a player in the chosen sector of business. There is little point in spending time on formal justification of such a system.

(1) Faith. The investment is justified as an act of faith based on the judgement or vision of senior management.

(2) Logic. The causal logic by which a business improvement will arise from the proposed IS is identified as the basis for the business case. The size of the improvement is not specified.

(3) Direction. In addition, an appropriate observable quantity is identified, which is then measured to check whether the business has indeed moved in the intended beneficial direction.

(4) Size. In this case the size of the change in the observable quantity is estimated and this is checked quantitatively when the system is in operation.

(5) Value. The quantified change(s) are given some considered weighting, so that disparate benefits can be compared with each other.

(6) Money. In this final stage, each of the benefits is given a financial value. Not only can they be compared with each other, but the impact on overall business financial statements and performance measures can be calculated.

A retail food supermarket will serve as an example to illustrate this progression. A Must-Do project might be to accept an electronic (EDI) interface with a major wholesale supplier who now requires this as a condition for doing business. This would be unavoidable if the retailer has no real alternative supplier. Another Must-Do system might be to modify software to produce tax returns in a new format required by the tax authorities.

In contrast, an Act of Faith might be to install and advertize a customer-operated information service to advise customers about goods on sale in relation to their own dietary and family circumstances. The system might produce an individual shopping list and indicate where to find the relevant goods in the store. It would take account of current special offers. Such a system is often proposed as a strategic use of IT in the retail sector. The benefits might include better company image, greater turnover, better understanding of local customers, and identification of the need for products not presently offered.

A Logic justification for the system would be to argue exactly how and why these business benefits would occur as a result of the project. Direction would specify which observable measures would change and in which direction. Thus, the number of people entering the store in certain periods of the day might increase; the proportion actually making purchases might increase; the average size of purchases might increase; the average time spent finding goods (and thus the number of people in the store) might decrease. A Size justification would give a quantitative estimate of these changes so that a numerical comparison before and after the system could be made. Value would consciously weigh the relative merits of the various benefits; whether, for example, it is better to get more people in the shop even if they buy less. It thereby establishes trade-offs between benefits. Finally, the Money justification translates this into financial terms such as increased revenue or profit.

The merit of the seven types of justification defined here is that they encourage managers to sharpen up the business case to a degree to which they still feel confident with the numbers. Often this will mean stopping short of financial figures and admitting that a value judgement is then necessary.

To determine current practice in this area, mid-career managers on courses at a UK business school were asked to identify the methods of justifying IT/IS currently used in their organizations. Reports from group discussion showed that most organizations have two main methods: acts of faith and financial justification. To make the survey more precise, the above seven categories of justification were explained and then the managers were asked to say which were used in their organizations. A histogram of the responses so far, based on a sample of 159 managers during the period January–May 1990, is shown in Figure 2.

The survey is still being carried out, but these tentative observations can be made:

(1) The ‘Must-Do’ category is important, being recognized in 50% of the organizations represented;

(2) For the remaining six methods, there is a polarization of practice between 'soft' and 'hard' methods;

(3) At the soft end, 'Faith' seems to be giving way to the slightly more demanding discipline of a 'Logic' justification;

(4) In the middle ground, there seems to be considerable scope for using the milestones for sharpening up the business case by identifying (and, if possible, quantifying) the changes in observable parameters;

(5) At the hard end, it seems accepted that value judgement is necessary because not all benefits can be expressed in financial terms.

## Other approaches

How does this seven milestone approach compare with others in the literature? The work on ‘Information Economics’ by Parker and Benson (1988) implicitly uses these progressive stages. It identifies which benefits can be quantified accurately enough to be aggregated before being put into conventional financial assessment methods (for example to identify Net Present Value, or Internal Rate of Return). It assesses separately and places a value judgement on softer benefits. These are added to the equation last.

Thus, eventually a single final figure is reached. That process, although attractive, perhaps pushes the figures too far. A majority of managers believe that there are benefits of IT/IS which simply cannot be handled in that way.

Reimann (1990) develops the topical concept of value management. A central idea is that the external world (particularly the stock market) values a company by its future cash dividends or Cash Flow Return on Investment (CFROI). The company as a whole, or any substantial project within it, must offer a CFROI above the cost of capital, otherwise investment cannot be justified. This approach has implications for corporate culture; it requires managers to think in the way that potential investors will think, rather than in strictly internal management terms.

The issue of IT/IS investment is therefore strongly related to corporate investment culture. Commonly, some 90% of capital investment will be made only on the basis of a financial case identifying Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Payback Period or some other simple measure. This leaves 10% which is available to top management for higher risk, less quantified investment based on their judgement. It is important that enterprises recognize that these two classes of investment exist, and that they apply to IT/IS as well as to other resource areas. It is only then that the idea of a portfolio of IT/IS investments, covering all the generic benefits, can be applied sensibly. Otherwise, sponsors of projects may find themselves compelled to make weak financial cases, or to package proposals so that the softer benefits are achieved as a bonus to projects which make a hard financial case. This situation is recognized particularly by managers from the public sector. It can result in lost opportunity.

A more detailed approach to the problem of portfolio investment is offered by the OTR consulting group and has been reported by Cane (1990). It is called the IT Investment Grid. On one axis the size of the investment is assessed as low or high; on the other the risk of the investment is assessed as low or high. The high/high category is called ‘turnaround’ and would be approved only if it were vital to the future of the company. It is suggested that an entrepreneurial company might have 20% of its investment in this category and 25% in the low/low category. A grid of this kind therefore enables managers to consider their IT/IS investment portfolio and to ensure a reasonable balance in the light of business circumstances.

## Summary

Overall then, we find that at the start of the 1990s Information Management is increasingly accepted as an integral part of general management. The generic benefits of efficiency, effectiveness and edge are recognized. However, organizations are not good at dealing with the wide scope of the corresponding business cases. There is a polarization of practice, between acts of faith and detailed financial justification. The milestones for sharpening up the business case therefore need to be applied and the investment culture defined honestly. This will help to achieve a balanced portfolio of investments which will manage risk rather than ignore opportunity.

## The challenge we face

We now turn to the challenge of the 1990s: why Information Management is likely to become even more important, and why it needs to be an integral part of strategic management.

## The Strategic Approach

Such an approach is often called ‘strategic’. It is concerned with the long term development of the organization as a whole. It is within such a framework that justification of IT/IS will increasingly have to fit. Therefore, it is worth recapping some principles of strategic management. The three levels of management can be related to the time horizon of their respective viewpoints:

(1) Operational management is about the here and now. Timescales are short. To take a nautical analogy, it is about continuing on the present heading (direction of travel);

(2) Tactical management is about mid-term planning. It may involve choosing and following a new heading;

(3) Strategic management is about the long term. It involves the helicopter view (MacMillan, 1986) of the organization and where it is going. In nautical terms, it is about planning the voyage.

The principles of these viewpoints are shown in Figure 7. The three questions at the top of the diagram must be addressed in the order shown. They involve:

(1) A review of the current situation, often as a SWOT (Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats) analysis;

(2) Constructing a vision of where we want the organization to be within a certain number of years;

(3) Identifying, in broad terms, how the organization can achieve its vision.

This is shown pictorially in the centre of the diagram. The lower part shows the 'voyage' in more detail. Critical Success Factors (CSFs) are marker-buoys along the way: things which must be achieved if the voyage as a whole is to be successful. CSFs and final objectives should be quantified and be as specific as possible. Each part of the voyage will then have its own Performance Indicators (PIs) which can be used at the operational and tactical levels of management to ensure success at each stage.

Strategic thinking of this kind is the stock-in-trade of senior general managers. It needs to become the framework for the justification of Information Strategies and of investment in strategic IT/IS. This is perhaps the most pressing challenge of Information Management.

## The response we must make

In this final section of the paper, we identify some guidelines for justifying IT/IS within such a strategic management framework.

## Management matrices

Taking a balanced overview of all types of IT investment and benefit, all within the context of general management, is not a trivial task. Managers have to deal with a wide range of issues, often subjective and elusive. Because the human mind can deal with only a limited number of concepts at once, various aids to management thinking have been developed. Prominent amongst these are two-dimensional diagrams which permit only a few categories for each variable. The majority of such management matrices are 2 x 2, with each variable categorized under either 'high' or 'low'. This gives four possible cases – a convenient number for purposes of classification. An overview of strategy concepts, with an illustration of several types of management matrix, has been given by MacMillan (1986). Perhaps the best-known management matrix is the Market-Share/Market-Growth Matrix called after the Boston Consulting Group (BCG). The four categories of product are known colloquially as Cash Cows, Dogs, Stars and Question Marks. Turner (1990) has described some recent developments in strategic portfolio analysis, including the Mark 2 BCG Matrix which relates size of strategic advantage and the number of ways to achieve such advantage.

The management matrix is thus a familiar and useful tool for the geneal manager. There have been many examples in the field of IT/IS; a thorough review of them has been given by Ward (1988). Recently Peters (1988) has developed a mapping technique to portray IS investment in a way which is meaningful to senior managers. The two axes of his map are the 'focus' and the 'benefit' of the IS investment. By taking a wide range of examples he has been able to quantify the axes more precisely than is usual for management matrices; hence the term 'map'. The Peters Map gives a good overview of current or proposed IS investment, and can be related to broad categories of business strategy to review whether the investment is appropriate (Peters, 1990). However, so far only two broad categories of strategy (cost-focus and differentiation) have been illustrated in this way.

The Y axis also has familiar categories: the three management levels of the typical organization. with Transaction Processing Systems (TPS) and Office Automation (OA) in Cell 1. These were used at the operational level of the organization and gave efficiency benefits – saving clerks and typists, for example. OA systems have gradually spread to the middle and senior levels of the organization, often in the form of local area networks supporting personal computers. However the benefit has (hopefully) been effectiveness: releasing managers' time to be spent in a more productive manner. Thus the broad migration of OA has been from Cell 1 to Cells 5 and 8.

TPS were quickly extended to include report generation and other Information Provision Systems (IPS) for middle management (Cell 5). In the case of a stock control system, for example, IPS would enable middle managers to make better purchasing and replenishment decisions. Gradually, this was formalized into Decision Support Systems (DSS) which incorporated a model of the system being managed, and allowed the user to experiment with 'what-if' type questions. In some cases, such as simple stock replenishment, the decision-making process could then be automated with a Programmed Decision System (PDS). This belongs in Cell 4 because it does make savings of staff at the middle level. Eventually, such systems became incorporated into more up-to-date versions of the TPS in Cell 1.

This cycle on the Benefit-Level Matrix illustrates an important point. Developments which affect higher levels of the business, if successful, will eventually become a routine feature of transaction systems in Cell 1. Cell 1 represents current routine practice for the business. Indirectly it may therefore reflect the current industry norm or some other standard of practice.

A second such cycle is shown where Strategic Information Systems (SIS) have been developed. Most SIS so far have operated at the transaction level, and are therefore in Cell 3. Examples are the American Airlines ticket reservation system SABRE, the

American Hospital Supply on-line ordering system, the McKesson supply system for US pharmacies, the Thomson Holidays TOPS reservation system for travel agents, and the Automobile Association's command and control system. Further information is given by Knight and Silk, 1990. In all these cases it was the novel development or extension of operational level systems which gave a competitive edge. Gradually, some of these SIS became the industry norm. For example Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) was once an innovative system which could give competitive edge by locking suppliers or customers into a unique system. As the benefits of EDI became more widely recognized industry standards (such as TRADANET and EDIFACT) became established, and within the industry they became a routine and essential part of doing business (Cell 1).

These two examples of cycles on the Benefit-Level Matrix illustrate an important lesson for management: strategic advantage from IT/IS is short-lived. Competitors, and the industry in general, catch up quickly. To succeed a business must, therefore, be ready with an encore. This will give sustainable competitive advantage when their earlier initiatives have reverted to Cell 1. In a recent comment on the value of the SABRE reservation system to American Airlines, Hopper (1990) has indicated the ephemeral nature of competitive advantage based on IT/IS. In fact, he sees the competitive advantage being increasingly based on the imaginative use of the system rather than the system itself. The company has therefore been able to sell the software (profitably) without undermining their competitive edge.

The second lesson from the Benefit-Level Matrix is that not all the Cells seem to have been exploited yet. Executive Information Systems (EIS) are top level versions of IPS, in Cell 8, and are intended to make senior decision-making more effective. Knowledge-Based Systems (KBS), in Cell 6, have been used to encapsulate the skills of middle managers and thus give competitive edge. In the finance sector, for example, the quality of decisions may be enhanced by expert systems built up over a period of time by a team of company specialists. Although now in Cell 6, it seems likely that such KBS will eventually follow the route of SIS and become a routine feature of Cell 1.

There are no obvious examples to put in Cells 7 and 9. Why is this? It could be that efficiency and edge benefits at the strategic level are simply not possible. Or it could be that the application of IT/IS, which has permeated bottom up in most organisations, is only now going to have substantial impact at the senior level. Managers need to take a view on this question, and the Benefit-Level Matrix gives a conceptual framework for this. There will be strong pressure to seek these new applications of IT/IS. Gongla et al. (1989) have already described a knowledge-based system which operates at the level of business strategy; it could be placed in Cell 7 or 9 of the matrix.

In summary, the Benefit-Level Matrix is in a format familiar to general managers. The axes relate easily to the structure of the business and the benefits which IT/IS can offer. The nine cells of the Matrix offer fine enough detail to plot the historical development of IT/IS. This highlights both the dynamic nature of the use of IT (and the ephemeral nature of competitive advantage) and directs the manager's thinking to areas which have not yet been fully exploited. It therefore has important advantages as a framework for management thinking.

## Management for benefit

The Benefit-Level Matrix may help to identify strategic and other opportunities for the business use of IT/IS. The seven milestone method may help to sharpen up the business case by which the investment is justified. A final step, which is often overlooked by organizations, is to manage the achievement of the benefits. ## Conclusion

## Key guidelines

This paper has been about taking the business perspective of the value of IT/IS. We can distil the findings into guidelines under four main headings:

(1) Corporate investment culture. The enterprise should recognize the three generic benefits of IT/IS, and the different type of business case which is appropriate for each. IT professionals must help formulate that case in a way which parallels other business investment, within a strategic framework. This may involve packaging proposals, or, better still, a consciously chosen portfolio which balances risk, opportunity, and size of investment.

(2) IT investment assessment techniques. The business case is becoming more important to general managers. They recognize, nevertheless, that the case cannot always be expressed wholly in financial terms. The seven milestone approach proposed here should help IT professionals and general managers to sharpen up the business case without stretching the credibility of the figures. Where value judgement is necessary, that fact should not be obscured by the way the figures are used.

(3) Management of the IT resource. The IT resource needs to be aligned more closely with the business. The topical move to develop hybrid managers who understand the business as well as they understand the technology, is an important contribution to this. The Benefit-Level Matrix, outlined here, can be a useful tool for discussing the overall IT/IS portfolio in a business context; it is both descriptive and heuristic. Other related measures include the decentralization of the IT function, and the cross-posting of senior IT and general managers.

(4) Monitoring IT value for money. The justification of investment in IT/IS must be seen as merely the first step in the process of benefit management. Project, people and benefits must be managed alongside each other. There needs to be a seamless process from the formulation to the implementation and operation of modern IS. Responsibility for this, and the monitoring mechanisms for achieving it, need to be agreed early on.

This paper has reviewed the current state of management practice on the issue of justifying investment in IT/IS, identified the challenge of the 1990s, and offered some tools and guidelines to help IT professionals and general managers to meet that challenge more effectively. IT/IS has no special claim on scarce investment resources. Justification of such investment, in business terms, will be an increasing priority for IT professionals in the 1990s. For that purpose they must speak the language of general management.

## Biographical notes

Dr David Silk MA PhD CEng FIEE read engineering at Cambridge and then did three years' research into the control of communication networks. For two years he was a Research Fellow of Queens' College. As an Engineer Officer in the Royal Air Force, he reached the rank of Group Captain, before leaving in 1984 to join Henley – The Management College. He teaches Information Management on Executive and MBA programmes, and since July 1990 has been Director of Studies of The Senior Course. He is the author of several papers in the areas of telecommunications and of Information Management, and is co-author of a recent book on Information Management.
