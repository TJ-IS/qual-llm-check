# 'Hybrids' – a critical force in the application of information technology in the nineties \*

COLIN PALMER

Chairman of the British Computer Society's Task Group on Hybrid Managers.

Hybrid managers are important. Lots of people say so. We carried out a survey of British Computer Society membership. We received well over 700 replies and 90% of these people rated hybrids as either important or very important. Similarly, in a recent questionnaire sent to members of the NCC's Impact Programme, seven out of eight of the companies asked, said that developing hybrid managers was very important. Virtually all those companies were either already undertaking hybrid manager development schemes or were planning to do so.

So if hybrid managers are so important, what are they and why are we discussing them? The term was coined by Michael Earl, Director of OXIIM at Templeton College, Oxford. It arose as a result of a number of pieces of research that he and his colleagues had been undertaking. They noticed that in all the significant cases of successful implementation of information technology for competitive advantage or for achieving major change in organizations, there seemed to be a person at the heart of the development who displayed certain experience and characteristics. These were: an understanding of the business and what was required within the business, combined with a technical competence that enabled them to understand what was required in technical terms, including the scope of what was being planned. In addition to this, they displayed two types of organizational skills. They knew how to get about the business, and this implied that they knew the business and the people around it well, and they knew how to get things done, possessing a set of excellent social skills — to listen, understand, negotiate and persuade.

It was also clear from these cases that hybrids were not operating in isolation. There were other important roles that were being performed by other people in conjunction with the hybrids. These roles were described as visionary, impresario and specialist. The visionary was normally the leader who conceived a particular idea for the use of IT in the business; it was that person who shared the vision with the hybrid, and the hybrid who took that vision, developed it and put it into action. The impresario was the provider of necessary resources and support to enable the hybrid to deliver what was required; this person was often the Information Systems Director. The specialist was the technical expert who was able to design and develop the specific system.

This issue of hybrids and their importance was picked up by the Strategy Review Panel of the BCS under the chairmanship of Dr John Spackman, Director of Information Systems for British Telecom. Over two weekend conferences, this group identified a number of areas for further investigation as part of an overall initiative within BCS to highlight major issues, not only for the Society but for the community at large in the 1990s. The hybrid manager was very high on their agenda. I was asked to chair a task group to look at this issue and to propose what BCS might do about it. The task group consisted of Dr Robb Wilmot, ex-Chief Executive of ICL and now Chairman of Oasis, Geoffrey Dart, Divisional Director of Food at Marks & Spencer, Ian Glenday, CCS Manager of Esso, John Aebrhard of A Plus, John Handby, Director of IT at the Post Office, and Michael Earl himself. Sue Ottley was seconded to us from ICL as a researcher. We carried out a number of case interviews of well-known hybrids and then put our heads together to produce a report at the very start of 1990. It seemed a good way to start the decade.

The report looked at why hybrids were important, as we quickly came to the conclusion that they were. It also looked at how one might go about developing them and at the benefits and costs to organizations in doing so. We adopted the description of hybrids and their associated roles from Michael Earl's work (Note 1).

The reason why hybrids are important is clear. A number of forces are driving change within organizations. Hybrids play a crucial role in enabling and delivering that change.

Let us look at some of those factors and how they impact (see Figure 1). It is increasingly clear that labour and capital are becoming commodities. In that context information is the prime value-added for organizations and for individuals. Information management is therefore at the very heart of organizational performance or lack of it in the 1990s. To put it another way, if organizations are about information, and managers are information processors, and information systems define people's behaviour, then what we have to do to achieve competitive performance in the 1990s is to re-engineer organizations. (see Figure 2) This is tied up with the compression of corporate hierarchies and the oft observed move to networked organizations. Information management not only enables that but is the source of its effectiveness. Added to this, we have the trend towards globalization, of which Europe 1992 is a subset. Already we experience 24 hour markets in financial services, for instance; the corporation that never sleeps is already with us. Furthermore, the development of technical standards and readily available packages means that organizations can develop and deliver applications much more quickly than in the past. The winners in the 1990s will be the people who understand and harness these forces.

Thus we have a picture of rapid change, of fierce competition in terms of quality, cost and speed to market. This requires integration of activities not simply across functions within business, not simply within the businesses themselves, not within industries, but across industries and across national boundaries.

If change is the essential feature, then those that can enable and deliver change are central to our success.

So how do we develop hybrids? A number of organizations have been working on this over several years. Their experience is worthwhile examining. It is clear that the true hybrid is not simply a person who has worked on the business and the IT side. They also have some particular characteristics (see Figure 3). They do tend to be high calibre people of great energy and curiosity. They are conceptually able, flexible and can cope with ambiguity and indeed conflict. They have a wide social network and are persistent in character. This means that selection is important alongside experience. To this end, BCS with OASiS, a consultancy specializing in the innovative use of IT and the management of change, are developing profiles of hybrid managers against other control groups so as to produce a set of selection tools.

The most successful organizations in developing hybrids are very clear on the importance of selection. They talk of recruiting ‘corporate citizens’ who have a natural aptitude to move across various parts of the company with little difficulty. Often this is done at the initial, probably graduate, recruitment level. In a number of cases, a high percentage (say 50%+) spend the first three years of their career within the IT function before moving to another area of the business.

The main elements of success in developing hybrids seem to be matters of culture and style. There are also one or two important guidelines on action. It does not seem to be enough to transfer people from one area to another for small periods of time – as Michael Earl puts it “six months in another department is better than nothing, though not a lot better” (Note 2). It is a matter of giving people real jobs for three years or more. To achieve this needs significant commitment and involvement from the top, i.e. the chief executive and fellow directors. Without that, as everyone will readily appreciate, it is difficult to persuade individual managers to give up their best person, or rather their best people, and it is equally unlikely that a far-sighted manager offering one of his/her best people to someone else will be believed! One or two organizations have successfully set up an internal recruitment agency where employees are able to seek jobs in other parts of the organization and by-pass the normal management chain. This needs chief executive support to set up, but once established clearly signals to executive and middle management that internal transfer is something to be actively encouraged and sought.

Hybridization is certainly not restricted to the IT function. It is organization-wide. It is equally valid for people to move from line and service functions into IT as it is for people to move in the other direction. BCS's interest in its own constituency of IT specialists has led it to focus on their particular needs. It is also interesting to note in passing that there is a very high degree of frustration amongst IT specialists, who are interested in moving to other parts of the business. Frankly, they find it extremely difficult. That is the reason that many of them move to consultancy firms. The BCS survey highlighted this.

To make the development process work it needs a supportive culture. There are significant risks in moving people to new jobs and their lack of knowledge and experience may be exposed. The organization has to be prepared to take these risks and to support their people through it. Education and training in the transition period helps and this needs to be carefully planned and designed. It is also important for hybrids to receive strong personal support during and after transfer. There are a number of cases quoted of people facing significant difficulties shortly after taking on a new job. These could in themselves be very minor, but were often severely damaging to their confidence, out of all proportion to the incident or issue arising. The paybacks for developing hybrids seem to be very good indeed. Organizations report that they are much better at identifying opportunities to use IT effectively. They are able to manage the change much better and control projects more effectively. Figures are quoted of very high percentages of projects on time and to budget after a number of years of hybrid development schemes. Interestingly, and contrary to expectation, IT departments have experienced significantly lower labour turnover – some as low as 5%. This seems to reflect the fact that the organization is dealing with the career aspirations of its best people, and giving them continuing challenge in their jobs.

There are, of course, also costs (Figure 5). Perhaps the highest cost is the risk of failure not only to the organization but also to the individual concerned. This can be a very significant problem if at the start of a hybrid development scheme one of the first people to undertake a transfer is seen to fail. Few, if any, will follow in their footsteps. There are significant education and training costs, but that is to be expected. What is more difficult to live with is the loss of 'best skills' from a particular department and especially from the IT function, where 'best skills' may be scarce. It is also reported that hybrids may be difficult to live with. An IT specialist who moves into a business area will approach a systems development project in a totally different way to most managers. This is a new experience for any systems department and there is inevitably some conflict. However, the relationship quickly builds into a very real and effective one.

The risks to the individual should not be underestimated. We have talked about the potential loss of confidence. There is also a risk, in unplanned career development environments, for a hybrid to be stuck in a project development role. After one, two or possibly three projects, it is almost inevitable that they will hit a failure. This is damaging in career terms, and good people are often lost from organizations as a result. This is a pity and needs to be taken into account.

Within BCS, we have already done a number of things. We completed our study of individual hybrid cases. In preparing our report, we were significantly helped by a scan of available literature completed by Dr David Skyrme, who was on secondment from Digital to OXIIM (Note 2). On the basis of our report we have raised the profile of the issue through the media. The coverage has been extensive, and highlights the importance of, and interest in this issue. We have carried out a number of presentations at conferences and meetings such as this one. We have already managed to encourage and initiate some research. The selection tools from the profiling exercise should be available before the end of the year. Further research is being done by Dr David Skyrme at Templeton on hybrid programmes. There is also the beginnings of research at Warwick Business School. We are seeking to initiate further research and anyone who is interested in undertaking this should contact Ian Glenday who is managing this area of the initiative for us, through BCS.

There is, however, one very important area that we are supporting to enable individual BCS members and their organizations to develop hybrids. We came to the conclusion that an excellent route for individuals to bridge the career move from their IT specialism, was part-time MBAs. This is a universally recognized qualification, and over 4000 places are available part-time (in many instances company-sponsored) each year within the UK. The BCS is now seeking to encourage this activity and beginning to do some investigation work to define the requirement. It does seem to be the basic MBA, but with some attempt to broaden the skills of the IT specialist. People undertaking such courses will of course be given some level of exemption from BCS membership. We are also beginning work to accredit such courses.

I hope I have summarized the critical need for the development of hybrids and given some guidelines on how this might be done. Any help that can be given to this BCS initiative will not only be gratefully received, but have significant value for UK Limited and indeed Europe Limited in the 1990s.

There is no doubt that we need significant numbers of hybrid managers to be developed. It may be as high as one in three managers in five years time. That is the scale of the challenge. If people are sceptical of the importance of this investment, let me simply leave them with something Rob Willmott said, “if people think education and training is expensive, then they should not think that ignorance is a low cost alternative”.

## Biographical Notes

Colin Palmer, was, until the end of 1989, Deputy Managing Director of Thomson Tour Operations where he was responsible for Information Systems strategy development and implementation. Previously, he was Sales Director and in the early 1980s was responsible for developing TOP — Thomson's very successful on-line reservation system. Currently, in addition to being an independent consultant, Colin is chairman of the British Computer Society task group on hybrid managers.
