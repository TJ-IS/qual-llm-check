# Software product design: an organizing concept for software engineering education

IAN BRAY, ORVILLE JONES and STEPHEN WEBSTER

Department of Computing and Cognition, Dorset Institute, Wallisdown, Poole, Dorset BH12 5BB

Abstract: This paper contributes to the continuing debate on the curriculum for undergraduate software engineering degrees. It describes the rationale for and content of the new honours programme in Software Engineering Management at the Dorset Institute. The well documented problems which persist in the software engineering industry are, we believe, largely attributable to the inappropriate education of software engineers. An alternative approach to the education of undergraduate software engineers is outlined; one which is based upon the concept of 'software as a product'.

## Introduction

One may start constructing an undergraduate curriculum by arguing for the inclusion of particular skills upon the often implicit grounds that, together, such skills represent an identifiable professional quality in the undergraduates. Whilst such an approach may produce a useful ‘tool-box’ of skills it risks inappropriate compartmentalization and lacks any organizing criteria. Indeed, it smacks of the end-game rather than the opening gambit.

We contend that successful engineering requires an holistic view and that the guiding principle behind the design of the curriculum should be the inculcation of a professional ethos which leads students to take such a view. Unless such an ethos is present then the graduate's ability to select from and apply their toolset will be more typical of the technician than the engineer.

The paper also sets out to refute the notion that offering such a holistic view necessarily restricts the depth of the academic study.

## The problem

Software engineering is concerned with meeting the needs of the user community through the delivery of appropriate products. We consider a product to be appropriate if it meets the market's requirements for functionality, reliability, timely delivery, cost etc. Note that appropriateness can only be judged in terms of the market requirement of the particular product, not in isolation.

Despite commercial incentives to conceal such information, the high probability of software engineering projects failing to deliver appropriate products is well known. The reasons are hard to pin down; those commonly profferred include:

\- The complexities of large-scale software engineering

● The difficulty in specifying the products adequately

● Over or under-engineering of solutions

\- Ineffective project management.

Rather than attempt the intractable task of arguing the relative contribution of such factors, we contend that a major underlying cause of most, if not all, is the inappropriate education of most currently practising software engineers.

A rapidly increasing number of 'software engineers' are employed within the economies of most developed nations. Yet the demand for such professionals has not been adequately supported by the availability of undergraduate or graduate programs explicitly aimed at this employment sector. In order to bridge the skills shortage, graduates from traditional Computer Science and Electronic Engineering courses (amongst others) have had to be recruited. Many employers then find it necessary to invest considerable time and effort to adjust the almost exclusively technical perspective of such graduates to one dominated by commercial requirements. In all too many cases, this is not successful, and many graduates remain primarily technical in orientation. The cost to organizations of employees following technical instincts and interests is seen in the lack of concern over users' requirements, 'over-engineering' of products and in the lack of control of the development process, resulting in longer development cycles, budget overruns and, ultimately, a serious mismatch between the product and its market.

To be successful in developing a professional software engineer we believe a radically different and integrating programme of study is required; not simply the modification and renaming of existing traditional courses, which has been witnessed in recent years. (Leventhall, 1987).

## The solution: software engineering management

We argue that a suitable strategy be based on the concept of 'software as a product'; that is to say that all software products must exhibit the qualities appropriate to their market. Our new degree course uses this concept as its organizing focus. The concept is elaborated by concentrating on four derived themes.

## The Conceptual Themes

Design All products are the result of a design process. This theme concerns the nature of the design process as applied to software products and embraces creativity, life-cycle concepts, methods, inter- and intra-method interfaces, and the fundamental techniques and notations required for the design of complex systems.

Quality This concerns the goodness of fit between the product and its intended market. This theme involves the elicitation of measures of quality and the techniques required to ensure quality is realized, including the management of the process through which the desired quality can be engineered.

The context theme This is concerned with the exploration of those facets of design and quality which are appropriate for particular applications. It represents the marriage of theory with practice in particular application domains.

The professionalism theme This is concerned with the development of the individual student's perception of his/her role in the software development process. This component builds towards the graduate's eventual achievement of Chartered Engineer status. Students develop an understanding of the industry along with an understanding of the roles that they may fulfil in team-based software development.

## Relationship between the themes

The BCS/IEE have suggested that design and quality could be central themes within a software engineering curriculum (BCS/IEE, 1989). Our view is that the skills of design and an awareness of quality are only a part of what we need to achieve. An understanding of the context within which these general themes exist must be considered as a theme in its own right. The range of application areas for software development is vast. For this reason we believe that our students need a map of the application areas and an awareness of their own strengths and limitations in these areas.

The software engineer's professionalism is the fusion of the design and quality concepts within a software development context.

The themes form a strong inter-related mesh: for instance, professional attitudes and skills play an essential part in assuring quality, also a poor design is unlikely to exhibit any of the characteristics usually associated with a quality product, and so on.

## Course structure

In addition to a variety of technical issues, our holistic view of the nature of software engineering embraces aspects of management, human factors, the business environment, project management, marketing, accounting, law etc. These components become part of the course as the teaching staff structure the development of the student within the framework provided by the themes. The themes are, therefore, a vehicle for the elaboration of ‘software as a product’, rather than a course structuring mechanism; an explicit ‘hidden curriculum’.

The course is a four year sandwich degree, with the third year being spent in supervised work experience. The first year builds fundamental skills; the second is more concerned with developing systems; the third provides the mechanism by which the students can appreciate those aspects of software development which cannot be replicated within the academic environment. The final year includes individual and group projects and further taught units which examine current issues in software engineering.

## The software development strand

Whilst the inclusion of the other strands is vital to the overall structure of the course, it is software development that forms its backbone. This strand has been shaped by a careful consideration of the knowledge and skills that must be developed; in particular, the role of programming in such a course has been thoroughly scrutinized.

While it might be argued that software engineers spend only a small proportion of their time coding, programming (design and coding) is, quite rightly, seen as an essential element of most computing courses. Software engineers will be creators of code for some time yet and, at its best, programming provides a microcosm of large system development, embodying within a manageable task many of the activities that are required for the production of large-scale systems.

At its worst, however, programming may provide entirely the wrong kind of experience and we argue elsewhere (Bray and Webster, 1990) that the way in which programming is commonly presented to students cannot be immediately accepted into the framework outlined above. A new approach is required.

Our teaching builds on the strengths of recent developments in programming languages for software engineering, in particular the facilities for data abstraction. We seek to establish in the students' minds that the central concept in a programmed solution is the object (or abstract data type). This is a level of abstraction (or two) above the programming language statement, and represents a more appropriate starting point for a software engineer.

The students are taught about high-level design in the following manner. They are presented with a fully working, reasonably complex, executable program. They are then encouraged to use this program and become fully familiar with the way in which it operates. Note that the program is acting as a fully functional specification of itself. Their next task is the production, through group discussion, of a written description of the functionality of the program. These descriptions are reviewed in groups with the aim of producing a better description. The students are then given appropriate sections of the source code to discuss. They receive a number of functionally identical, but internally different, versions to promote further discussion and comparison. Finally they are directed to make changes to the program which enables them to confirm their understanding of how it is working. More ‘traditional’ information regarding syntax etc. can be provided now that the purpose has been established and that examples of good style ‘in the large’ have been absorbed.

Low level design is addressed through the medium of ML and the design and implementation of small functions. The structuring feature of this hands-on section of the course is the definition and creation of abstract data types. The interactive nature of an interpreted environment provides good motivation for the students, while the mathematical simplicity of the language constructs allows a painless introduction to reasoning (formally) about program fragments.

Almost incidentally, the student comes to view the product in two senses, first the executable thing itself must meet its requirement, second the actual texts produced (code and documentation) are for other people to maintain and extend. Thus the emphasis is shifted from a personal battle for mastery over a machine to the creation of a product for others.

## Conclusion

Harlan Mills has said that, 'A degree in software engineering should first of all be an engineering degree, dealing with engineering design and construction' (Mills, 1980). We believe that the 'software as a product' concept gives us a framework within which to meet this criterion. The graduate of this degree will have in-depth understanding of system construction and a grasp of the technical and commercial issues which have to be balanced in the engineering of a product appropriate to its market.
