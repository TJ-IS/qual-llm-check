# Excellence and the IT factor: information technology inside excellent companies in Britain

THIS REPORT IS REPRODUCED BY KIND PERMISSION OF MSA

## Introduction

No in-depth insight has ever been gained into how Britain's successful firms approach information technology (IT).

The aim of the MSA Business Survey was to reveal the policies and attitudes towards IT of some of the best firms operating in Britain. Fundamental questions about the strategic role of IT in their businesses are explored, along with major operational and staffing issues, including: What is the corporate view of IT? How do they find and keep staff? How good are their disaster recovery plans? Do they use IT consultancies? What are their buying policies? What new developments matter to them? Is 1992 an issue?

MSA commissioned one of Britain's foremost research firms, HR&H Consensus, to conduct a qualitative survey based on in-depth interviews with top IT management of ten companies from a range of industry sectors. In addition to the interviews, more information was gleaned from a written questionnaire about the facts and figures of IT, completed by the ten participating firms and a further five companies. From the research emerged common themes, enlightening comments and surprising reactions.

In choosing to link this survey to excellence, a subject of inherent fascination to most of us in business who are working constantly to achieve it, MSA acknowledges the work by Tom Peters and Rob Waterman, whose In Search of Excellence book, and the management thinking that followed it, prompted the idea for the research.

Finally, MSA would like to express its gratitude and thanks to all those individuals who gave time in a busy day to this survey and whose opinions, attributed and non-attributed, are the essence of this report.

## Defining excellence

For the MSA Business Survey, firms were assessed for excellence primarily in terms of their financial performance over the last five years. All the companies are quoted on the London Stock Exchange. The firms chosen fulfilled (at the time of writing) at least two of the following performance and other basic criteria:

\- they have maintained impressive or steady growth in both sales and pre-tax profits for the last five years (allowing for the 1987 slump);

\- they have sustained a steady growth in terms of assets;

• they have a good reputation;

● they are one of the leading firms in their sector.

The selection process excluded companies which had been trading for fewer than five years, privately held firms, conglomerates and holding companies.

A shortlist of 60 firms was compiled across a range of industry sectors, from which came the final ten in-depth and five telephone interviews.

## Key findings

\- Information technology strategy is inextricably linked to business strategy.

\- These companies do not use technology for technology's sake; they are 'early followers' in new technology and not pioneers.

\- Training is crucially important for the operation of the IT group and also to keep scarce staff.

\- IBM is the natural choice for most.

\- Networking and distributed processing are of major and increasing importance.

\- External consultancies are viewed as facilitators and not a strategic or management resource.

\- Future IT planning is dependent on business planning and high-level management involvement.

\- Two to three years are maximum realistic planning horizons.

\- Ability to cope with growth necessitates system and software flexibility.

\- IBM's System Application Architecture is a key concept.

\- 1992 is not a major issue.

## Information technology and the business

## Strategic importance of IT

The overriding theme emerging from virtually all the companies is the increasingly strategic role being played by information technology (IT), with its significance changing as top management becomes more aware of its benefits. Several described their IT operations as 'state-of-the-art', 'very high profile'. Two stated categorically that their businesses depended on it: 'we depend on technology' (ICI); 'IT and the business are one' (SA). Two of the respondents were in industry sectors where technology has been slow to arrive, but they both had very positive things to say about their own company's use of it, one saying that IT was 'becoming a plank (of the business)' (UN1).

All the IT managers, bar one, reported to a board director, in eight of the cases that person being the financial director. Only one company had designated a director of computing. Two reported to managing directors.

When asked in the factual questionnaire to summarise the IT policy in a sentence, replies from the 15 included:

\- cost effective information technology support for the business;

\- to develop an IT strategy linked to our business strategy;

● delivery of effective solutions for the business;

\- emphasise the bottom line profitability of the company through the use of IT;

• provide solutions to real business problems;

\- delivery of systems which support all business functions and enable significant competitive advantage;

\- deliver effective solutions to business needs;

\- support the strategic objectives of the group;

An interesting indication of the significance of IT in the company is the number of senior managers who have workstations: in two-thirds of the 15 companies surveyed, most or some senior management had terminals or PCs on their desks.

## Long v. short term view

An underlying theme throughout the survey – which was clearly a source of irritation or frustration to certain respondents – concerned long-term investment versus immediate payback on IT. Although IT was positioned as fundamental, there was still the tendency to view it tactically: 'senior directors . . . know they have to have it [IT] in order to be . . . able to . . . drive the costs of the business, but they don't really understand the opportunities for deriving benefit' (AS); another said that 'most people are looking at a more practical level, just to get their processes sorted out' (GM); a third that 'it slows things down because people are looking for an immediate payback and invariably . . . IT applications [offer] in the long term more strategic benefits than short-term financial ones' (UN2).

## Autonomy and control

How much rein do the excellent firms give to IT operations, strategically and tactically? Freedom rather than restriction emerged very strongly in the research. As the IT department receives recognition as a business-orientated, mature and responsible operation, so control is withdrawn enabling the departments to devise their own strategic and tactical solutions to business technology issues. The words autonomy and control came up often, as 'head office' tried to give freedom to operating companies or groups whilst maintaining direction. Many were emphatic about the importance of autonomous responsibility: 'the operating companies have the responsibility to deliver; they can prepare their own plans, taking into account group requirements, but they have got to deliver' (GM); 'the division has total responsibility for performance' (TQP); 'a cultural organisation such as ours encourages individual initiatives, with a very loose central direction' (ICI). One said that they would be exercising increasing control, but 'by persuasion rather than edict' (AR). Another highlighted the importance of central control, but in terms of strategy; 'the key thing is to have central control of the total IT resource ... you can devolve responsibility ... but it's all orchestrated to the total plan' (AS).

## Leaders or followers in technology?

Are these excellent firms leaders or followers in their use of technology? They want to adopt new technology swiftly but avoid being guinea pigs: more than half commented that they were followers – but early followers. IT is about meeting business needs, rather than technology for technology's sake. One commented wryly: 'I would like that we were followers, but unfortunately we keep finding that we are leaders' (ICI). Some were pioneering: 'we were the first people to install DB2 in 1984 . . . we use very current and recent developments' (SA). Many returned to discussing this issue when discussing recruitment: it is clear that being an 'early adopter' of new technology is an important factor in retaining staff for several of the companies.

## Competitive advantage

Surprisingly, competitive advantage was hardly mentioned explicitly in relation to IT although it was implicit in many of the comments made in the survey. The clearest statement that emerged was a company that recognises 'that we have to use technology in order to secure competitive advantage for the future' (EM).

Did competitor activity in IT have an influence? Companies seemed reasonably aware of the IT activities of their closest competitors and it seemed that action by one prompted moves by others in the technology-for-business race. 'I think we are aware. We are considering a study of competitors' use of IT (GM); 'things happening in our industry are well written up and there's quite a lot of interchange . . . we use it to our advantage' (AR); 'we are fairly active in most of the areas that will give us the information we require' (PG); 'In every possible way - it's amazing how people will talk' (AS); 'it is very important . . . we get understanding in other ways [than asking directly] . . . we can get that' (SA). One respondent made two interesting comments: 'markets are changing to such an extent, your competitor might not actually be the person that you see as your competitor' and 'certainly suppliers . . . keep us posted on how far we are behind or not' (UN1).

## People

## Staff background

Boffins are not on the shopping list for these firms. This section of survey investigated the general policy towards staff, particularly whether academic qualifications counted. Only one firm stated that they took on staff with specific technical and scientific degrees.

All but one of the remainder emphasised that they looked for experience, not 'boffins'. The one remaining said that the intake was always a mixture. Arts graduates should not assume that a career in a state-of-the-art IT department is out of the question: 'one of the best guys I ever had was a classicist' (AS). The words 'practical, experienced and delivery' came up often. Several made the point that business understanding was important, one saying that: 'we are seriously looking at taking people in the industry and training them in IT, rather . . . than doing it the other way round. The IT becomes more business related.' (UN2).

Two-thirds of the companies had an IT manager in his forties. There were no women interviewees. The youngest was in the 25–29 age bracket. Most had been in the position for less than five years, the average being just over three years. All but one had been with the firm longer than four years, with two having more than 20 years' service behind them. The average length of employment was over eight years.

Experience at work was the major qualification for the IT managers of the firms: only three of the 15 had academic backgrounds, specifying these as 'an NCC systems analysis course', 'analysis and methodology' courses and 'computing as part of an engineering degree'.

## Characters

Are typical IT staff a bunch of techies talking jargon? Not in these firms. The keyword in most of the replies was 'pro-active', and 'creative' was often mentioned. Although several respondents said that their IT people were a complete mix, there was evidently the belief that departments flourished because people took the initiative. One cautioned letting proactivity and creativity go too far: ' . . . because the technology can enable you to do so many things nowadays that it'd be frightening if you let too many people have their head too often.' (UN1).

Another couldn't answer the question about general character of staff: 'if I knew, it would make recruitment much easier!' (UN2). Only one firm said it had mostly 'technology centred' people, another that they have in their mix 'beards and sandals . . . who we don't let out' (AS).

Some specific comments about the type of person at the head of the operation emerged: 'they have to love change . . . then be a good communicator. People think IT is a young man's game. I actually think quite a few people who like change are quite old'. (UN1) Another major firm highlighted a significant problem resulting from the introduction of non-IT management at senior levels: ' . . . blocking the career paths of the IT professional'. (ICI)

When asked to characterize people in IT compared with those in other departments, two-thirds of the 15 termed them 'somewhat more backstage' but more than half saw them nevertheless as 'more dynamic' and 'more up-to-date' as well as younger. Two thirds also saw themselves and their colleagues as a group apart from the rest, disagreeing with the proposition that they might be very similar in character to their peers elsewhere in the firm.

## Keeping people and finding new staff

Turnover in skilled staff – ‘nomadic’ as one interviewee put it – and the well-established fact of severe shortages of good people have the potential to give the excellent firms major problems, but it was in this area that some of the most enthusiastic and confident responses came. Almost all the answers to the question of keeping people lay out clearly the positive reasons why the issue is not a problem to these firms: ‘we provide an open, challenging and stimulating environment’ (GM); ‘we are creating the environment where they want to stay and see things through – we haven’t had the problem of IT staff disappearing’ (TQP); ‘we manage them carefully and train at the right times, then you retain a higher proportion and our experience shows that’ (AR); ‘good leadership, confidence . . . they understand the business’ (AS); ‘. . . create the type of environment where they want to work and stay’.

Several firms mentioned maintaining a technological edge as a major retainer of staff: 'people move where the state-of-the-art is, and we . . . have the latest available technology which tends to hold people' (PG); 'the introduction of new technology can actually help us to retain good staff (EM). Offering a structured career came up in several instances. Only one firm said explicitly that being a success as a firm played a part in keeping people but again this fact came out implicitly at many points throughout the interviews. Salary was mentioned as important but not the key. Several felt that good training was a significant contributor to keeping staff turnover low.

The reliance on the graduate intake was marked: almost every firm mentioned their graduate recruitment policy, some ahead of all else. Several confirmed that younger recruits from schools or through YTS were also important. Four said they were looking for general abilities rather than science degrees or actual computer science qualifications, and one said that being good at English was more important than anything else: 'if they can't communicate they're not really going to be much good' (AR); another commented 'I never go with a fixed view [that I] must have someone with a particular qualification, it's really the person that we consider is best to do that job' (PG); a third 'we are looking for basic ability . . . people who have management abilities with drive and initiative' (ICI).

Quantifying the level of staff departures amongst the 15 showed it to be on average 7% for the last year. On average, staff numbers rose by over 16%, a net 9% growth overall. Those joining, totalling 178, came primarily from outside (122), with 27 graduate trainees and 29 transfers from within the company itself.

What encourages people to stay? Job satisfaction was the clear motivator cited as the key reason by 13 of the 15. The right environment followed and involvement and resultant loyalty came next, along with career prospects on an equal footing. Only then does salary appear, in fifth most important position.

When employing new staff, the 15 were looking for IT experience first, followed closely by a high level of self-motivation, the right track record and special skills.

## Training

Some of the most forthright comments came out of this area of questioning. Training is clearly crucial and investment of time and money is heavy. Interestingly, no company made any references to staff turnover as a reason for a heavy training burden. One of the strongest themes was the requirement for good business skills that these people must have in the current and future working environment, as opposed to simply technical and managerial skills.

The interviewees were unequivocal about the seriousness of training: 'Training is strategic . . . a great emphasis placed on training . . . the company is extremely forward thinking in its training' (AR); 'we train all the time at all levels' (TQP); 'we have a very strong policy on training . . . [it is] crucial to the successful operation of IT' (EM); 'training is one of the main things we can [use to] generate loyalty' (AS); 'we invest very heavily in technical and development skills' (SA).

Two companies had identified how important it was for IT people to be ‘trained’ to communicate with people in the rest of the business: ‘it is very important that they are good managers and good communicators as well as being good technically’ (EM); ‘you’ve got to train the IT people to be able to talk to the business people’ (UN1).

Quantifying the significance of training, 12 agreed with the statement that training was 'a central activity crucial to the division' and 11 that 'training is an important tool for the promotion of staff. Training budgets averaged 4.4% of IT budget, at just under £120,000 per year. Of the 15, 11 submitted figures for the annual number of 'person days' spent in training, averaging 440. The average time spent per person in training was 5.7 days.

## The IT operations

## Structure and interaction

How do the excellent firms organise their IT? Along classical lines, with large operations and development departments in almost all cases. These two departments just form the basis for complex IT structures, however; most respondents also named between two and six further separate 'departments' covering areas such as communications, equipment planning,

distribution and CIM.

One respondent made an emphatic point about having development and operations people together: 'we're much better these days at getting the operations side involved at the earliest possible stage for any new project . . . it's very important to have the whole lot under one roof (AS).

Staff numbers in the departments averaged 77, with the largest two topping 270.

The most recent hardware budget ranged from £200,000 to £15 million. Six firms spent between £200,000 and £500,000; two spent £1 million, one £2 million, two £10 million and one £15 million. Three of the 15 firms did not answer the question.

## Hardware

Three familiar letters cropped up time and time again. IBM was the easy choice for the majority. As one put it: 'I did not want to spend a lot of my time or other people's evaluating alternative scenarios . . . it was much easier to make the commitment to IBM architecture and then concentrate on the business issues' (SA); 'there's only one company that actually offers medium and large-size computers and that's IBM.' Many pointed out that IBM was not a 100 per cent supplier to their firm. DEC was mentioned by four firms, one only for technical work. ICL was the supplier to three of the companies, two of them were happy but the third about to change. One made a comment about a continuing issue between suppliers and their customers, that 'the technology is developing so fast they [suppliers] want to introduce it quickly [which] encourages companies to make technology changes fast which . . . disrupts the ability to deliver business solutions' (GM).

PC strategy was mentioned by a few of the interviewees – or rather in the context of a lack of a strategy. Most seemed to give a fairly free rein and a variety of models of PC featured.

One firm, and only one, looked at things from the software perspective when asked about hardware: 'it is the software that is going to be dictating the way we go' (TQP).

When asked about the primary hardware systems, ten were centralised, mainframe based operations and five decentralised on minis, with one moving from centralised to decentralised.

Three firms had mid-range systems as secondary to the primary mainframe.

The main suppliers were IBM (8), ICL (3), DEC (1 primary, 2 secondary to IBM), Hewlett-Packard (1 primary, 1 secondary to IBM), with two firms not specifying.

Nine specified PCs as significant in the set up and when asked to describe the current PC population and the growth of PCs, 11 said they were 'reasonably widespread' and the remaining four that PCs had 'some presence'; 11 said that the number of PCs were growing quickly, the remaining four 'fairly slowly'.

## Software

Major in-house development is still important to this group. When asked about the company strategy in software, packages were established but by no means dominant. In the package v. in-house debate packages were feasible if they closely met the need, but almost all the firms were also undertaking in-house development as a matter of course: 'there are large amounts of money going into development' (GM). The benefits of packages as speedy solutions were obviously mentioned, but firms also cautioned against the 'instant solution' belief: 'you have to be careful with packages . . . it's a myth to suggest that they are necessarily a quick solution' (AR). One firm 'had concerns about the changes that happen with software developments with . . . new releases continually coming out' (GM).

Several firms had standardised group-wide on a particular software supplier, including MSA. The requirement to tailor was mentioned by several respondents and the need to integrate with existing systems.

In-house development – as opposed to maintenance, which took up a large chunk of resources – met specifics such as accommodating acquisitions, interfaces and integration of systems as well as unique applications.

Six of the 11 firms who specified their software suppliers used from three to six different mainframe package suppliers.

Nine said they frequently developed software in-house, two sometimes, three occasionally and only one not at all. Selling their own developed software to other companies was not big business for these firms: 12 did not, two only to a small extent and one 'to some extent'.

## Networking

All but one of the companies had invested in networking, some heavily. 'It's increasingly become impossible to run the business without the network' (AS); 'we have to be highly efficient and highly effective, the network is [therefore] absolutely crucial' (SA); 'crucial at all stages' (UN2). Communications as well as computing networking was frequently mentioned.

Ten companies in the factual questionnaire termed networking ‘extensive or partial’, five little or none.

Four of the 15 IT managers questioned were personally on-line to their companies from home and five said that other staff – primarily support personnel – had home computer links, with only two companies appearing in both groups.

## Restraints on purchasing

Questions in this area drew answers of two distinct types: policy restraint and budgetary restraint.

How tight is the policy control on buying technology in these firms? The autonomy mentioned as a philosophical point becomes manifest in the way that many of the companies operate their buying policies. Guidelines and policies are set, but if managers make a case, then going beyond those guidelines and policies is accepted, with the knowledge and agreement of 'head office': 'we have the right sort of relationships and understanding [that] if someone is looking outside those guidelines they will come and talk to us and explain the reasons why . . . it could be that we could get a group deal . . . learn from [it] and get a financial benefit' (GM). Another, distinctly expressed policy, mentioned it very specifically in terms of dependence on particular software suppliers: 'we like a mix to spread the risk' (EM). Another, in contrast said succinctly that: 'all the hardware is purchased by direct control. The software is left entirely to the individual businesses' (ICI). Hardware was also controlled through the capital budget, except PCs, which 'I think . . . miss the net' (UN1).

When restraint was couched in budgetary terms, autonomy again was apparent, although one said that 'departments were given absolute autonomy to spend their budgets . . . as long as the IT policy setters agree it will do the job' (GM). One firm had totally flexible IT budgeting: if the need arose, budgets would be reviewed.

Companies were questioned on how autonomous other company departments were in buying IT hardware themselves. Half allowed no purchasing by other departments and six only peripherals and small pieces of equipment.

Restraints on purchasing software were less stringent: eight of the firms allowed other departments to buy software to a greater or lesser extent, although seven still allowed no purchasing outside the IT operation.

## External consultancies

In all but two cases, these firms view external consultancies as facilitators and not a strategic or management resource. Several state that they have high calibre internal management to set strategy and policy. In essence, consultants are expensive – to be used when resources are stretched, when the company is entering an entirely new area where there is no existing internal knowledge, or to carry out specific studies; 'caution' sums up the interviewees approach to external resources. 'Used [consultancies] when we had particular problems and wanted some options put to us. We [in IT] were not felt to have the expertise by senior management – though we have and could have made the recommendations and saved the consultancy costs!' One of the two strong advocators of consultancy said 'there was no way we could have [replaced every system] under our own steam [in four years]' (AS).

None of the 15 said they never or frequently used consultancies; 11 used them ‘at times’ and the remaining four ‘rarely’. Seven said the reasons they used consultancies were for specialist work, six to make up a resources of skills shortfall, and only two for strategic purposes.

## Back-up and disaster recovery

Only one firm said that things were ‘hopelessly inadequate’ in this area, but surprisingly three firms had done little in the direction of disaster recovery, feeling that high cost and unlikely critical need for such facilities made the arrangements unnecessary; ‘We have to try and establish the point at which it becomes critical and balance spending the money against the inconvenience and financial loss of not having the system’ (UN2).

Generally and predictably, the more critical IT was to the business, the more sophisticated the back-up and disaster recovery arrangements: 'if we lost both computer rooms, the company would be dead' (AS). Most of the firms in this category backed up regularly (daily and some weekly) or ran the system with a second system in parallel, on and off site, and disaster recovery had been planned and catered for carefully. In two cases, this was elaborate. The most impressive involved turning the car park into a computer centre using pre-fab buildings and sub-car park cabling, 'a portable recovery centre' (AS) within three days. Many mentioned the maximum downtime: 24–72 hours to recover with a 'major hit', and having one or more data centres on other sites provided the necessary safeguard in most instances. One firm had involved an outside disaster recovery organisation.

## The future

## Coping with growth

Good planning and system designs that are highly flexible, leaving room for manoeuvre, summarises the responses in this area. 'We are taking a four year look so that there are not many surprises' (GM); 'it is quite simple – most of the systems are expandable. The IT department didn't grow at the same rate as the business, it didn't need to, the systems could cope.' (TQP)

Staff are also the key to keeping a structure in place under pressure and during phases of growth. The quality of the people in charge counted enormously: 'a team, watching each other and helping, leaders in their own right and surrounding themselves with good management.' (AS)

Using consultants in growth periods was mentioned by several: 'peaks should be coped with by bringing in people from outside' (UN1).

The pressure on departments to deliver was implicit throughout the interviews, and it was stated clearly by two respondents in this section: 'we certainly have times when we're under enormous pressure' (UN2); 'we have a continuous 30 to $40\%$ per annum growth in demand . . . we have never seen any downturn in demand for resource . . . our problems in the last decade have been actually taking projects away' (ICI).

## Identifying future needs

The majority consensus was to build IT plans from the business plan, further evidence of the significance of technology to the mainstream operation. 'I produce the group strategy for IT which is a distinctive addition to the business strategy' (ICI); 'you can't have an IT strategy unless you've actually written up your business strategy' (AS); 'we have an information systems strategy that comes out of the business strategy' (UN2).

Two companies approached the subject from the user viewpoint, through regular meetings or a formal survey.

The need to have high level involvement in the company by the IT directorate emerged: 'we have systems directors in the operating companies that sit on their respective board' (GM); 'I now have the chief executive chairing [the systems strategy group]' (AS); 'my job is much easier because I have a group board representative who understands and supports IT... and understands my policy [depends upon] a good business reason to do it' (UN2).

Asked how far ahead they planned, the majority saw two to three years as the realistic maximum. Budgets were obviously set on an annual cycle. 'We used to talk in five and ten-year horizons, but we found that [beyond three] was so much of a projection it had little credibility. A three-year horizon is much more meaningful in our business' (AR); 'I have a broad outlook, reaching into the second year. I wouldn't ever pretend to go any further than that. The environment just doesn't allow it.' (AS). Another was going the opposite way: '[We have a] two year look [but] the objective with the IT strategy is to give us something that will operate for ten years'. (TQP)

## Setting priorities

Priorities were generally agreed at operations manager level with higher involvement if necessary. Again, autonomy came out clearly and often priorities were business driven and hence reactive: 'My priorities have been swamped in the last two years very much by the [need to] put in new distribution centres' (AS). By contrast another stated, 'we set out priorities as those which would affect the business the most' (SA).

## Future technology needs

These were completely varied as they were generally industry specific, including teleshopping, logistics, information provision and customer service technology. Others answered the question more philosophically: 'what we are discussing for IT currently is how to use most effectively the staff we have currently got to affect the future' (TQP) and 'the first challenge is to make the investment in all the businesses who need IT' (UN1).

One worry was about future expenditure on software: 'I think we have to worry a lot about the future in software terms, because the expenditure in the future is going to be software based'; another was on the increasing power and storage of PCs which was not being harnessed: 'how do you secure that data?' (AR)

One foresaw a need to become workstation-based and have much more distributed data management, 'moving away from mainly central large mainframe systems . . . to a balance between those major repositories of data and more de-centralised use of IT at workstations' (GM).

## New developments

Reactions to questions of new developments were often referred back to previously stated aims of not being on the leading edge. The interviewees were also high-level management and response in terms of detailed technical knowledge was not expected. However, for the IBM-supplied firms, SAA was of major significance: 'in terms of overall concept, SAA is good, but in terms of the products we wouldn't necessarily use all of them' (GM); 'the concept (SAA) is absolutely desirable'. Databases were mentioned by three respondents, and networking by a third, CASE by another.

## 1992

Issue – what issue? 1992 does not figure largely as an issue for nine of the ten firms. As one put it: 'I was on a conference [for] two days . . . my conclusion was that information management in a borderless Europe is going to be much the same as information in a bordered Europe . . . maybe 1992 is accelerating the need [for co-ordinated responses] . . . we don't see [1992] as a major issue' (GM); 'I'm getting somewhat concerned that we're all pretending 1992 is . . . going to be very significant, but we haven't got a bloody clue what it means . . . if you ask me, 1995 is going to be the big headache, because by that time things will have happened . . . it will be really serious' (AR); 'I've got to blandly say no [to implications of 1992] . . . I've attended various workshops and unless we're missing something enormous, my conclusion . . . is that there is no impact on IT . . . I might be totally wrong' (AS); 'there is nothing special about 1992 in terms of our strategy' (ICI); 'Business is the driver of IT, so what do they think of it [is the question] . . . I do not think that IT offers any restrictions . . .

if they exist, [they] are that we haven't got the business vision in the UK that we should have' (UN1).

The one exception in the group is the company saying that 'they have spent a week with [new European partners] developing our thoughts both from a corporate and an IT point of view . . . I think we are leading in considering what Europe will mean in the future' (SA).

## Appendix 2

## MSA (Management Science America) Ltd

MSA is a leading supplier of IBM mainframe and minicomputer software with more than 7,500 customers using around 24,500 software systems in 61 countries. The company was founded in 1963 in Atlanta, Georgia, with the international division and UK company being established in 1987.

In the UK some 350 customers, many of them Times Top 500 companies, are using MSA solutions which are targeted at financial services, manufacturing, public sector, retail and healthcare.

The software applications offered by MSA are designed to help companies get the maximum return on their personnel and hardware investment. MSA has a full line of applications for financial management, human resources, manufacturing and production control, materials and distribution management.

The company is committed to delivering IBM Systems application architecture-compliant software (SAA) which facilitates the portability of computer systems across IBM hardware platforms. On average, MSA reinvests 20% of its worldwide revenue in research and development.

As well as supplying software, MSA supports its customers by providing consulting, education, customisation and project management services.
