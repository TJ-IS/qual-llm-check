# Global information systems: a joint venture between business and education

MAHMOUD M. YASIN and RONALD F. GREEN

Department of Management and Marketing, College of Business, East Tennessee State University, Tennessee, USA.

Abstract: The expanding role of computers and information processing in today's global business environment makes it imperative that business students receive the appropriate computer education. This article evaluates the role of business schools in preparing students to meet the challenges of the global information age. Business and academic perspectives on computers and information processing and their influence on computer education in the business school are presented. The authors conclude that the general lack of enforceable standards in computer business education is a global concern, affecting businesses, educational institutions, and computer hardware and software vendors alike. Recommendations for the globalization of computer education in business schools are provided.

## Introduction

Recent developments in the utilization of computer technology has resulted in a more efficient flow of information, both within and between business organizations. A concept as basic as that of electronic mail has reduced the cost of sending messages by as much as 80 % when compared to traditional means (Reiss and Dolan, 1989). Computers in business not only reduce costs, but also enhance the overall effectiveness of the communication process. For example, point of origin computerized inventory control systems result in one error for every 10 000 transactions, where as non-computerized systems normally result in one error for every 1000 transactions (Laudon and Laudon, 1988).

In manufacturing environments, computer-aided design (CAD) systems reduce the cost of product design as well as increasing the engineer's ability to modify the product design in order to meet changing consumer demand. CAD systems are used extensively in the automotive industry with impressive results. In addition, automobile manufacturers utilize on-line computer systems to link with suppliers in order to facilitate the exchange of information. This is particularly useful in view of the current emphasis by firms such as General Motors and Toyota on the just-in-time delivery of raw materials and component parts.

While seen as a tremendous opportunity for businesses to improve in terms of productivity and performance, this technological growth has also been associated with a special challenge to the globalization of businesses and industries. Improved methods of collecting, analyzing, and disseminating information results in tremendous potential for the proliferation of business activity in an expanded global setting. This is particularly important in light of the role of international players in today's dynamic business environment. The threat of foreign competition, coupled with very lucrative and relatively untapped foreign markets, has forced businesses to assume an international perspective. This includes the development and utilization of sophisticated global information systems which promote efficient operations in this dynamic environment (Tomlin, 1988; Dyson, 1989; Mead, 1990).

This article examines the importance of computers and management information systems within the global business environment. Of particular concern is the role of the business school in preparing students to function in this setting. Inconsistencies in computer education programmes nationally and internationally, are discussed, and recommendations for the development of a global orientation towards computer education in business schools are offered.

## Computer needs in a global business environment

The global business environment consists of a wide variety of firms with numerous approaches to competition. While many firms, such as those from the petroleum industry, follow true global strategies with tremendous resource commitments, others pursue international opportunities through exporting, product licensing, joint ventures, and strategic alliances. Regardless of the extent of the participation, businesses operating in a global business environment are faced with a myriad of problems. These problems stem from differences in cultural values, political systems, financial systems, and languages, to name just a few, and affect all businesses which compete on a scale globally.

Transborder data flow is a significant problem for multinational firms, particularly those with headquarters in the United States and subsidiaries in Europe or Canada. Canadian and European laws restrict the ability of subsidiaries to transfer certain types of data, especially personal data, to centralized databases maintained by the parent firm located in the US. While these laws are designed to protect legitimate interests, they result in inefficiencies in terms of the overall administration of centralized databases in multinational settings (Samiee, 1984; Chandran, Phatak, and Sambaharya, 1987).

Another problem concerns the ability of multinational firms to transfer computer applications that have helped to sustain a competitive advantage in domestic markets, subsidiaries or operating units in other countries. This problem can be attributed primarily to the existence of incompatible computer information systems (MIS Quarterly, 1986) and represents a considerable obstacle to international firms as they attempt to create synergies. In today's global business environment, creating a sustainable competitive advantage requires the efficient use of many tools such as robotics, computer-aided design, and database technology. European firms are considered to be more advanced in terms of automation, while US firms have the advantage with regards to database technology (Freeman, 1985). Due to restrictions in the flow of information and incompatible technology, firms with multinational operations may find that different subsidiaries may possess tools of competitive advantage that cannot be made available to the entire organization.

## Computer information systems: a common ground

An important element in establishing a common ground to assist in overcoming these problems is the development of globally compatible computer information systems consisting of a group of similar sub-systems. The hardware sub-system should consist of machines of a similar make, i.e. IBM or be able to emulate one another. This leads to similar character representation and data read/write capabilities, as well as compatible central processing units. The procedure sub-system should be designed so that the same software and rules are used, regardless of the user. Finally, the human element portion of the system must be compatible in that programmers, system analysts, and database administrators should have similar backgrounds and should have received comparable training. This should facilitate the communication process, both between specialists, and between specialists and end-users throughout the system.

Global business would benefit greatly if firms operating in different cultures and with different languages could communicate through compatible information systems (IS). IS is a broad term that includes day-to-day transactional processing systems (TPS) such as inventory control and accounting related systems, as well as strategic decision-making tools such as decisions support (DSS) and executive support systems (ESS), and political intelligence systems that gather and analyse political information. Political information includes any information that may alert the multinational company to anticipate political changes. International firms operating in Iran before the revolution would have benefited greatly from such an information gathering system. More recently, American Telephone and Telegraph (AT&T) used such a system to assist in the safe evacuation of its employees from Kuwait during the Iraqi invasion of August, 1990.

Examples of successful cooperation in establishing common information systems can be seen in the joint ventures between companies in the automobile industry where different components are manufactured and then assembled in different countries. This success would never have been possible without compatible information systems to facilitate standardization and coordination of multi-country operations. Several US banks with international operations have implemented global communication networks to exchange operational and market data between their international branches (Eom, 1990). Texaco developed a decision support system (DSS), Omega, to aid in the process of gasoline blending. Due to its success and its compatibility with other systems, Texaco licensed it for use in other refineries around the world (DeWitt, et al, 1989). Unfortunately, while these examples represent the tremendous potential for firms able to develop globally compatible information systems, they are not indicative of global businesses in general.

The importance of a compatible IS for global business is underscored when considering the differences in time zones, operating hours, work weeks, and holidays that multinational companies must face. Consider the firm based in the US which must conduct business in Middle Eastern countries such as Saudi Arabia. Cultural differences notwithstanding, the US firm must be prepared to conduct business outside of normal business hours in the US. To ensure this, a sophisticated network of multi-level computing devices must be in place to communicate even in the absence of human operators. A properly designed TPS with access to a current database is necessary in order to maintain customer order and inventory status files, as well as work-in-progress files. Without complete and current information, the marketing effort around the globe will be severely hindered. The problem is compounded when one considers that the TPS feeds information to decision support systems that help determine resource allocations and executive support systems used for strategic planning.

While the development of compatible computer information systems and protocols for information exchange is an on-going process, it should be noted that this can be successful only if managers are properly trained in the development and use of such systems. The question that must be addressed concerns the type and extent of computer education that must be provided in business schools. Unfortunately, this varies greatly, both domestically and internationally, restricting the development and ultimate usefulness of compatible computer information systems. This inconsistency in computer education can be attributed to both rapidly changing technology and the lack of agreement as to what constitutes appropriate computer training for business graduates. While the following discussion addresses inconsistencies identified within business schools in the US, it is anticipated that these same concerns are global and warrant further discussion. This is particularly important since many countries look to the US as a leader for computer related technology and related training and education programmes.

## The global challenge in computer business education

There are several interrelated problems in the development of curricula to ensure the proper computer education for the business student. These problems centre around differences in technological sophistication, both between and within countries, and differences in attitudes towards what constitutes a minimum level of basic computer education for all business students. These problems are interrelated in that the level of technological sophistication helps determine attitudes on appropriate computer education.

## Stages of computerization

Internationally, business information technology and related computer education may vary greatly depending upon the technological sophistication of the nation being considered. Developing countries such as Portugal tend to have a mainframe orientation in training business students, using third generation programming languages (3GLS). This is attributed to a lack of resources needed to upgrade to a more modern personal computing system, rather than to a fundamental disagreement as to the appropriate orientation to be followed. Other countries, such as Saudi Arabia, have the resources necessary to install microcomputer systems which emphasize fourth generation programming languages (4GLS), but lack the strategic systems planning to insure the purchase of compatible hardware and software. A contributing factor is the lack of internally developed computer technology. All computer hardware and software must be purchased from outside vendors, with purchase decisions often made on the basis of cost and service, rather than compatibility.

These differences in technological orientation between different countries are not surprising given the inconsistencies found within business schools in the US. These schools have been described (Yasin, Green and Wafa, 1989) as having evolved through different stages with regard to computerization of the business curriculum. These stages ranged from the random accumulation of hardware and software, without regard to compatibility, to the use of systems such as computer integrated manufacturing (CIM) to teach students the role of computers in managing the total business enterprise (Table 1). Since most countries around the globe lag behind the US in terms of computer technology and education, it is not surprising that business schools of less developed countries would typically fall within stage I and II.

## Defining computer literacy

Other inconsistencies identified in business schools in the US centre around the establishment of a common definition of computer literacy and the development of computer related programs of study. While some schools may equate computer literacy on the part of business students to a survey course in computers as used in business, others interpret it as an understanding of the intricacies of software, hardware, and system design within the context of business information systems. Other schools feel that students become computer literate through experiences with packaged software such as spreadsheets and word processing, while some extend literacy to include skills in fourth generation programming languages (4GLS) such as Dbase III Plus and Focus. These differences have been directly attributed to the various stages of business school development in terms of computerization efforts, a situation that is felt to be even more severe in countries less developed than the United States.

Another area of inconsistency identified is differences in relative emphasis on managerial versus technical orientations within the various computer-related programs of study. Some business schools offer degrees in management information systems (MIS) with primary emphasis on business and organizational studies and secondary emphasis on technical computer studies. Other business schools offer degrees in computer information systems (CIS) with more emphasis on the technical and less on the managerial aspects of business. Other schools offer degrees that fall somewhere between the two extremes, yet may take on a variety of names. The differences in the degrees are significant and may vary from one educational institution to another (Carland et al., 1988). The business community is confronted with students whose qualitative versus quantitative skills may vary widely across educational institutions. The confusion with regard to computer education in terms of the nature and extent of courses and programmes of study should be of great importance to the business community. Not only do businesses represent sources of demand for the students being trained in business schools, but also act as suppliers of the technology around which the training evolves. At a minimum, the business community must become involved by providing input as to their expectations with regards to the training given to prospective employees, whether they be general business students or specialists with MIS or CIS degrees.

## Conclusions and recommendations

Today's global business environment is faced with a myriad of challenges arising from the global manufacture and design of products, the need for instantaneous international communications, and the development of sophisticated technologies such as CAD, CAM and CIM. These challenges also place tremendous demands on business schools to provide students with the necessary skills and tools in order to perform in this highly automated global environment. Business schools around the world must re-orient their general education and computer education curriculum in order to meet these demands.

While the task of standardizing educational programmes to promote the development of globally compatible computer information systems may never be achieved, all concerned must work to make computer education more uniform and useful. The following general directions and specific recommendations are provided:

(1) The business and educational communities must reach a consensus concerning a definition for computer literacy. This is necessary so that all business students can be assured of having, at a minimum, the level of computer education necessary to become computer literate. Computer literacy in a global business context can be defined as the working knowledge of basic hardware, software, telecommunication technology, and networking. This should include an understanding of basic information systems designed to increase efficiency, such as transactional processing systems (TPS), and strategic systems such as International Decision Support Systems (IDSS) and Expert Systems (IES). Such systems tend to be culture sensitive (Eom, et al., 1987/1988) and must be carefully designed for international use.

(2) The nature of the introductory MIS course normally required of all business students must be established. This course could be taught as

(i) a computer literacy course where the basics of hardware and software are presented;

(ii) as a third generation language (3GLS) programming course; or

(iii) as a DSS/ES course using 4GLS.

The alternative that best serves the business community of the nation being considered should be chosen. The first two alternatives seem to be best for developing nations with a mainframe orientation. As technology improves and business schools upgrade resources, then the third alternative becomes more realistic. Global uniformity in this area will not be possible until all nations are comparable in terms of resources and technologies.

(3) Programmes of study must be delineated in terms of basic orientation. This will require that the various disciplines within the academic community establish responsibilities for teaching each of the required courses and emplanation of quantitative versus qualitative requirements for each of the various programmes. Students trying to select the right programme of study offered by different institutions currently have very little specific information on which to base their decisions. The global business community also needs assistance when matching job requirements with the educational training of applicants from different schools and from different countries.

The experiences of business schools in the United States should assist in the development of globally compatible business school computer curricula. Yet, to achieve this would require a tremendous amount of coordination and cooperation. The following list provides guidance concerning how an integration of efforts could result in a more globally oriented computer education for those pursuing careers in the business community:

(1) Business schools should stress the international dimension throughout the curriculum due to the current trend of the globalization of business. Emphasis should be placed on the development of courses, such as those stressing the design and utilization of international decision support systems, that reflect the needs of the international business community.

(2) Educational programmes that facilitate the mobility of business students and educators must be established. These programmes could be modelled after the educational programmes designed by the European Economic Community 1992 for students and faculty. These programs allow for the movement of students and faculty between member academic institutions.

(3) Accords must be reached allowing for the free exchange of electronic information between countries. Involvement by an international organization such as the United Nations may be required.

(4) The frequency of international conferences dealing with topics relating to international information systems and the establishment of global perspective on education must be increased. The exchange of information and ideas will facilitate the development of international decision support systems and expert systems and will encourage research in these areas.

(5) The computer industry must sponsor programmes and conferences to encourage standardization of computer technology where possible. Standard operating systems and the ability for different systems to communicate with one another will allow for the development of globally compatible information systems.

(6) Business sponsored training centres must be established. These centres will be used to supplement business school training for students and to provide continuing education for employees. This will be necessary to keep up with developments in sophisticated computer technology that should result. Business schools will be hard pressed to provide this service due to the costs involved.

It should be realized that achieving the objective of establishing globally compatible information systems is similar to hitting a moving target. Changing technology requires a continual updating of expectations as the business and educational communities join with the computer industry to coordinate efforts. The challenge may be great, but the potential rewards are even greater.
