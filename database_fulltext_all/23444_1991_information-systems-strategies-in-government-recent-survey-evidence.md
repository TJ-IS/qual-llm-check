# Information systems strategies in government: recent survey evidence

NIGEL BACON

Principal Consultant, Hoskyns Group plc, London

Abstract: Twenty-three government organizations, which had or were developing Information Systems (IS) or Information Technology (IT) strategies; were interviewed in most cases the scope was the whole organization. Many internal reasons led to the development of these strategies but external factors were also often present. Management structures were similar and tended to follow CCTA guidelines. The need to 'sell' strategies was widely understood and regular reviews were planned. Strategies most frequently covered an implementation period of 5 years. In most cases, business objectives drove the definition of information requirements; and business issues included the need to improve efficiency, the impact of legislative change and problems caused by the creation of executive agencies. There was a widespread move towards Open Systems and security was tending to become a departmental issue. Expenditure on IT was frequently about 5–7 % of programme expenditure. Departments were reluctant to discuss the value of intangible benefits. Steady progress was being made with the implementation of strategies being subject to various problems and constraints. Lessons learnt included the needs for clear business goals, senior management involvement, managing users' expectations and selling strategies. Departments are moving slowly in the right direction and may be expected to match the best practice in the private sector within a few years.

## Introduction

## Background

This article is the outcome of a survey carried out by the Government Division of Hoskyns Group plc during the Summer of 1990 into the current state of development and implementation of Information Systems (IS) strategies in central government organizations. The survey was sponsored by CCTA and follows a study into IT Strategies in government undertaken by Hoskyns in 1988; the results of the earlier study were published to participating departments.

There is currently a great deal of interest in IS strategies as organizations in both the public and the private sectors are increasingly realizing that information systems can be of vital assistance in achieving an organization's aims and objectives. CCTA, in particular, is encouraging government departments to develop business-led IS strategies and has published guidelines to assist those departments that have decided to do so.

## The sample

Twenty-three government organizations of varying size and structure, and at various stages of IS or IT strategy development and implementation, assisted in the study by providing information on how they set about developing and implementing their strategies, and the impact of those strategies on their departments. The chart in Figure 1 indicates the spread of organizational characteristics covered by the survey sample. For comparative purposes, five organizations outside central government, three in the private sector and two local authorities, also agreed to participate in the survey and provided similar information.

Although the participating central government organizations are referred to throughout this article as 'departments', they included several non-departmental bodies and a number of executive agencies. The other participating organizations, broadly defined as 'non-central governmental bodies', are abbreviated to NCGBs.

## Methodology

Interviews were conducted, wherever possible and with appropriate senior personnel on both the user and IT sides of the departments. There were 16 interviews with senior users and 21 interviews with directors or heads of IT functions, the primary interviewees often being accompanied by supporting staff. A questionnaire was sent to all participating departments for completion before the interview in order to establish certain basic information: 18 completed questionnaires were received.

Questioning at the interviews followed standard lists of topics, circulated in advance, but there was considerable scope for wider discussion. In eleven instances departments kindly made available summaries of their strategies or other supporting information for reference and in amplification of what was said at the interviews.

## Summary

All the organizations that participated in the study had or were developing Information Systems (IS) or Information Technology (IT) strategies and in nearly all cases their scope was the whole organization. Their general appreciation of the reasons for having such strategies was supported by a combination of the following specific reasons:

(1) The need to use IS to support the achievement of their business objectives;

(2) Perception of the need for a strategy by key personnel;

(3) Perception that the current use of IT was unsatisfactory;

(4) External factors such as CCTA or Treasury pressure.

A third of the departments, but none of the NCGBs, had carried out Scoping Studies and most departments (19) had followed CCTA's guidelines for the development of strategies. These guidelines had been used pragmatically and had been found to be very helpful. Most departments had IS or IT Strategy Steering Committees which reported to the Management Board (or equivalent) and had IT Steering Groups or IT Executive Committees reporting to them. User involvement was substantial. This management structure followed CCTA's recommendations closely and was also frequently found in the NCGBs.

Many departments had taken steps to 'sell' their strategies to their staff and the need to do this was widely appreciated. Most departments expected to review their strategies regularly, often as part of their business planning cycles, and had allocated staff (either full- or part-time) to maintain them. IS strategies were owned either by the department or the Steering Committee, never by the IT function. Strategies were usually developed for implementation over a period of about 5 years.

Almost all departments stated that business objectives had been the force behind the definition of information requirements from which their IS strategies were derived although in some instances there had been problems in formulating clear business objectives. IS strategies would, in a number of cases, enable both departments and NCGBs to take advantage of new business opportunities but those opportunities had not been identified as a result of the strategy development process.

Organizations with several business areas most commonly had an IS strategy for each business area, possibly with a corporate strategy for headquarters.

Many business issues were mentioned by departments; the following are worth highlighting:

(1) The need to improve such matters as efficiency, effectiveness, quality, customer service;

(2) The impact of legislative change;

(3) The problems caused by the creation of executive agencies — for both the agency and its sponsoring department;

(4) A desire to regain control of the business from the IT function.

When discussing technical issues we found that there appears to be a concerted move towards relational databases supporting SQL and Open Systems and to OSI. Several departments stated that procurement would be software-led, made possible by their commitment to Open Systems. In relation to security, the use of CRAMM was frequently mentioned though we found that reactions to it were mixed. Most significantly, security and risk management seemed to be becoming departmental rather than IT issues.

Many departments provided budgetary information which showed that a third of them expected to spend between 5 % and 7 % of their total PES bid over the next 4 years on IT — we believe that this compares well with the private sector. A fifth of departments planned to spend less than 2 % and a quarter planned to spend more than 15 % of their total PES bids on IT. Although projected costs were well known there seemed to be a reluctance to discuss benefits which were frequently regarded as intangible. The whole question of evaluating potential intangible benefits and of subsequently verifying their attainment is an area where further work is required.

Departments with strategies in place generally thought that steady progress was being made but did not provide quantified assessments to support this. Various problems and constraints had arisen of which the following were most common:

(1) Shortage of IT resources;

(2) Management of users' expectations;

(3) Technical and standard matters;

(4) Shortage of finance;

(5) Departments' ability to absorb change.

Departments, in expecting to achieve benefits such as greater productivity, streamlined work practices or staff savings, anticipated some impact on departmental organization outside the IT function. The latter, in particular, had frequently been reorganized as a result of developing the IT or IS strategy. In several cases implementation of the strategy required additional numbers of IT staff.

Lessons learnt by departments as a result of developing their strategies are:

(1) Clear business aims and objectives are necessary;

(2) Senior management must drive the strategies;

(3) Users' expectations must be managed;

(4) Strategies must be 'sold' to more junior staff;

(5) Care must be taken not to underestimate the effort required.

Comparison with the findings of the 1988 study shows a wider perception of the need for IS and IT strategies; a closer link between strategies and business aims and objectives; the replacement of IT strategies by IS strategies; and an increased awareness of the need to monitor and review strategies regularly.

In conclusion, we believe that if departments continue to move effectively in their present direction the use of IT in central government will match the best of the private sector by the turn of the century. In terms of developing strategies the government already matches best practice in the private sector.

## Why have an information systems strategy?

This section considers the reasons for developing an IS strategy, how many departments had IS and IT strategies and the scope of those strategies. For the purpose of clarity it is important to establish the difference and relationship between IS and IT strategies. An Information Systems (IS) strategy, defined more fully in the CCTA guide 'Strategic Planning for Information Systems', encompasses the definition and agreement of the following:

(1) Information and process requirements to support the achievement of business aims and objectives;

(2) Information systems (both IT and paper-based) to meet the information requirements;

(3) Management and technical policies to support the development and operation of the information systems.

In the above context an Information Technology (IT) strategy should thus provide the IT systems and infrastructure needed to support the IS strategy. In some cases, however, IT strategies are produced by IT people in isolation, without consideration of business objectives or involvement of users. During the course of our interviews we found a spectrum of strategies varying between a full IS strategy and an IT strategy produced in isolation, with very few at either extreme. IS strategies tended to focus on information that could be provided by IT (ignoring registry systems, for example) while IT strategies frequently had a measure of user involvement. For the purpose of this article we have accepted at face value the departments' assertions as to whether they had IS or IT strategies.

## Motivation and rationale

Most government departments are now highly dependent upon information systems and the IT that supports them. IS can greatly improve effectiveness and efficiency provided that information systems are planned and used properly (see the CCTA guide referenced above). To achieve this, information systems must be fully co-ordinated with business and policy requirements; if they are not properly planned they can limit effectiveness and constrain policy options.

In addition to these general reasons, we found that a large number of specific motives were identified as leading to the development of IS and IT strategies, with each department identifying two or three contributing factors. Most frequently the reasons given for developing a strategy for the departments were a mixture of the following:

(1) Appreciation of the need to use IS to support the achievement of business objectives;

(2) Perception of the need for a strategy by a key person (e.g. the Chief Executive) or a number of senior persons or the general appreciation that the development of a strategy was appropriate and desirable;

(3) Perception that the current use of IT was unsatisfactory in some way and needed to be improved;

(4) External factors such as pressure applied by Treasury or CCTA, the impact of new legislation or the need to respond to externally imposed restructuring.

A detailed analysis is shown in Figure 2.

A good example of the need for external pressure to be applied in some cases is provided in an extreme form by the department which stated that the development of an IS strategy had been ‘on the cards’ since the early 1980s and that they had developed one when they did (2 years ago) because of Treasury pressure. It can be inferred that the development of IS strategies in government would be far less advanced if pressure from the Treasury and CCTA had not been applied. The factors in Figure 2 correspond broadly with those identified by the NCGBs. However, the commercial organizations mentioned two additional reasons for developing strategies: (1) to support business growth; and (2) to reduce costs. No single event causes an organization to develop an IS or an IT strategy, although there is little doubt that one person with authority and good timing can influence the organization in that direction successfully.

Before departments formalized their strategies, many IT systems had been introduced in government, ranging from very good to very bad — just as in many other sectors of the economy. Analysis of past success or failure was hard to apply to the immediate future because the organization had changed and so had its work and environment. In particular, the scope of IT had changed. If nothing else, the impact of the DHSS Operational Strategy was to cause envy and alarm in other departments that saw serious long-term investment in IT leading to a major change in the work and staffing of that department. Put crudely, IT money going to one large department perhaps meant less IT money for other departments.

Meanwhile, management consultancies were becoming effective in promoting the benefits of strategic planning, in parallel with emerging and revolutionary ministerial views on the future of the civil service size and responsibilities. By 1982/83 plans for strategies were underway in many departments. Any reluctance to initiate strategies for IS or IT was simply overcome by the Treasury requiring any case for significant IT expenditure to be reference to the departmental strategy.

One consequence of this intervention was to delay some IT projects that had not been thought through sufficiently. The two major effects were first, to encourage IT awareness at higher levels in departments because of the proliferation of incompatible systems that did not treat data as a common resource — some of these systems due for replacement might replicate their isolation simply because that could show the best return on investment. Second, if departments were indeed to be reviewed as government policy indicated, then the use to which IT investment and time were committed should be well-understood at an appropriate level.

The CCTA guide, referred to above was published after most departments had initiated IS or IT strategy development. Prior to that, departments were expected to justify the cost of developing such strategies themselves.

## Numbers and scope

All the organizations interviewed had either IS or IT strategies. Fifteen of the departments had IS strategies and eight had IT strategies; of the latter, three were developing IS strategies. Two of the NCGBs had IS strategies and three had IT strategies; of the latter, one was developing an IS strategy. Figure 3 illustrates these findings. There is a strong trend, more in government than in the private sector, towards the development of IS strategies.

It should be noted that some organizations, particularly NCGBs, had problems such as a variety of hardware and software suppliers, a variety of systems, often overlapping facilities, and a number of computer centres. In these circumstances it can be argued that a necessary first step is to formulate and implement a coherent IT strategy, albeit with user involvement, before proceeding to develop an IS strategy. The scope of the strategies in virtually all cases covered the whole department.

Of the 23 departments interviewed, 21 had strategies covering the whole organization, and one of the remaining two expressed the intention to extend their strategy to the remainder of their department should the need arise. Thirteen of the departments were regarded as having a federal structure and, of these, 11 had conducted, or were conducting, separate strategy studies for each of their component business areas. Three of the NCGBs interviewed had a federal structure and one of them had conducted separate IS strategy studies for its component business areas.

## Developing the strategy

## Approach to developing strategies

Eight of the departments had carried out Scoping Studies, often with the participation of CCTA's Consultancy Service. No such studies had been carried out by the NCGBs. Nineteen of the departments had followed the CCTA guidelines, fully or in part. In many cases, not all of the guidelines were followed, and, of course, not all were appropriate to the development of IT, as opposed to IS strategies. Further, they were generally taken (correctly) as true guidelines rather than prescriptive techniques. Thus, there was a range of comments such as '... useful but not followed in detail ...', '... followed by and large ...', '... much influenced by ...', '... used with modifications ...' and '... used pragmatically ...'. The overall conclusion is that the departments found the guidelines extremely helpful and that they used them pragmatically, modifying them as circumstances showed to be appropriate. The NCGBs did not use the guidelines.

Consultants were used by 18 departments, not only in Scoping Studies but in developing the strategies where they were most often used as members of departmental teams. Three of the NCGBs made similar use of consultants. Carrying out a Scoping Study, as recommended by CCTA, is a clear benefit in focusing minds on the work ahead. If it is not done as a separate task, then it is likely to require time during the start period of the strategy development itself. A amjor part of this study is to identify the effort and time required of the strategists. Other benefits of the Scoping Study are to support the strategy champion in selling, to make others aware of the impending demands on them and to initiate the intra-departmental PR that will improve the input to the strategists.

## Management structure

Seventeen departments had either IS or IT Strategy Steering Committees, of which 13 reported to the Management Boards, or their equivalent. The committees' responsibilities were to direct the formulation of, or oversee the maintenance of, the strategies. In some instances, there were steering groups for individual business areas which reported to the main strategy steering committee. Figure 4 illustrates the structure.

The IT or IS Strategy Steering Committees mainly comprised senior users plus the heads of IT and, frequently, CCTA representatives. Below these committees were frequently IT Steering Groups or IT Executive Committees, sometimes having user representatives, and below these were Project Boards. Within the NCGBs interviewed, there were two instances where there was a structure similar to that found in the departments for controlling the development and implementation of IS strategies. One instance was where users were involved only in setting priorities within IT and another where an IT strategy had been produced with no user involvement. Where IS strategies were being developed or were in place, departments predominantly involved users and management at a high level and in the manner recommended by CCTA. NCGBs appeared to adopt a broadly similar structure. PROMPT or PRINCE project management methodologies were widely used by the departments.

The composition and use of a Steering Committee must be a decision of the organization at the top level, or the level to which the part of the organization undergoing the strategy development reports. The most common problem is to ensure that its members do reflect views through the span of their command and do communicate downwards while the development is in progress. This is particularly important for ‘user representatives’ since they are likely to be far removed from the individuals collecting or using data, and even more so from those delivering output service to the public.

## Selling the strategies

The need to 'sell' IS and IT strategies was appreciated by many departments and they were, on the whole, sold from the top. Standard presentation and marketing techniques were used including

(1) Roadshows to take the strategy round the department (two departments);

(2) Formal and informal presentations of either the complete strategy or significant aspects of it (two departments);

(3) Workshops and seminars to discuss aspects of the strategy (two departments).

Two of the departments had gone to the trouble of publishing abstracts of their strategies and distributing them to their staff thus giving them a reference point within the strategy. Two published regular newsletters.

The techniques mentioned above seem to have been successful and only two of the departments seem to have had other than minor problems. The NCGBs surveyed have used similar techniques to sell their strategies. One problem encountered by both departments and NCGBs was that when a strategy had to be 'sold up' to more senior management, or to business users when converting an IT strategy to an IS strategy, there were sometimes problems in convincing the senior management and business users of the need for the strategy (perhaps in the latter case a lack of 'business credibility'). When selling the strategy 'down' it was sometimes felt that the strategy was at too high a level for the users. In some cases the active selling of the strategy took place as a result of pressure for more detailed information from within the department itself.

It may be that the need to promulgate and sell strategies, and to do so in a form which can be readily understood by more junior staff, should be more widely appreciated; both the strategy itself and progress with the implementation need to be explained and communicated to all appropriate levels of staff. This is particularly important where an IT project is itself a major plank in a department's overall strategy. It is public knowledge that the Inland Revenue was particularly effective in this respect with COP, while the DHSS had difficulties with their Operational Strategy as did the Home Office with the Passport Office project. Overall, there has been increasing attention paid to this aspect by departments and by executive agencies.

Private sector organizations have in the past been worse in some cases – Sock Shop is an example – and better in others. It is now recognised that significant shortcomings in this area can put the whole business at risk. 'Selling up' is still regarded with more diffidence in the public sector. There are good examples of this working well, but it may well need to be more widely undertaken.

## Monitoring and review

Nineteen of the departments expected to review their strategies regularly, often as part of an annual business planning cycle, and 15 had allocated staff (either full-or part-time) to maintain them. This follows CCTA's recommendations. Reviews fell into three distinct categories:

(1) Complete strategic reviews which were usually intended to be carried out at about three-yearly intervals;

(2) Formal review of strategic progress against the original objectives, usually intended to be carried out annually;

(3) Informal reviews conducted at intermediate points.

In most cases, again, the management structures created by departments in order to control the development of the strategies remained, or were expected to remain, in place with responsibility for monitoring and review.

There were similarities between the departments and the NCGBs but the latter were somewhat less likely to have made arrangements for monitoring and review. Regular and careful monitoring and review are clearly essential in order to keep strategies up-to-date and fully aligned, if they are IS strategies, with possible changing business aims and objectives.

## Ownership

The strategies were uniformly regarded as belonging either to the IS or IT Steering Committee or to the organization as a whole. Similarly, data and systems were uniformly regarded as belonging to users. The days are clearly passing when IT strategies, data and systems were regarded as belonging to the IT function. This is a crucial ingredient for the successful use of IT. Ownership is not a simple concept since it carries the connotation of 'for richer, for poorer; for better, for worse; in sickness and in health'. While ownership may be foisted on someone, ideally an IS strategy should be adopted willingly and enthusiastically. This is more likely to happen as a consequence of monitoring and review proving that a realistic and constructive strategy is developed. It is likely to help the person responsible for IT (if there is one in the organization) if the strategy is owned by another, or others.

## Methods and techniques

Overall, it seems that a wide variety of approaches and techniques were used. Eight of the departments mentioned specific techniques but their comments were often of the type ‘. . . critical success factors were used...’. One proprietary methodology was mentioned on four occasions and two others were mentioned once. Only one out of five NCGBs used (partially) a proprietary methodology. Techniques can be very useful in analyzing information and provoking thought. It is surprising that only a third of the departments admitted to using them in developing their strategies. As the use of such techniques is usually a key component of a consultant’s ‘tool bag’ their application may have been transparent to clients.

## Strategic issues

## Business goals

Of the 15 departments which had IS strategies, 13 stated that business goals drove information requirements from which their IS strategies were derived. This is in accordance with the generally recognised method of producing such a strategy and with the CCTA guidelines. Three departments with only IT strategies also stated that business goals drove their IT strategies. Three departments mentioned problems that they had had in formulating their business objectives and there were a further three instances where business objectives were clarified as a direct result of pressure arising from IS strategy studies.

Within the NCGBs the picture was less definitive but, nonetheless, business goals were often important factors influencing the formulation of their strategies. Clearly defined business aims and objectives, well understood by senior management, are obviously an essential precursor to the development of an effective IS strategy.

A relationship between an organization's information requirements and its business goals or aims and objectives is clearly desirable, but it is not always obvious. Clear business goals could be:

(1) Collect 60 % of liable community charge within 6 months of issuing demands;

(2) Answer 70 % of written PQs within 24 hours;

(3) Close 12 locations in the next year and re-locate as many staff as possible.

While information to measure progress in achieving any of those goals could clearly be sought, rather more consideration might be needed to find out how progress was being made and how it might be speeded. So the information required is not obvious.

On the other hand to reduce accidents on present motorway junctions may be a woolly aim, but there is doubtless a lot of knowledge about the information requirement that is relevant to an IS strategy. Generally, where the business aims are a continuation or extension of previous aims then the IS strategies have a good base-line. They must always be prepared, however, for changes in the environment in which the organization operates.

## Business opportunities

Six of the departments commented on the identification of new business opportunities. All of them stated that their strategy studies were not a factor in identifying those opportunities but that implementation of their strategies would enable them to achieve these opportunities. Of these departments, five of them mentioned selling data, i.e. the sale of information held by the organization, as a business opportunity. Only the departments mentioned the possibility of extending their services or of marketing new ones.

Within the NCGBs, two mentioned the creation of new products or services but, again, these would be facilitated by the strategy rather than being attributable to the strategy development process. It would be surprising if the effort required of the strategists in thinking through and discussing options, in the course of developing an IS strategy, did not produce some new business opportunities. Consideration and evaluation of these must, of course, be distinct from the strategy thinking. It may subsequently test the flexibility of the strategy if such opportunities are seized.

## Application priorities

All the departments and NCGBs had prioritized their applications with the assistance of users (in one instance this was the only input by users to the production of an IT strategy). In two instances there were difficulties in agreeing and establishing priorities because of competing demands from different business areas. IT departments clearly found it helpful to have agreed priorities for application development.

Simply to list a string of computer-based application systems to be developed or re-developed, with some words wrapped around to say why project 4 should precede project 5, is an abnegation of the task of producing an IT strategy. There will need to be an IT development plan, particularly where there is a shortage of necessary resources, and business areas are competing for them. The IT strategy should argue the case for the proper allocation of resources between previously competing projects as part of the strategic resolution of those priorities. If the IT strategy is published and greeted with cries of dissent, then the strategists have failed either to close discussions or, perhaps, to have some painful decisions made while strategy development was in progress.

## Multiple business areas

Thirteen of the departments interviewed had multiple business areas, each of which could be regarded as a distinct business. In most cases each of these areas had its own strategy — usually an IS strategy. Five of these departments had, or were developing, corporate IS strategies for headquarters covering such matters as

(1) Information for ministers;

(2) Information necessary to manage themselves;

(3) Information necessary to manage their component business areas.

They typically had an underpinning IT infrastructure and the overall structure is illustrated in model A of Figure 7. ## Executive agencies

Agencies, of which five contributed to the survey, are single businesses, sharply focused on a business area, and given, at their creation, clearly defined aims and objectives contained in a framework document agreed with their parent department. All had a business plan. They thus have an easier task than many departments in developing an IS strategy, fully in support of their aims and objectives, which they most commonly review and revise as part of their annual planning cycle.

Executive agencies justify a separate study — and they will no doubt be subjected to many reviews, enquiries, examinations and audits from every organization imaginable. At the date of writing, some 34 agencies have been created with more to come, and one or two already winding down. At present, those we met were fully occupied with running their own businesses. One future area of interest will be the IS between individual agencies and their parent departments. These will no doubt show wide variances.

## Business issues

The business issues mentioned by departments were very broad but the following points are worth highlighting.

(1) Most of the business issues were fundamental to the business and were stated in terms of:

(i) Efficiency;

(ii) Effectiveness;

(iii) Productivity;

(iv) Customer service;

(v) Quality;

(vi) Competitiveness.

(2) Legislative change and its impact on the organization was mentioned by five departments. This is an influence which cannot be predicted in the medium to long-term and can have a substantial effect upon strategies that are in the course of implementation.

(3) The creation of executive agencies has had a marked effect on both the body becoming an agency and its sponsoring department. The former have had, in particular, to change their methods of accounting and the latter have had to make changes in order to manage the new relationships with their agencies.

(4) Regaining control of the business from the IT function was mentioned by three departments. In these departments the business had been led by the IT function in pursuance of IT strategic goals. There had been general recognition of the unsatisfactory nature of this situation and the development of an IS strategy was being used to move control back to the business users.

Many points arise under this heading that might justify lengthy discussion. It is still stated frequently that the solution to a business problem requiring an IT-based project must achieve ‘optimum efficiency and optimum effectiveness at minimum cost’. When an organization declares itself moribund and ignores the outside world, this might be an objective to be considered. Meanwhile, no organization can ignore changes outside its control that are almost certain to arise but whose nature may be impossible to predict. Even legislative change initiated by the organization may have consequences that are difficult to foresee. A more cynical view is that whoever writes words such as those quoted above is certain to move elsewhere before the project is completed!

One comment notable by its absence was the intention to improve job satisfaction, despite the fact that this can lead directly to improved delivery of service and better productivity. A second absent business-related issue is any reference to seeking competitive advantage. Perhaps departments see no opportunity for this. From outside government, there is a view that departments do indeed seek competitive advantage in terms of size, authority and finance compared with other departments.

## Technical issues

A variety of technical issues were raised, of which the most common were:

(1) Relational databases/SQL;

(2) Open Systems;

(3) Networking/OSI/use of GDN;

(4) PCs/terminals/workstations.

Four departments were decentralizing their IT functions and moving to a distributed provision of services. Fourteen departments were moving towards relational databases supporting SQL and Open Systems (POSIX) (recommended in the CCTA guidelines as procurement standards). Several departments specifically mentioned that procurement would be software-led, an approach made possible by their commitment to Open Systems. Thirteen departments also mentioned the use of OSI as a networking standard (again mentioned in the CCTA guidelines) but it is surprising to find that none mentioned GOSIP.

Although discussed as issues by the departments interviewed, it seems that the above were strategic objectives rather than issues; in other words, the issues had already been decided in favour of these objectives. What was seen as an issue, however, was the use of the Government Data Network (GDN). Four departments were still to decide on its use and some had already decided not to use it. Cost seemed to have been an important factor in this decision. The use of CASE tools was mentioned by only two departments.

IT Strategies in government clearly indicate a preference for Open Systems as a means, presumably, to establish a commonality of access to data by various application systems and their users. An Open Systems platform is not necessary for this. More likely is a determination to avoid being bound to a hardware manufacturer's software. Whether or not the alternative of being bound to a software supplier's 4GE is more attractive in the long term has yet to be proved.

## Security

We discussed security with eleven departments and found that CRAMM was used or planned to be used by seven. Reactions to CRAMM were mixed. A number of problems were identified which included a statement that its use leads to over-secure systems. Another problem was that considerable effort was necessary to conduct such an assessment and that a high level of detailed information about the department was required. One department commented on the ‘unintelligible output’.

Most significantly, security and risk management seem to be becoming more important to departments as a whole. They are increasingly seen as matters for departmental policy rather than as purely technical issues within the IT world. Several of the departments interviewed discussed security plans or the incorporation of security as part of the IS strategy. Security is not often a major plank of an IT strategy. Security of data, security of access control, and system reliability are all relevant issues. Where the first two of these are taken seriously, little information is made available. Achieving the third can be very expensive indeed and the general view seems to be that government is prepared to underwrite the cost of any incident.

## Impact of the strategies

Although we found that in most cases implementation of the strategies had not started or had not been long underway, it is possible to consider some aspects of their likely impact.

## Costs

The departments were invited to provide information on their total PES bids (excluding programme expenditure) and their IT PES bids for the 4 years 1990/91 to 1993/94. The figures were averaged for the years covered and expenditure on IT was expressed as a percentage of the total. ## Benefits

Of the participants in the survey only 12 identified impact on their business as a result of implementing a strategy. Those that did not were either involved in the process of formulating a strategy, or had only just begun its implementation and, therefore, felt that it was too early to comment on what benefits might arise.

The benefits that were expected to arise as a result of implementing IS strategies can be broadly categorised as

(1) The streamlining of work practices;

(2) Better productivity;

(3) Greater use and better perception of IT;

(4) Reduced staffing levels;

(5) More readily accessible information.

We found, however, that most departments were reluctant to discuss the value of the benefits they expected and tended to digress into the difficulties of placing values on intangible benefits in advance and of verifying their subsequent achievement. They were clearly unhappy, in many cases, with regard to the value of benefits set out in the business cases for their

strategies.

Related to the question of benefits is the use of performance indicators, but there was little positive response on this subject either. Few departments stated that they had performance indicators or intended to put in place the mechanisms by which appropriate information could be collected. There is recognition that measurement of the success of the strategies will be very difficult because of the lack of performance indicators for large areas of business where the benefits are intangible.

The questions of developing performance indicators and of valuing benefits are very important matters for the successful use of IT. It is an area where more work is necessary within the government and one which we understand CCTA has in hand. A point worth making is that if the department is not seen to worry about this, then perhaps someone else will. Our impression is that executive agencies are generally more alert to this need and are better placed to react.

## Progress to date

Most departments were unable to provide quantified assessments of the progress of their strategies. In many cases this was because their strategies had been approved only within the last 2 years or because they had recently converted from an IT strategy to an IS strategy. We gained the overall impression, however, that those departments with strategies in place were making steady progress towards their strategic objectives although in some cases over-optimistic plans had had to be revised.

It seems that progress on earlier IT strategies was not reviewed when IS strategies were developed. Progress (and the achievement of benefits) clearly needs careful monitoring. This ties in with the previous comment. There is a natural tendency to heave a sigh of relief when a strategy and an associated business plan are finally accepted and endorsed. The view that real progress is being made — even if it is hard to measure at present — is an encouraging outcome of this survey.

## Problems and constraints

The problems and constraints experienced by the departments varied greatly, but we found some common themes. These are as follows:

(1) Human resources: eleven departments mentioned problems in this area. These embraced the shortage of IT staff generally; the shortage of experienced IT staff; the lack of sufficient users' time to specify requirements; and staff recruitment and retention problems.

(2) Management of expectations: in all, five departments mentioned problems which resulted from the lack of effective management of users' expectations. When the strategic systems which were first implemented had long timescales, users could gain the impression that 'nothing was happening'. In one instance, users expected things to happen without their involvement.

(3) Technical and standards: seven departments had problems relating to technical issues or standards, all of them different. One department stated that the commitment of a major supplier to OSI was questionable.

(4) Finance: as might be expected, shortage of financial resources was commonly reported — five departments mentioned problems in this respect.

(5) The pace of change: some departments commented that their ability to absorb change was a significant factor in determining the speed of implementation of their IS strategies. This was also a factor in their deciding upon an evolutionary as opposed to a 'big bang' approach. The rate of change, both within some departments and externally, and the impact of legislative change were identified as problems by four departments.

(6) Use of consultants: unsatisfactory performance of external consultants was reported by some departments.

(7) Delays: in some instances there seemed to be a long delay between the approval of an IS strategy and the start of implementation.

## Impact on the organisation and on IT

We found that the development of IS strategies had had a limited impact on departments outside the IT function. In seven cases staff savings, better productivity or streamlined work practices were expected to result, as discussed earlier. However, there were only four instances where departments expected to re-organize themselves as a direct result of the implementation of their IS strategies.

The situation was somewhat different within the IT function where, in a number of instances, we found that re-organization had either taken place or was planned. There were some indications that the use of fourth generation languages (4GLs) would improve the productivity of IT staff but this was not yet being achieved. We found four instances where the number of IT staff was being increased in order to implement the strategy. As we expected, we found that there were difficulties in recruiting staff in the London area but that training, interesting work and career prospects could make this less of a problem.

## Lessons to be learnt

We asked departments what lessons they had learnt in the course of developing and implementing their strategies and a number of common themes, summarised below, emerged:

(1) Clear business aims and objectives were a necessary precursor to the development of an IS strategy;

(2) The development and implementation of IS strategies must be driven by senior management;

(3) It was necessary to manage users' expectations;

(4) It was necessary to describe and explain strategies to more junior levels of staff;

(5) It was easy to underestimate the effort required not only to implement the strategy but to develop it. It was also important to allow for the resources required to implement projects underway at the time the strategy was developed and for resources to deal with new demands that might arise from time to time.

## Comparison between the 1988 and 1990 surveys

When the findings of this survey were compared with those of the survey of IT strategies carried out in 1988 the main changes found were as follows:

(1) There is a wider and better perception of the need for IS and IT strategies. Although CCTA and Treasury pressure is a useful catalyst, as stated earlier, it is now less important in persuading departments to develop IS and IT strategies in the first place.

(2) There is now a closer link between strategies and business aims and objectives. Departments were moving towards such links in 1988 but they now exist more widely, particularly of course in the context of IS strategies (the 1988 survey was mainly concerned with IT strategies).

(3) IT strategies are steadily being replaced by IS strategies.

(4) The need to develop separate strategies for individual business units is more widely appreciated. Moreover, federal departments have begun to recognize the need to integrate these various strategies into a coherent whole.

(5) There is increased awareness of the need to monitor and review strategies regularly to keep them up-to-date in the light of changing circumstances and fully aligned with business aims and objectives.

Note that the CCTA guidelines have been published since the earlier study; they are widely used and are uniformly found to be useful. The overall conclusion from the above is that departments are making steady progress, albeit slowly.

## Conclusion

A number of major factors contribute to the successful use of IT:

(1) A clear business plan;

(2) A formal and comprehensive IS or IT strategy based on the business plan;

(3) Concentrating IT investment on areas where it provides maximum value;

(4) Benefits identified and planned for, with thorough post-project reviews;

(5) System requirements based on business needs;

(6) Commitment from senior management, driven down through the organization, and IT represented at high level;

(7) IT owned by users, who manage their projects, and who are accountable.

We found that most of the above factors existed in most departments. The weakest aspect was related to the quantification of intangible benefits and monitoring their achievement, but otherwise departments are making steady progress in all these areas.

It is believed that only 10–15 % of private sector organizations use IT successfully. If government departments continue to move effectively in their present direction the use of IT throughout central government will match the best (and be ahead of most) of the private sector by the turn of the century. Government departments, in general, have made substantial progress in developing IS strategies and in this aspect are more advanced than most organizations in the private and wider public sectors. However, many departments are in the early stages of implementation and this will provide the real test as to whether the resultant benefits outweigh the considerable cost and effort involved.

Whilst it is encouraging that departments believe that IS strategies are a good thing for themselves, rather than an imposition, there must be concern that less than adequate mechanisms are in place to measure and monitor performance. It is all too easy to argue that changing circumstances make this process less relevant. That is not a valid argument. A strategy need not comprise a long term plan and it is curious that so many do, when they are to be implemented in a period of continual change.

Where the development of an IT strategy is to be followed by implementation, it is first necessary to provide a business case to do so. Even where subsequent events invalidate the assumptions used to support such a business case, it is required by the Treasury and should itself be reviewed and updated for each new IT project that is proposed.

There is little evidence to suggest that there has been innovative or imaginative use of IT as a result of developing an IS strategy. On the contrary, any fundamental changes to the way departments do business or identify new business opportunities appear to be independent of IS strategy development, although the strategy may well be framed to support such initiatives. Even in the commercial organizations surveyed, IS strategy planning rarely produced any competitive advantage. More usually it was geared to maintaining competitive position. The real competitive advantage secured by a department that produces a good IS strategy, an IT strategy and then a business case for implementing the latter is that it will be in a better position to earmark finance for its IT than a less well-prepared department would be.
