MANAGEMENT REPORT

# Case study: implementing new systems at Penguin Books

JULIE BADDELEY

Managing Director of Baddeley Associates, 126/130 Tenison Road, Cambridge CB1 2DP, UK

Abstract: As the costs of computer systems continue to rise, and the commercial advantages become harder to maintain, the implementation of IT projects is coming under close scrutiny. An analysis of the methods used at Penguin Books suggests that a coherent, carefully planned programme, giving priority to the needs of the end-users, can greatly improve the levels of efficiency and reduce support costs once the system goes live. This article outlines the steps taken in drawing up an integrated package of research, documentation and training, and explains how the users were encouraged to contribute to the design of the overall implementation programme. The methods described are put forward as a means of reducing the massive costs currently incurred by implementing new systems, and improving the return on this investment. By prioritizing the role of the user, this article offers a coherent solution to some of the current IT investment problems.

## The implementation challenge

Implementing a new computer system is regarded by many companies as an effective means of increasing efficiency and maximizing profits. This commitment can be very costly, however, and the demands upon investment are often greater than is initially anticipated. A recent survey carried out by Romtec indicated that many companies are currently spending in excess of £5 million a year on new hardware and software. The costs of training are also increasing as the importance of user-familiarity with new systems becomes increasingly recognized.

The initial outlay required before any return is seen in terms of efficiency and profitability is vast, and this cost is rising as the scope and complexity of the technology itself increases. As the opportunities to establish a competitive lead by introducing new technology become more scarce, and as these opportunities depend increasingly upon the initial months before new developments become industry standards, the success of the implementation itself is becoming increasingly critical to the viability of many IT projects. One recent example of the success that can be achieved by introducing new systems effectively is that of Penguin Books.

## Background

Like many companies, Penguin had systems which dated back to the early 1970s and which had been enhanced over the years to keep pace with the company's changing business needs. As the speed of information provision within the corporate environment and in response to customer demands became firmly established as the key issue for IT management in the 1980s, the limitations of these systems were becoming increasingly apparent. Brenda Johnson, Group Computer Services Director at Penguin, explained in a speech on 'Going Live' at a conference run by the Economist in 1990:

We needed to make some drastic changes and look at a major modernization programme. We needed especially to have integrated, on-line systems that would give us more up-to-date information about sales and marketing.

## Planning the implementation

Once the decision to implement an integrated system throughout the company had been made, careful thought had to be given to the planning. The change in working practices would be profound, and would affect a large number of people. Equally, as has been explained, the margin for error in the implementation programme was very small, and the timescale critical to achieving commercial advantage. The key requirements for the project were:

(1) Introducing software that was reliable, readily available and suited to the business environment at Penguin (the emphasis being on effective distribution);

(2) Setting up an overall implementation strategy to ensure that the project was co-ordinated efficiently, the benefits of the new system were communicated to managers and users effectively, and the people involved were kept informed.

(3) Designing documentation that would provide clear information and guidance about the system for use during the training programme and in the working environment.

(4) Organizing a training plan that would enable users to carry out their particular tasks confidently and at the required performance level.

## Choosing the software

The best software solution was a package developed by Vista which already had a proven record of success in publishing houses and which could be customized easily to meet Penguin's particular requirements. Software packages which retain such flexibility are being seen as an increasingly desirable method of meeting the demands for integrated systems.

## Creating an implementation strategy

Given the size of the project, the implementation programme was complex, requiring careful planning and expert communication skills. The bulk of the attention had to be given to the distribution department, as this alone accounted for 60% of the whole project. A range of other departments had also to be considered.

With the distribution system in particular, the first challenge to be met was, as Brenda Johnson observed, 'deciding who actually owned the distribution system'. As is often the case with large projects which affect several departments, the assumption that the responsibility for implementation lies solely with the IT staff, and not with the end-users, can take hold quickly. This results in the loss of a valuable area of input at a vital stage, with user departments abdicating the responsibility for ensuring that the system is introduced successfully. This can undermine the success of the project as a whole, as Brenda Johnson noted:

Areas like documentation and training were left unresolved, the attitude being that the system should be sorted out first.

As the time taken between going live and achieving peak user efficiency is critical to the success of any IT project, the correct balance between technological issues and user needs must be struck at an early stage. This requires careful and objective thought by the departments concerned, and we were asked at Baddeley Associates to come into the project to help with this process.

## Doing a task analysis

To ensure that training and documentation are given a high profile within the overall strategy, it is important to define at the outset exactly who the users concerned are. Undertaking such an analysis in itself can be a complex task.

In a large organization such as Penguin there are many different groups of users, ranging from senior managers to warehouse staff. Establishing what tasks were performed by whom, and what level of information needed to be provided in each case, was an important part of the overall strategy. It enabled the training programme and the relevant documentation to be tailored to individual needs.

Implementing such an analysis at this early stage also has other important benefits, as Brenda Johnson observed:

It also generated a lot more feedback from the users than had previously been possible. A number of concerns and worries about the changes came out at this stage, and the whole process was very successful.

The result of this analysis was that the user groups had sufficient time to plan and organize their requirements, and the project team as a whole was able to ensure that the major objectives of the implementation were being met.

## Producing the right documentation

Because of its involvement in the analysis, Baddeley Associates was asked to produce a range of documentation for the different user groups. The aim was to keep everybody involved, and to inform them about any developments, as well as providing the basic guides. Penguin wished to encourage a positive and confident attitude towards the changes, as this sort of commitment from the users is vital to the success of any project once the system has gone live.

We first produced an overview booklet, summarizing the changes that the system would bring and explaining the benefits. The information was presented in a clear, straightforward and attractive format, both to help build confidence and to encourage the sense of ownership of the system that was originally lacking.

As well as the overview, we produced a user guide which explained clearly the basics of using the system. The approach was to structure the documentation according to the tasks that the users needed to perform, rather than placing too great an emphasis on the features of the system itself. This helped bring the training programme and familiarity with the live environment more clearly into focus.

The remaining documentation comprised a series of short, task-based guides for the different user groups, and mini-guides which provided quick-reference information on the tasks that each group had to perform most frequently. By splitting up the documentation in this way, the users were able to find the information they needed quickly and easily, thus increasing their confidence and efficiency when the system went live.

## Designing an integrated training programme

Task-based documentation helps users find their way around a system quickly, but they also need training which is carefully targeted to their needs and which, together with the documentation, forms part of an integrated package.

The first step in developing the training programme at Penguin was to produce a series of detailed learning objectives for each user group. These set out the targets to be achieved during training and the tasks that each user should be able to perform once the training was complete. This enabled the process of system learning to take place in the context of establishing good business practices. The relevant groups were consulted fully at this stage, emphasizing the role they had to play in their own programme.

Training notes were then prepared for each group, based on the targets outlined in the learning objectives. Together with the guides, these formed the basis for the training courses. As well as providing material, the other important element of a training programme is its timing. If training takes place too early in the implementation timetable, users can forget what they have learned by the time the system goes live. If, however, training does not take place until the system has already gone live, the learning curve is slower because the demands of 'real' work put more pressure on the users, at a time when establishing peak efficiency is critical. In both cases, the end result is a loss in productivity and an increase in support costs.

After consultation between Penguin, Vista and Baddeley Associates, the decision was made to arrange the training immediately prior to the system going live, so that the material was fresh in the users' minds. Training took place at the users' ordinary workstations, rather than in the classroom, to help bridge the gap between the learning process and the actual work patterns.

## Going live

As the aim of the programme was to make the transition to the live environment as smooth as possible, the courses included practical examples and the opportunity to practise on the system. Of equal importance in this respect was the involvement of the user groups in the planning of their training, as it enabled them to think carefully about what their jobs involved, and the changes the new system would bring to their work. As a result of developing a carefully structured implementation plan, Penguin were able to go live with their system on schedule and with the backing of a confident and committed group of users. In the words of Brenda Johnson:

I believe that the methodology which evolved out of the approach we took – that of using a task analysis to find out what people need to know, and then targeting the training to each group of users – is an appropriate and right one, certainly for our organization. It is a method we intend to use again and, indeed, have started to use in our future systems.

The key to this success was that Penguin put their users first, and recognized the importance of this at an early stage. To get maximum benefit from a new system, companies need to ensure that the users are consulted and that they are confident about the system and the benefits it would bring. Only in this way can users be expected to exploit the system fully, and produce the sort of productivity figures that determine the success of any IT project.
