# Introduction: human resource and organizational issues in the 1990s

LESLIE WILLCOCKS

City University Business School, London UK

## Introduction

There is now considerable evidence that a major neglect in the development and implementation of IT-based systems has been human resource and organizational issues, broadly conceived (see for example Eason, 1988; Keen 1991; Scott Morton, 1991; Walton, 1989; Willcocks and Mason, 1987; Willcocks, 1991a). Computer-based systems have too frequently been designed and introduced with little concern for the social, organizational and political contexts in which they are supposed to operate, and for the human resource implications of the IT-based systems design, for its implementation and for its effective performance. One view, the one adopted throughout the Introduction is that this can be usefully conceived as a management problem. Not least of the human and organizational issues may be the quality of management and how management is organized and staffed. These issues receive attention in the first part, while other human resource and organizational issues are discussed later in this Introduction. A framework is then offered for analysing the issues inherent in managing information systems.

Firstly, however, it is important to understand what is meant here by ‘management’. One way of looking at management is to identify what constitutes appropriate management functions in a specific organization, and these will be dependent on a range of factors. On one classification of these may be seen as environment, dominant coalition, technical core and performance standards (for a detailed discussion see Willcocks and Harrow (1992)). Differences in environmental influences, objectives, dominant coalition, technical core and performance standards can influence what management functions will be appropriate and how these will be distributed throughout the organization. For example, such management functions may turn out to be those envisaged by Harrison (1979) – setting objectives, formulating plans, organizing, staffing, directing and controlling. What then becomes important is how these management functions are translated into ‘managerial’ and ‘non-managerial jobs’, activities and behaviours to produce an emergent organization-specific model of management more or less related to the achievement of a level of organizational effectiveness desired and defined by various stakeholders. In all this, arriving at a management division of labour for the organization becomes critical.

In the management of information-based systems and of information technology, it is clear that such management divisions of labour, staffed appropriately and geared to delivering on the potential of the systems being designed, delivered and operated, are frequently not forthcoming. For example, a range of research reports suggest that general managers (as opposed to IS professionals) have been marked by confusion about the potential of IT, lack of awareness of new developments, and an absence of IT know-how at senior levels (Griffiths, 1987; Tomlin, 1990; Walton and McKersie, 1990; Willcocks, 1991b). The lack of managerial knowledge/know-how on IT would seem to feed into the relatively slow adoption of IT in the UK in the 1980s, managerial resistance to IT and a lack of clarity about what objectives to pursue with regard to information technologies (see Willcocks, 1989 for a summary of the research). Research supports some further generalizations. Not surprisingly, management has also been marked by setting limited objectives for IT, including cost-minimization, reducing the headcount and running existing things better. General managers have also tended to abandon projects to the technologists; their perspectives, requirements and modes of working have then tended to predominate in the development and implementation of IT projects. In practice, the predominant managerial tendencies in many IT projects may be summarized as: limited choices, financial, short-term focus together with technocratically driven perspectives and outcomes. This conclusion is supported by a range of case study work (for example Boddy and Buchanan, 1986; Mason, 1987; Morton, 1991; Rowe, 1984; Willcocks and Walton, 1989).

## Management development

What can be done about this? It is clear that success with IT is linked strongly with awareness of IT by senior managers and what it can do for the organization, senior managers' commitment to IT and their ability to clarify and focus the IT effort, and the ability of the organization to foster IT champions and sponsors (Kearney, 1990; Kobler Unit, 1990; Tomlin, 1990). One strand is a reorientation of senior management, including board members, through education and development processes. Some approaches to this were detailed in an earlier issue of this journal (see Volume 5 No. 2 pages 110–114). Roger Tomlin (1991) found that very successful IT users generally make more use of all methods of senior management learning investigated in his survey. These included day-to-day contact, internal and external seminars and presentations, and learning from systems suppliers and visits to other organizations. Also useful would seem to be research studies into technological possibilities, competitors' use of IT and IT usage in other market sectors. See also the paper by Janet Windeatt and Isobel Knox in this Issue, p. 204.

A further useful development has been the recent emphasis on the education and development of 'hybrid' managers. Colin Palmer, JIT, 5 (4), (1990) described how managers with business, IT and organizational skills have been lynchpins in successful IT projects in several organizations. These can be developed through long exposure to different, real jobs within the organization, and interdisciplinary courses. However hybrid managers are no panacea. It is more helpful to see them as part of a process of formulating a new division of labour for managing IT. Earl (1990) is useful in suggesting one way forward. Several roles may need to be developed for comprehensive information management, each with the need for different skills in organizational, business and technological areas. 'Professionals' will be experts in the technological domain but also need further education in the other two areas. 'Hybrids' operate in the user/doing domains with abilities across the three skill areas while 'Leaders' will be needed in the user/driving domains and are particularly strong in business/organization skills. Finally 'impresarios' are needed at senior IS levels and operate in the specialist/driving domains. This classification implies that 'hybridization' cannot really be restricted, as a process, merely to those called hybrids in Earl's classification.

Two papers in this Theme Issue would seem particularly relevant here. Yash Gupta suggests one way forward in discussing the need for developing a ‘strategic partnership’ between the Chief Executive Officer and the Chief Information Officer. The paper on IT Directors and IT Strategy by Colin Coulson-Thomas also provides valuable commentary, ideas and survey evidence on the role of board members in IT matters, the characteristics needed by IS professionals to become board members and the role of IS professionals in IS management.

## Divisions of labour: management

Information Systems management can be usefully divided into strategic, project and operational levels. In addition to those enumerated above, frequent observable problems in organizations include: failure to establish clarity in the managerial arrangements and responsibilities at and between these levels; lack of a strategic dimension linked with business/organizational objectives; IS professionals dominating the management processes in practice; poor user manager-IS professional liaison; lack of integration between the different levels of management (Ernst and Young, 1990; Rockart and Short, 1990; Scott Morton, 1989; Scott Morton, 1991; Willcocks, 1991a). One way forward here is to see the strategic dimension as aligning objectives and plans with those of the business/organization, establishing management structures, resource allocation, monitoring and financial evaluation. Project management could then be seen as the development and delivery of systems to defined requirements within time and budget, user liaison and the management of systems staff. Operational management would be primarily concerned with the detailed management of routine operations, managing systems staff and user liaison.

Case research and active participation in IT projects by the writer would suggest a number of points. At the operational levels IT professionals need to look after detailed technical operations while taking responsibility for user liaison, support and much more user IT education than is commonly provided. Specific IT projects are often better managed by those with business and organizational skills rather than IS professionals. To endorse the findings of Beath and Ives (1988), champions, with power and resources, are needed at the project management level, as well as the strategic level, to ensure that projects maintain momentum on a daily basis. Experience also suggests that mixed project teams tend to produce better long-term results; there should also operate succession planning for key project staff, including project champions, both to encourage IT and management learning, but also to ensure project time and valuable learning is not lost when key skilled staff are, as often happens, attracted into other work or organizations. It is the cross fertilization of skills and the creation of opportunities for learning that should be built into the way IS management operates as a process. At all levels this means regular reviews to learn from mistakes inevitably made in what are often high risk activities; at the strategic level it means a mix of skills, cross-learning and IT professionals actively involved in board and senior management decision-making.

The papers by Yash Gupta and Colin Coulson-Thomas explicitly deal with such concerns. Much of their argument and evidence suggests that IS management by its practice should be in itself a learning process. This would seem to be one way of addressing the limitations in general and IS professional management practices detailed above.

## Users and IS professionals

Further human resource and organizational issues emerge from a reconsideration of end-user participation and the role of IS professionals in designing and developing IT-based systems. These are issues explicitly addressed by the paper on the Information Systems Use Design Model by Mike Hales (1991) in this Theme Issue.

There is now considerable evidence that traditional design and development techniques, with technical design considerations and the skills and perspectives of systems professionals dominant, tend to drive computerization processes (Eason, 1988; Davies, 1989; Willcocks and Mason, 1987). The impact has been very restricted forms of end-user participation, despite the immense lip-service often paid to the latter. Not least important when considering user participation is establishing the degree and type of participation most beneficial in particular circumstances. A missing link in much of the participation debate is detailed consideration of how IS professionals fit in, and what they have to contribute. Tomlin (1990) has found that among successful organizations there has been a reversal in the 1980s trend towards devolution of IS initiative and responsibility to users generally. There were, instead, moves towards partnerships on IS projects, with IS professionals developing more user-focused outlooks and becoming enablers and facilitators. In his paper Mike Hales (1991) also suggests that these developments are useful ones.

Another frequently missing element in the participation debate is consideration of the portfolio of information systems in existence or being developed in a specific organization. The McFarlan and McKenney (1983) 'strategic grid' – describing systems as 'strategic' (critical to sustaining future business strategy), 'turnaround' (may be important in achieving future success), 'support' (valuable but not critical), or 'factory' (organization currently depends on these for success) – is useful for framing discussion here. Earl (1989) comments that in support contexts, specialist dominance is quite likely and generally works; in factory contexts specialists can concentrate on delivery but need a user-service mentality; in turnaround contexts users need to direct the IS strategy but specialists may well best be able to direct and implement the strategy, while in strategic contexts user dominance is most appropriate as IS and IT become integrated into the business. However, several other dimensions need to enter the discussion.

Systems classified as support and turnaround are likely to be stand-alone, individual user and/or group-or department-based. At early stages of technology adoption, end-user participation in design and development decisions and processes will be highly useful, not least for both user and organizational learning (Earl, 1989). However, systems proliferation can create a range of problems, not least duplication of information held, security issues and cut-offs in innovation and learning as users incorporate into their work patterns software and technology that is more accessible with quick, if limited, returns (Mason and Willcocks, 1991). As technologies mature, and as systems potential for shared, corporate information becomes recognized, IS professionals will need to take more responsibility for control and further systems development, including systems routinization and integration. This means that many systems move into the factory quadrant over time. It is in this quadrant that traditional IS design and development approaches and IS-user relationships are most appropriate, as systems become standardized and pressed down into the organization's 'technology platform'.

From the discussion so far, Earl (1989) would seem to be correct in predicting for the foreseeable future that the likely balance will be a delegation of direction and some development of IT to users, with IS professionals providing staff functions and much of the operations. From this perspective, across all IS development and applications, IS specialists will need to have reserve powers in the areas of standards, IS human resource development, technology research, utility services, vendor relations, monitoring and audit, and overall IT architecture. However, IS professionals will be increasingly in a service role as users and managers become the drivers of IT. In this Theme Issue the paper by Mike Hales (1991) on a Human Centered Model for IT Systems Development is particularly useful for extending this debate. In going beyond the 'hybrid' manager concept Hales in fact develops an alternative way forward for developing information systems, suggesting new roles, relationships and skills whilst also suggesting the factors and circumstances which may sometimes act as barriers to the full adoption of his proposals.

## Human resources and information technology

So far in this Introduction human resource and organizational issues have been understood and dealt with in quite a restricted manner. A broader approach will now be followed. Human resources are commonly represented as vital to effective computerization. In practice human resource issues – for example staff numbers, commitment, know-how, availability, employee relations, reward systems, levels and timing of training and development, change management – are almost systematically neglected, then receive detailed attention at the implementation stage of projects, all too often where that neglect induces firefighting responses. One particularly neglected area is frequently employee relations. The paper by Changtseh Hsiek and Michael Menefee – entitled ‘An Application of Group Decision Support Technology in Collective Bargaining’ – is therefore particularly interesting in turning this tendency inside out, and showing that creative use of information technology can actually assist rather than represent a problem to what is traditionally conceived as ‘industrial relations’ and the collective bargaining arena.

More typically, however, the provision of skilled IT professionals, health and safety and workstation design and (all too perfunctory) user training receive most attention in IT projects. Broader human resource planning, and its implementation, gets lost through technocratic perspectives, budget constraints, tightening deadlines, and the need to demonstrate progress in the form of physical hardware, software and equipment up and running. Unfortunately, this scenario is supported by a range of research (see Willcocks, 1991a for a summary).

Further studies show that the 'holy grail' of alignment in business/organization, human resource and information objectives and strategies is rarely achieved (Scott Morton, 1991; Willcocks, 1991a). A 1990 report on 83 companies found only two examples of such strategic alignment (Ernst and Young, 1990). Much of this flows from the lack of strategy per se. However, greater alignment could be achieved through personal involvement of personnel and IS specialists at senior decision-making levels, as partly recommended by Gupta and Coulson-Thomas in this Issue.

A further set of problems occur in the underestimation and under-budgeting for human resource costs in computerization programmes. Also prevalent is the view that human resources represent costs to be minimized rather than investments likely to enhance IS effectiveness (Sankar, 1991). In detailed research on the economics of office work Strassmann (1985) found that investments in human capital and other reorganization costs exceed the costs of any information technology over its lifetime. Arguing from case studies, Hochstrasser JIT, 5 (4), (1990) estimates that indirect human and organizational costs might well have to be four times as high as direct technology and equipment costs, but are consistently underplayed or even omitted in how IS investment feasibilities are evaluated. This is supported by research into 50 organizations by Willcocks and Lester (1991), and Symons JIT, 5 (4), (1990) and argues for improved IS evaluation methodologies leading to improved funding of human resource areas. The paper by Dan Remenyi and Arthur Money in this Theme Issue extends the notion of evaluation by providing and illustrating the use of, tools for assessing levels of user satisfaction with IS effectiveness. It would seem that these tools could be used during pilot or prototype schemes, as well as during and after implementation of an information system.

## IS management: towards a broader perspective

In the light of the discussion so far, and the papers in this Theme Issue, a broader view of what constitutes IS management is needed. Figure 1 is presented as an attempt at a framework for illustrating some major concerns that will form a fundamental part of the agenda that IS managers need to address in the 1990s. What is clear from the 1980s experience is that the major managerial emphasis has fallen on the IS management-technological relationship. The major questions and issues that tended to be addressed were techno-centred, for example: the reliance on technical know-how in the face of IS professional staff shortages; what level of innovation did the industry require; what technical systems did the organization need? More recently a great deal more emphasis, at least in the literature, has fallen on the Environment-IS Management-Organization axis, particularly on the degree of organizational openness to its environment (see for example Holtham, JIT, 4 (4) 1989; Jackson, JIT, 4 (3) 1989; Rotemberg and Saloner, 1990). The Theme Issue paper on 'The evolution of interorganizational systems' by Johannes Meier and Ralph Sprague is particularly useful here for extending this debate and the focus on the networking of systems within and between organizations, as detailed in the Landmark Management in the 1990s Research Program (Scott Morton, 1991). Colin Coulson-Thomas, in his paper, also discusses the need to move towards a networked organization model, and also details the human resource, structural and other organizational implications of interorganizational information systems networks.

However, despite these contributions, in practice IS management has rarely given due attention to the human resource and organizational corners indicated in Figure 1. Perhaps this has contributed to the situation highlighted by the most recent evidence showing that the major perceived problems in IS management are now human and organizational, not

technical in nature (Kearney, 1990; Walton and McKersie, 1990). 1989–90 research in over 130 organizations by the SOMIT group at City University Business School reinforces this evidence. The major problems perceived by those responsible for computerization have been, in order of importance: (1) Shortage of IT specialist staff; (2) Inadequate definition of initial software requirements; (3) Timescales greater than planned; (4) Lack of top management support; (5) Disappointing equipment performance; (6) User resistance to IT systems; (7) Installation costs exceed budget; (8) Recruitment/retraining of other staff for computer literacy. The reasons for sub-optimization in systems development and operation are complex, but it is noticeable how far many of the actual problems experienced can be traced back, in fact, to human resource issues. Nor is it clear that technology itself is a solution to human resource problems, as Pamela Coats makes clear in this issue in reviewing critically the business uses of expert systems.

This discussion raises a number of points. It is clear that IS management in the 1990s will be a complex multi-faceted set of activities. Not only will it be necessary to manage on the four fronts – technological, environmental, human resources and organizational – at the same time giving each due emphasis; the interrelationships between these four fronts will also need to be managed. Traditional management divisions of labour will probably no longer be adequate to the tasks in hand. For the moment it is important to point out that one thing Figure 1 establishes is the likely IS management education and development agenda. In particular, it highlights the need to redress the balance by managing from the human resource corner much more than ever before. This means managing culture, know-how and labour market issues. It also highlights managing from the organizational corner. This means handling organizational ‘fit’ with culture, degree of openness to environment required, and information systems design for use. Of course such ‘fit’ is not unproblematic. A whole range of perceptions, beliefs, values and interests are operationalized by stakeholders in informatization. The resulting struggles, compromises and levels of cooperation, represent perhaps an even more fundamental set of human resource and organizational issues to be managed.

## Towards an information systems culture?

One way forward on Information Systems Management may be to see a key task as the development of an information systems culture. 'Culture' was presented in the 1980s as the key to most organizational and management problems. In the last 2 years the term has been increasingly used in the debate about how the introduction and operation of IS can be managed (see for example Scott Morton, 1989; Heller, 1990; Tomlin, 1990). Managing culture is inherently political. An important power resource lies in the establishment of the legitimacy of IS change and subsequent IS operation. Establishing legitimacy is tied in with politics as the management of meaning. A further development in understanding is implied by talking of information systems (IS) rather than IT. Technology is often considered quite narrowly as equipment, for example machines, tools, instruments, hardware, software. Technique is sometimes added to the definition, that is skills, routines, methods, procedures. Another approach is to conceptualize an IT-based system in which people play an integral part as designers, maintainers and users of output from activated equipment and techniques. Information also needs consideration. An information system is a repository and flow of 'data with a purpose' which may or may not be held in computerized form. Information as a resource, who controls and receives it when and why, import further human and political dimensions into the notion of how an information systems culture can be developed.

These considerations help in arriving at a fuller understanding of what is meant in the context of this paper by IS culture. They also imply changes in perspective amongst many of those responsible for computerization as one basis on which to begin to build appropriate IS cultures. A working definition of an effective IS culture, then, is the shared/sharing of: norms, values and understanding, visions, knowledge, skills/competencies, continuous learning related to IT needs, cooperative relationships with IS professionals, and commitment – supportive of existing and required IT usage at organizational, department, group and individual levels. It is not intended to provide here a detailed guide to building an appropriate IS culture.

(For a detailed treatment see Willcocks, 1991a). However, British Airways has been one of the few organizations that has devoted much time and resources to developing their culture, technology use and organization in an interdependent manner over the last 5 years. The paper by Janet Windeatt and Isobel Knox – ‘Catalyst: a programme for change within British Airways’ is therefore valuable for giving insight into how one large private sector organization has chosen to proceed on the major issues raised by this Theme edition of the Journal.

## Conclusion

Information systems management has become a complex, multi-faceted activity. There is a need to manage at several different levels a range of dynamic, interrelated issues. In the 1980s IS management tended to be dominated by the primary objective of getting a technically efficient system into an organization within time and budget. This emerged as a necessary but insufficient, often self-defeating activity. As computer-based systems begin to penetrate to the core of organizational functioning, thus bringing more users on-stream, and as technology costs get driven down, against IS labour supplies being in continual shortage, the 'technical efficiency' approach to IT-based systems becomes ever less appropriate. In particular, the movement of IS management into the 1990s needs to be driven as much from the human resource and organizational as from the technological and environmental fronts. The key to IS management in the 1990s will be delivering on the above objective by developing people willing and able to exploit IT to achieve business or organizational advantage. The implications of this still remain to be confronted by most organizations. Hopefully this Theme Issue will make a contribution to redressing the balance.

## Biographical notes

Leslie Willcocks is Director, Centre for the Study of the Use of Information in Management, City University Business School, London, UK. He worked for 7 years for Torche Ross, and subsequently several smaller firms. Present research projects include methods of evaluating information systems investments; building information systems cultures; and a recent report on managing information systems in Central Government for the Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency, UK. He is co-author of three books. A forthcoming title is “Of Capital Importance: Evaluating IS investments (Chapman & Hall).
