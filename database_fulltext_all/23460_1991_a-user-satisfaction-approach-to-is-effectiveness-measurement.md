# A user-satisfaction approach to IS effectiveness measurement

DAN REMENYI and ARTHUR MONEY

The Management College, Henley, UK

Abstract: This paper reports on research conducted by the authors into how to measure the effectiveness of a personal computer network used by a business school to deliver a range of personal productivity tools to MBA students, as well as academic and administrative staff. The use of user-satisfaction is recognized by many IT researchers as an appropriate surrogate for IT effectiveness. The authors decided to use a user-satisfaction model which is based on the gap between users' beliefs of what is important and their perception of what is delivered by the ISD, to measure the effectiveness of the personal computer network.

In brief, the research showed that all was not well with the personal computer network in use. The gaps on the two perceptual performance factors, namely effective benefits and system access, are significant in assessing overall satisfaction with the network. The gap on effective benefits is approximately twice as important as system's access in terms of its impact on overall satisfaction. This research study showed that this gap approach is quite effective. It is inexpensive. It does not require an enormous amount of expertise, although an acquaintance with factor analysis is necessary. The study need not take a long period of time; the total lapse time for the above research was six weeks, requiring the equivalent of five person-days work.

## Introduction

This paper reports on research conducted by the authors into how to measure the effectiveness of a personal computer network used by a business school to deliver a range of personal productivity tools to MBA students, as well as academic and administrative staff. The business school believed that the IT benefit issues was very important to all the systems' stakeholders and the director of the business school agreed to this research study because he suspected that the system was not being used effectively and thus the users were not obtaining an adequate level of benefit.

The issue of IT benefits is of growing concern today (Remenyi, et al. 1991). By far the most obvious reason for this is the rocketing level of IT expenditure experienced by many business organizations (Remenyi, 1991), academic institutions as well as government agencies. For about 40 years business, industry and government have been acquiring more and more computing power. This expenditure has been in the region of tens or even hundreds of thousands of pounds.

However, by the beginning of the 90s many firms were spending millions or even tens of millions of pounds on IT. According to Willcocks (1991) UK companies are currently spending over 10 billion pounds per annum on IT, an average of 1.2% of annual turnover, while the public sector, excluding the Ministry of Defence operational equipment, is over

2 billion pounds, or 1% of total public expenditure. Assessing the value of this expenditure in IT is becoming progressively more difficult (Peters, 1988).

In addition, computers are employing vast numbers of staff, including data input personnel, programmers, analysts, systems co-ordinators, computer operators, computer managers, information systems directors etc. The phenomenal growth in this industry is totally unprecedented by any other human endeavour and is expected to continue to expand for the foreseeable future. However, although computerization has generally been welcomed by most business participants and observers there has always been a certain amount of scepticism about its real beneficial effects, especially in as far as it claims to be able to improve business effectiveness. This is because the measuring of IT benefits, and especially IT effectiveness, has traditionally been an extremely difficult business (Miller and Doyle, 1987).

There are several reasons why it is difficult to measure IT effectiveness, not the least of which is the problem of agreeing a suitably robust definition of the term IT effectiveness. Kim (1990) states that measuring MIS effectiveness is an intricate task due to the difficulties of tracing and sorting out the effects of MIS through a web of intermediate impacts which the IT function has upon organizational effectiveness. IT effectiveness is certainly a multi-faceted concept (King, 1984), and therefore it must be viewed from a number of quite different perspectives.

In such circumstances, where it is very difficult to isolate the core of effectiveness, a surrogate measure such as user-satisfaction may be used. The use of user-satisfaction is recognized by many IT researchers as an appropriate surrogate for IT effectiveness. According to Raghunathan and King (1988), user-satisfaction is often used as an effectiveness measure. Baroudi and Orlikowski (1988) state that user-satisfaction has become a pervasive measure of the success or effectiveness of information systems. Kim supports these views by pointing out that user-satisfaction considers the relationship between the MIS and its environment. It is a holistic approach (Remenyi et al., 1991), as individual systems are not addressed, but rather the IS function as a whole.

With the above in mind, the authors decided to use a user-satisfaction model to measure the effectiveness of the personal computer network used by a business school. The effectiveness of this system was the key issue of concern to the stakeholders. This approach was considered especially appropriate as the institution concerned wanted a holistic measure of the personal computer's effectiveness.

## User satisfaction (US)

User-satisfaction is generally considered to be measurable by a comparison of user expectations (or needs) of the IS with the perceived performance (or capability) of the IS on a number of different facets of the IS. US is measured as the discrepancy between user expectations and the perceptions of the system. Further, the model assumes that overall US can be explained by the gap in scores given to systems factors or attributes.

More specifically, overall attitude to the IS function can be considered to be reflected in the size and direction of the discrepancies (or gaps), between expectations and performance. A positive (negative) gap results when perceived performance exceeds (is below) expectation. A ‘large’ gap implies dissatisfaction with the IS capabilities. A ‘large’ positive gap can be interpreted as indicating that IS resources are being wasted, whereas a ‘large’ negative gap indicates a need for improved performance.

A variant to the above approach is to use the correlation between expectations and performance scores as a measure of 'fit'. The correlations provide a means for assessing the overall effectiveness of the IS function, where high positive correlations can be taken to imply 'consensus' of views.

These models have their roots in the theory of organizational and consumer behaviour. This is easy to comprehend if one accepts that the IS function has an impact on the whole organization and is aimed at satisfying the user (i.e. customer) who can be both internal as well as external to the organization.

## Description of the instrument

The instrument is designed to measure the perceived effectiveness of the overall IS function and involves the use of a questionnaire. The questionnaire comprises four parts, an introduction and three additional parts labelled Part A, Part B and Part C. The study involves the use of a self-completion questionnaire, which is to be found at the end of this paper. The first section (see page 1 of Figure 8) captured background information relating to position in the business school, years of work-study experience, years of work experience with a PC, and finally years of work experience with a PC network. This is followed by three parts labelled A, B and C. Parts A and B use the same set of 24 questions, where these questions capture information on various facets or attributes of the system. Part C involves a general question about the individual's satisfaction with the computer network system, as well as providing the respondents with an opportunity to supply general comments on the systems.

Part A measures the extent to which the attributes are perceived to be important to the effectiveness of the system. Expectations were measured on a 4-point scale ranging from 1=irrelevant to 4=critical. Part B uses the same items but now the respondent is asked to rate the performance of the Information Systems Department on a 4-point scale from 1=very poor to 4=excellent. A small focus group of four individuals was used to help determine the systems attributes which should be used in the research. In addition, a small pilot study of five potential respondents was used to attempt to identify poorly worded questions.

A total of 110 questionnaires were distributed of which 86 were returned and were thus available for analysis. Of these 76 were completed by the MBA students. The analysis that follows was performed on the MBA responses. The analytical approach is similar to that of Brown and Swartz, 1989.

## Factor identification

In order to gain a better understanding of the perception scores, a factor analysis was performed on both the expectation and performance data. The underlying factors were determined through the principal component procedure of the SPSS/PC software package. Each of the sets of expectation statements and performance statements were analysed by using the principal components procedure to extract the factors followed by a varimax rotation. The resulting factor matrices are displayed in Figures 4 and 5. Only those statements with a factor loading of 0.5 or more are listed in the tables.

## Expectation scores

Eight factors with eigen values greater than 1 were extracted from the expectation data, accounting for 62.2% of the overall variance. Only the first four of these were interpretable. It could be that perceptions of what is important is not clear in the respondents' minds.

Ease of use emerged as the factor explaining most of the variance. Modernness of the system, system control, and technical competence available, in order of variance explained, were the remaining factors. These factors, and the attributes loading on them are given in Figure 4. The KMO measure for these scores was 0.5439 and therefore this procedure is only just appropriate (Kerlinger, 1969).

Only four of the twelve gap scores are significantly correlated with overall satisfaction. This implies that the dimensions identified by the factor analysis of expectation scores are likely to be weak measures of user satisfaction with the system.

## Performance scores

Four factors, with eigen values greater than 1, were extracted from the performance data, accounting for 49.7% of the total variance. Effective benefits realization emerged as the factor explaining most of the variance. Modernness of the system, system access and quality of service were the remaining factors. These factors, and the statements loading on them, are given in Figure 5. The KMO for these scores was 0.7439 and therefore this procedure is more appropriate than for the expectation data (Kerlinger, 1969).

The multiple correlation $R$ indicates the extent of the correlation between the overall satisfaction scores and all the explanatory variables collectively. The interpretation of the $R^{2}$ value of 0.4196 is that the explanatory variables collectively explain 41.96% of the variation in the overall satisfaction scores. The relative impact of each explanatory variable can be inferred from the, so called, beta weights, which are standardized regression coefficients. The beta weights are used in preference to the ordinary regression coefficients as they are independent of the unit of measurement for the variable itself. In the fitted model, effective benefits with a highly significant beta weight of 0.4597 is by far the factor which has most impact on the overall opinion score. The impact of effective benefits can be considered to have approximately 2.5 times as much impact as system access which has a beta weight of 0.1797.

It is interesting to note from the second column of Figure 6 that while all three gap variables are significantly and positively correlated with overall satisfaction, both the PC and PC network experience variables are not significantly correlated with overall satisfaction. It is of additional interest to note that, albeit not significant, the correlations in the sample between overall satisfaction and both the PC and PC network experience variables are negative. It is just possible, therefore, that overall satisfaction can be inversely related to these experience variables, which would imply that the more experienced users are more difficult to satisfy.

The regression model was re-computed using the stepwise regression procedure and the results are presented in Figure 7. The stepwise regression retains only two of the original five variables included in the full regression model. Despite this the two models are very similar in terms of fit. ## Summary of findings

Firstly, from a practical point of view, the business school found the results of this research very interesting and very useful. It confirmed their suspicion that all was not well with the personal computer network in use. The results suggested areas which they could address as well as issues which they

needed to reconsider.

Secondly, from an academic point of view, the gaps on the two perceptual performance factors, namely effective benefits and system access are significant in assessing overall satisfaction with the network. The gap on effective benefits is approximately twice as important as system's access in terms of its impact on overall satisfaction. Previous PC and PC network experience do not impact significantly on overall satisfaction with the network system.

The fitted model in this case only explains about 40% of the variability in the overall satisfaction scores. Thus, there are other factors, not identified in the study, which impact on overall satisfaction. This in turn suggests that the evaluation of an office automation/network system is far more complex, involving more than just the two dimensions identified.

Finally, this research study showed that this gap approach is quite effective. It is inexpensive. It does not require an enormous amount of expertise although an acquaintance with factor analysis is necessary. It does however require access to a statistical package which provides factor analysis, but this technique is available in many inexpensive statistical packages today. Also, the study need not take a long period of time. The total lapse time for the above research was six weeks, requiring the equivalent of 5 person-days work.
