# The evolution of interorganizational systems

JOHANNES MEIER

McKinsey and Company Inc., Hamburg, Germany

RALPH H. SPRAGUE Jr.

College of Business Administration, University of Hawaii, Honolulu, HI, USA

Abstract: In the literature on interorganizational systems (IOS) one encounters mainly examples of information systems that support highly structured transactions between organizations. However, the range of interorganizational activities also includes less structured interactions, such as negotiations and joint problem solving. This paper wants to contribute to a better understanding of the potential of IOS by developing the notion of a high-level IOS. Technological and managerial challenges are delineated that have to be mastered in order to reach high-level IOS. Underlying the analysis of these challenges is the prediction of an evolution of IOS that is based on the analysis of the evolution of internal information systems (IS).

## Introduction

Interorganizational systems (IOS) have attracted a lot of interest in the literature on the strategic management of information technology. Most generally, IOS have been defined as ‘automated information systems shared by two or more companies’ (Cash and Konsynski, 1985). Today, most IOS focus on the support of highly structured interorganizational activities. Examples include exchanging orders and receipts between manufacturer and supplier, making and confirming reservations in the travel industry, and transforming funds electronically in the financial sector. However, many interorganizational activities clearly go beyond these well-structured transactions. Negotiations between companies are among the most important interorganizational activities. Joint product development and other forms of cooperative problem solving are increasingly common examples of strategic alliances between companies. In this context the research question arises – how to support the wide spectrum of interorganizational activities. This paper focuses on IOS as a means supporting all interorganizational activities.

In the next section, a review of the literature on IOS gives examples of current IOS use. The issue of competitive advantage through IOS is analysed. The realization that investment into IOS tends to turn from a source of competitive advantage into a strategic necessity points at a more general pattern of information technology adoption. In fact, this suggests a parallel between the evolution of interorganizational systems and internal information systems. This parallel is traced in more detail later on in this paper. A key notion of this paper is that the parallel between intraorganizational systems and interorganizational systems not only reflects a general pattern of IT adoption but can also be extended in other dimensions. A most important dimension in the evolution of internal IS is the degree of structure in the tasks supported by the IS. The implications of this dimension for the future evolution of IOS are discussed leading to a set of challenges concerning the development of innovative IOS in the future. The concept of a high-level IOS that supports complex and less-structured tasks is also delineated further on in this paper by pointing at conceptual and architectural characteristics of such systems. To further illustrate our ideas, some possible examples of high level IOS are described. In the conclusion it is emphasized that the evolution of interorganizational systems poses an inherently interdisciplinary challenge.

## Literature on IOS

## Overview of current IOS use

A lot of case studies have emphasized the importance of interorganizational systems in the context of the strategic use of information technology. Immediately, the well-documented systems of American Hospital Supply (Cash et al., 1988) and Foremost-McKesson (Clemons and Row, 1988) come to mind. They are examples of ordering systems that constitute the most prominent category of IOS. Ordering systems connect a manufacturer with his/her suppliers or a retailer with his/her customers. These systems provide efficiency benefits, as electronic data interchange provides faster and more accurate communication (Benjamin, et al., 1988). Elimination of paper, postage premiums for fast delivery, and data entry personnel results in cost savings for the participants in the IOS. A theoretical result pertaining to these efficiency benefits is the conclusion by Bakos (1987) that exchange of information in vertical markets and inventories are economic substitutes.

By increasing the response time and capacity of the communication link between a supplier and manufacturer, inventory costs can be decreased. According to Silverman (1990) additional benefits from these systems derive from the increased timeliness of information. 'Instant products' may command higher prices, product quality may be enhanced through shorter information flow cycles, marketing of products may be more effective given faster information on buying patterns, sales reports, and pricing patterns.

A second major category of IOS is the use of IOS to implement markets. These electronic markets tend to be much more efficient than the traditional means of implementing a market. This has become most obvious in the introduction of electronic trading facilities in the London stock exchange. In fact, the electronic means of trading had virtually substituted the traditional means of trading within days of their introduction (Clemons and Weber, 1989). One may also cite airline reservation systems in this category. Although initially these systems were heavily biased towards the airline of the provider of the systems, there is now a clear trend towards a more neutral provision of travel information (Copeland and McKenney, 1988; Feldman 1987, 1988; Hopper, 1990). Thus, a consolidation of the current airline reservation systems into a few systems that perform primarily a market function is to be expected. The merger of Pars and Datas II and the cooperation between the new European reservation systems Amadeus and Galileo and their American counterparts, Covia, System One and more recently Sabre, point into this direction.

Finally, we briefly want to mention another important category of IOS. IOS make it possible to efficiently disseminate specialized information. The on-line database industry is rapidly growing. Examples include the Lexus and Nexus systems by Mead data for legal and medical information. Another example is provided by consortia of libraries in the USA that use IOS to connect databases in order to provide better service to their users.

In summary, it is important to note that most of the current IOS support the exchange of standardized transactions. This indicates that most IOS today are de facto electronic data interchange (EDI) systems which effect 'intercompany, computer-to-computer exchange of business documents in standard formats' (Hinge, 1988).

## From competitive advantage to strategic necessity

Most research on IOS has focused on the competitive advantages to be gained from introducing an IOS (McFarlan, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Porter and Miller, 1985). More recently, the competitive advantage of IOS has been traced to first mover advantages in many cases (Clemons and Kimbrough, 1986). As Max D. Hopper, the senior vice-president for information systems at American Airlines, has put it: 'While it is more dangerous than ever to ignore the power of information technology, it is more dangerous still to believe that on its own, an information system can provide an enduring business advantage. The old models no longer apply' (Hopper, 1990). Further caution concerning the claims of competitive advantage based on IOS is warranted in the light of a recent formal modelling approach to competition among IOS providers (Meier, 1990). It is argued that the issue of many IOS investments for competitive advantage put the competing IOS providers in a Prisoner's Dilemma. The outcome predicted by game theory is that the providers have to invest into IOS to avoid competitive disadvantages. Thus, the quest for competitive advantage through investments into IOS is likely to result in IOS becoming a strategic necessity.

In fact, we increasingly observe IOS becoming a ‘must’ for doing business in many industries:

(1) In the health care industry the ordering process relies heavily on IOS as these systems constitute the primary distribution channel nowadays (Cash et al., 1988);

(2) Similarly, IOS in the automotive industry are increasingly common and are likely to be even more important in the face of just-in-time manufacturing environments (Keen 1988);

(3) The travel industry relies almost totally on computerized reservation systems with 95% of all travel agencies connected to one of the major airline reservation systems (Economist, 1987);

(4) Other examples of IOS becoming a strategic necessity can be found in the use of IOS in the financial sector. ATM networks constitute another example. These ATM networks are increasingly interconnected to provide customers with the widest possible access to teller machines (Clemons, 1989).

In this context the following prediction on EDI use is elucidating. Although EDI is used today by fewer than 10 000 US companies, the predicted growth rate of installations of about 50% annually, will lead to more than 100 000 companies using EDI in 1995 (Dillon, 1989).

With technology for implementing IOS readily available, the challenge of using IOS for competitive advantage is to come up with new ways of using IOS and finding innovative extensions to the existing IOS rather than being caught in a Prisoner's Dilemma. By using a strategy of constant hustle, opportunities for first mover advantages can be looked for in the context of IOS. The next section suggests possible directions for IOS innovations and new opportunities for competitive advantage by pointing at a parallel between the evolution of intraorganizational systems and IOS.

## A parallel between the evolution of intraorganizational and interorganizational systems

## Competitive advantage and strategic necessity in the evolution of internal IS

So far, it is becoming clear that IOS of a given functionality are likely to be imitated by competitors within a short time, unless the systems are coupled to specific resources of a company (Clemons and Row, 1989). Thus, these types of IOS tend to become a strategic necessity. It is important to realize that a similar pattern of information technology adoption can be identified in the evolution of internal IS (Vitale, 1986).

In the evolution of internal IS one can identify an initial period where IS pioneers were able to introduce IS for competitive advantage. This competitive advantage was based initially mostly on cost savings. For example, payroll processing and accounting packages allowed significant labour savings. With IS technology becoming more readily available, information systems tended to become a strategic necessity. Most major corporations automated their backoffice in the 1960s and 1970s, thus increasing the efficiency of whole industries with individual firms achieving no competitive advantage. Later, product differentiation with the help of IT became important in addition to cost savings. For example, Porter and Millar (1985) point at the possibilities of enhancing the information component of products. For example, IT is increasingly being used to support the focusing of companies on customer segments through information gathering and processing. In most cases, IT has changed the way we compete (McFarlan, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985). Similarly, use of word processors and other productivity enhancing tools has become a virtual necessity in most corporations today. The current introduction of expert systems and AI-based IS into the corporate environment is also likely to be replicated across industries within a short time.

This evolution of IS reflects the same general pattern of IT moving from a source of competitive advantage to a strategic necessity that we have identified in the case of traditional IOS. This admittedly simplified general pattern of technology adoption, both in the case of internal IS and IOS, suggests a more general parallel in the evolution of these systems. The key notion now is that the parallel between the evolution of internal and interorganizational systems can be extended in the other dimensions that help in finding future innovative IOS systems.

## Evolution of internal IS towards less structured task support

Internal IS comprise a variety of applications ranging from payroll processing and accounting systems to decision support systems and executive information systems. The evolution of IS can be analysed by differentiating along this variety. Initially, IS developments were most successful in automating well-structured procedures. First, well-structured procedures are more easily automated than less-structured procedures. Second and no less import, the potential for cost reductions was greatest in this area, as these activities tend to involve high volumes of transactions. With increasing sophistication of IT less structured activities became accessible to IS support. They are characterized by a low volume of transactions with a high value or cost per transaction. The output in these problem oriented information systems is less defined than in systems that support or automate well-structured procedures. A wealth of research in the last decade on decision support systems, executive information systems, and executive support systems attests to this evolution, that is visualized in Figure 1.

In terms of an evolution of IS, IS that focus on well-structured activities are the 'wave of the past' (Sprague and McNurlin, 1986, p. 10). This does not imply that these systems are less important. Rather they have become a strategic necessity. One may think of an incremental evolution where IS support started with well-structured activities and increasingly added less-structured activities. The idea is not so much that one technology is replaced by another, but rather that new types of IS support are added to the existing ones that have become a common part of the IS portfolio.

## Implications for the evolution of IOS

Analysing the existing IOS along the dimension of the structuredness of the tasks supported, it is clear that current IOS focus mainly on standardized transactions. Most activities supported by IOS today are well-structured activities, such as the ordering and purchasing of a part within a given contractual frame between a manufacturer and a supplier, the making and confirmation of a reservation according to a standardized procedure between airline and travel agency, or making a transaction on a stock exchange according to well-defined rules. In fact, the focus on well-structured data is exactly reflected in the definition of EDI as given above. IOS tend to be cost-justified first of all through increases in efficiency leading to a natural emphasis on transaction volume. In terms of an evolution of IOS we should thus expect a continuing application of IOS to automate well-structured transactions that cross company boundaries, but at the same time our parallel to the internal IS evolution predicts an increasing interest in IOS that address less structured activities.

In some areas first steps in this direction can already be observed, for example, the providers of airline reservation systems are starting to extend their systems to provide support for the travel agent's backoffice (Belitsos, 1988). This support is likely to evolve towards support for travel agents in making decisions regarding the management of the travel agency. Decision support systems are likely to be incorporated into these backoffice systems.

Thus, the overall challenge is to use IOS to support less structured tasks. This idea provides a key to finding innovative new uses of interorganizational systems.

## A new research agenda: high-level IOS

A major research agenda can now be delineated. The goal has to be to gain a better understanding of interorganizational systems that support all activities that cross organizational boundaries. This goal calls for combining the research from areas, such as DSS, that have traditionally focused on internal IS, with research on IOS. Such research is likely to develop new concepts for the use of IOS. In fact, this type of research may give substance to the vision of EDI as ‘a general approach to do business electronically’ (Silverman, 1990).

The research agenda we propose contains several steps.

(1) As a first step characteristics of a high-level IOS need to be identified by drawing on the parallel between internal IS and IOS. Some first characteristics are identified below;

(2) Prototype applications need to be developed. Some candidates for such prototypes are also outlined below;

(3) The effectiveness of these prototypes needs to be evaluated. A major challenge is to come up with appropriate measures for assessing the effect of high-level IOS. The competitive, organizational and economic impact of high-level IOS needs to be evaluated. In the next section we point at research that can be incorporated in this step.

## Characteristics of high-level IOS

We expect high-level IOS to show one or more of the following characteristics.

Process orientation: High-level IOS are likely to be process oriented. While EDI systems, that rely on standardized transactions, are driven by standardized data, such as orders or reservations, the concept of high-level IOS calls for the solving of problems. Research on decision support systems shows that the solving of problems requires a focus on processes rather than data in the system design and control. The process orientation implies that the decision criteria are not completely determined as in EDI systems. One may interpret the contracts, protocols, and standards underlying an EDI system as a highly structured decision model. This characterization of EDI makes EDI an extension of intraorganizational distributed computing. With regard to high-level IOS we expect to see semi-determined decision criteria underlying these systems. Semi-structured decision models with a process orientation will provide appropriate support. In contrast, interorganizational activities, where the decision criteria are completely undetermined, are best served by distributed communication networks where there is no support through structured decision models. Adapting a framework of Swanson (1990, p.32) for distributed decision support, we can now summarize the context of high-level IOS (see Figure 2).

Distributed architecture: Some architectural characteristics of high-level IOS can be deduced from traditional models of DSS. The support of less-structured tasks will have to rely on a collection of models and data (Sprague and Carlson, 1982). As different organizations participate in a high-level IOS, each with some control over the system, distributed data bases and distributed model bases will be a key characteristic of high-level IOS. This calls for a modular architecture of the system possibly with a black-board type communication and coordination structure. High-level IOS will have a distributed architecture with regard to space, time and functions. Thus, the architectural problems faced in the development of high-level IOS are essentially the same problems faced by AI researchers working on distributed problem solving (Durfee, 1988).

Applying Swanson's research on distributed decision support to high-level IOS, it becomes clear that a high-level IOS as an extension of the notion of distributed decision support system 'should both support and be supported by both distributed communications and distributed computing' (Swanson 1990, p. 131). This is indicated by arrows in Figure 2. At this point it is important to realize that high-level IOS may not always be an appropriate form of supporting interorganizational activities. Tasks that cannot be well-structured and that do not rely on a set of clearly defined decision criteria will continue to be best served by more traditional means of distributed communications, such as the telephone or electronic mail.

Filters: The assumption that high-level IOS support more complex tasks implies an increased complexity of information to be exchanged in high-level IOS. To deal with this complexity we expect to find 'filters' in high-level IOS. Filters may take the form of organizational profiles and individual user profiles. In fact, one may think of a hierarchy of filters that defines the access to the system for participants. This is in line with the expectation of more complex user involvement in high-level IOS where it is most likely that these IOS will have sophisticated interface components with users in the participating organizations. While interfaces to low-level IOS can be completely defined, for example in the form of a hierarchy of choices in different menus we expect high-level IOS to provide more flexible interfaces where filters can be adapted and modified to suit each user's needs.

These likely characteristics of a high-level IOS lead us to take a closer look at more practical research challenges. In the next section we point at possible examples of high-level IOS that may be candidates for the development of prototypes of high-level IOS.

## Examples

A general framework for the development of prototypes of high-level IOS may be provided by research on computer supported cooperative work (CSCW) and distributed decision support systems (DDSS). CSCW has attracted a lot of research interest in recent years with the emergence of prototypes of group decision support systems (GDSS). The concepts of experiments involving GDSS, however, tend to focus on cooperating groups within a single organization, even within a single location. It would be highly interesting to expand the research on CSCW by incorporating the potential of IOS. Swanson (1990) has described the three strategies for distributed decision support: information sharing, decision ordering, and negotiated choice. The following two examples try to give directions on how to apply these notions to IOS in order to come up with prototypes of high-level IOS.

## Example 1 - intelligent mail system

Current research on intelligent mail systems may already be regarded as a step towards a working high-level IOS. The key idea is to impose a structure upon mail messages upon which filtering mechanisms can work, e.g. translation between languages not only between protocols. Research on semi-structured messages in electronic mail systems by Malone, et al. (1987a, 1987b) may be a first building block. The underlying structure of messages can be the basis not only of controlled information sharing but also of prioritizations of information which in turn provides the key to decision ordering. A prototype of an intelligent electronic mail system has recently been developed in the context of the CIM environment (Sulonen and Pietikäinen, 1990).

Intelligent mail systems can be directly applied to joint research projects between companies. Given an increasing trend to outsourcing this may become very important. In fact, the idea is to use a high-level IOS as the coordination mechanism for high-level outsourcing in the development process.

## Example 2 - negotiation support systems across organizational boundaries

Secondly, we point at the high-level IOS in contexts that do not reflect a high degree of unison and cooperation. In fact, in this area the most interesting applications may be found eventually. Decision ordering and negotiated choice are the key elements of such a system. The idea is that an IOS may be able to speed up negotiation procedures considerably by independently analysing the options and bits of the negotiating parties. Underlying current research on NSS is the intention to make the choices of negotiating parties visible in relation to a pareto optimal frontier. A high-level IOS that contains tools for negotiation support may eventually be even able to automate the negotiating process in many cases especially if repeated negotiations are involved as in established supplier-manufacturer relationships, as can be found for example in the automobile industry. Research on NSS has produced tools, such as strategic assumption surfacing and testing (Mason and Mitroff, 1981) or multiple criteria decision-making (Bui, 1987), that may be incorporated into high-level IOS.

These examples are only a first step towards practical high-level IOS that will eventually support all interorganizational activity. The multitude of interorganizational activities predicts a corresponding multitude and complexity of high-level IOS.

## Conclusion: an interdisciplinary challenge

The concept of ‘high-level’ IOS extends the notion of IOS to support complex interorganizational interactions. In the long run, this type of research on new higher-level types of IOS is likely to have profound implications on the way work in organizations is viewed. The idea of dynamic networks of specialist organizations (Miles and Snow, 1986) assumes new importance in this context. Similarly, high-level IOS are likely to be another stepping stone towards quasi-firms (Luke, et al., 1986). Once interorganizational systems allow cooperation on complex tasks, such as design and engineering of products, systems design and programming, new forms of organization can be conceived. IOS can then be viewed as a manifestation of collaborative strategy (Astley and Fombrun, 1983). Clearly, one needs to change the perspective from a single organization to a network of organizations as unit of analysis in this context. This in turn poses new challenges for researchers concerning the evaluation and measurement of the effectiveness of these new forms and the underlying information systems.

Another interesting question arising in this context is the problem of the effective introduction of high-level IOS into organizations. First one needs to study the impact of high-level IOS on internal IS structures. Already many organizations need to adapt their internal data structures in order to be EDI-capable today. Second, one may widen the focus and direct attention to nontechnical problems of implementation. A major hurdle in the implementation of current IOS is the lack of skills necessary for introduction, control, maintenance, and adaption. Not only does an IOS cross an organizational boundary, these skills must cross organizational boundaries, too. This problem is likely to be even more pronounced in the context of high-level IOS. Finally, it is an interesting research question to analyse the impact of high-level IOS on organizational structures. The use of high-level IOS may make it interesting to review questions of vertical and horizontal integration.

Implementation issues become even more complicated when interorganizational systems cross national boundaries. Transborder flow of information can create an imposing set of technical, organizational, legal, and political problems. If high-level types of IOS cross national boundaries, cultural aspects of cooperation, however, may provide a most interesting field for research.

In summary, the concept of high-level IOS may be the starting point for a rich set of interdisciplinary research issues that are at the same time of uttermost importance to the practitioner.

## Biographical notes

He received a Diploma degree in computer science from the University of Technology Aachen, FRG, in 1987 and the Ph.D. degree in communication and information sciences from the University of Hawaii, USA, in 1990. His research interests include organizational and systems design theory, interorganizational systems and strategic use of information technology. Dr Meier is a member of ACM, GI, TIMS and SIM.

Ralph H. Sprague is professor of decision science in the college of business administration of the University of Hawaii. He heads the Pacific Research Institute for International Systems Management (PRIISM) and is co-chairman of the Hawaiian International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS). Dr Sprague has published and consulted extensively on decision support systems and strategic information systems.
