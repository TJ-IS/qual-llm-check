# IT directors and IT strategy

COLIN J. COULSON-THOMAS

Salford University, Salford, UK

Abstract: Organizations are transitioning to more flexible and adaptable forms. There is a strategic opportunity for IT that supports the facilitation of change, the building of closer relationships with customers, and the harnessing of human talent. Yet in many organizations there is a gulf of suspicion and misunderstanding between the chief executive and board, and the IT community.

There is a requirement for IT professionals, and particularly IT directors, who share the vision of the ‘network organization’, and who can work at board level to bring it about. There is a need for facilitating directors in the boardroom.

More IT professionals would secure board appointments if they could demonstrate the skills and qualities needed by a company director. IT directors require directorial qualities which are distinct from those demanded of IT managers. They need a broader, and more strategic awareness and perspective.

This article examines the qualities that distinguish directors, and the key issues which should feature on the IT directors agenda. It draws upon three surveys and a report concerning directors and boards, and four organizational surveys.

## Introduction

In a turbulent and demanding business environment companies are facing multiple challenges and opportunities and some of the opportunities derive from the use and application of IT. In response to both longer term trends and short term economic pressures, Chief Executives (CEOs) are seeking to create more flexible, responsive and adaptable organizations.

CEOs are beginning to define their organizations in network terms. As fewer companies are able to deliver 'total value' to customers by themselves, increasingly they are creating networks of relationships, with electronic links forward to customers, backwards to suppliers and sideways to business partners. These networks and supply chains are becoming global, bringing together all those who share a common vision or a particular mission. As a consequence the formulation and implementation of IT strategy increasingly involves co-operation and collaboration across organizational and national boundaries.

While the external priority is relationships with customers, harnessing the talents of people is becoming an internal CEO priority. People are a critical success factor, and the people of the network organization need to be supported by a communications network that is itself flexible and adaptable, and able to facilitate multi-functional, multi-location and multi-national team working.

Companies are seeking to integrate working and learning. IT is not sought for its own sake, but in order to facilitate desired changes. The opportunity for IT derives from the fact that the technology that best supports new ways of working in network organizations is often that which facilitates new approaches to learning. The challenge for IT professionals, and particularly IT directors, is to facilitate and support learning, adaptation and change, the integration of learning and working, and the new networks of relationships which are emerging.

In many companies however there is a communication gap between the CEO and the IT community. IT directors require personal and directorial qualities which are distinct from those demanded of IT managers. They need to share the CEO vision of the responsive organization, and require a broad, strategic awareness and perspective.

IT is of strategic importance to many companies, and there is no upper limit to the number of directors which a company can have. Why is it that IT professionals are not securing more positions on company boards? Why do so many senior IT professionals only carry courtesy director titles, being managers within, rather than directors of, their companies? Do they lack the qualities that would enable them to gain entry to the boardroom? Does the vision of the typical large organization IT function match that of the CEO and board? In what ways should the perspective of an IT director differ from that of an IT manager? This AGM presentation to the Association for Information Technology considers three surveys and a report concerning directors and boardroom qualities, and also four organizational surveys which suggest some answers to these questions. The work upon which the surveys are based was carried out over a 3 year period from May 1988-April 1980.

## Director and boardroom surveys

The first ‘director’ survey (Wakelam, 1989) was undertaken by the Management Centre of Exeter University for the Training Agency. It is based upon a survey of Institute of Directors (IOD) members and non-members. Over 3266 questionnaires were distributed and 373 of 390 responses were analysed. Almost two thirds of the respondents held the job titles of Chairman or Managing Director. The most common turnover of the respondents' companies was represented by the band of £1–10 million, and this covered over four out of ten respondents.

A parallel survey (Coulson-Thomas, 1990a) undertaken by Adaptation Ltd for the IOD involved a questionnaire and follow-up interview programme. 2700 copies of the questionnaire were distributed to members of the IOD who were chairmen of various company boards. It is believed that the sample group selected is representative of the distribution of company size in the UK. Consequently, the most common company size was represented by a turnover in the range of £1–10 million. Two hundred and eighteen completed questionnaires were received, and 134 of these respondents indicated that they would be willing to be contacted to discuss their views regarding professional development of and for the board. Three-quarters of the responses were from those holding the job titles of Chairman or Chairman and Managing Director. Over a third of the respondents' organizations were engaged in manufacturing or production as a main activity.

In order to relate the findings of the Exeter survey (Wakelam, 1989) to the role and purpose of the board, a follow-on survey (Coulson-Thomas and Wakelam, 1990, 1991) of the views of chairmen and CEOs was carried out jointly by Exeter University and Adaptation Ltd. Out of 122 questionnaires distributed 56 replies were received which covered 60 boards. Subsequently a further exercise (Coulson-Thomas, 1990c) was undertaken by Adaptation Ltd for the Department of Computing of Surrey University, which related the findings of these director studies to the development of IT directors.

## Organizational surveys

In the ‘responsive organization’ or first ‘organizational’ survey (Coulson-Thomas and Brown, 1989) a questionnaire was sent to some 300 individuals. The 136 written replies included 102 completed questionnaires of which 100 received prior to the cut-off date were used. Some 38% of the respondents were chairmen and over 60% held the job titles of either chairman, chief executive or managing director.

The returned questionnaires covered in excess of 2.97 million employees. Almost half of the responses were from organizations employing in excess of 10 000 people, while 46% of the respondents identified themselves as from the manufacturing sector. Approaching two thirds of the responses were from organizations with a turnover in excess of £500 million, while 38% of responses were from organizations with a turnover in excess of £1 billion.

In the ‘beyond quality’ or second ‘organizational’ survey (Coulson-Thomas and Brown, 1990) a questionnaire was sent to those most likely to have an overview of the relationship between an organization and its customers. Sufficient questionnaires were distributed to obtain 100 replies. A response rate of over 34% was achieved. Of the respondents 44 were chairmen, chief executives or managing directors.

The participants represented predominantly large organizations. Forty three of the organizations employed in excess of 10 000 people. Over half employed more than 5000 people and 85% more than 1000 people. Overall the survey covered more than 2 million employees.

The turnover of 60% of the respondents' organizations exceeded £500 million. The turnover of about half of the organizations exceeded £1 billion, while the turnover of 17 of the 100 organizations participating in the survey exceeded £10 billion.

The third ‘organizational’ survey (Coulson-Thomas, 1990b) was concerned with ‘internationalization’ and was undertaken by Adaptation Ltd for Surrey European Management School. Approximately 540 questionnaires were distributed to European and international companies, and to a selection of UK professional associations. Responses were received from 93 organizations, covering over 2.7 million employees and with a combined turnover of some £320 billion. Over 1 million people are individual members of the professional associations participating in the survey. About half of the responses were from individuals at board level.

About a fifth of the organizations, and over a quarter of the companies, returning questionnaires had in excess of 50 000 employees, and eight out of ten of the companies employed over 10 000 employees. The turnover of over four out of ten of the participating organizations exceeded £1 billion, while over seven out of ten had a turnover in excess of £500 million. Over a third of UK company respondents, and over half of the European and international respondents, gave ‘manufacturing/production’ as the main activity of their organizations.

The fourth organizational survey (Coulson-Thomas and Coe, 1991) was designed to validate the findings of the earlier surveys and monitor the achievement of major organizational change. Out of 422 questionnaires distributed, 62 completed responses were obtained. In total the survey covered organizations employing 1.3 million people. The turnover of over six out of ten participating organizations exceeded £1 billion, and the combined turnover of all the participants was in the order of £180 billion. Over six out of ten of the respondents were the chairmen and/or CEOs of their organizations.

An IT professional seeking a board level appointment needs to develop and/or demonstrate a capability that relates to the function of the board. Before considering the question of specific boardroom competencies and qualities one must first determine the overall function of the board as perceived by those making board appointments. In the 'follow-on' survey (Coulson-Thomas and Wakelam, 1990) questionnaire recipients were asked, not about the legal duties and responsibilities of directors, or the functions of boards in general but about how they saw the function of their board.

The top ten functions of the board as described by respondents are listed in Table 1. The largest single number of responses could be summarized as setting the policy, objectives, strategy or vision of the company. If one includes the direction of policy and strategy, then seven out of ten respondents described the function of their boards in pro-active terms of establishing policy, objectives, strategy or vision.

The next largest number of replies could be grouped as a reactive or monitoring function of controlling or reviewing strategies, objectives, shareholders or staff. If one added those who referred to monitoring the performance of top executive management, then over seven out of ten of the respondents referred to a reviewing, monitoring and controlling role. Establishing and reviewing objectives and strategy are clearly related.

Discussions with company chairmen (Coulson-Thomas and Wakelam, 1990, 1991) suggest that in future more boards will spend less time establishing and monitoring procedures, and will devote more time to initiating and facilitating processes for ongoing adaptation and change. Creating a flexible network, and a framework within which change can continue to occur, is crucially important when the life of a requirement may be less than the timescale for achieving a significant restructuring or change of attitude (Coulson-Thomas and Coe, 1991).

If significant change is to occur, sustained and shared board and top management commitment is of crucial importance. Increasingly, the directorial challenge facing the board (Coulson-Thomas, 1991a) is to:

(1) Define the opportunities in the external business environment;

(2) Ensure that the company has access to adequate people, technology and organization;

(3) Ensure that a flexible and supportive network exists that embraces all those who need to cooperate in value chains;

(4) Ensure that the company has effective management processes for ensuring that this network is utilized and relevant resources are applied to the profitable exploitation of those opportunities.

IT professionals seeking board level positions should consider the extent to which their perspective, skills and experience would enable them to work effectively with others in response to this challenge.

A key priority of the board should be to ensure that a company is able to tap human talent, and learn and adapt in order to survive. This requires processes for continuing learning and adaptation (Coulson-Thomas and Brown, 1989, 1990). Three-quarters of the participants in the project undertaken for the Training Agency believe the effectiveness of their companies' boards could be improved (Coulson-Thomas and Wakelam, 1991). Little evidence emerged during this project of experimentation with new ways of organizing boards.

## Implications for IT professionals with boardroom aspirations

These findings suggest that efforts to define and build management competencies will not of themselves lead to the competencies considered desirable in company directors. 'Hybrid managers' may or may not have the potential to become IT directors. Experience derived from a track record in moving between functions will not necessarily lead to what is sought in new appointees to a board. Possession of such qualities as strategic awareness or objectivity may be the key question, not the means or route by which these qualities are acquired.

In addition to directorial competencies an effective director, whether or not a director of IT, needs knowledge of the role of the board, and of the legal duties and responsibilities of directors, together with an understanding of financial and other requirements. Directors also need an awareness of developments in the business environment such as ethical and environmental considerations. There are also boardroom matters to consider such as the succession, assessment and remuneration of directors, the structure and operation of the board itself and effective boardroom practice.

Directors need to retain their objectivity and independence, and a sense of balance and perspective. A criticism of some IT professionals is that they can appear, on occasion, to be ‘in the pockets of IT suppliers’. Many CEOs would like their IT team to be ‘more robust’. At the same time CEOs, on occasion, bypass and undermine the authority of their IT departments by dealing direct with IT suppliers.

As more companies operate ‘total quality’ programmes, customers may assume reliability and efficiency and look beyond quality and, where they have a choice, associate with the companies whose values they share. This suggests that an ability to understand and empathize with values may become of increasing importance in the boardroom.

## Assessing boardroom contributions

There is little consensus concerning the contribution expected from members of boards, and only one in eight companies appears to operate any form of periodic, formal appraisal of personal effectiveness in the boardroom (Coulson-Thomas and Wakelam, 1991). It has been suggested that the role of the Chairman should be extended to cover those items listed in Figure 3 (Coulson-Thomas and Wakelam, 1991). Chairmen should monitor the effectiveness of their boards, and that of individual directors on an ongoing basis. The board itself could participate in such a review, perhaps assisted by external and objective facilitators. The chairman should also periodically review the size and composition of a board.

Increasingly boards are being expected to add value and bring about significant change. As a greater number of boards focus on the need to create more flexible and adaptable network organizations, one may see further appointments of facilitating directors to supplement or replace traditional functional directors in order to enhance the ability of boards to cope with change. Rank Xerox (UK) Ltd has adopted this approach. Such facilitating roles will be occupied by those who can manage change and work with others, those who understand how IT can best be applied to harness skills and build external relationships.

Facilitating directors with longer-term responsibilities relating to reshaping the organization to meet strategic business development opportunities, and establishing and supporting its business systems requirements, may assist a company to achieve longer-term change without losing a grip on the need to deliver current business objectives. IT professionals with directorial qualities might aspire to such facilitating roles in the boardroom.

So far as the development requirements of senior managers and potential directors are concerned, human resources and marketing are the functional priorities, followed by business policy and strategy, and corporate planning (Coulson-Thomas, 1990b). Information technology does not rank highly as a development priority in a functional sense. This is because it appears to be increasingly regarded as a supporting and integral element of other functions either as well as, or rather than, a function in its own right.

Many CEOs put a higher importance than IT practitioners upon management aspects of IT such as organizational and human resources issues. They are also more concerned with networking-up organizations. The IT professionals with their narrower perspective tend to be preoccupied with the bewildering range of new products and services emanating from suppliers, recruiting and retaining staff with scarce skills, ‘networking-up departments and work-groups’, and corporate demands for overhead cost savings.

As more work is undertaken in project groups and teams, and non-core activities are spun off or set up as distinct service companies, it is likely that there will be a blurring of executive and consulting roles. The possession of a broader view would equip the IT practitioner with a more balanced perspective and allow short-term considerations to be seen and assessed in a wider context.

## IT requirements

The IT architectural requirements of the network organization are likely to be different from those of the traditional and more bureaucratic organization. Key distinguishing features identified by Adaptation Ltd (Coulson-Thomas, 1990c, 1991a) are presented in Figure 7. In the network organization information is valued to the extent that it improves understanding and the quality of decisions. There is likely to be less emphasis upon processing standard transactions, and more emphasis upon manipulating and refining information to build and share understanding, allow a more flexible response to individual requirements, and support new ways of working and learning.

The people of the network organization are supported by IT that facilitates both their work and their learning (Coulson-Thomas, 1991b). This facilitating technology needs to be flexible and adaptable in allowing multi-functional, multi-location and multi-national teams to work together, thus facilitating learning and development. It should also allow not only data, but also thoughts, insights and concepts to be shared, refined and amended.

IT professionals need to give more attention to the external dimension. The achievement of flexibility, and supporting relationships and learning, will become of greater importance than the traditional IT management issue of centralization versus decentralization.

As more network organizations embrace customers and suppliers, compatibility of systems is likely to become a more significant issue. Incompatibility of systems is emerging as a major problem within the field of joint ventures, arrangements, mergers and acquisitions. It is also a significant barrier for some companies who are contemplating geographic expansion into Eastern Europe. The management of integration in the establishment of multi-functional, multi-location and multi-organization networks would appear to be a common element of a number of the problem areas identified by organizational survey (Coulson-Thomas and Brown, 1990) interviewees.

To build flexible and international networks which meet a distinct set of requirements may require new forms of relationships between suppliers and users of IT (see Figure 9). Rather than packaging and marketing IT goods and services, more IT suppliers may seek to enter into strategic partnership relationships with major multi-nationals, making their underlying technology available and working in joint supplier-customer teams to customize both hardware and software to the requirements of particular networks. Adopting such a role enables these suppliers to break out of the commodity products trap.

## Management implications

Organizations are becoming more fluid, facilitating networks rather than bureaucracies (Figure 10). Interest in rules and procedures is waning, but a desire for the identification of tasks, the establishment of teams, and new processes for achieving adaptation and change is growing.

A more balanced perspective on information technology is emerging among organizational (Coulson-Thomas and Brown, 1989) survey interviewees. Cynicism, resulting from an earlier generation of technology which has not delivered the hoped for benefits, is giving way to a realization that what is important is the use and application of technology not the technology per se (Coulson-Thomas and Brown, 1989). In the main, the major barriers concern people, their skills and attitudes and the management of technology rather than the technology itself. There is recognition that managers, especially senior managers, are unwilling to change the way they operate to meet the demands of technology. Technology is seen not as an end in itself but as a set of tools to be used, where appropriate, to support the way managers prefer to work and think and enable more of them to move closer to their full potential. Technology needs to be applied to support professionals and knowledge workers, and management processes to achieve change and adaptation.

## Human resource requirements

There appears to be a shortage of those with the breadth of view and other qualities required for board level appointments (Coulson-Thomas and Wakelam, 1990). Many Chairmen are supply constrained when it comes to making new appointments to the board. The key requirement of Chairmen and CEOs is for individuals who are able to develop a perspective of the organization as a whole and facilitate change.

In theory an IT professional concerned with the strategic use of IT ought to be well placed to develop a relatively broad perspective. The building of effective electronic links with customers, suppliers and business partners ought to extend a perspective beyond the confines of one organization. In this sense, a period spent within the IT function could provide valuable experience en route to a board level appointment.

## Perceptions of IT professionals

A further and fundamental question arising from interviews carried out in the course of the 'organizational' surveys (Coulson-Thomas and Brown, 1989, 1990) is the extent to which experts in various functional fields are barriers to, or facilitators of, change. IT professionals are often perceived by CEOs as introverted, departmental in their thinking, insecure and overly defensive of past practices and historic investments in IT (Coulson-Thomas, 1990c). These are not perceptions that open up a path to the boardroom.

A perception also appears to be growing that it is sometimes easier for those in other functions to take a broader view and be objective than it is for those in the function that, in an organizational sense, should be most concerned with the problem in question. Hence outsiders are brought in to head IT functions.

IT professionals appear to be perceived by CEOs as more technical and introverted than managers in general, and lacking in ‘people’ skills. Some chairmen and CEOs have suggested that the IT function attracts those with an interest in things rather than people (Coulson-Thomas, 1990c). People skills are becoming increasingly important as more IT professionals are expected to work in multi-functional teams, and barriers to the successful implementation of IT solutions are identified as behavioural and managerial rather than technical. An appreciation of how people build their understanding and work together in teams is needed if these are to be effectively facilitated through the application of IT.

Many IT professionals appear to specialize within a particular area such as systems management, WAN or LAN management (Coulson-Thomas, 1990c). The IT management challenge will increasingly be to integrate and encourage mobility between these areas, and encourage greater interaction with users and colleagues in other organizations in order to make the greatest value added contribution to customer requirements.

## Conclusions

The ambitious IT professional should consider the lessons set out in Figure 13 which lists those factors which would appear to be of some value in achieving a directorship. An aspiring director of IT, or an IT professional seeking to obtain a seat on the board, needs to acquire a perspective and capability relevant to the function of the board. An ability to understand the respective contributions of, and inter-relationships between, facilitating technology, facilitating processes and facilitating competencies within the context of the organization as a whole, is likely to become more important.

Key roles for IT directors and central IT functions are the integration of IT strategy into general business strategy and the definition of the core technology that will provide the framework of the more responsive and flexible network organization that is desired by CEOs. Other roles are the management of change, and the facilitation of more effective team working and continuing learning and adaptation.
