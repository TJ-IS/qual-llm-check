# Catalyst: a programme for change within British Airways

JANET WINDEATT

IM Strategic Development Manager, British Airways

ISOBEL KNOX

IT Education Manager, British Airways

Abstract: It is perhaps a truism to state in today's information age that an organization's ability to exploit IT is dependent on the extent to which its managers understand how to apply IT to business advantage. But the biggest challenges are often simple to state and this is one of them. The question is 'how' do you get managers to understand? And how do you know when they understand well enough? These were questions within British Airways which needed to be answered in order to develop an IT education programme for senior management in 1987.

## The business background

By the mid 1980s competition in the airline industry had become fierce, especially in the USA where airlines were jockeying for position after deregulation. In BA privatization was looming and there was a feeling worldwide that airlines had to learn to cope as businesses rather than as state protected flag carriers. They looked to IT to help them compete. One of the most dramatic success stories for American airlines was the computer reservation systems. These enabled airlines to increase market share by creating a new type of distribution channel. They also generated revenue and gave airlines useful data on their customers, enabling them to set up schemes such as frequent flyer programmes. Where in the past systems had been used largely to automate existing processes, these new systems made it threateningly clear to the whole industry the changing role of IT and its potential for competitive advantage in several areas.

British Airways, along with other major airlines, had been a pioneer of computing since the mid-60s when the reservation system was first built. By the mid-80s British Airways Information Management group had grown to be one of the largest commercial systems departments outside the US, with two large datacentres at Heathrow, 160 mid-range processors, and over 200 000 terminals connected to its worldwide network. Information systems were already at the heart of the airline's business. Marketing systems supported a worldwide sales force, while operational systems assisted in the complexities of planning and controlling the day-to-day delivery of airline services. Executive information systems were being developed so that managers could monitor the organization's effectiveness and performance, while decision support systems were being designed to provide the capability of manipulating information in order to gain a better understanding of the potential consequences of business decisions.

Like most successful companies, British Airways continued to look to IT to provide business advantage by reducing costs, improving service, providing better control of the business and improving management decision-making. The challenge for the future was for business managers to manage this growing investment and to understand the strategic potential of IT. They needed to learn more about the opportunity that IT offered for transforming the business and to understand more fully the consequences of implementing a strategic system on the marketplace, on their customers and on their own staff. In addition, they needed to be able to articulate their business requirements in ways that their IT colleagues could understand.

On the IT side, the systems people needed to improve their understanding of the business and to be able to explain technology in business terms. Both sides needed to work together more effectively if BA was to compete with the fast-moving, innovative carriers that were competing in the global marketplace.

It was against this backdrop of mounting business pressures that John Watson, Director of Information

Management (IM) for British Airways, launched a culture change initiative in 1987 called 'Reorientation'. This programme was designed to revolutionize attitudes toward IT on both sides. He wanted to build a business-focused IT department and to educate managers in the rest of the business about the changing role of IT and their changing role in managing this resource. 'Reorientation' was built on the theme of partnership and consisted of a number of components including a restructuring of the department, a significant change in practices and procedures and the introduction of a major IT education programme for senior airline managers. This education initiative was called Catalyst.

The Catalyst programme was established with the stated objective of ‘increasing the awareness level of non-IT managers concerning the issues involved in managing IT, the benefits from its application and its fundamental importance to the airline’s current and future success; while also providing managers with the knowledge and understanding to more effectively plan, manage and exploit the IT investment.’

## How was the programme developed?

In developing the Catalyst programme, extensive research was conducted in the external marketplace, meeting with a variety of consultants, academics and business managers of other large companies. The aim was to gain a better appreciation of the approaches to IT education and to understand what was available externally.

In parallel, research was conducted within BA, meeting with senior managers individually and in groups to discuss their views and priorities concerning IT education. We also reviewed existing training programmes within the airline to determine which programmes could best integrate IT within their syllabus. After collecting a wide range of data, a detailed strategy paper was developed, defining the main objectives of the Catalyst programme, and outlining the plans for implementation. This paper was reviewed with a number of senior executives within the organization to (a) get their views on our planned approach, and (b) solicit their support for this major initiative. It was felt that clear, visible senior management support was and continues to be crucial to the success of this type of initiative.

The Catalyst programme was also believed to be a 'window on the IM department' and, as such, it was important to develop an effective technology-based administrative system to assist with a range of support activities including the tracking of enrolments, attendance, feedback, correspondence, etc. Therefore, a PC-based database was developed that not only contributed to the marketing and administration of the programme, but also provided valuable management information as the programme grew and developed.

In addition, it was decided to brand the programme with the name ‘Catalyst’, and develop a distinctive logo and letterhead to be used in all communications (See Figure 2). The catalyst name helped to position the programme as an enabler rather than an initiator of change.

## What are the main components of the programme?

The first component to be introduced was a monthly management briefing series which provided BA managers with an opportunity to hear some of the best IT speakers in the world including Dr Peter Keen of the International Centre for Information Technology in Washington, DC, Dr Warren McFarlan, MIS Director of Harvard Business School, Paul Strassman, President Strassman, Inc., Tony Cleaver, Chief Executive, IBM UK, and many others. The briefings were designed to stimulate thinking and to provide an external view concerning IT management issues. Speakers were invited to present on a variety of IT-related subjects, but to do so in business not technical terms.

As Catalyst was a voluntary programme, we had to ensure that the content was both interesting and relevant so that managers would consider it a worthwhile investment of their time. In addition, a marketing plan had to be developed to ensure that the ‘customers’ (i.e. airline managers) were aware of the programme details and what benefits they could achieve through participation.

The management briefing series proved successful in attracting the participation of senior managers from a variety of departments. The success of the series seemed to be attributed to three key points:

(1) The series only featured very experienced speakers who were well-known authorities in their field of expertise, thus building a reputation of quality for the programme;

(2) Subject areas were carefully chosen and speakers were reminded that they were addressing a business audience and that their presentations should focus on the business aspects and issues rather than technology;

(3) A British Airways Director shared the platform each month with the external guest and presented an airline perspective of the theme being addressed.

These briefings played a key part in raising the awareness level of managers concerning the issues surrounding the management of IT. In 1990, we broadened the range of topics offered under the Catalyst briefing series to include an emphasis on other business subjects of relevance to the airline, i.e. creative thinking, with Edward de Bono, and environmental issues with John Elkington, Director of Sustainability. Each of these speakers was asked to include the IT implications when addressing their subject area. This allowed for the continued introduction of high quality speakers while emphasizing the integral part that IT plays across business areas. In the 1992 programme currently under development, a range of business leaders are again being invited who will speak on a variety of subjects with the intent of focusing on business issues and the implications for IT rather than IT itself. Sir Colin Marshall, the Chief Executive, and a number of directors will also participate during the coming year, as participants' feedback has indicated that this is considered a worthwhile and interesting part of the programme.

As the programme developed we introduced a new element. In conjunction with the main briefing, either a lunch or dinner was organized so that invited airline managers could meet informally with the main speaker and have an opportunity to talk in more depth about the issue at hand. Although this was initially a sideline to the programme, it is now viewed as an important part of the briefing series. Many of our directors and senior managers have indicated that they find these informal sessions to be extremely beneficial.

• senior management input and commitment

In addition to the briefing series, we worked with external consultants on the development of three courses for managers aimed at varying needs and experience levels. The first course, entitled 'The Primer', was a one-day introduction to important IT concepts and an overview of the use of IT in the airline industry.

The second course, entitled ‘Managing IT’ was a two-day course exploring a range of management issues such as analysing the systems portfolio, developing an effective business case, and identifying strategic opportunities for IT. Both courses were tailored to the airline and reviewed ways in which systems were used to meet the business needs of a variety of departments across the company.

The third course is called 'IT as a Strategic Tool' and has been designed exclusively for senior management. Attendance is by invitation only from the Director of Information Management. This three-day course provides senior managers with an opportunity to look at ways in which other companies have achieved competitive advantage through the application of technology and to discuss together some of the ways that BA can gain increasing value from its IT investment.

Each course has been designed to benefit from a combination of external consultants working in conjunction with internal presenters. This provides a breadth of ideas and experiences from other industries while ensuring its relevance to British Airways and its use of systems.

## What have been the results so far?

Many education programmes have objective measurements in the form of course work or exams. We took a conscious decision not to do this as we felt it would discourage some people from attending. One consequence of this decision, however, is that it is not easy to measure in concrete terms the impact which the programme has had. Instead, we have chosen to rely on regular feedback, occasional surveys, interviews with participants and anecdotal evidence to measure the success of the programme.

As a voluntary programme, attendance rates are certainly one valid measure of success. To date, more than three-quarters of the senior management population of the airline have participated in a Catalyst event. The directors continue to support the programme and often attend events even when they are not the scheduled speaker!

Two surveys conducted over the last few years have indicated that through participation in the Catalyst programme, managers are increasing their awareness of IT issues and gaining increased confidence in their use of IT. One interesting side-benefit of the programme that became apparent through survey results, was that managers felt they had also increased their understanding of the business by looking at IT in relationship to business issues. In addition, managers have indicated that the programme has improved their working relationship with their Information Management colleagues as they have an improved understanding of the terminology, concepts and issues surrounding IT. The standing of the IM Department is much higher now than when the programme started and undoubtedly Catalyst has played a key part in achieving this change. Managers have indicated that they appreciate IM taking the initiative to bridge the gap between technology and the business. The Catalyst programme also received external recognition by winning the Silver Award in the Computer Weekly/PA Consulting IT Training Awards in 1988.

## What are the challenges ahead?

The main challenge is to evolve the programme in conjunction with the increasing demands being placed on our managers to exploit new IT opportunities and gain increased advantages from our existing IT investment. Catalyst is well-positioned to help managers use IT more effectively in their business and to this end, the programme will take more of a coaching role and rely less on teaching concepts. This will require focus on areas such as:

(1) Aligning systems with business strategy;

(2) Changing technical platforms;

(3) Understanding the system's lifecycle;

(4) The HR implications of new technology;

(5) The potential of new technologies;

(6) Corporate portfolio approach to systems development;

(7) Value for money from the IT investment.

During the current business climate, when management numbers are being reduced and there are many demands on managers' time, the challenge, with a voluntary education programme, will be to continue to offer relevant, timely, quality modules that managers will determine to be a good investment of their time.

To achieve this we will build on the lessons learned from the early days of the programme.

(1) Careful research including a review of the external marketplace is an important aspect of development;

(2) Senior management input is critical to ensure that issues of relevance to the business and the IT departments are addressed. Gaining the commitment of the senior executives also creates a climate where the education is valued and encouraged;

(3) Quality of content and presentation is fundamental for success;

(4) Close attention must be paid to marketing and administering the programme – however good, it won't sell itself.

Our aim with the on-going development of the Catalyst programme is to help British Airways' managers continue to develop the necessary skills and competencies in IT management so that their use of IT continues to become a major competitive weapon for the airline in the next decade.

Is it easy? No. Challenging? Yes! A number of challenges still remain – what is the best way to continue to market the programme and ensure that the right level of management participate? How should we reinforce the learning to ensure that a change of behaviour results? How do we strike the right balance between what an audience wants in a programme and what we want to give? And how do we do all this in an environment where there is an increasing need to constrain costs with an equal need for fresh stimulus, ‘entertainment’ and high standards?

These are challenges that many people in the education arena face. By applying some of the principles used in business, i.e. focus on quality, customer service, market research, and on-going improvement, a strong base can be established on which to build. As British Airways strives to be the world's favourite airline, we are applying similar principles to become the airline's favourite education programme.

Janet Windeatt has extensive experience of both information technology and management education in the USA as well as the UK. Before joining British Airways in 1987, she worked for one of the world's premier computer manufacturers and a major international management consultancy.

Isobel Knox has worked in British Airways for 3 years in the areas of management education and communication.
