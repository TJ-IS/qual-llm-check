# Information requirements prototyping for decision support systems

DANIEL N. BRAUNSTEIN, THOMAS W. LAUER and DAVID P. DOANE

School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Michigan, USA

Abstract: Information requirements determination for Decision Support Systems (DSS) is often inadequately handled due to premature structuring thus precluding dynamic changes in the framing of the problem by managers. A review of relevant concepts in the behavioural decision making literature is provided. Case studies from three large business organizations illustrate system problems with information requirements. The authors suggest prototyping as a more valid approach to determining information requirements.

## Introduction

The research literature on decision support systems has pointed out the relevance of behavioural decision research for effective system design (Sage et al, 1983; Stabell, 1983; and Ginzberg and Ariav, 1986). The system user needs an appropriate information structure plus a valid model of the decision process. The promise of powerful DSS may be unfulfilled because of inadequate understanding of the unfolding dynamic nature of the decision making process and its impact upon the information requirements. DSS developers may be prematurely structuring the managerial decision task due to inadequate information requirements determination (IRD).

The DSS concept was developed by Keen, Scott-Morton, and others in the 1970s to couple the capabilities of the computer with individual cognitive processes for the purpose of improving decision quality for semi-structured managerial problems. Nevertheless, Turban (1988) comments that the majority of DSS are used by technical professionals (financial analysts, insurance brokers, production schedulers, auditors), planners and researchers. Sprague and McNurlin (1986 p. 364) state that 'DSS to most practitioners meant a computer-based financial planning system'. Most of the decision tasks for these functions would be quite structured.

If these descriptions are accurate, DSS have not yet reached large numbers of their intended users. Part of the reason may be the changing nature of the information requirements for semi-structured managerial problems (Mintzberg et al, 1976). We believe that DSS often fail to meet user expectations because technical design of database management systems and model building occupy most designer attention, and because developers typically structure their systems based upon arbitrary definitions of information requirements. This paper examines why information requirements change by reviewing behavioural decision research and three ‘mini’ case studies, and suggests a strategy for dealing with the problem.

## Problem overview

In order to analyse why DSS designers make this fundamental error, it is helpful to take a perspective similar to that of Markus and Robey (1988, p. 587) who show that although information system managers rationally attempt to design systems in order 'to satisfy organizational needs for information', change often takes place due to unpredictable interactions between the new technology and the users. System managers find it difficult to obtain a valid reading of the information needs of potential DSS users because

(1) Users cannot possibly understand their own long-run needs until they gain experience using an initial version of the system;

(2) System managers possess a frame of reference, and therefore a political point of view, based upon their own previous experiences and professional needs which may, or may not, coincide with those of the end-users;

(3) System managers may not know how to investigate and document requirements.

We believe that intendedly rational behaviour from the separate and limited perspectives of the information system manager, and from the end-user organization, combine through organizational political processes to produce a prematurely structured result – a DSS which often becomes a hardwired political compromise. IRD cannot be structured in the same manner as an electrician would determine the wiring requirements of a house. Unlike common systems development life-cycle methodology, a final IRD check-list cannot be completed before managerial experience, with a prototypical DSS design, is obtained. Although systems analysis and design textbooks [Powers et al, 1984, p. 84] refer to analysing goals and organizational structure, they tend to view information gathering in mechanistic terms, casting the systems analyst as an investigator of Platonic absolutes, not unlike the aforementioned electrician. An observer may conclude that involving users in feedback loops with the design team is a political afterthought, rather than a functional need.

Some extant studies have examined the factors involved in IRD [Naumann et al, 1980]. Following a review of cognitive, organizational and logical considerations in DSS information requirements determination, Sage et al (1983) point out that there is no way we will know in advance everything regarding IRD for a specific decision problem. Nevertheless, they imply that early IRD is quite possible and proper, since they state [p. 161] that ‘relatively straightforward concepts, when properly applied, can often provide very good explanations’ of managerial information requirements.

Others have not been so sanguine regarding this possibility. In his contribution to Bennett's 'Building Decision Support Systems', Stabell (1983, p. 223) states that 'the implications of building DSS for the manager-as-decision-maker-as-user are seldom explored. The key use of the word decision in DSS is to motivate the need for systems that support rather than replace decision makers'. He advocates the use of DSS to create a normative channelling of support for the managerial decision maker. In a study which supports this position, Carlson (1983) examines 56 DSS case examples and reports that DSS do not provide decision makers with familiar representations of their problems to support better conceptualization. He also reports that existing DSS often unrealistically segment the phases of the decision process, without providing convenient short-term memory aids.

When many end-users think of decision support, they immediately mention their use of spreadsheets. The growth of spreadsheets for decision support purposes has been substantial as a result of the availability of inexpensive personal computers. End-users find them easy to use and exceedingly useful for combining information and examining its sensitivity on a 'what-if' basis. This is certainly one form of decision support. However, after a period of use, managers may find that spreadsheets have shortcomings. Sprague and McNurlin (1986) claim that the analytical capability that can be embedded in a spreadsheet is often limited and relationships become locked in to the tabular format. In packaged software, these limitations can be overcome only at the cost of increasing the user's time and effort in learning more sophisticated software tools.

Proliferation of personal computers in the last few years has increased DSS potential, but not necessarily DSS achievements. Hidden costs of an end-user DSS (including database maintenance, revision and backup) comprise frustrating barriers to its use unless the user is thoroughly convinced of DSS value, and is familiar with DSS technology. At present, most end-use seems to make use of existing technology rather than assimilate new technology. Given these limitations, it is not surprising that researchers, familiar with management applications areas, continually wonder when DSS will begin to realize the intentions of the designers (Wynne, 1982). Are the designers of DSS creating the same kind of schism between themselves and managerial users as that which has plagued operations research? The high cost of such internecine warfare can be observed in the often acrimonious debate on managerial use of operations research applications models (TIMS/ ORSA, 1989).

## Research on the decision process

Although experts on decision-making research have many different notions as to what constitutes the managerial decision process, they do agree that it includes individual cognitive components, a search activity, and an organizational framework for action (Ungson and Braunstein, 1982). The field can be viewed as consisting of three research areas: information processing, problem solving and organizational decision processes. Information processing deals with cognitive interpretation of information inputs and adaptive mechanisms which are employed to deal with the information. The work of Tversky and Kahneman (1974) on individual heuristics and biases is a well-known example of this approach. Problem solving covers the actual processes which decision makers use to approach information interpretation. Simon (1979) and his various colleagues have provided milestones in this research path. Organizational decision processes focus upon interpersonal mechanisms which both stimulate and respond to individual and multi-person decision-making. The 'garbage can' theory of March and Olsen (1976) is a note-worthy example.

Decision theorists from all three areas have reported research findings which suggest that stable behavioural mechanisms could be augmented by DSS. For example, Tversky and Kahneman's work describes individual adaptive heuristics i.e. anchoring forecasts to small biased samples of data, underestimation of range and variance, and ignoring known base rates.

These heuristics are often adaptive for managers because their work requires handling many sources of information inputs in very short time cycles (Mintzberg, 1971). Bazerman [1986, p. 41] claims that 'it should be vehemently stressed that the use of these heuristics results in far more adequate than inadequate decisions'. An appropriately designed DSS should take these heuristics into account (Lauer and Jenkins, 1984). For example, graphical and tabular displays could provide very striking signals to the user regarding sample size, range, variance, and base-rate characteristics (Chambers et al, 1983).

What about differences in cognitive interpretation of the display? At first glance, it would seem that managerial differences in cognitive style could be assessed and interpreted by an interactive DSS design. Unfortunately, reviews of the research literature have shown that this may be impractical (Huber, 1983; Ramaprasad, 1987). This is mainly due to the fact that current measurement of cognitive style is neither reliable nor valid enough for such a purpose. Even if improved, it may not help to handle overlaps between and within user styles. System designers must also be aware that preferences in formats for information display may be a function of the 'framing' of the context of the decision problem as well as the individual manager's cognitive preferences.

Huber (1981) suggests that unique information requirements of organizational decision environments would be aided by DSS functions expressly designed for them. Environments must be considered in the context of the point that information systems facilitate an organization's evaluation of decisions already made (Feldman and March, 1981). In making sense, often retrospectively, of initial commitments to action, organizations create easily interpreted symbols for their membership, as well as signals which influence behaviour. Therefore, information representation as well as information evaluation by a DSS should be structured in a form which is sensitive to the symbolic use of information in each decision environment.

## Implications for DSS Design

Ginzburg and Ariav (1986) discuss three DSS development methodologies – prototyping, (Naumann and Jenkins, 1982), “ROMC” (Sprague and Carlson, 1982) and Stabell’s (1983) version of decision analysis. Prototyping requires delivery of a simple working version of the system to the user. The developer quickly iterates and then refines the system by incorporating corrections and modifications suggested by users. The cyclic trial and error process continues until the user is reasonably well-satisfied with the system, or the project is abandoned. “ROMC” characterizes DSS by representations which support cognitive processing of data, operations which support decision search processes which enable the user to manipulate the objects of his decision environment, memory aids which relieve some of the cognitive burden, and control structures to facilitate user/system communications. Stabell's decision analysis approach compares actual decision processes with a normative model, to manage incremental improvements towards normative decision making forms.

According to Ginzberg and Ariav (1986), these three methodologies lie on a continuum with prototyping on one side, decision analysis on the other side, and ROMC somewhere in the middle. Thus, moving from prototyping towards decision analysis implies

(1) Greater conformity to a linear project life-cycle design philosophy, with concomitant importance of getting the system information requirements correct early in the system development process;

(2) Reduced recognition and respect for the user's current approaches to decision making;

(3) An increasingly normative approach, in general.

## Information requirements prototyping

Errors in IRD occur in early stages of the development life cycle. Their correction in later stages is very costly. According to one study, IRD had to be repeated over 50% of the systems studied because critical information requirements were not appropriately specified (Jenkins et al, 1984). Another study showed that greater effort in IRD and the early design phase resulted in greater budget compliance and higher user satisfaction (McKeen, 1983).

The implication of this finding for DSS design is that information requirements errors are likely to be a major source of system deficiencies as the development process becomes more like a life-cycle approach. For prototyping, this potential error is less pronounced because the methodology forces the system developer to respond to the user's identification of deficiencies in information requirements in successive versions of the working prototype.

Typical portrayals of organizational decision processes in the behavioural literature show that they are as much time and circumstance driven, as driven by previously constructed rational portrayals of alternatives and consequences. Because managers need decision support in ill-structured problem situations, prematurely structured models can cause much harm. A biased heuristic for one managerial problem may be an optimal portrayal of the data for another, when one does not know the appropriate population mean, variance and underlying base rate.

Designers of DSS software must remember that there is often an unforeseeable coupling between events framing a decision problem which may be analysed by the decision maker with appropriate software support. Helping managers to optimize the evaluation and choice phases of the decision process in ill-structured situations means helping them structure their own problems rather than structuring problems for them. Managers also need help in sorting out information centrally related to a decision from that which is tangential to it. Generating models can help managers to simplify the complexities of a loosely structured decision process. The structure they choose may or may not be the rational model envisioned by the developer of the DSS software and the developer's rational model may or may not have been adequately communicated to the manager in the first place. As the user learns both the systems model and his/her real-world problem, information requirements are bound to change.

Since the DSS should not attempt to replace the decision making capability of managers but to aid it, we feel the key is to provide managers with alternative model-generating power. In fact, the ill-structured problem may contain a functional ambiguity by creating an opportunity for trial models and trial actions, and a more careful examination of the utility of possible outcomes. Trial-and-error IRD can occur through ‘trial balloons’ floated in front of peers, superiors and subordinates.

A powerful characterization describing this enactment of decision-making has been provided by Weick (1977), and examples can be found in the organizational behaviour literature (Staw, 1980, Connelly, 1982). This interpersonal enactment of the decision process has been refined to a high art in the Japanese decision making tradition, in which a draft of prospective decision outcome is carefully circulated among a wide variety of managers in one's organization before any formal action is taken. In western organizations, there is a tendency for managers to act rapidly by taking the first steps to implement the intended decision (steps which are relatively trivial in cost, but which may point towards the major outcome of the ultimate decision) and then to evaluate feedback about the consequences of these initial moves.

The following three cases illustrate how inadequate treatment of IRD during the initial DSS installation led to ignoring a major phase of the decision process.

## Case 1: Automobile production war room

A large automobile company set up a 'war room' as a single location where all the information regarding the many activities covering engineering, production, and financial planning for a major new car line could be stored and analysed for general project management. The information was intended to give individual managers an overall view of the intersections between the current status and expected achievements of each major activity contributing to the total car production effort. For example, the system was intended to help a manager examine in one location, the status of tooling for fender production, research on an improved transmission and financial planning leading to costing of optional engine packages.

Overall management of marketing, finance, personnel and production planning was supposed to be supported by the DSS. Summary data required for effective management control are often difficult to collect, but a member of staff can see, touch and walk around a 'war room' full of individual activity graphs, tables, and pictures. Other than the opportunity to 'show and tell', it seemed apparent to those intimately connected with the project that information in the room was not organized as a function of any particular insight into the evolving decision needs of the project's management. An early attempt was made to use a standard PERT model to organize the various contributing project flows. However, this normative approach was not related to realities of the project progression, nor was feedback obtained from a prototype system to obtain a more responsive design.

Divergent needs of the technical and general management personnel connected with the car line often led to conflicting scenarios to explain the data in the war room. Merely coincidental connections between events brought together in the room could be used by a manager to buttress a commitment already made or an intuitively-based action already taken. If the summaries of events being posted in the room were to be truly supportive, a series of 'what if?' scenarios or sensitivity analyses should have been displayed. A possible method of IRD would have been to 'debrief' key programme management as to the form of decision analyses which they thought particularly promising. However, this was not done.

## Case 2: Telephone switching control centre

A major telephone company with facilities spread out over a large geographic area created a series of centres for bringing together and displaying information regarding the quality of transmissions throughout the system, as well as the status of repair work and new construction. Management needed support for day to day decisions regarding hardware construction, software development and personnel assignments. Large mainframe computers were installed for the purpose of storing and updating summaries of information and controlling service. The information was packaged in glossy scoreboards placed around the office spaces and also made available in voluminous hard copy, a great deal of which was ignored.

Unfortunately, the same management hierarchy which traditionally had existed before the centres were constructed was also used in the new facilities. Technicians immediately found that they could not respond to system needs at the speed anticipated by top management because of the inefficient information flow through the hierarchy. At least one layer of lower management seemed to have little to do but forward information further down the line to the technician level.

Technical design of the information system by one of the world's leading electronic communications research facilities had neglected to include the flow of information as part of the system requirements. Instead, a black box model of the management system was used. This assumed that management would have the capability to handle the entire telephone switching technology as if it were one supremely insightful superperson. System developers ignored the powerful impact of the new technology on changing the information requirements of the technicians and managers who were about to use the system.

Top management needed to hire consultants to study the information flow between management functions in the system. From a study of the management/technician activities which involved the staff of the switching centre, recommendations were made regarding a reorganization of centre management. Given that the hardware investment was already made in ignorance of the changing management system surrounding it, the consultants, working in conjunction with a management advisory team, were at least able to suggest a number of compromises which would enable the company to use the system, but not to the extent of effectively using it for decision support. Therefore, the system was a disappointment despite the sophisticated hardware and software it contained.

## Case 3: Manufacturing production management

A DSS was constructed to support the overall goal of real-time assessment of production status and performance in an entire automotive manufacturing plant. Efficiency and productivity increases were desired. The DSS had three utility subsystems which handled plant definition, communications and graphics, and three applications subsystems – production monitoring, statistical quality control and maintenance management. The production monitoring subsystem provided plant personnel with up to the minute information regarding factory floor operations. Such operations could be monitored and analyzed hierarchically from department and sub-department through to the production line and down to the level of individual machine and station. The user could examine and interactively modify such processes as personnel and production scheduling, and assembly line in-process buffers. In addition, information was provided regarding reject levels, volume of parts produced, station status, machine status, buffering of in-process part production, and machine downtime.

The statistical quality control subsystem captured, monitored and analysed quality related data. This system consisted of a number of editors which permitted the user to define processes, control mechanisms and corrective actions for statistical analysis. For example, it included a gauge calibration utility which automated record keeping relating to historical data on instrument calibration and, through the use of memory aids, reminded management when new calibration was required. The maintenance management subsystem was intended to schedule and track the progress of maintenance procedures. This included scheduled preventive maintenance as well as emergency jobs. In addition to scheduling both machines and personnel, this subsystem analysed machine/work station history in order to assist troubleshooters who were attempting to diagnose the cause of specific symptoms.

The DSS was intended for use by four classes of plant personnel – operators, quality engineers, maintenance engineers, and managers. However, only the latter two groups were to use the DSS as decision makers. In Alter's terms (1988), the machine operators served as feeders and maintainers of the system. Quality control engineers served as intermediaries between the system and the plant managers, since they interpreted and explained output from the statistical quality control subsystem to these individuals, who were ultimately responsible for production decisions.

Unfortunately, the system failed to assist these managers, who were the primary intended users. After the introduction of the completed DSS, they proceeded to ignore it. During the IRD phase of the DSS development, system designers assumed that plant managers followed a standardized rational decision model. They ignored the potential impact of many other formal and informal information sources, as well as the user/manager's own intuition.

One of the reasons for this outcome was that the plant managers needed to be aware of the many factors which were a part of the plant's operating environment and which significantly affected production decisions. For example, sales rates in the industry, quality of supplier parts and presence of labour unrest were all factors that may have been relevant to production decisions. In other words, the actual decision environment enacted by the managers was probably closer either to a 'political' or 'garbage can' model rather than a purely rational one. Thus, the determination of information requirements should have taken this into account. Huber (1981) has suggested that these kinds of organizational settings essentially require two kinds of information – data on competitor activities and continuous scanning of key players in one's immediate environment. More effective prototyping might have disclosed these concerns among the plant managers.

## Conclusions

These case studies concerning disappointments with DSS are based on contact with middle management of a number of large corporations with regard to their experiences with DSS. The cases disclose an almost cavalier attitude towards the treatment of end-user information requirements within the system. In each case, little prototyping was done with intended users and emphasis was placed upon sophisticated displays with little knowledge of how they would aid the decision makers.

It is clear that these information requirements were not known in advance by the respective system developers. In fact, the gaps between the information requirements assumed by the developers and those which emerged from the initial user experience with the systems may be largely responsible for user disappointment. One cannot unduly blame the respective system developers, because for each of the three cases, crucial information regarding the decision environment, processes and outcomes could not have been definitely known in advance. Instead, these information requirements emerged from enactment of many decision cycles during which the true needs became much clearer.

However, it is equally clear from these case studies and the research literature that the process of IRD through prototyping could have been a central focus for integrating individual and organizational decision research into DSS design. Since managerial information requirements evolve as a function of the introduction even of a primitive DSS, system designers should have planned for changing information requirements in their software, and perhaps even in their hardware capabilities. An information environment for managerial decision making is definitely not fixed in advance by some super-rational observer, external to the perceptual framing of the semi-structured, or unstructured, decision problem enacted by the participants in the decision process. According to Taylor (1984, p. 206), given the portrayal of organizational choice by Weick's enacted model, 'events are assumed to occur and, after the fact, decisions to which these events can be logically attributed are identified. It seems fruitful to continue to advance and test a variety of models of choice.'

Some subsidiary points emerge in our analysis. First, cognitive heuristics and biases must be taken into account when determining managerial information requirements. Both software and hardware designers should include mechanisms which help the manager to deal with these sometimes adaptive, sometimes destructive, socially determined processes which occur as part of problem definition under the pressure of information overload, and can be resistant to change because of the enactment of organizational conditions which frame them. DSS software must portray information in ways which can create an effective intervention in the manager's perceptual system.

Second, managers need support with identifying their own problem solutions and not those of the software builders. Individuals do not approach problems with a blank slate, and it is true to say that organizational history of dealing with past problems has an impact upon methods of dealing with current ones.

Third, among general design issues which need to be given more prominence in the development of DSS, perhaps the most ubiquitous is the tendency toward premature structure which may eliminate functional ambiguity, opportunity for trial actions, and a careful examination of the utility of possible outcomes.

Fourth, DSS developers should acknowledge the interpersonal enactment of a decision process. Along with this social process, there comes a tendency to act rapidly and to evaluate the alternatives afterward. Making retrospective sense of what the organization has already committed itself to do is not an unusual occurrence in organizations, even those that are the most sophisticated users of information systems. Increased availability of DSS generators for 'quick hits' will be an efficient solution only if they are flexible, easy to use, and as widely understood as spreadsheets are today.

Fifth, IRD must be subject to prototyping processes. We can prototype cognitive representations of data, the operations needed to manipulate them by the computer, and the memory aids and control structures necessary to build an operational DSS. By all means we must be ready to determine information requirements by a second and third generation of prompts in the software system which stimulate the user to review stages of the decision process, consider newly relevant information requirements and make revisions to the problem evaluation model on the basis of the generated feedback. Prototyping counteracts the premature structuring associated with a linear life-cycle methodology. IRD prototyping should be used in conjunction with a life-cycle approach to ensure a balanced design in which user preferences are understood before normative models are implemented.

## Biographical notes

Daniel Braunstein is professor of management and psychology in the School of Business Administration at Oakland University in Rochester, Michigan, where he has also chaired the Department of Management. He received a PhD from Purdue University in Industrial Psychology, and a BA (psychology) from Cornell. Previously, he was at the University of Rochester, and also conducted research on officer-training for the US Navy.

Mr. Braunstein's research interests centre around cognitive and organizational issues in decision making. His book, 'Decision Making: An Interdisciplinary Inquiry' (jointly annotated and edited with G.R. Ungson), argues that progress in behavioural decision theory will come from grounded research in applications disciplines. To this end, he has been working with information system researchers, as well as auditors and accountants. Professor Braunstein has also published in the Journal of Applied Psychology, Administrative Science Quarterly, Interfaces, the Journal of Vocational Behavior, and Business Horizons. Recently, he was a visiting faculty member at the University of New South Wales and the Copenhagen Business School.

Thomas Lauer is an assistant professor of management information systems at Oakland University in Rochester, Michigan. He holds BA (Mathematics), MBA and PhD degrees from Indiana University. He is a member of the Decision Sciences Institute, the Society for Information Management, The Institute of Management Science, and the Association for Computing Machinery. He has published articles in Behavior and Information Technology, Discourse Processes, and MIS Quarterly. He is the co-author of a forthcoming book entitled 'Questions and Information Systems', Lawrence Erlbaum and Associates.

Mr. Lauer is involved in research that relates the cognition that occurs during the acquisition of information with various information systems topics. Most recently, this research has focused on the elucidation of these processes through the direct examination of questions.

David P. Doane holds a PhD in economics from Purdue University and a bachelors degree with highest distinction in mathematics from the University of Kansas. At Oakland University, he has served for more than a decade as Chair of the Department of Decision and Information Sciences.

His research and publications apply quantitative methods to diverse problems in economics, number aesthetics, judicial administration, and health care. He is the author of Exploring Statistics with the IBM PC and the Explore Statistical Software Package.
