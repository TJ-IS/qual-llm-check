MANAGEMENT REPORT

# Overcoming the barriers to the implementation of information system strategies

TOM WILSON

Department of Information Studies, University of Sheffield, UK

Abstract: This paper reports on a study of the 500 major UK companies and 50 financial service companies, which used interviews and self-completed questionnaires. The key feature of information system strategies are presented, together with an identification of the barriers to setting up and implementing a strategy which companies perceive. Finally, strategies for overcoming the major barriers to successful information system strategies are outlined.

## Introduction

The term ‘information system strategy’ (ISS) is used in this paper, rather than the alternatives – ‘information strategy’ and ‘IT strategy’, because it puts the emphasis upon the total system, not simply on the technology.

According to Michael Porter (1985) a strategy is, ‘... the route to competitive advantage that will determine ... performance’. Following from this definition an ISS is the analysis of the role that information systems can play in helping business units or companies to define a route to competitive strategy.

This paper is based on work carried out with the support of Andersen Consulting (Wilson, 1989) – the overall objective of the study was to discover the extent to which major UK companies had adopted a strategic view of information systems development. Among the subsidiary aims were several of relevance to this paper:

(1) How the effectiveness of a strategy was monitored;

(2) What aspects of information systems development featured in existing strategies, and what were future plans for investment;

(3) How successful strategies were deemed to have been, and what barriers to the establishment and implementation of strategy had been experienced.

Information was also collected from companies which had not implemented an IS strategy, but clearly those responses are not of relevance to this paper.

These objectives were satisfied by the investigation, at least so far as the responding companies are concerned. All companies from the Times 500 and 50 financial services companies were surveyed. Nine of the Times 500 companies and three financial services companies were surveyed by interview, the remainder by a self-completed questionnaire.based on the results of the interviews. The response rate was over 35% and would have been higher but for the intervention of a postal strike. Even so, this is a high response rate for a mail survey of businesses, and much higher than many other studies.

## The nature of information system strategies

Of the questionnaires returned, 75% were from companies which claimed to have information system strategies. However, my definition of what constitutes a strategy was rather strict. This definition stipulates that it must be:

(1) Corporate in character;

(2) Initiated at Board level;

(3) Formally documented;

(4) Monitored through planned reviews;

(5) Incorporate the concept of competitive advantage;

(6) Deliver information based on a formal investigation of management information needs;

(7) Include access to external information sources.

When I supplied this strict definition to the analysis of responses only 24 companies (six in the financial services sector) could be said to have a strategy, i.e. only 17% of those claiming to have a strategy. The remainder were rejected on the basis of one or other (and usually more than one) of the criteria listed above.

The literature on ISSs identifies a number of elements which may be treated in strategies and this study sought to discover the importance attached to a variety of these elements. ## Barriers to strategy

The barriers suggested in the literature include a number which affect any innovation, i.e. the hostile attitudes of management levels in a company and the problem of recruiting appropriate staff. Some, such as the difficulty of measuring benefits, are more specific to IT implementation.

Companies with a strategy were asked to identify the barriers either to setting up or implementing a strategy and to state whether the barrier had been major or minor. The results are shown in condensed form in Table 2.

The ranking is based on the proportion of companies citing a barrier as a major impediment. It is interesting, however, to look at the ranking which emerges when the change in proportions citing a barrier as a problem at set-up and implementation is used. As the table shows, some of the barriers assumed greater significance at the implementation stage. Some of the changes might be called logical shifts e.g. one might expect greater difficulty in recruitment when an organization actually needs to recruit, at the implementation stage; one might expect the lack of resources for user-education to become more apparent when user-education for the new systems needs to take place; and it is not surprising that the technology lag makes itself felt after the initial optimism of the planning stage.

Explanations for some of the changes are more difficult to find, however; the nature of the business (degree of diversification, rapidity of growth, etc.) is perceived as a major barrier initially and by an even greater proportion during implementation. What is the explanation for this? Perhaps, it is that anticipated problems in this area actually materialize. Also, the attitudes of middle management assume a greater significance at implementation stage – this may be related to the lack of resources for user-education and to the perception that technology lags behind needs, i.e. is failing to deliver anticipated benefits.

Perhaps the smaller increase for senior management attitudes is related to the marked decline in the significance of political conflicts. The downward shift in political conflict may also be associated with the decline in doubts about benefits, although the difficulty of measuring benefits remains a problem.

Overall, the difficulties in recruiting appropriate staff, the lack of resources to engage in user-education, the nature of the business, and the difficulties of measuring benefits, emerge as the key features of IT strategies which are likely to cause problems for companies. Further details of barriers and problems surface in the interviews. Even companies with a strong commitment were not immune to blind spots. For example, one interviewee in the financial services sector said that:

... while there is this very strong belief in information technology, the top management have not believed in end-user computing... The main drive... is coming from DP because we see opportunities for managers, professionals, to help themselves. But, it is not something for which there is a strong damand, and it is something which top management has treated as, you know, "Why do you want to play with that, why don't you get on with your real job?"

The same person highlighted another inhibiting factor:

'... another aspect of corporate style – the attitude to cost. The organization will quite happily invest millions, almost at the drop of a hat, in large-scale technology for big operational systems. I can get authority to spend a million or two on new hardware to improve the system in a ten minute meeting with the managing director. And yet the same man will say, "I might find a PC on the desk quite useful, but how much does it cost?" [and then] "It's not worth spending £2,500 for a piece of junk on my desk".

The nature of the barriers to success were also touched on in other parts of the questionnaire. Those who chose to say that the strategy had been only 'partly successful' generally advanced reasons for not having done better, with two reasons more frequently quoted than others – (1) poor investment or low acceptance by managers, and (2) having more work than can be coped with.

'Difficulty in gaining full approval for strategy. This makes long-term, integrated systems development difficult.'

'Pressure on building short-term applications to cope with immediate business requirements inhibits progression towards long-term goals . . .'

A wide variety of other reasons were put forward, including lack of human and other resources, poor education and training, unsuitable technology, resistance to change, and a volatile market-place. Among the comments, some are particularly instructive.

'While the existence of a separate computer services company within the Group has given a professional focus to 'data processing' services, the remoteness from end-users has led to a shortfall in identifying their 'informational' needs, which has lead managers to a poor opinion of computing generally.'

'Problems with staff recruitment with appropriate expertise coupled with the requirement to develop most applications 'in-house' due to major investment in minority supplier restricting application package choice.'

## Overcoming the barriers

One way of identifying how to overcome the barriers to success is to look at how successful strategies were claimed to have been, and why.

From the comments made on the choices, two factors emerged as leading to success:

(1) Appropriate choice of systems, for example: 'Rapid development of systems using 4GLs has meant systems can be delivered within acceptable time-scales and before business needs change. Sophisticated software has meant high level of acceptability of systems by users.'

'Able to produce required information significantly faster and more efficiently than 2 years ago, with beneficial effects for management and staff actually doing the work.'

(2) Senior management and Board support for strategies, a factor which has been frequently reiterated in the literature:

'ISS has been most successful in operating companies where senior management maintain interest beyond the stage of preparing the first ISS. It is less successful where an ISS is prepared and IT management are left to implement it.'

'Enthusiastic backing from, and the direct involvement of the Chief Executive. The Company's senior managers have a natural inclination towards using IT and [the company] is moving towards central business planning which is fully compatible with the thinking behind an ISS.'

## Recruitment

Two factors are likely to prevent the removal of difficulties in recruiting appropriately qualified staff. First, the present government's educational policies, both in higher education and in more job-specific training, have actually made it very difficult for educational institutions to respond to the need for new modes of education and training. Secondly, even if government policy changes, we still have the demographic downturn with which to contend. The following are actions which can be taken by companies to counteract these problems.

(1) Companies can devote a greater proportion of their profits to training. This country has an appallingly low rate of participation in higher education and training compared with our competitors. We need vigorous, company-led training schemes to tap the undoubted talent that is hidden in the 86% of the age-group that does not go on to higher education;

(2) Companies can recruit more broadly from the population at large, accepting non-conventional levels of education and training as good enough to start, and then implementing effective in-house training programmes;

(3) Companies can look inward to their existing resources of people to retrain and relocate. The most imaginative companies are already doing this – and with considerable success;

(4) Companies can recruit from abroad, where either supply exceeds demand, or where the conditions of employment are less attractive than in the UK. Perhaps the opening-up of Eastern Europe will provide a source of supply.

Most of these strategies demand a diversion of resources into training – but the UK's present levels are so low that this is not surprising.

## Nature of the business

The nature of the business presents another rather intractable problem. What we mean by this is that either the business itself is growing and diversifying at a faster rate than the strategy can cope with, or that the environment in which the business operates is so turbulent as to make a virtual nonsense of planning (or both of these things together).

One thing to remember in this area is that, as Henry Mintzberg (1987) notes, it is sometimes smarter to allow a strategy to emerge, by stringing together in a logical fashion, within a cohesive framework, the responses to the consequences of growth or environmental uncertainties. In this respect, companies which experience this particular problem (and it seems that most do) need to put a higher priority on the development of an overall systems and standards architecture than appears to be the case at present. For example, any organization which is not now working towards some open systems architecture, with departmental networks as an essential part of that architecture, and EDI as an essential part of communication with suppliers, wholesalers, retailers, and subsidiaries, is hardly to be thought of as having a strategy at all. Within that overall architecture, many flavours are possible, and the integration of equipment from diverse suppliers is becoming much more of a reality, albeit not without difficulties. An alternative answer may be to hand the problem over to someone else, by using a facilities management company.

## Benefit management

The measurement of benefit is another difficult area, although it appears to be less important as a barrier now, as organizations realise that further cost reductions, through the application of IT, are unlikely to emerge. From this one can see that a much greater proportion of firms are now looking to IT to improve product or service performance. In fact, Scott Morton (1990) suggested that IT cannot lead to sustained competitive advantage, but has become a competitive necessity.

This is not to suggest that there is no need to try to determine the benefits of information system implementation, but crude measures of money benefits can hardly apply to areas of effectiveness. Perhaps it is time we regarded the technology as today's equivalent of the telephone – how did the early implementers of that technology cost-justify their actions? Does anyone now do cost/benefit calculations on the installation of a new computer-based exchange, when communications are so dependent on the technology?

## Resources for user-education

The lack of resources for user-education may well be the result of a failure to recognize that such resources will be needed. If so, the answer is to build the need for user-education into systems development. However, suppose the problem is just another part of the general problem of recruitment. Then the strategies for dealing with that problem apply here. However, we can add another strategy – that of building applications with user-education built in, through the incorporation of expert system and hypertext-based instructional modules. At Sheffield, for example, we are working on an analogous situation – how do firms inform their staffs about the implications of the Open Market? Our answer is to build and test a hypertext-based training package; the same thing is clearly possible for training in the use of IT.

## Existing investment in IT

Finally, what can we do about this problem of existing investment in IT? It is a very real problem, of course, and there is a limit to what can be done when installing new systems. The costs are very much more than simply buying new hardware. Apart from the obvious things like rewriting programs, and the learning load on staff of a new operating system, there is also the new user-education load.

Sometimes firms will simply have to 'bite the bullet' and invest the money, particularly if they need to establish a competitive advantage in their sector. This is one of the reasons, of course, why some companies are turning to facilities management.

In other circumstances a phased transition to new systems may be possible. For example, large, (sometimes virtually stand-alone) applications like the payroll may be rebuilt on dedicated machines while the rest of the systems continue to run, for the first phase, on existing equipment. Or, a strategy of end-user computing on PC networks or mini-computers for certain departmental applications may be evolved, to take the load from central DP facilities. New applications may be built in the expectation of future integration through the adoption of open-system architectures. In other words, there are strategies, none of them without its problems, but then no change is problem-free.

## Conclusion

Looking back to the objectives of the study, the following points emerge as having relevance to overcoming the barriers to the implementation of strategies:

(1) The importance of a formal link between the ISS and the business plan was recognized by almost half of the respondents with strategies, and, clearly, that link may provide guidelines to preventing or overcoming problems in system implementation;

(2) Strong Board level involvement is evident in more than half of the companies with strategies, and even in those without strategies the person responsible for information technology is likely to report to a Board member. That degree of involvement was recognized as one of the major contributing factors to successful implementation;

(3) The need for planned, formal performance reviews to monitor the effectiveness of strategies is recognized by almost two thirds of the 'strategy' companies. Such formal reviews must focus on the business objectives and on the satisfaction of user-needs at all stages of the implementation process. If this does not happen, its lack will constitute a barrier;

(4) The evolutionary development of systems towards more effective management information systems within an architecture of common standards is the main feature of strategies and the main aim in ‘non-strategy’ companies. These aims are also reflected in future investment plans. That kind of evolutionary development is essential if the barrier of existing IT investment is to have less significance.

These, and the specific strategies I have outlined earlier, constitute the best hope for overcoming the problems.
