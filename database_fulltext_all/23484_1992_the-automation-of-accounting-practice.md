# The automation of accounting practice

R.A. WILSON

Department of Accounting and Finance, University of Strathclyde, Glasgow, Scotland

A. SANGSTER

University of Aberdeen, Aberdeen, Scotland

This article examines the use of computer technology by the UK accounting profession. The first part of the paper examines the nature of the accounting environment and identifies a number of reasons why accounting automation should be an issue of general concern. A particular accounting task, auditing, is examined in detail to illustrate some of the factors affecting accounting automation. The second part of the paper reports the findings of an empirical examination of computerization amongst a number of different accounting groups with different working environments. Practitioners' views on several factors constraining further technological innovation, and on factors motivating innovation, are analysed and examined. It is concluded that domain characteristics, historical factors and skill availability are amongst the most significant variables influencing automation.

## Introduction

There are many accounting tasks, such as invoicing, payroll and book-keeping, which involve the processing of large amounts of numerical data. Such routine transaction processing is a substantial cost burden on organizations and the desire to reduce these costs provided a motivation for the early introduction of computer systems. Though the volumes of data are substantial, the processing algorithms involved are relatively simple and largely uniform between different organizations. Processing large volumes of numerical data through simple algorithms was an ideal form of application for the early EDP systems and it is therefore not surprising that an accounting application provided many organizations with their first experience of computing $^{1}$ . It was not unusual in many companies to find the Financial Director also in charge of data processing. Initially, financial computing was largely confined to the larger organizations and carried out on centralized mainframe systems. The development of minicomputers, from the PDP-8 in 1963, allowed medium-sized organizations, including some professional accounting practices, to begin using computers for accounting tasks. The introduction of business microcomputers, with the Apple II in 1979, extended the use of computers to the accounting functions of smaller organizations, including small accounting practices. Microcomputers have also been very influential in increasing the computer literacy of accountants. The existence of a number of suitable transaction processing tasks meant that the accounting profession had an early involvement with computers and subsequent developments in technology have greatly increased the overall level of computer usage and broadened the range of tasks that have been subject to computerisation (Collier, 1984; Carr, 1985; Coopers and Lybrand, 1985; Wilson, 1989).

Some accounting activities have been more affected by computerization than others. Tasks which are based on simple algorithmic processing of numerical data, such as bookkeeping, have been readily computerized and computer-based systems have almost totally replaced manual systems for most simple transaction processing tasks $^{2}$ . Other areas of accountancy (e.g. insolvency) which involve high levels of interpersonal contact and the exercise of diverse professional judgement have proved more difficult to automate. In some areas, such as taxation, computers are widely used but anecdotal evidence suggests that their use has not affected the fundamental modus operandi; whilst in management accounting computers have given accountants the capability to take a much more proactive approach to information delivery and managerial decision support through the use of various modelling techniques. Expert systems are being developed for accounting applications (Connell, 1987; Powell and Connell, 1988; Edwards and Connell, 1989), particularly in areas such as auditing (Messier and Hansen, 1987) and taxation (Gleeson and West, 1987; Tindall and Susskind, 1988; McCarthy and Outslay, 1989), but as yet their impact does not appear to have been significant, and their development and use has been mainly confined to the larger professional accounting practices.

## The importance of accounting IT

The accounting profession's ability and willingness to use advanced computer technology is of considerable relevance to both its clients and to society in general. Accounting is a service activity, providing financial information to clients for a price. The clients of accountancy practices pay this price directly in the form of fees; industrial and commercial clients pay for it in the form of the overhead cost incurred in maintaining an internal accounting function. Accounting expertise is expensive and its cost is born directly by clients, and ultimately by society in higher prices. If accounting productivity can be increased by computerization, society will ultimately pay a lower price for financial information.

However, the effect that computerization might have upon the costs of financial information is less important than its effects on the quality of information that accountants provide to their clients. Accountants use expert judgement to interpret complex data and then provide filtered information and advice to decision makers. Their contribution is often crucial in determining the actions of others. The influence of the 'bottom line', or final, figure is considerable, and it is accountants who calculate it. Many important decisions are dependent upon the information provided by accountants. Shareholders judge the performance of their investments on the basis of financial statements prepared by accountants and validated by other accountants. Government gets its revenue from a variety of taxes and, in almost every case, the amount due to the government is dependent upon accounting calculations. There are few management decisions that do not have a financial dimension; and the results of accounting calculations can significantly affect major, long-reaching decisions including, for example, whether an investment is made or a plant is closed.

Prior to computerization, accountancy was obsessed with transaction processing tasks such as maintaining accounting records and completing tax returns. Clients received only limited financial information, much of which was historical. Whilst accounting data was stored on paper, it was often difficult and time-consuming for accountants to prepare the analyses and forecasts needed by clients. Computerization has largely freed accountants from the burdens imposed by routine transaction processing. The digitization of financial data has lead to the development of computer-based accounting information systems capable to converting accounting data into valuable information in a fraction of the time that it would take to do so manually. In addition, it is beginning to make information provision more proactive (King et al. 1990). Whilst historical information has value (for example, in performance evaluation), managing an organization solely with historical information is akin to navigating a ship by its wake. Organizations need good quality financial forecasts as well as historical reports. Financial forecasting is data intensive and computers are essential if anything other than the most basic level of data analysis is to be undertaken. The widespread availability of powerful microcomputers and their associated software has lead to an increasing use of modelling and other decision support techniques to enhance the quality of predictive information provided by accountants.

A further limitation of the pre-digitization period was that financial information was presented in the form of tabulations, despite non-numerate clients often having great difficulty in deciphering the messages contained in such statements. This user-incompatibility of the tabular presentation medium is accentuated by the way in which tabulations, while providing precise values, can conceal underlying patterns, trends and relationships which only become apparent when a graphical analysis is adopted. These disadvantages of the traditional tabular approach to data presentation are verified by research which has found that, in appropriate circumstances, graphical presentation can be significantly superior to data tabulation as a decision aid (see Remus, 1987). The development of powerful microcomputer graphics packages has allowed financial information to be presented in a readily comprehensible form by even the smallest organization.

There are also employment implications arising from accounting automation. Approximately 120 000 qualified accountants and 90 000 student accountants work in industry, commerce, local and national government, consultancy and professional offices in the UK. There are also over 12 000 qualified and 30 000 student accounting technicians. No precise figures are available on the number of clerical staff engaged in accounting tasks but it may be conservatively estimated at more than 500 000 people. Each year 10% of UK graduates begin accountancy training. Any major changes in the productivity of the accounting profession could have significant long-term effects on white collar employment levels. There is evidence (Wilson, 1991) to suggest that, to date, the effect of information technology on accounting has been to increase the overall level of professional employment but marginally decrease the overall level of clerical employment. Recently, the larger professional accounting firms have begun to reduce professional staffing levels $^{3}$ but it is unclear whether this is attributable to an economic downturn, organizational downsizing or the effects of technology.

## The accounting environment and technological change

The majority of UK accountants work in industry, commerce, banking and government, providing an accounting service for their employing organization. However, approximately 45% of qualified accountants work in professional accountancy practices: partnerships that provide a wide range of accountancy services to their clients. Five main services are provided by these practices: auditing, accounts preparation, taxation, insolvency, and management consultancy. Some professional practices have only one qualified member, whilst others operate on an international scale with thousands of partners. The larger accountancy practices have specialized data processing functions, and many sell their IT expertise as well as offering accountancy services. The basic function of both types of accountants is the provision of financial information to several client groups, including shareholders, management, and the revenue gathering functions of government. Although there is a fundamental commonality in the work of both types of accountants, there are sufficient differences in the working environments and tasks of those in professional practice compared with those in industry for it to be appropriate to consider the two groups separately. For example, some of the work performed in accountancy practices, such as external auditing, is not done at all by accountants in industry.

Accountants in industry and commerce provide a service to their employing organization in two important ways: they maintain the organization's accounting records (a stewardship function) and they use computer-based information and decision support systems to provide information to the management (a decision support function). An organization's accounting records will contain details of its financial transactions, including payroll payments to employees, invoices from suppliers and to customers, and records of assets owned by the enterprise. Even in a medium-sized organization this will involve processing very large amounts of data. One result of this is that industrial systems are often heavily dependent on mainframe computers whilst, in accountancy practices, microcomputer-based systems are more the norm $^{4}$ . The computer-based information and decision support systems employed to provide information to management can be of varying sophistication, with microcomputers and spreadsheet's being widely used for financial planning and decision support.

Accountancy practices provide a wide variety of accounting services to their clients, and require a wide range of systems to support their work. Much lower volumes of data are involved and the high-volume transaction processing tasks that are common in industry are rare in accountancy practices. An accountancy practice will use information technology both to provide services to clients and to support its own internal operations in the form of computer-based systems for internal administration and accounting. (For example, as fees are charged by the hour, time recording systems are needed to ensure that work is properly recorded and charged to clients.) Some of the more computer literate practices use the systems which were developed for their own use to provide bureau services for their clients.

Each of the main services offered by the accountancy practices (auditing, accounts preparations, taxation, management consultancy, and insolvency) has its own particular characteristics that make it more, or less, suitable for computerization. Each is also subject to different economic and operational pressures influencing the pace of technical innovation within it. Of the five areas, auditing is the key activity area for many practices $^{5}$ , and a very important source of fee income for almost all practices $^{6}$ . The effect of information technology on auditing is examined below.

## Auditing and information technology

Auditing involves the examination of a company's financial records by an independent accounting practitioner who reports to the company's owners upon whether its financial statements fairly reflect its financial affairs. It is a labour intensive activity employing highly-paid labour and represents a stagnant market with little opportunity for volume growth. Accountancy practices can, generally, only build up their audit business by taking clients from other practices. This is well understood by clients, many of whom are becoming increasingly aware of the size of their audit bills, and pressure is being exerted upon accountancy practices to reduce the costs of this service. This has resulted in some very significant discounts being offered by accountancy firms hoping to 'win' audits from other firms and, in at least one case, this has gone so far as to take the form of a 12-month charge-free service. These factors are combining to create pressure upon accountancy practices to automate audit work as much as possible, thereby enabling costs, and consequently fees, to be reduced without any loss in quality of service provided.

Software can be used to reduce the labour content of many clerical and management tasks associated with audit work. For example, audits have to be planned and managed; records have to be kept; letters have to be written to verify balances in the client's accounts. These tasks can be accomplished by audit toolkits, comprising standard PC software packages, including standard word processing, project planning and database packages, as well as purpose written software $^{7}$ . Software tools can also be used to interrogate clients' data files. A practice can expect that all its larger clients, most of its medium-sized clients, and some of its smaller clients will keep their accounting records on computer systems. Provided an auditor has the necessary expertise, the data files created by these systems to hold records of assets and liabilities can be read and processed using CAAT's (computer assisted audit tools) $^{8}$ . In a manual audit, a sample of a client's accounting transactions are examined. An auditor equipped with a CAAT can not only conduct his tests much faster, but can examine all the transactions in a file and conduct a much greater variety of tests. Therefore, the use of CAATs not only increases the efficiency of the audit process, but also its effectiveness. Those accountants who have the necessary computer skills can use these tools to carry out economical and effective audits; those who do not are only able to carry out a satisfactory audit by applying much lengthier manual tests on their clients accounts, thus increasing the cost of the audit and incurring a greater degree of uncertainty in the result.

Clients are also becoming increasingly litigious and this is creating pressure on practices to ensure that high and uniform standards of expertise are being applied within audits. Especially in the larger practices, it can be very difficult to ensure that the same high professional standards are applied in all audits, and that the skills of head office tax and audit experts are available where it is required, on site during an audit at a client's offices. Conventional software has little to contribute directly to this problem and the larger practices have, for some time, been examining expert system technology for possible solutions. The objective is to develop expert systems modules that can be added to existing audit tool kits and used to apply uniform high level expertise to specific audit problems. A number of systems have been developed for various audit tasks; including provisions review, auditing advanced computer systems and going concern evaluation (for a review of auditing expert systems, see Jacob and Bailey, 1989 or Dijk and Williams, 1990). Many auditing tasks are carried out by reference to questionnaires or check lists. Paper-based lists can be difficult to use but expert systems techniques can be used to develop 'intelligent' checklists (Shpilberg et al. 1985; Bickerstaff, 1988) that are more effective and easier to use.

## The pace of automation

The auditing example illustrates the variety of software tools which might be employed to carry out accounting tasks and also suggests how the use of computers might affect both the efficiency and effectiveness of accounting practice. The overall pace of accounting automation will be affected by a number of different factors. There will be some pressures forcing change, such as pressures from clients (internal and external) to reduce costs, meet performance targets, or introduce new services. There will also be constraints limiting change. One of the constraints may be the nature of the working environment. Some tasks are easier to computerize than others and it may be hypothesized that there are more of the ‘easier’ tasks in the industrial and commercial accounting environment than in professional practice. Industrial and commercial accounting has the large-scale transaction processing tasks, whilst professional practices undertake a greater variety of work and are more dependent upon qualitative judgemental processing than numerical data processing. Another factor may be organizational size. Larger organizations may have more funds available for IT investment and the in-house skills needed to implement complex projects, but may also have more bureaucratic resistance to change. To examine some of these issues a study was undertaken of a sample of practising accountants.

## Research methodology

Information on the accounting profession's use of information technology was collected through a postal survey which was completed during 1990. The survey population comprised those members of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Scotland (ICAS) who were engaged in accountancy employment in Scotland. An analysis of ICAS membership statistics was used to divide the population into two groups: those working in accountancy practices and those working in industry and commerce. The distinction was made because of the different working environments and tasks of the two groups. A further distinction was made between those working in large and small organizations, because it was suspected that both organizational size and the nature of the working environment might significantly affect IT usage. The size distinction was found to be relevant for the public accounting group and the abbreviations LAF and SAF will be used below to distinguish between respondents working in large and small accounting practices. It was not found to be relevant for the industrial and commercial accounting group and the abbreviation IAC will be used to identify all those respondents from industry and commerce.

A random sample was selected from each of the three groups, the percentage selected being higher for the two accountancy practice groups as it was suspected that they might have a lower response rate. Data was then collected using a questionnaire. This contained 50 questions in a highly-structured format and made extensive use of check lists and inventory methods. The survey did not attempt any objective measurement of IT usage in terms of the number of computers and software packages in use, or the value of IT equipment in the organization. It was considered that this information would not only be difficult to obtain (respondents were unlikely to have reliable values available), but also, as any physical equipment would have been obtained at different times and on different financial bases, that it would be relatively worthless for comparative purposes. Furthermore, data on physical assets provides no guidance on the actual effective usage of those assets. Instead, the questionnaire asked respondents to provide subjective assessments of their own and their organization's use of computers and other relevant issues. Over 54% of the IAC sample, and over 25% of both the SAF and LAF groups returned their questionnaires. A summary of the survey statistics is given in Table 1 below. The response rate may have been positively affected by the offer of a summary of the findings (32% of those who completed the questionnaire requested the summary); it may also be a reflection of the general level of interest in IT issues. The frequencies shown in the various tables which follow reflect the fact that not every respondent answered all parts of the questionnaire and that a few of the responses were unusable.

Non-response bias was tested by using late respondents as surrogates for non-respondents. The last 15 responses in each group were compared with a random sample of 15 earlier responses. The results of these tests gave no reason, at the 95% confidence level, to reject the hypothesis that in each of the three cases the two samples were drawn from the same population. However, given the limitations of the surrogate method (Wallace and Mellor, 1988; Wallace and Cooke, 1990) there must be more confidence in the absence of significant non-response bias with the IAC group because of their higher response rate.

## Constraints on computer innovation

After examining the respondents' current and prospective use of computer systems, the survey considered the underlying factors that were affecting the process of innovation. A pilot study identified five factors which respondents believed were significant in constraining the further development of computer applications. These factors are referred to below as: funding, change, skills, policy, and equipment. These factors were defined as follows:

Funding: the availability of funding for investment in IT projects;

Change: individual and organizational resistance to technological change.

Skills: the availability of the skills needed to implement and maintain IT systems.

Policy: the absence of a positive organizational policy on the use of IT.

Equipment: the availability of suitable hardware and software.

The respondents were asked to indicate whether they considered these factors to have been significant in constraining their organization's use of computer technology. Structured questions on the five factors were included in the main survey. The responses are presented in Figure 1, which shows the percentage of each group of respondents that considered a particular factor had been important, or very important, in hindering systems development.

Figure 1 shows that the availability of funds for the development of systems was considered to have been an important, or very important, constraint by 53.1% of the SAF respondents, the least significance being attached to this factor by the LAF group (37.3%). While it would not be unreasonable to expect that the members of the SAF group would have limited resources, and thus find problems with funding, the assumption would not apply to the same extent with large accountancy practices. The IAC group placed almost as much emphasis on funding as the SAF group, but their reason for doing so may be different: an accounting practice is a business operation in direct contact with the marketplace and with a clear motivation for increasing its own efficiency if it can do so; in contrast, the accounting function in industry and commerce is a service department with no directly saleable output and it may be that the IAC respondents suffered from their organization's unwillingness to invest in a service function.

The small accountancy practices viewed policy as being more of a constraint than the other two groups. This may be due to a lack of influence (in a small accountancy practice there will often be one person responsible for technological innovation, as opposed to a group or department in larger accountancy practices; industrial-based qualified accountants are more likely to be managers and thus better placed to revise existing policy).

Behavioural inertia has long been recognized as both an individual and an organizational issue (Child et al. 1987; Mohr, 1987). The survey question on change referred to a general inertia that the respondents might have considered present in either their colleagues or their organization. Overall, it can be seen from Figure 1 that the least significance was attached to this factor, though the LAF group considered it more important than funding, policy, or equipment as a constraining factor. It may be that the larger accounting practices are more bureaucratic than their smaller counterparts and it is therefore harder to introduce change.

## Motivations for change

During the pilot study, several factors were identified as particularly significant in motivating technological innovation. These factors were:

Productivity: the desire to increase professional or clerical productivity and therefore reduce costs.

Information: the desire to utilize computer systems to improve the quality of information supplied to clients.

Services: the desire to use computer systems to offer new services to client groups.

Tasks: the desire to use computer systems to complete accounting tasks more rapidly.

## Conclusions

There are good reasons why the rate of IT innovation in accounting practice should be of general concern. Some of these reasons have been explored above. Financial information has a significant cost and it may be possible to reduce this cost by automation. Accounting is a significant employer and if employment levels are altered by automation this will have general social effects. Perhaps the most important consideration lies in the area of information quality. There are a variety of ways in which computers can beneficially affect the quality of the information provided to accounting clients. Financial forecasts can be based on sound quantitative analysis rather than solely on management intuition, reports can be graphically enhanced, audits can be improved and online demand reporting can be increased. If technology can be used to improve the quantity and quality of financial information this may result in improved decision making and better organizational management.

Although the overall use of computers in accounting is high, some activities have been much more affected by computerization than others. This may be explained partially by the nature of the tasks involved – for example, transaction recording is an obvious target for computerization, whereas the generation of an audit plan (a list of tasks to be performed on an audit) is not, because of the wide range of options open to the planner, and the judgement involved in selecting the correct combination of tasks to include in the plan. It can also be partially explained by software availability and acceptance – spreadsheets and transaction recording packages are commonplace and widely used, expert systems are not. Market forces are also a contributory factor, as illustrated with reference to the auditing function, with the resultant need to improve efficiency and reduce costs if a secure position is to be obtained in the market. However, such is the commonality of tasks performed by accountants that these factors alone do not adequately explain why there should be a significant difference between computer utilization by industry-based accountants compared to those working in accountancy practices.

In an attempt to explain this sectoral difference in the adoption of computer technology, possible constraints and motivating factors were examined. While differences were found in the priority attached by the three groups to constraints on technological change and to the ranking of motivations for such change, these were due to size differences within the accountancy practices sector, i.e. they were the result of size differences arising between organizations engaged in similar activities, rather than size differences generally. No significant difference was found between the industry group and either of the others, nor between the two accountancy practice groups (other than confidence in the use of computers).

Attitudinal views were examined and, while approximately 90% of all respondents felt that they and their subordinates could make greater use of computers, accountants working in industry were found to be more confident in the use of computers than those in accountancy practices. They also considered that their organizations made more use of computers than did those who were based in accountancy practices.

It is concluded that the constraints and the motivating factors are not responsible for the sectoral imbalance detected, nor is the accountants' attitude. Rather, it appears to be due to the difference in the nature of the tasks performed in the different environments, possibly combined with the historical lead that industrially-based accountants had in the adoption of computer technology.

Algorithmically-based accountancy tasks have been readily computerized, but those requiring judgement and the processing of qualitative information have only gradually been subject to computerization. As a result, industrially-based accountants (who perform tasks of a more algorithmic nature than those based in accountancy practices) have made more use of computer technology than those based in accountancy practices. It is not a question of size, but rather one of environment and task. As software is developed which will enable more of the tasks of accountancy practice-based accountants to become computerized, so the sectoral imbalance is likely to diminish.
