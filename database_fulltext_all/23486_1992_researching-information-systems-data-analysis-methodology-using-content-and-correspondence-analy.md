# Researching information systems: data analysis methodology using content and correspondence analysis

DAN REMENYI

Henley Management College, UK

This paper describes the use of content analysis and correspondence analysis in respect of case study research performed by the author in the field of strategic information systems planning. During the collection of data from the firms participating in the case study research the author collected in excess of 200 pages of text. It was necessary to analyse and summarize the data in order to see what general principles relating to the formulation and implementation of a strategic information system could be derived. The main process adopted for this analysis and synthesis was content analysis. Correspondence analysis was then applied in order to help corroborate the findings of the content analysis.

## Introduction

This paper describes an approach to the analysis of data collected from ten case studies. During this research 46 interviews in ten different firms were undertaken. Each of these interviews were recorded using a portable cassette tape recorder and subsequently transcribed resulting in the production of a document in excess of 200 pages. This represented a substantial amount of mostly qualitative data which did not lend itself to any of the standard quantitative data analysis techniques.

The challenge then faced was the analysis of the data in such a way as to establish principal concepts which could be used in formulating a theory. Having determined the concepts it was then necessary to synthesize these concepts in such a way that a theory could be postulated. Having established a theory it was then the intention subsequently to test this theory using a larger sample and a more traditional approach incorporating an opinion survey and statistical measures. The tools chosen for the more qualitative analysis were content and correspondence analysis. The purpose of this paper is to demonstrate the applicability of these modes of analysis in researching information systems.

## Content analysis

Content analysis may be defined as the process of determining or establishing a fuller, detailed meaning of a portion of document, manuscript, speech or any type of communication in a way which is both reliable and replicatable. A more formal definition of content analysis is supplied by Berelson (1980) as 'a research technique for the objective, systematic and quantitative description of the manifest content of communications'. It is a process of investigating the frequency and intensity with which concepts are addressed in the text. It is a simple but laborious process of closely examining the transcript for concepts, particularly those which are repeated several times. It is by nature a subjective process which relies on what Kerlinger (1969) refers to as manifest content categories being set up by the researcher, and the researcher then counting the number of occurrences of these categories. The establishment of the manifest content categories is one of the main areas of possible subjectivity bias. The initial categories used in this research were major issues of discussion raised by informants again and again during the research interviews, and as such were the obvious ones to choose. However, as the content analysis is a procedure which evolves, issues such as case tools, centralization and organization structure were added to the list of categories. Although personal bias will never be totally absent it is believed by the author that there was relatively little personal bias in the choice of the concepts used here.

According to Berelson, content analysis may be used with different units of analysis including words, themes, characters, items, and space time measurements. In this case the unit used was themes. This is regarded as one of the more difficult units as a theme can be raised or eluded to in several different ways. For example, industry drivers were sometimes referred to by the word drivers, sometimes deregulation was discussed directly, sometimes economic shifts or technological leaps. On other occasions drivers were referred to by reference to competitive activity etc.

Therefore, the content analysis required the author to identify the main concept categories addressed by the informants and then to allocate ideas or themes to these categories and thus maintain a record of how frequently informants referred to these concepts. For this research no attempt was made to measure or associate a weight with the intensity of references and thus only a count of the occasions on which the concepts were addressed was made.

## Summary results of the content analysis

In Table 1 a list of the concepts raised by case study informants and their frequency is shown. This table shows that in the transcripts of the case study interviews there were 72 major references to the importance of industry drivers/outside influences in the formulation and implementation of SIS, and 47 major references to corporate strategy, as well as 43 to critical success factors etc. Of course there were numerous other issues discussed during some of the interviews, such as artificial intelligence and data modelling to mention two, but these were not repeated by other informants and are therefore not included in Table 1. It was believed by the author that the list of concepts to be considered for the theory should not be overwhelmingly long. Theories only incorporate a limited number of concepts and in this case it was believed that six to ten concepts would be capable of being incorporated into a usable theory or thesis.

From the histogram in Figure 1 it may be seen that some concepts were addressed much more frequently than others. The first nine concepts were discussed 20 times or more during the data collection activity. It was felt reasonable that a concept must be very important to the informants if it was to be incorporated into the theory which this research developed. A definition of the importance of a concept to the informants was established pragmatically by asserting that if the concept was mentioned 20 times or more it would be included in the theory. Concepts mentioned under 20 times were omitted from the final theory formulation. This complied with the view that the number of concepts in the theory should be limited. In addition the cut off point of nine allowed 86% of the results of the content analysis to be included. It is also worth mentioning that there is a substantial gap of nine between the number of times vendor selection and IS management was referred to by the informants.

## Theory formulation

The primary purpose of conducting this case study research was to collect evidence with which to build a theory or thesis about how firms conduct the formulation and implementation of strategic information systems (SIS). Thus the case study work was exploratory leading to a theory which could then be tested using a survey approach.

There are a number of different concepts of theory (Bailey, 1987). For the purpose of this research a theory may be defined as the explanation of a particular phenomenon, or a mechanism which will allow the prediction of how certain matters will interact. To be useful in the social sciences a theory must be sensible, logical, useful and testable. The test requirement relates to Popper's suggestion that a theory must be falsifiable. A theory consists of concepts which are related in statements known as propositions. A set of propositions may be interrelated to form a theory (Nachmias and Nachmias, 1989). It is the purpose of this paper to (1) establish the concepts which are important to understand in achieving SIS formulation and implementation; (2) state these concepts in the form of propositions; and (3) combine these propositions into a theory.

The purpose of developing the theory is to explain how management go about the business of SIS formulation and implementation. If a satisfactory explanation can be developed, it will provide a basis for evaluation of management and allow predictions to be made as to the likely success of SIS in a particular firm, according to whether or not the management complies with the procedures expressed in the theory.

From the results of the content analysis it can be deduced that industry drivers, corporate strategy and critical success factors are very important issues in the formulation and implementation of SIS. From the discussions with the informants it is obvious that the three issues are often considered together. The other issues of support, training, staff communications and user reaction are also clearly important and must be incorporated into the theory.

Cost-justification and vendor selection are also seen as important issues. However in the case of SIS it is the absence of emphasis of the necessity to do these which is important.

In order to assist with the interpretation of the results of the content analysis two diagrammatic conceptual frameworks (Miles and Hubermann, 1984) were developed which indicate how these concepts are linked. These are shown in Figures 2 and 3. Figure 2 addresses the macro issues which involve the environment, drivers, corporate strategy, critical success factors, etc. This diagram shows how drivers principally originate from the environment. The drivers impact the firm and where appropriate its corporate strategy and its CSFs. The corporate strategy and CSFs may actually be seen as filters through which the effect of the drivers must pass before having any influence on the firm's SIS. SIS being essentially outward looking and frequently extending the boundaries of the firm will of course have some impact on the environment.

Figure 3 addresses the internal or micro issues concerning decision making and subsequent system evaluation. The financial assessment of SIS is normally made differently to other more general capital expenditure. In addition, there is more emphasis on staff communications, training and support facilities. And, when assessment is made, there is considerable emphasis placed on user reaction to the system.

## Empirical generalizations

Using the above content analysis as well as other evidence suggested by the various informants, the following empirical generalizations have been derived or synthesized:

(1) SIS occur as a result of pressure or opportunities directly related to industry drivers.

(2) The firm's response to this pressure or opportunity is influenced by its strategy and by its CSFs, and these issues determine the formulation of the SIS.

(3) The decision to attempt to take advantage of SIS is made with little attention to detail concerning cost-justification and vendor selection.

(4) More attention is given to communicating with the staff, training appropriate people and setting up support facilities.

These propositions have been expressed in this way because, unlike hypotheses which strictly have to be stated in a testable form, allowing the relationship between two or more variables to be examined, empirical generalizations do not have this format requirement. An empirical generalization is a proposition which is derived as a result of an exercise in deduction whereby propositions are constructed after observing concepts and relationships in a real life situation. They do not necessarily have to be expressed in terms of the relationship between two or more variables. In the same way as hypotheses may be tested empirical generalizations may also be tested to see if they have the significant support of practitioners in industry. These empirical generalizations will be tested using a large scale survey to see if they can be applied in a more general way.

## The thesis

The four empirical generalizations may now be combined into a theory which offers an explanation as to how SIS are formulated and implemented:

SIS occur as a result of pressure or opportunities directly related to industry drivers. The firm's response to this pressure or opportunity is influenced by its strategy and by its CSFs, and these issues determine the formulation of the SIS. The decision to attempt to take advantage of SIS is made with little attention to detail concerning cost-justification and vendor selection, but with more attention to communicating with the staff, training appropriate people and setting up support facilities.

The above thesis and empirical generalizations comply with the requirements of sensible, logical as well as testable or falsifiable.

As the theory is developed directly from the empirical generalizations, to establish the acceptance of this theory all that is necessary is to test the empirical generalizations. This was done by using a postal survey. However, while proceeding with the larger scale survey to test the empirical generalizations, correspondence analysis was used to help corroborate how the findings of the content analysis were interpreted to develop the theory. As correspondence analysis provides a perceptual map of the data, it can provide a deeper insight into the multivariate nature of the data.

## Correspondence analysis

Correspondence analysis was developed in France, being the brain child of Jean-Paul Benzecri (Greenacre, 1984). The English word, correspondence, has been taken over directly from the French word correspondence, which of course has a different meaning of system of association (Greenacre, 1984). Thus in English the term association analysis might in fact be more appropriate.

Correspondence analysis is an exploratory data analysis technique for the graphical display of contingency tables and multivariate categorical data (Green et al., 1989). Its objective is to represent data graphically, in such a way that the nature of the associations among the variables will be seen, and that they can be more easily interpreted. According to Greenacre (1984) 'Correspondence analysis is a technique for displaying the rows and columns of a data matrix (primarily, a two-way contingency table) as points in dual low-dimensional vector spaces'.

It is a mathematical procedure which uses categorical data in the form of a two-dimensional rectangular matrix of non-negative numbers as a two-way contingency table for the geometric mapping of frequency of binary data.

The two-dimensional matrix actually used was the frequency table in Table 2 showing the occurrence of concepts discussed during the case study interviews. The primary result of the correspondence analysis is a map identifying the row and column attributes as points in a space of two or more dimensions. This is a graphical display or perceptual map which represents the relative positions or grouping of the various concepts listed in the matrix or frequency table. The perceptual map may be presented in two or more dimensions. However, it is the object of correspondence analysis to present the results in as simple a form as possible.

The main concepts underlying correspondence analysis are profile, mass and chi-square distance. Correspondence analysis is similar in style to principal component analysis, but in correspondence analysis the total variation in the data matrix is measured by the usual chi-squared statistic for row-column independence, and it is this quantity which is decomposed along the principal axis (Greenacre, 1984).

Table 3 is a table defining the different organization numbers. A sub-section of Table 2, i.e. rows 1 to 9 for all columns, was extracted for processing using a correspondence analysis programme. The programme used was SIMCA which was developed by Professor M.J. Greenacre from the Department of Statistics at UNISA.

SIMCA produces seven tabular reports and one graph. The seven tables show the basic data which is used by the system to produce the graph. Two of these reports are of significant importance to this research. These are the inertia report and the graph.

The ‘inertias and percentages of inertia’ is both a table and a histogram and is shown in Table 4. This figure provides inertia or Eigen values. In this context inertia is defined as a measure of the total variability in the original data set. This report determines the number of dimensions required to explain the original data set. It may be seen from Table 4 that one dimension will explain 45.5% of the total data variability and that two dimensions will explain 71% of the variability. Therefore, by using two dimensions more than two thirds of the variability may be explained.

The next report is the perceptual map or graphical display which shows the relative position of the different concepts and the different participating informants. This is displayed in Figure 4. As previously mentioned ID, CS and CF represent respectively industry drivers, corporate strategy etc... and C1, C2 and C3 etc... represent the different participating firms. The C was used to indicate column data and 1 to 10, as in Table 3, to represent the different firms.

The axes in Figure 5 represent the relative chi-squared distances of the data points in the frequency matrix from the centre of gravity of the original data set or matrix. The centre of gravity is the arithmetic mean profile of both the rows and the columns of the original data set. The chi-squared distance is a measure of the deviation of the observed distances from the expected distances under some assumed model which is frequently a no association model. The axes are purely numerical scales which are produced to show relative distance from the centroid in a graphical way. These axes may be thought of as two artificial variables synthesized from the original data set which give the maximum explanation of the differences and the similarities between the originally observed values. The position of the row data points is determined by both row data values and the influence of the column values associated with that row variable. The perceptual map is the key product of correspondence analysis which shows how the row and column variables may be grouped. It is up to the analyst using the correspondence analysis actually to attribute meaning to these axes. Figure 5 shows the centre of gravity and the chi-squared distances.

The projection of these concepts onto the vertical axis may be seen to be close and therefore there seems to be an association between them on this dimension. Both staff communication and vendor selection are operational issues and firms in this area could be described as operations orientated. This provides the second bi-polar dimension or vertical axis.

Figure 8 shows the full system of grouping of these concepts. Concepts which are grouped together on the perceptual map are said to be associated. The association of such variables may be thought of as a type of correlation in that the association shows that there exists a relationship between these variables. The perceptual map does not indicate the type of relationship. The specification of the nature of the relationship depends on the environment being described by the correspondence analysis.

Therefore from the correspondence analysis described in this paper we can say the firms that participated in the case study as informants perceive the issues of industry drivers, corporate strategy and critical success factors as being associated. The issues of vendor selection, staff facilities and cost-justification all have similar values on the horizontal axis. Therefore, they can be seen as a group or a band of issues. However, two points here are important: correspondence analysis provides a measure of concept quality, which may be defined as the extent to which variables are well represented in the number of dimensions being presented. The quality rating for staff facilities is low and therefore it is not necessarily close to the horizontal axis, as it is actually on a third dimension which cannot be represented on a two dimensional piece of paper. The other two variables of cost-justification and vendor selection have very different vertical axis scores. This suggests that cost-justification and vendor selection are treated entirely differently. This perceptual map shows cost-justification to be somewhat of a strategic issue, whereas vendor selection appears to be more relevant to operations considerations. This does correspond to the idea that some firms will follow a generic corporate strategy of cost leadership and that this will be reflected in their IS thinking.

In the same way as the concepts have been grouped, so the participating firms may be grouped. For the purpose of this research however this is not of much value and has therefore not been done.

## Interpretation of the correspondence analysis results

It is clear from the perceptual map that the concepts fall into a number of groups, i.e. people focus, efficiency focus, operations orientation and strategy orientation, which largely match the empirical generalizations derived from the content analysis earlier in this paper. The correspondence analysis, by placing the horizontal axis values for CF, CS and ID closely together on the perceptual map, suggests that these activities are associated. These three concepts have the strongest orientation. The concepts of CJ, SF and VS have vertical axis values which are close, and thus the suggestion is that these concepts are also associated. The same thinking applies to the other two groups of concepts. These groupings may be interpreted as being supportive of both the empirical generalizations and the thesis derived there from. It is also interesting to note that the correspondence analysis suggests a bi-polarity in these groups of concepts. Thus firms may be either people focussed or efficiency focussed. In a similar way they may be operations orientated or strategy orientated. Correspondence analysis in the current context has been used only to help corroborate the thesis which it has effectively done. Further research is required to test the findings of the contents analysis in a more rigorous way and this is achieved through the use of a large scale postal survey.

## Conclusion

In this paper the analysis of the case studies has been described and the development of empirical generalizations and a theory have also been discussed. The use of correspondence analysis to help corroborate the empirical generalizations and the theory was shown. The correspondence analysis was also used to gain a greater understanding of the data. This was specifically achieved by looking carefully at the configuration of the data on a perceptual map which helps to verify and assign meaning to relationships and associations between the data points. It also allowed the vertical and horizontal axes to be interpreted. While both modes of analysis have their limitations they also have considerable strengths, and represent useful tools available for those contemplating or undertaking information systems research.
