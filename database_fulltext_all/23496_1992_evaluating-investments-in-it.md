# Evaluating investments in IT

BARBARA FARBEY

Independent Consultant, Mill Hill, London, UK

FRANK LAND

London School of Economics and Political Science, London, UK

DAVID TARGETT

University of Bath, Bath, UK

Evaluating the costs and benefits of IT projects is currently a major issue for senior general managers. This paper focuses on a particular aspect of the problem: how organizations appraise IT investments in taking the decision whether to go ahead with them or not. The paper looks first at the different roles of evaluation; second at what is done in practice and third at what might be done given the wide range of techniques that are available and the widely different circumstances in which appraisal is undertaken. A speculative heuristic is proposed for matching an evaluation method with a particular situation. The method is presented in the hope of stimulating further research into the matching problem.

## Introduction

Evaluating the costs and benefits of information technology (IT) projects is currently a major issue for senior general managers. Many organizations report that they are uncertain how to measure the impact of their IT investments. Concern is regularly expressed through formal surveys as well as informal working groups (McFarlan and McKenney, 1983; Tomlin, 1988; Wilson, 1989; Berenbaum and Lincoln, 1990; Hochstrasser and Griffiths, 1990)\*.

The general unease masks more specific issues such as whether the organization has achieved value for money for its IT expenditures and how corporate and IT strategies should be aligned. This paper focuses on one particular issue that is of major practical concern: how can organizations appraise IT investments in taking the decision to go ahead with them or not?

As the role of IT has changed from one of support to one of strategic importance, this issue has become more difficult. When the purpose of an investment is to support an efficiency drive, the benefits of reduced cost and headcount may be quantifiable in cash flow terms. But how can organizations compare a strategic investment in an IT infrastructure which has a range of intangible and uncertain benefits with other corporate investments whose benefits are more tangible? In many organizations this issue surfaces as a tension between advocates of IT-induced change and those who have to approve the investment in the change. Hirschheim and Smithson (1988) illustrate the problem in the prologue to their paper.

This article first looks at the different roles of evaluation as a prelude to focusing on evaluation as part of the investment decision. Second, it investigates what organizations do in practice. It describes research into the way 16 major IT investment decision in different organizations were taken. In the main the projects were either evaluated by a variant on the Return on Investment (ROI) method or not evaluated at all.

Third, the paper considers what organizations could do by investigating the techniques that are available for appraising this type of investment. ROI techniques might seem the natural choice since they are in widespread use for evaluating other types of capital expenditure. However, ROI is unable to capture many of the qualitative benefits that IT brings. Nevertheless some form of evaluation is needed even if it is only to provide a ranking of alternative projects (Clemons, 1991).

Last, the paper suggests how organizations might move away from and improve on their current practice. The apparent success of ROI for non-IT projects has led organizations to search for some other single technique which can deal with all IT projects in all circumstances. This quest for the 'one best method' is proving fruitless because the range of circumstances to which that technique would have to be applied is so wide that no one technique can cope, even though some authors have claimed that the method they espouse provides the answer for all situations. However, research shows which characteristics of a project and its organizational environment affect the way an investment decision is handled and therefore indicates which of the alternative techniques might be suitable for a particular investment.

## What organizations require of an evaluation technique

Evaluation serves a number of different objectives (Hawgood and Land, 1988). First, it may be used as part of the process of justification for a system, either an existing system or a proposed new system. In this case the process of evaluation needs first to provide the organization with a good estimate as to what the outcomes of maintaining or installing the system in question might be, and second to evaluate the outcomes in terms of the organizational interest. This interest will be on a number of levels. For example, the required prediction may focus on costs and benefits. At another level the predicted impacts of the system on the organization's competitive position are important. Yet another focus might be on the impact of the new system on industrial relations.

A second objective is that evaluation enables an organization to make comparisons of the merit of a number of different projects competing for resources. If the evaluation of very different types of projects are based on a common evaluation framework – such as ROI – it is, in theory, possible to allocate resources to the ‘best’ projects.

Thirdly, evaluation provides a set of measures which enable the organization to exercise control over the project. Without an estimate of the resources needed to develop a new information system it is not possible to manage the development project. Without an evaluation of the benefits the new system should yield it is not possible to control the harvesting of the benefits.

Fourth, evaluation and the subsequent measurement and comparison with actual achievements provides the learning experience which is necessary if the organization is to improve its system evaluation and system building capability.

An organisation may want to evaluate (assess, cost justify or appraise) an IT project at any of several stages in its development and implementation. The main stages are when:

(1) Strategy is being developed. An IT strategy should be developed alongside the business strategy and the role of IT will have to be assessed in general terms before either strategy can be finalized. The outcome may be a portfolio of projects, some concerned with specific IT applications and others with the required IT infrastructure.

(2) A specific project has been defined. This may be an application or a decision to install IT infrastructure. At this stage the project has usually to be cost-justified in the context of other capital investments, and a choice has to be made of the most appropriate design which will meet the requirements defined in the specification.

(3) The project is in the developmental stage. Checks must be made to ensure that internal and external changes have not affected the feasibility of the project. At the same time progress on the project has to be assessed to ensure that the project is keeping within its budget.

(4) The project has reached the point of 'sign off'. Responsibility is being transferred from the IT department to the user department. The users have to sign that the system does what is required.

(5) The project has just been implemented. The system is checked to ensure that it is working as planned and is beginning to deliver the anticipated benefits.

(6) The project has been in operation for some time. Evaluation at this stage monitors the project's impact, compares actual costs and benefits with planned costs and benefits, identifies unexpected benefits and costs and records lessons for the future.

(7) The project is nearing the end of its life and the feasibility of replacement options is being investigated.

A recent article (Kumar, 1990) reporting on the behaviour of a number of companies in the United States found that organizations are not clear about the different purposes of evaluation and may confuse these stages. In particular, what is seen as an evaluation by an IT manager, and even by senior general management, may not be so. It may merely be the 'sign off' or closure of the project.

## Results of the research project

The research investigated 16 IT projects all of which had just been implemented or were about to be implemented. The main focus of the research was to study how the projects had been evaluated. The systems concerned were management-related and in the area of office automation. Broadly, 11 of the 16 were specific applications, three were providing infrastructure and two were senior management support systems.

## The evaluation methods used

Figure 1 illustrates how the justification process and context of evaluation was handled in all 16 cases. The projects can be categorized according to whether a standard justification technique or an ad hoc procedure was used or whether no justification was carried out.

## Standard technique (four projects)

For three projects the technique was ROI, for one it was cost benefit analysis. In all these cases the organization insisted on this type of cash flow analysis. For only one of the projects were the main benefits of the efficiency type that could easily be quantified. The other three projects were justified via efficiency benefits even though the main benefits were of a higher order.

## Ad hoc procedure (five projects)

These projects went through a formal justification procedure based on arguments about expected benefits presented in a report. In three cases the argument was that present systems were obsolete and needed to be improved. In one case the argument was that corporate headquarters (overseas) required that the investment was made to conform to worldwide company standards. In one other case the argument was that the investment would contribute to the corporate strategy.

## No justification (7 projects)

The decisions to go ahead with these projects were taken at a high level without any detailed attempt to analyse expected benefits. In four cases the project was approved because it was an essential part of the corporate strategy. In one case the project was approved because corporate headquarters insisted that a functional division went ahead with it; in 2 cases the project was agreed on a 'got to do' basis because without it the organization would lose out to the competition. In five of these seven projects the organization had a formal justification procedure which was by-passed because the project could not fit into it.

Overall about half the organizations had a formal justification procedure but most of them found that for some reason it could not be applied to the IT project satisfactorily and did not use it. Even when a formal prescribed method was used, it was sometimes modified in an ad hoc way to suit the exigencies of a particular situation. Consequently the overwhelming majority of the organizations in practice justified the investment by some ad hoc procedure (12 projects in all, 'ad hoc' and 'no justification' procedures).

The rationales behind these ad hoc procedures can be classified under the following headings.

(1) Top-down strategic: senior management believes that IT is fundamental to the success of the organization ('act of faith').

(2) Top-down by dictat: corporate headquarters make rules on what divisions can do ('obeying orders').

(3) Incremental change: the project is determined by technological change or obsolescence ('step by step').

(4) Competitive imperative: the organization must use IT to survive ('got to do').

## What evaluation techniques are available?

The literature confirms that there are a multiplicity of evaluation methods or approaches available, each with its own characteristics and focus. Only two of the techniques, Return on Investment and Cost Benefit Analysis were used in any of the 16 projects that were the subject of research.

Below we provide an outline of some of the methods referred to in the literature, starting with the most specific and then broadening. Some of the methods can be used to help estimate the costs and benefits of the project and to evaluate the worthwhileness of the projects. Others can only be used for estimation or only for evaluation.

Return-on-investment (ROI) approaches (Brealey and Myers, 1988) include a number of formal investment appraisal techniques. Perhaps the best known of the ROI methods are those which are based on evaluating the current value of estimated future cash flows on the assumption that future benefits are subject to some discount factor. ROI techniques tend to be used by organizations with tight financial disciplines. Managements often set a 'hurdle' rate which defines the minimum acceptable ROI for a project.

The main strength of the methods is that they permit decision makers to compare the estimated returns on different investments. The weakness is that some good investment possibilities are withheld because the benefits are difficult to assess in cash flow terms. Historically some of the IT projects which have provided the best returns in terms of competitive advantage would not have satisfied the prevailing ROI criteria.

The most basic and hence widely used method is cost/revenue analysis (Horngren and Sundem, 1987). The method is derived from classical cost accounting. Costs of developing, implementing and operating the system are estimated and compared with the value of the benefits it is anticipated the system will generate. The method is used where the benefits can be directly attributed to the change of system and are realized in the form of cost savings or cost displacement.

A variation of the method, SESAME, has been proposed by IBM (Lincoln, 1986; Lincoln, 1988). In this method the cost-benefit from a project is derived by computing what the cost would have been if the same functionality as the proposed system had been delivered by non-computer-based methods. The net benefit is the computed cost minus the costs of the new system.

Cost benefit analysis (CBA) (King and Schrems, 1978; Radcliffe, 1982; Earl, 1989) is an approach that attempts to find (or compute) a money value for each element contributing to the cost and benefit of a development project. The approach originated as an attempt to deal with the problem that some elements regarded as benefits or costs have no obvious market value or price whilst other elements incur costs or achieve benefits which are ‘external’ to the company making the investment.

In CBA all benefits and costs are assigned a money value based on some notion of valuation. The resulting cost-benefit values can be fed into a decision model based on one of the standard ROI methods. The main weakness of classic cost-benefit analysis (Self, 1970; Stern, 1976; Abrahams et al., 1978) is the artificial nature of some of the surrogate measures. In practice the recommendations coming from cost-benefit analysis are often overturned by decision makers who cannot accept the values selected by the analysts.

Information economics (Parker and Benson, 1987; Parker and Benson, 1988) is a variant of CBA developed specifically for evaluating information technology projects. It seeks to identify and measure or rank the economic impact of the changes brought about by the introduction of the new system on an organization's performance. The method is intended to cope with systems which provide benefits by improving the linkage and communication between departments or even as with EDI between organizations. It applies innovation and investment evaluation when the financial issues change from measuring to evaluating and choosing amongst new, untried and unproven alternatives. On the cost side it recognizes strategic uncertainty, organizational risk, definitional uncertainty and technological uncertainty as cost elements which have to be valued.

Multi-objective, multi-criteria methods (MOMC) (Land, 1976; Vaid-Raizada, 1983) start from the assumption that the value of a project can be measured in terms other than money. MOMC allows decision makers to appraise the relative value of different outcomes in terms of their own preferences: they can rank goals by applying a preference weight to each. The end result is to evaluate the project in terms of a utility measure rather than money.

The approach recognizes that in any organization different stakeholders may have very different ideas on the value the various elements of a project will deliver. It permits an exploration of these different viewpoints and exposes potential conflict at the decision making, rather than at the post implementation, stage of a project.

MOMC is best used where there are a number of possible objectives to serve a number of different units or persons in the organization. It is of particular value at the stage at which strategy is being decided. It is also useful where there are a number of design alternatives and there is difficulty in choosing between them because they do not all provide the same outcome.

Boundary values (Martin, 1990) provide a crude check on the comparative spend on IT systems. They are often based on ratios of total IT expenditure against other known aggregate values. A typical ratio would be total IT expenditure against total revenue, or against operating expenses. Other ratios used include IT costs per employee, and net benefits attributable to IT systems against IT expenditure.

The ratios can be used in two ways. They can be used as a rough guide on the level of IT expenditure for which the enterprise should plan. Alternatively, they can be used as a check on the efficiency of the IT group in the enterprise. Boundary values are concerned with aggregate statistics. Their use has little to contribute to the evaluation of individual applications or even to the decisions to be made on the installation of IT infrastructure, though if ratios are confined to expenditure on infrastructure they can be helpful in providing useful comparisons with other enterprises in the same industry sector.

Another way in which the technique is used is to provide a scale with which to assess applications. A company introducing a sales management decision support system might compare the cost of the system to sales revenue and conclude that it would require only a 1% increase in revenue to justify the cost and, of course, such an increase in revenue should be achievable.

Return on management (ROM) (Strassmann, 1985) is the value attributable to an Information System as an incremental change to an already established level of management productivity. The method is to express the outcome of the introduction of a new system as the change to the value added by management stemming from the introduction of a new system. ROM is defined as the residual value after deducting from total revenue the cost and value added by each resource, including capital, but excluding management, and the cost of management. The return on the new system is the difference between the ROM computed before the introduction of the new system and the ROM computed after the system has been implemented.

At the strategy formulation phase, changes in ROM must be based on an estimate of revenue after the change is implemented and estimates of changes to resource costs and contributions. The difficulty of making such estimates suggests that the ROM method of evaluation is better suited to ex post evaluation of information systems projects.

Value analysis (Melone and Wharton, 1984; Money et al., 1988; Rivard and Kaiser, 1989) attempts to evaluate a wide range of benefits including intangible benefits. The method is based on the notion that it is more important to concentrate on value (added) than on cost saved. To get at value the intangibles must be assessed. For example, value analysis may use an iterative approach, such as the Delphi method, to provide answers.

Where the proposed system is expected to deliver a variety of benefits, the value analysis method groups the benefits into homogeneous categories using statistical techniques such as cluster analysis. Having summarized the benefits in their separate categories a value has to be determined for the benefit. Like MOMC, value analysis permits the calculation of utility scores by attaching utility weights to each category of benefit.

The use of experimental methods is a recent development in the context of project evaluation. Until recently the precise impact of introducing new systems ex ante could only be estimated because the investment in developing a system to the stage of getting actual impacts was very high. Today a range of software development tools and simulation methods make it possible to develop a prototype or model of the new system rapidly and cheaply. This enables the designer to test and modify the system and its impacts experimentally before decisions on introducing a fully engineered version have to be taken.

There are three main categories of experimental method:

(1) Prototyping (Alavi, 1984; Earl, 1978) involves the rapid development of a prototype form of the system typically using a 4th generation language. The prototype is tested and evaluated and if necessary modified and tested again. Prototyping is best used where the impact of the proposed system is highly uncertain, and that usually involves systems which are likely to affect the behaviour of the users, e.g. decision support systems.

(2) Simulation probably has the longest history (Herz, 1990; Kleijnen, 1979). It involves building a model of the proposed system and using the model as the basis for experiments. The relevance of simulation to IT projects is that sensitivity analysis is particularly useful where assumptions and projections are more than usually uncertain.

(3) Gameplaying can be used to assess the outcome of a revised way of doing certain tasks (Kleijnen, 1979; Hirschheim, 1985; Etzerodt and Madsey, 1988). For example, a mail order company was considering the introduction of office systems to support its agent relation activities. The sponsors of the scheme had clear ideas of the benefits that could be achieved. Management needed more evidence. Rather than building a prototype, consultants suggested that managers and staff should role play each other's jobs, first on the assumption that the existing system was in force, and then assuming that the new office system had been introduced. The outcome was a much clearer appreciation by management and staff of what the new system could accomplish and equally important what it would not be able to do.

The literature also describes a number of what can best be termed as ‘composite methods’ which incorporate aspects of many of the methods outlined above (Earl, 1989; Schafer, 1988). In practice, many organizations combine parts of a number of methods and vary the methods to suit the situation. Probably the most frequently used methods might be described as ad hoc methods. See (Farbey et al., 1990a) for more details of the evaluation methods that are available and those that were found in the research project.

## Improving on current practice

The research on the 16 projects suggested that the range of evaluation methods actually used is strictly limited. What do organizations need to know or take into account if they are to apply more appropriate evaluation methods?

The descriptions of the techniques make it clear that each is applicable to a particular set of circumstances. At the same time a number of factors which influenced the way the investment decision was handled were mentioned in the interviews. These influencing factors can be classified into five main groups: the role of the evaluation, the decision environment in which evaluation will take place, the system characteristics, the organization characteristics, and the specificity with which cause and effect can be linked.

## Role of evaluation

The role of an evaluation is linked with the time and the level at which it is carried out. Both have a bearing on the questions that need to be answered.

In the very early stages the main issues are broad-brush and involve general requirements; at later stages the concerns are more detailed involving exact specifications of what the project is intended to do. At the earlier stages management is most concerned with defining the scope of the strategy and evaluation methods should help in defining that scope. At a later stage the problem is more one of measurement – of defining the precise impact of the system both in terms of costs to be incurred (development as well as operational costs) and of the benefits which are expected to accrue from the use of the system.

At senior levels within the organization the issues are wide, e.g. defining strategic goals and sketching constraints. At lower levels the concerns are more detailed, e.g. the quantification of benefits.

## Decision environment

The environment in which a decision has to be made may constrain the choice of evaluation method. IT decisions do not occur in a vacuum and the choice of a method of evaluation should match the culture of the organization. The prior history of implementing information systems has a strong influence (Reilly and Schreck, 1990). Where there is a proven record of success in the use of information technology, management is more likely to accept the estimates of the IT group and to be satisfied with assurances from champions. But a history of doubtful applications or failure is likely to make management much tougher about making investment decisions, and require a much tighter appraisal technique.

The evaluation may have to conform to an existing corporate procedure, or there may be no established practice. The decision-makers may expect only hard quantified benefits to be considered, or they may be happy to deal with soft qualitative benefits. The cost of the evaluation procedure may also be an issue, as may the availability of staff trained in the use of a method.

## The system

The criteria by which a system should be judged must reflect the nature and the purposes of that system. An evaluation method must therefore include, or provide a means for establishing, these criteria. For example, if a system investment is made for strategic purposes, to increase revenue, then the system must be evaluated using a method which includes increased revenue as a criterion for evaluation. If it does not, or if there is no means of identifying an increase in revenue as attributable to the system, the method is not valid.

## The organization

The competitive position of the organization may also affect evaluation. One factor is the industry situation: whether it is stable or whether there is, or is forecast, a lot of change re-structuring, turbulence and high levels of IT development. The second factor is the leadership role of the organization: whether it aims to pioneer or to follow.

## Cause and effect relationships

The degree to which it is possible to predict the impact of the new system is an important factor in determining how to do an evaluation (Kydd, 1989). The impact of the new system may be direct – putting in a payroll system will directly reduce the cost of calculating pay and producing payslips. But a system designed to provide a manager with 'better' information in order to improve decision making depends on the capability of the manager to use the better information to deliver the expected benefit. The impact is indirect.

The degree of certainty is equally important. The analyst may be able to make a precise calculation of the number of staff saved by putting in a computer-based sales-order system. But the computation of reduced stock-outs from a new inventory control system is far less certain.

Where the information system is part of a wide ranging set of changes – for example, changing from local distribution, via a number of regional warehouses, to a centralized system based on electronic point of sale (EPOS) it is almost impossible to determine the proportion of any benefit which can be said to stem from any component of the change. It is only possible to evaluate the costs and benefits of the whole package of changes.

The organization wishing to sharpen its IT investment decision-making must first recognize that there are evaluation techniques other than ROI. It must then try to find which technique is most suitable for its IT investment.

Each of these techniques is applicable to a particular set of circumstances. The task for the organization is therefore to characterize the circumstances in which the IT investment is to be evaluated, using the above five dimensions as a guide and then to search through the evaluation techniques to find one which most closely matches the investment.

## Matching a project with an evaluation method - a speculation

The last section provided information making it possible to see which evaluation methods might be suitable in which circumstances. The purpose of this section is to speculate on how a systematic means of performing the matching process might be constructed.

The method was derived by trial and error from the factors which seemed most relevant to the choice of evaluation method. It has, at present, no theoretical foundations, and is not based on any testable rationale. However, using the data from the 16 case studies the method produced a ‘sensible’ choice of evaluation methods for the different circumstances encountered in the study.

Clearly a great deal more work needs to be done in terms of defining a rationale for the method, and in testing its robustness. It is presented here as a speculation in the hope that other researchers will be sufficiently intrigued by its apparent success to carry out further research.

The process has three stages:

(1) Represent the circumstances of the project which is to be evaluated as points on a series of $2 \times 2$ matrices.

(2) Use the information about evaluation techniques to locate each technique at some point on a $2 \times 2$ matrix.

(3) Overlay the matrices to match project with technique.

The process will be illustrated by applying it to one of the organizations involved in the research project: a financial services organization which puts together financial packages involving loans, mortgages, pensions and savings plans for individual customers. The IT investment was the installation of a large system which linked the headquarters office with agents' offices located throughout the country. The system allowed products to be customized according to individual needs and provided immediate information on prices and alternatives.

In this case evaluation was taking place at an early stage in the development of the project: when the first decision to go ahead was being made, and before the details of the system has been specified.

## Stage 3. Matching

The location of the crosses, whether clustered or dispersed, are used to suggest the range of techniques that might be applied. For some projects all crosses might fall within the same quadrant giving a strong indication of the techniques which are suitable. In other projects the crosses might be spread around indicating that the choice is not clearcut and that several techniques might be suitable.

In the case of the financial services system, the matrix indicates that several techniques could be used. Return-on-management would try to capture the wide range of intangible benefits for a system that is at the core of the company's business. In a competitive environment 'boundary values' would monitor and compare expenditure levels. MOMC methods would satisfy the need for quantification. Value analysis would analyse the benefits which, at the time the system was being planned, were far from certain. At the same time the method indicates that the more straightforward techniques such as ROI might not be appropriate.

## Conclusions

The purpose of this article has been to report on research into the way organizations made decisions to invest in IT projects. In spite of the fact that a wide range of techniques specifically intended for the evaluation of IT projects are available very few had been used. The research also provided evidence on the factors that influenced the way organizations handled the investment decision. This led to a speculative proposal of a method for matching an IT project with a suitable evaluation technique. The process of applying such a method could be just as important as the outcome because it creates awareness of evaluation techniques and what they can do.

The other way to achieve a match is an ad hoc method based on a detailed study of projects and techniques. However, such a lengthy approach may not always be possible and will in any case be subjective. The matrix method deals with a shorter and more objective procedure for achieving a match. In order to make the matching process workable, a trade-off between complexity and practicability had to be made.

The matching technique was developed ex post from the investigation of 16 case studies. It has not been validated. This should now be done by conducting 'live' research on some IT projects as they develop and move into the investment decision-making system. This further research would also serve to refine the technique and make it more practicable.
