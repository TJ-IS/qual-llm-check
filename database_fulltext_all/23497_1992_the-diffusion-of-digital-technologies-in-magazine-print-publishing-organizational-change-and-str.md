# The diffusion of digital technologies in magazine print publishing: organizational change and strategic choices

STEPHEN DRIVER

School of Social Sciences, University of Sussex, UK

ANDREW GILLESPIE

Newcastle Programme on Information and Communications Technologies, CURDS, University of Newcastle upon Tyne, UK

The new information technologies have transformed the techniques of print publishing. This paper charts the diffusion of digital desktop publishing (dtp) systems within the magazine publishing sector, and assesses the threats and opportunities of the new information technologies for print publishing. In particular, the paper focuses on the organizational changes accompanying the new techniques as publishers internalize page make-up, and in so doing bring into question the long term future of an independent pre-press sector. The paper highlights the obstacles in the path towards full digital publishing, and examines the factors behind the slow diffusion of dtp amongst magazine publishers. Finally, the strategic implications for decision makers are assessed, and some wider issues arising from our study for the information technology community are considered.

## Introduction

The diffusion of information technologies within print publishing has been slow and uneven, marked by uncertainty and conflicting claims over the cost and capabilities of the new systems. Moreover, the introduction of computers into editorial offices has soured industrial relations, the result of which has been a marked shift in the balance of power between management and unions.

In this paper we shall explore the diffusion of digital editorial and pre-press systems in the magazine publishing sector. We shall highlight the obstacles in the path towards full digitization, in particular, the rapid pace of technical change which is giving rise to great uncertainty for decision makers in the industry. We shall examine the implications of the new technologies for the organization of work between publishers, pre-press firms and printers. Faced with a rapidly moving technical environment, we shall assess the long term future for the pre-press sector as publishers use the new information technologies to create pages in-house. In doing so, we hope to raise wider issues concerning the management of technical change in a competitive and uncertain market system.

The case of magazine publishing is an interesting one. In part, the industry remains wedded to pre-digital publishing techniques. Unlike newspaper publishing, the diffusion of digital technologies in magazine publishing has been a slow, albeit less painful, process which is as yet far from complete. To a large extent, the frequency and cost structure of newspaper production has made investments in new technology a much more attractive financial proposition for the newspaper owners. However, the development of more powerful micro computer hardware and software, and the falling costs of these systems, appears to be leading to an acceleration of digitization within magazine publishing. To date, the most far reaching developments have been in the origination of magazines; that is, in the preparation of pages ready for printing, known in the trade as the 'front end'.

Inevitably there are losers in this diffusion process and this paper will focus on the long-term problems faced by the pre-press sector, that is, those companies which have traditionally received text and pictures from publishers and which have prepared pages for printing. Figure 1 presents a schematic outline of the division of labour in print publishing, and it also acts as a summary of the developments to be outlined in this paper. To make sense of these changes, it is necessary to take a step back to a pre-digital age and the world of typewriters and phototypesetters.

## Pre-digital publishing technologies

Figure 2 illustrates the established structure of magazine publishing under photo-mechanical (analogue) pre-press technologies. The functions of editorial, typesetting, reproduction (repro) and printing were performed by independent firms engaged in market exchange. Put simply, journalists made up copy on typewriters which was then passed on to typesetters with the necessary typographical instructions, who input the copy on a computerized phototypesetter (called 'double keying'). The copy was then returned to the publishers in the form of gallies, which were edited and pasted on to layout boards before being returned to the typesetter to be made up into final pages. Pictures were sent separately to a repro house, where they were developed to the right size, and then the text, with the pictures inserted, was photographed. The film (separations) was then sent to the printers for plate making and finally printing.

Figure 2 can only hint at the multiple and enduring nature of the communication exchange between publisher and pre-press. The established system of print publishing required frequent interaction between publisher, typesetter and repro house, which promoted a spatial concentration of the publishing and pre-press sectors in central London (see Driver and Gillespie (1991) for further discussion of the geography of magazine publishing). This traditional structure of print publishing remains in place today in many magazine publishing houses, including some of the mass market weeklies. In some cases, word processors have replaced the manual typewriter, and the galley stage has been removed by publishers making up pages before the copy is sent to the typesetters. However, the basic features of the pre-digital system remain. We will explore the reasons behind the continued viability of the pre-digital organization of production towards the end of the paper. However, to understand more fully the transition from traditional to digital systems of print publishing, we need to explore the breakdown of the established industrial relations situation which underpinned the old system.

## The union question

Any account of the break-up of the traditional structure of magazine print publishing would be incomplete without a discussion of the erosion of the power of the unions, especially the National Graphical Association (NGA), within the pre-press and printing sectors.

Under the traditional structure of magazine print publishing, typesetting, repro and page make-up were performed by highly skilled NGA members within independent pre-press firms. Moreover, the NGA and SOGAT, the second print union, were powerfully represented in the print sector. The large magazine publishing groups such as Reed, IPC and Maxwell Communications, had themselves large, in-house, printing divisions. The declining power of the print unions in these two sectors is key to the diffusion of new technology, and is rooted in the profitability crisis of the 1970s.

Figure 2 Traditional division of labour: pre-digitization

By the end of the 1970s, most magazine publishers were facing an alarming fall in profits, brought about by declining circulation and advertising revenues. From the publishers' perspective, the power of the unions in the pre-press and printing sectors gave rise to unacceptably high production costs. In the large vertically integrated publishing houses, under pressure to keep the presses running, these fixed costs had to be absorbed and, in some cases, passed on to the consumer in the form of high cover prices.

The employment legislation of the Conservative government and growing anti-union confidence of employers were tested on the streets of Warrington and Wapping. These two disputes had long-term implications for the whole of print publishing in that they allowed publishers to marginalize the influence of the print unions within their companies. Moreover, both disputes explicitly raised the issue of new technology and employment. We would argue that the anti-union stances taken by Eddie Shah and Rupert Murdoch, for good or ill, forced the issue of new technology onto the publishing agenda. Post-Wapping, the balance of power in print publishing shifted decisively away from the unions to management.

The Post-Wapping industrial relations climate had a significant impact on the magazine print sector. The three major colour printers owned by publishers — British Printing Corporation (Maxwell), Odhams (Reed/IPC) and Bemrose (Murdoch) — underwent radical surgery, including closures, investments in new plant and capital and new working practices. Indeed, by selling off their printing interests, the major publishers freed themselves of any direct print union influence.

As publishers sought lower print prices in an increasingly competitive print market, so they also wanted to cut the cost of pre-press. With the development of computer-based publishing systems, the central issue became the direct input of text by journalists, thereby removing the system of double keying by typesetters. Primarily for reasons of cost, publishers were anxious to cut their typesetting bills by investing in their own direct input facility, either in the form of simple word processors outputting onto computer discs, or more complex newspaper-type mainframe systems such as Atex.

As early as 1981, the NGA proposed that their members in the pre-press sector could accept copy on computer discs from publishers. However, this offer was conditional on a number of changes in working conditions, including a 35 hour week. This was rejected by the employers. Despite the NGA's recognition of the problems posed by new technology for their members, and the union's willingness to enter into negotiation with publishers over direct input, the subsequent history of the direct input issue has been one of union retreat (NGA, 1985). A series of agreements between the NGA and individual employers have resulted in publishers controlling the input of copy to the de facto exclusion of the print unions.

From the union side, the direct input issue is largely dead; in 1989 a ballot of NGA members failed to register sufficient support for industrial action on the issue. The current merger between the NGA and SOGAT is indicative of a shift of emphasis by the unions from pre-press to print, where union membership remains strongest. We will now turn to the impact of digital publishing systems on the organization of magazine publishing.

## Digitization: a desk-top revolution

Digitization is basically the transformation of text, and now increasingly (colour) pictures, into a machine-readable binary code. In its most advanced form, this allows text and pictures to be manipulated on-screen by a computer to produce fully made-up pages, which can then be used to drive an image setter to output film for the printer. For most magazine publishers, the major area of interest since the mid-1980s has been desktop publishing (dtp) based on micro computers, in contrast to the highly centralized mainframe publishing systems found in most newspaper offices. Whilst it would be a mistake to see all subsequent uses of computers within magazine publishing as purely dtp, most of the current systems have evolved from this basic technical model of publishing. Most magazine titles are organized as semi-independent editorial units, even within the large publishing groups; the analogy favoured in the business is with small-scale cottage industries. This decentralized structure of editorial units acts against economies of scale across titles. The Economist, produced on an Atex mainframe system, is one notable exception to this rule, though significantly it sees itself more as a newspaper than a periodical.

The development of dtp in the mid-1980s — the term was first used by Paul Brainerd of Aldus in 1985 when launching the PageMaker system — illustrates how digitization has revolutionized magazine publishing. Figure 3 shows how all the processes of print publishing are integrated within a single system. Text is input on a PC and pictures are input using a desk top scanner. Pages are then made-up on screen using a dtp software package, and the document is subsequently printed directly by a desktop laser printer.

Until very recently, PC-based dtp systems could only print black and white and rather crude colour and half tones, effectively preventing its application within the commercial magazine sector. However, technical improvements have improved the range and quality of desktop scanning and printing (laser and ink-jet), thereby widening the market applications of the technology, especially to low cost and low quality magazines.

Figure 3 Single-user desktop publishing system

The attraction of a dtp type system to publishers is in reducing the cost and time associated with the traditional system of publishing (Figure 2), especially typesetting. By bringing certain pre-press functions in-house, publishers have the opportunity to reduce costs and to cut publishing deadlines, the latter being crucial for attracting advertising revenue. However, the advantages, especially in terms of cost savings, have been exaggerated in the tide of hyperbole which accompanied dtp in the second half of the 1980s, of which more will be said later.

The present range of dtp systems fall far short of the quality standards necessary to produce magazines, especially in the area of colour reproduction. Despite the increasing range and sophistication of dtp software packages, they have yet to reach the quality standards achieved by traditional typographical and reproduction skills. Most magazine work requires much more powerful, and much more expensive, pre-press systems to design pages and scan pictures, as well as litho or gravure printing, in order to produce magazines at the right price and quality.

The force behind the dtp revolution was the microcomputer industry, with little early involvement from the publishing and printing sectors (St John Bate and Wilson-Davies, 1987). This has important implications for our discussion of the diffusion of digital publishing systems below. The development of advanced (fourth generation) phototypesetters by companies such as Linotype, proceeded independently from the dtp hardware and software manufacturers. In the 1980s, these two sets of technologies converged to the extent, for example, that Linotype Linotron phototypesetters could be run directly from Apple Mac PCs. In other words, pages could be composed on-screen producing camera-ready copy for the repro houses. In their turn, the original dtp manufacturers, such as Crosfield and Scitex, began to develop powerful front-end systems aimed at the publishers and the pre-press companies which would effectively marginalize phototypesetting. These remain PC-based systems, but are linked to much more sophisticated front-end systems for the reproduction of colour pictures and full page make-up.

In short, the development of dtp technology outside mainstream print publishing, and the continued evolution of phototypesetting, are central to understanding the uneven spread of digital technology within the industry. Moreover, the current development of integrated digital publishing systems, encompassing both text and pictures, marks a radical break in the technical development of print publishing. It is to the implications of these technologies which we shall now turn.

## The organization of print publishing: the ‘prepress dilemma’ (I)

Figure 4 illustrates how dtp technology is networked in many magazine publishing offices. The single PC is replaced by a series of terminals for journalists, sub-editors (subs) and the arts department. Copy passes from the journalists' desks to the subs and art people who lay out the pages on screen, leaving holes for the pictures to be inserted at a later point. These pages are then sent on computer disc (or telecommunications line) with the picture transparencies to a repro house. There the pages are downloaded onto the repro house's own page make-up system. The pictures are scanned on high resolution digital scanners and once in a digital form, they are merged with the text. An image setter then produces the film separations, from which a proof is made for the publisher's approval. Finally the film is sent to the printers.

Figure 4 Multi-user desktop publishing

How are the new information technologies affecting the structure and organization of magazine print publishing? Figure 2 presented a clearly defined division of labour between publisher, typesetter, repro house and printer. We want to argue that these boundaries are being eroded and blurred by the diffusion of the first generations of digital publishing and pre-press systems. These organizational changes remain, as yet, unresolved as many magazine publishers cling to old systems of production, wary of the projected cost savings and the rapid pace of technical change. At present, some publishers are quickly embracing the new digital technologies, whilst others are in a painful period of transition. We will leave further discussion of the diffusion of digital technologies to the next section.

Figure 5 presents a summary of what we call the 'pre-press dilemma'. This diagram attempts to show the impact of digital technologies on the organization of magazine publishing. As can be seen, the functions of text input and on-screen page layout are migrating from the pre-press sector to the publisher. In this transitional division of labour, traditional typesetting is disappearing as publishers 'capture the keystroke', i.e. abolish double keying. This has tended to take two broad stages. First, publishers simply input raw text on their own PCs, which typesetting bureaux receive on disc (or on-line) to make up into fully laid out pages. In the second stage, publishers input text and make up pages in-house, simply leaving holes for the pictures to be inserted by the repro house. In some cases, black and white pictures will be scanned by the publisher in-house, leaving the colour reproduction to the pre-press. The distinction between stage one (direct input) and stage two (page make-up) is somewhat stylized for ease of explanation. The actual systems in place can be variations between the two 'ideal' types. For example, a publisher might produce camera-ready copy from a laser printer rather than send a disc to the repro house.

Under this transitional system, pictures are still sent to a repro house, which may well be part of an integrated pre-press company, or a printer with advanced pre-press facilities. The pictures are either processed using traditional (camera) reproduction techniques or, more likely, are digitized on high resolution scanners and are then integrated with the text, either from the typesetter (stage one) or direct from the publisher (stage two). A final proof is made from the film separations and sent to the publisher, primarily to check the colour and, once passed, the separations are sent to the printer. This process of incremental technical change raises problems for publishers and pre-press firms alike, as we shall explore in the next section. For example, a key issue for individual publishers is at what stage should they take the plunge and enter the new technical publishing paradigm given the knowledge that the pace of innovation will more than likely make any investment obsolete within a year or two?

## The diffusion of digital systems

We shall now attempt to identify some of the factors which have contributed to the uneven spread of digital publishing systems amongst magazine publishers. The pace of change in the industry makes it difficult to give an accurate picture of the use of digital publishing systems within magazine publishing. Nonetheless, a number of features stand out.

The use of computers varies between publishing groups, between titles within a publisher and within individual magazines. The overriding feature of the diffusion process has been incrementalism, that is, step-by-step adoption of hardware and software, rather than a once and for all jump from traditional publishing methods to full page make-up. The incremental approach to technical change reflects, in part, the continuously changing technical horizons in print publishing. Decision makers in magazine publishing are perfectly aware that their knowledge of future technical developments is at best imperfect. This uncertainty makes them err on the side of caution when it comes to making investment decisions which, as we shall see, can run into many hundreds of thousands of pounds. Moreover, incrementalism as a strategy to manage technical change also reflects the imperfect knowledge publishers and their staff have of the new technical systems, in particular, as regards their impact on the organization of work and the need to retrain staff, redesign titles and so on. In other words, incrementalism also reflects the process of learning-by-doing as publishers introduce information technology frequently on a title-by-title basis.

Our research focused on medium to large publishers, rather than one title publishing where simple dtp technology has probably been easier to apply. Our research, which covered many of the major publishers of consumer and business titles, revealed widespread use of simple direct input technology (stage one). However, the diffusion of page make-up systems was less pronounced (stage two), although at the time of our research (1990), most publishers who had yet to move to page make-up were in the process of introducing such systems, or at least considering such a move.

We should stress that the incremental approach adopted by most publishers means that within any publishing group, some titles, and even some sections of individual magazines, are produced on stage one or stage two systems, whilst others are still produced using typewriters and cut and paste methods. Increasingly, new titles, and re-launched ones, are designed to be 'dtp friendly' and are set up on full page make-up systems. The long established titles and the market leaders are often the last to go over to the new technology because publishers prefer to make mistakes on their less important magazines.

What have been the factors behind the slow diffusion of digital publishing systems within magazine publishing? We have already suggested that the original dtp technology was aimed not at magazines but company documents and the like, and the newspaper-type mainframe systems were not really applicable to the organization of magazine titles. The cottage industry organization of magazines also resulted in a lack of coordination in computer technology, although decentralized editorial has many clear advantages in other respects.

Unlike newspaper publishing, magazines are low frequency, less time sensitive and more dependent on (high quality) colour pictures. Economies of scale in magazine publishing are difficult to achieve because individual titles are organized as separate editorial and production units, even within the large companies. The decentralized structure of magazine publishing — titles are their own profit and cost centres — also meant that it was often difficult to justify large investments on the basis of one magazine, as the cost savings were often negligible. Publishing executives, understandably concerned with the bottom line, preferred profits today to possibly bigger profits tomorrow. Such a short-term perspective to new technology has been reinforced by the highly competitive nature of most magazine markets. The decentralized structure of magazine editorial also indicates how organization can affect the development and diffusion of new technology as well as the other way round.

Furthermore, publishing has, or perhaps had, a very conservative culture towards the actual production of magazines. Publishers publish magazines; the dirty business of pre-press and printing is left to others. In one case, the publisher of a leading up-market women's monthly had never visited a repro house until he accompanied us on a visit to one!

In terms of investments in information technologies, the key financial equation is the balance between the investment and running costs of direct input and full page make-up, and the savings achieved by cutting the typesetting bill. The complex profit and loss equation for a full page make-up system has recently been estimated (Lewis, 1990). The initial capital cost, including hardware, software and training, was estimated at £20650 for a single system. The operating cost in year one was £6000, and in the second, £7000. Against this, savings of £24000 per year were estimated in typesetting, giving a net saving of £18000 in the first year. Given that most computer equipment is written off over 3 years, there are clear cost advantages. These figures are for full page make-up. The cost savings for simple direct input would be considerably less. One publisher during our research estimated that stage one (direct input) results in a saving of approximately one-third of the typesetting bill, and stage two (full page make-up), 60–65%. Other publishers said that direct input resulted in no tangible cost saving on their typesetting bill.

Publishers have been wary of these projected cost savings for a number of reasons.

(1) The experience of publishers of the actual cost savings, especially those of simple direct input, are less than the projected savings once training, health and safety provisions, extra staff, technology payments to journalists and so on have been added;

(2) By bringing typesetting functions in-house, publishers are faced with their own problems of cost control, which had before been regulated by market relations with typesetters;

(3) Because of their power in the market — there is over-capacity in both print and pre-press markets — publishers are currently able to secure very competitive pre-press prices;

(4) The rapid pace of technical change tended to act as a brake on the early direct input systems as publishers preferred to wait until pages could be made-up fully in-house (i.e. miss out stage one);

(5) There are the internal organizational problems of actually introducing digital publishing systems. The management of technical change is difficult on already existing titles, especially when publishers have had little experience of going over to the new technology.

Despite these problems, the pressure to invest in digital publishing systems is becoming almost irresistible for most magazine publishers. There are cost savings to be made on typesetting, but perhaps the most important factor tilting the balance towards the new systems has been time. Bringing page make-up in-house has cut lead times, and is allowing magazines to stay open later. This is important for the weekly titles, where up-to-date news is vital, but more importantly, it allows the advertising copy deadlines to be shifted closer to the print day. This is important for attracting last minute advertising at higher rates. There is also a technical momentum which is leading to cheaper, simpler and more user-friendly systems, which are capable of producing a final product to a typographical standard very similar to the old typesetting systems. Moreover, journalists, once wary of the new systems, are now growing up on direct input and page make-up systems, leaving the old typewriter as a museum piece.

## The organization of print publishing: the ‘pre-press dilemma’ (II)

The first part of the pre-press dilemma was concerned with the migration of typesetting from the pre-press sector to the publishers. The next stage is the final integration of mono and colour allowing all pre-press functions to be performed by the publishers (Figure 6). This may lead to the elimination of an independent repro sector. Text would be input in the normal way, a high resolution digital scanner sitting in the publisher's office like a photocopier would input all the pictures, the text and pictures would be integrated, and a disc or telecommunications link would pass the pages direct to the printers. They would either make up film or use the digital data directly to engrave the printing plates, as is already technically feasible with gravure printing. This, at least, is the theory. What about the practice?

One way of approaching the problem is to think of how far publishers will want to go? How much of the pre-press are they willing to bring in-house? What are the advantages and disadvantages for them? At the present level of technical development, the costs associated with high resolution scanning technology are too prohibitively high, often in the region of £250 000 per scanner, to justify such an investment. Further, the high costs of such technology require a very high level of utilization in order to cover the initial capital costs, a level which few repro houses, let alone magazine publishers can achieve. Few magazine publishing houses generate the amount of colour repro work which would cover the investment costs of this technology.

The issue of ‘good enough colour’ raises a number of questions for publishers. It is possible on the present technology for publishers to define colour electronically in-house on their mono screens. This can be used to drive an advanced phototypesetter, producing four colour separations, but the quality is not high enough for most magazines. This effectively means that part of the repro process is brought in-house by the publishers, whether or not they actually own a phototypesetter. However, high quality colour work can only be done using traditional repro methods or on high resolution digital scanners. Publishers, as we have argued, are not investing in high resolution scanners and the computer hardware to integrate text and colour pictures because of the cost and risk involved in a fast moving technological world. For example, the present cost of a Scitex colour pre-press system is over £1 million.

For the repro houses, this presents them with real problems. Publishers, as they move to in-house digital page make-up, are looking for repro houses with the kind of pre-press systems capable of receiving their pages on disc (or on-line), and integrating them with their colour work. However, the publishers do not want to pay any more for their repro work than under traditional manual methods. Digital scanning is much faster than the old manual system, which is good news for the publisher, but is often not fast enough for repro houses to charge the kind of prices which satisfy both the publishers and the demands of their own capital investment.

The shift from high labour costs to high capital costs in repro is resulting in moves to intensify working patterns, such as three shift working. It is also leading to the development of large integrated pre-press groups, which are better able to cover the costs of pre-press technology over a much broader number of titles (economies of scale). The historical small firm structure of the pre-press sector is certainly acting as a brake on investment, especially in an area where costs are so high and technical change so rapid. Further, we are also seeing printing groups establishing their own pre-press facilities, although there is a general feeling that these facilities are 'loss leaders', designed more to attract printing contracts than to actually make a great deal of money.

For the present at least, these factors are preventing a radical break-up of the repro sector. However, the contraction of the pre-press sector, especially typesetting, is leading to large job losses. In May 1991, the NGA reported 6400 unemployed members across the whole of print publishing, of which 60–70% are estimated to be in pre-press. In the first half of 1991 there were substantial job losses — over 1200 in the first quarter alone — and a number of large, technically advanced pre-press companies have been closed or are in receivership. According to the union, this level of employment loss is likely to continue, with more and more of the jobs going in the repro sector.

What then does the future hold? Where are the future technical boundaries going to be drawn? Much depends upon the future development of the technology, especially its cost and capabilities, the details of which we are not competent to comment on. However, a number of paths of development do seem possible.

In the short to medium term — say 5 years hence — publishers are very unlikely to make the kind of investments which will lead to a radical break-up of the present repro system. Publishers are perfectly willing to make incremental investments in page make-up hardware and software, but will leave the uncertainty and risk of major investments in pre-press hardware to others.

However, technical change, especially in the area of digital colour systems, are opening up a whole range of possibilities. For example, one computer manufacturer has produced a colour system which allows publishers to scan and size colour pictures in-house and to assemble pages with the colour pictures in position. The publisher can check these pages using a thermal printer. The pages and the original transparencies are then sent to the repro house where the pictures are re-scanned at professional resolution. This system is currently being used on a number of high profile publications (Stokes and Slater, 1990).

Once the cost of full colour digital pre-press systems falls, as they are sure to do, and as these systems become simpler and more user friendly, it is not difficult to envisage publishers bringing all pre-press functions in-house, thereby cutting out what remains of the typesetters and repro houses. The key stumbling blocks appear to be the computer software capable of producing high definition colour separations and the compression of data to speed up these systems, rather than the actual scanning hardware (Gordon-Hall, 1990). However, the progression towards what has been described as the 'pot of gold' — colour — is by no means straightforward, and the problems of software, cost and ease of use will probably mean that it will be the end of the millennium before the diffusion of colour systems becomes widespread amongst magazine publishers (Brewer, 1989).

In terms of the structure of the print publishing filière, over the coming 10 years the pre-press sector will become increasingly concentrated as a result of the large investment costs of new technology, and also more integrated with the printing sector (either printers establishing/acquiring pre-press, or pre-press moving into printing). However, once publishers start investing in pre-press systems, the existence of an independent pre-press sector will be seriously undermined. Despite the 'small is beautifull' tag attached to dtp technology, the digital revolution in print publishing favours the large publishing and printing groups with their investment resources, their volumes of editorial copy, and their powerful market position.

For the present at least, pre-press and printing groups are willing to invest in pre-press technology because these firms can write-off their investments before there is any serious possibility of the publishers themselves investing in full colour page make-up systems. Consequently, we feel that the pre-press sector faces a short period of grace before its future is seriously threatened. After which, we would suggest that only a rump of a pre-press sector will remain, servicing the smaller publishers who are unable, or unwilling, to invest in the new technology.

In the future, publishers will deal directly with printers, sending fully made-up pages on disc or down a telecommunication line. At present, the line option is not usually cost effective for a magazine publisher because of high costs, low quality and slow speeds. A complete page, even with data reduction, consists of between 40 and 50 megabytes, and only newspapers can afford the high costs of a digital telecommunications link such as Megastream which can cope efficiently with such a volume of data. Again, however, technical developments in data compression should overcome these problems in the future.

## Conclusions

In this paper we have examined the diffusion of digital technologies within magazine print publishing. To end we shall assess the strategic implications of our study for the industry and, at the same time, make some wider points concerning the management and diffusion of information technologies.

The case of magazine publishing illustrates the problem of managing technical change within a competitive and uncertain market environment. The diffusion of digital technologies within magazine publishing also highlights the point that changes in techno-economic paradigms — such as the information technology revolution — carry with them ‘many clusters of radical and incremental innovations’ (Freeman and Perez, 1988, p47). In practical terms, as we have seen, this means that technical horizons can draw attention away from real technical possibilities, and that the actually existing technologies may well be intermediate or hybrid systems. Moreover, recognizing the incremental nature of technical change focuses attention on uncertainties thrown up by the innovation process, and the need for managerial strategies which emphasize learning-by-doing problem solving.

Furthermore, the case of magazine publishing illustrates that technical change can take place across firms and sectors, and that this can compound the problems of strategic planning. For publishers, pre-press firms and printers, we would suggest that more collaborative institutional arrangements are necessary if costly mistakes are to be avoided and efficient inter-firm working practices are to be established. With the rapid pace of technical change, a number of publishers, pre-press companies and printers have already moved to collaborate over their respective investment requirements, given problems over computer specifications and compatibility, as well as asymmetric knowledge between parties. Whilst the long term future for the pre-press sector is not secure — and these firms must develop new businesses beyond basic print publishing to secure their future — the next decade requires a considerable degree of cooperation and understanding between firms if investments in information technology hardware, software and infrastructure are to be successful.

Moreover, collective bodies, such as the Periodical Publishers Association, are a necessary complement to the market system, encouraging agreement over such issues as standards. We would go further to suggest that the fiercely competitive nature of publishing, pre-press and printing markets can work against the development of such collaborative arrangements, making the intervention of third parties even more vital. With publishers holding the upper hand in the pre-press and printing markets, the incentives for them to force down the costs of production by playing the market are great. In doing so, however, they contribute to the problem of managing the development and diffusion of the new information technologies by increasing uncertainty, by lowering profit margins, and by adding to the air of mutual distrust between parties. Here we would agree with Peter Senker (1989) that market forces, whilst providing incentives for profit making activity, can act as a brake on research and investment in new technologies.
