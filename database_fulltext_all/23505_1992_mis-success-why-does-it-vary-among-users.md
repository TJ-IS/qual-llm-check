# MIS success: why does it vary among users?

PATRICIA ALLINGHAM

James Cook University of North Queensland, Australia

MARCUS O'CONNOR

University of New South Wales, Kensington, Australia

Organizations have been developing and implementing computer-based management information systems (MIS) at an increasing rate for the last 35 years. However, evidence indicates that many computer-based MIS are not as successful as they should be and many may be considered failures.

There has been much research to investigate organizational factors, individual differences, user involvement and their relationship to MIS success, and in particular, one indicator of MIS success, user information satisfaction (UIS). Little attention, however, has been paid to whether UIS for any particular MIS varies for users from different organization functions and different management levels within an organization.

This paper reports the results of a field study of MIS in different organizations in Australia which investigates the relationship between type of user, user involvement and user information satisfaction. The results show that MIS success (UIS) varies between users of the same MIS based on their organizational function and that user involvement in the systems development life cycle (SDLC) has a significant positive effect on UIS. The significance of the effect of user involvement varies for users based on their organizational function. Technical users are less satisfied with MIS than administrative users. The relationship between user involvement and user control of the systems development process and UIS is significantly different for administrative and technical users.

## Introduction

Organizations have been developing and implementing computer-based management information systems (MIS) at an increasing rate for the last 35 years. However, evidence has indicated that many computer-based MIS are not as successful as they should be and may be considered failures (Lucas, 1975; McDonald, 1983). This failure is evidenced in under-utilized systems, systems that do not increase an organization's efficiency and/or effectiveness in any area, systems that do not provide a return on investment and systems that are abandoned. Management concern with this problem is confirmed by the results of a 1987 Delphi study of key issues for information systems management (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987). The results of this study show that 'measuring IS effectiveness and productivity' and 'increasing understanding of role and contribution of IS' are ranked in the top ten issues by both general managers and IS executives.

This paper reports a study that examined the relationship between users MIS satisfaction and management level and organizational function. It also examines the relationship between user satisfaction and user involvement in the systems design process.

## Background

Much research has been carried out on how to measure computer-based MIS success, and the factors that affect it. A wide variety of approaches to measuring MIS success have been advocated including MIS usage (Lucas, 1975), cost-benefit analysis (King and Schrems, 1978), utility analysis (Ginzberg, 1978, information economics (Maish, 1979), information attributes (Epstein and King, 1982) and user information satisfaction (Bailey and Pearson, 1983). Similarly a wide variety of factors have been studied in an attempt to determine what influences MIS success. The main categories of factors are organizational characteristics (Cheney and Dickson, 1982; Franz and Robey, 1986), individual differences (Fuerst and Cheney, 1982; Culnan, 1983), development process variables including user involvement (Powers and Dickson, 1973; Schewe, 1976; Baroudi et al., 1986), technical system characteristics (Tait and Vessey, 1988) and user task characteristics (Culnan, 1983).

User task characteristics have been considered less often than the others. When they have been considered, it is usually investigated in terms of complexity and uncertainty. Goodhue's (1986) model of IS attitudes and performance suggests that the correspondence between task characteristics and IS functionality is a determinant of systems value and 'the efficacy of information systems characteristics or policy' (p. 191). Goodhue further suggests that 'systems and functionality have no value in themselves, but only in relation to the tasks' (p. 191). Hence, research investigating MIS success should take into account the appropriate task domain. This proposition is confirmed by Specht (1986), Trice and Treacy (1986) and Iivari (1987). Specht (1986) found that the job characteristics of cognitive complexity, job level and decision making purpose are all related to data requirements. Trice and Treacy (1986) suggest the nature of the task that users must execute affects MIS utilization, another MIS success measure. Iivari (1987) concludes that 'depending on the position of the user, his/her information requirements may be mainly determined by his/her job, without any substantial subjective elements.' It can be postulated in this context, therefore, that at the individual level UIS is largely determined by the match between the information needs of the individual's job and the information provided by the MIS. In the context of this paper a job is defined as a number of tasks.

An important factor in determining whether the information requirements of an individual's job are met by the MIS will be knowledge of those requirements at the time of developing the system and inclusion of those requirements in the system. In the context of this study, it is anticipated that user involvement should positively affect the job–system fit. As previously mentioned, many studies have sought to investigate the relationship between user involvement and system success (Powers and Dickson, 1973; Schewe, 1976; Baroudi et al., 1986; Tait and Vessey, 1988). A variety of reasons have been given for why user involvement will improve system quality and user acceptance (Baronas and Louis, 1988), and hence the success of the information system. Empirical studies of the relationship between user involvement and system success have reported mixed results. However, meta-analytical reappraisals of the literature on user involvement (Straub and Trower, 1988; Pettingell et al., 1988) have found that user involvement is a factor that must be considered in explaining the success of information systems.

This paper reports on an investigation of the success of MIS developed to satisfy more than one task domain or organizational function. The Davis and Olson (1985) definition of a management information system provides a framework for carrying out this study. They define a management information system as 'an integrated, user-machine system for providing information to support operations, management, analysis and decision making functions in an organization' (p. 6). They state that 'two approaches to defining the subsystems of an MIS are according to the organizational functions which they support and according to managerial activities for which they are used' (p. 14).

Many MIS subsystems do support a number of management activities and a number of organizational functions, and hence have to satisfy a variety of information requirements for users in jobs in different organizational functions carrying out different management activities. Organizational function and level of management activity were used in this study to determine the task characteristics and information requirements for a user's job. The relationship between UIS and user involvement in the development of MIS is also investigated. Baroudi et al. (1986) indicated that this research is necessary as a replication of their study of the impact of user involvement on system usage and information satisfaction. Their study was limited to middle managers in production, a restriction which does not characterize the study reported in this paper.

## What was researched

As indicated in the Introduction, this study examined three factors that are expected to have an impact on MIS success: management activity, organizational function and user involvement.

## Independent variables

## Management activity

Management Activity was examined using Robert Anthony's (1965) framework and as in many other studies it was operationalized as level of management within the organization hierarchy: Strategic Planning as Top Management, Management Control as Middle Management and Operational Control as Lower Management (Cheney and Dickson, 1982; Davis and Olson, 1985; Franz and Robey, 1986; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; Gorry and Scott Morton, 1971).

## Organization function

For the purpose of this study, the organizational function of users was categorized as technical or administrative. This distinction is the same as that made by Damanpour and Evan (1984) and correlates to the distinction made between line and staff activities made by Bittel and Ramsey (1985) abd Henderson (1985).

## User involvement

Much research has focused on the extent of user involvement in the systems development process and its relationship to MIS success (Olson and Ives, 1981; Tait and Vessey, 1988; Koh and Lawrence, 1988). This is the aspect of user involvement that was focused on in this study. Two dimensions of extent of involvement will be measured: involvement of the particular user in the various stages of the SDLC, and the degree of user responsibility and control for the various stages of the SDLC.

## Dependent variable

User information satisfaction (UIS) is the most widely used surrogate measure of MIS success (Bailey and Pearson, 1983; Ives et al., 1983; Koh and Lawrence, 1988; Montazemi, 1988; Raymond, 1987; Rao and Soh, 1986; Tait and Vessey, 1988) for which there is a standard measurement instrument (Ives et al., 1983). It is defined by Ives et al. (1983) as 'the extent to which users believe the information system available to them meets their information needs'. This concept can be traced back to Cyert and March (1963) who suggest that an information system which meets the needs of its users will reinforce satisfaction with that system. UIS, as defined and measured by the Ives et al. instrument, can be described by an attitude towards a system (Goodhue, 1986; Iivari, 1987; Tait and Vessey, 1988). Practitioner support for use of the UIS measure is seen in Cerullo (1980), who states that practitioners consider user attitudes as the single most important success factor.

## Objectives of the research

## UIS by management level

Research question 1: Is there a difference in the UIS scores for the different managerial levels of users?

Anthony (1965) described the information characteristics (information requirements) for the different levels of management activity, and, as expected, they vary markedly. Franz and Robey (1986) suggested that users at lower levels saw systems as more useful than higher level users, and Cheney and Dickson (1982) suggest that the level of information satisfaction differs across levels of management. It is reasonable to expect that UIS for a MIS will be higher for lower levels of management.

## UIS by organizational function

Research question 2: Is the UIS score for users in administrative functions different from that of users in technical functions?

Hamilton and Chervany (1981) observe that IS requirements 'tend to be subjective' and 'can be sources of disagreement and conflict between different functional groups'. Macintosh and Daft (1978) and Specht (1981) showed that user information requirements vary with the nature of the task or technology in different departments. It is reasonable once again to expect that UIS for a MIS may differ across organizational functions such that administrative users are more satisfied than technical users are with the same MIS.

## UIS by user involvement

Research question 3: Is there a difference in UIS scores for different levels of user involvement in the development process?

Gibson (1977) indicated that user involvement should lead to realistic expectations about system capabilities and hence to higher levels of user acceptance of MIS. This view was confirmed and expanded by Robey and Farrow (1982). They proposed that user involvement in the SDLC should provide a more accurate and complete assessment of user information requirements and avoid development of unacceptable or unimportant features. It is expected that UIS scores will be higher for higher levels of user involvement in the SDLC.

## UIS by user leadership

Research question 4: Is there a difference in UIS scores for different levels of user leadership/control in the development process?

Ginzberg (1978) states that the effective implementation of an information system is dependent on the user being 'responsible for delineating the project goals and objectives... and evaluating the progress' made by the system developers. This high level of involvement, leadership and control is also indicated as necessary by Edstrom (1977). It is expected that UIS will be positively related to higher levels of user leadership and control during the SDLC. This is consistent with predictions that such involvement will lead to system ownership by users (Robey and Farrow, 1982) and committing users to the system (Lucas, 1974). Similarly, Oliver (1988) reports findings of a study which found that MIS staff have greater power than users in the systems development process. He suggests that a facilitative, rather than controlling, approach should be taken to the management of systems development projects to ensure a more successful systems implementation.

## Methodology

A field study of eight information systems in diverse organizations located on the east coast of Australia was used to investigate the research questions presented in this paper. Each organization nominated an information system for the study. An MIS was included in the study if:

(1) The information system

(i) was developed to provide key information for both administrative and technical functions;

(ii) was developed to provide key information for a number of management levels;

(iii) was rated as a key system in the organization, i.e. the operation of the MIS is fundamental to the operation of the organization, and its success (UIS) is of importance to the organization;

(iv) was developed in-house;

(v) had been operational for at least one year;

(2) The system's users

(i) were easily identifiable;

(ii) were willing to participate by completing the survey instruments;

(iii) were willing to participate in a follow-up interview;

(iv) came from different levels of management and different functional areas within the organization.

These criteria are based on a set of criteria developed by Lientz and Swanson (1980, p. 33) and modified for this study.

A total of 77 users were surveyed. Table 1 details the number of users across management levels and organizational function.

All of systems studied included on-line data entry, inquiries and report generation, and batch processing. Eighty percent of the 77 users in the sample had used other computer-based information systems. Sixty-seven percent of the users interacted directly with the system to make inquiries and generate reports. The remainder interacted through intermediaries to generate inquiries and reports. All users received standard reports and also generated reports and inquiries at their own discretion.

The stages used for the SDLC were based on Brookes et al. (1982). This SDLC seemed to relate exceptionally well to the development process in the organizations studied.

Data was collected by questionnaires and interviews. All 77 users were interviewed. Each interview lasted about 1 hour. The purpose of the interview was to confirm and clarify the questionnaire response and see if there were other factors associated with the users' rating of UIS for the information system under study. Interviews were generally conducted about 3–4 weeks after completion of the questionnaire.

This study uses an ANOVA two factorial design. The independent factors are management level (strategic planning, management control and operational control) and organizational function (administrative and technical). The study investigated the effect of these two factors on the UIS scores and the relationship between the UIS scores and user involvement and user leadership.

## Results and discussion

## UIS by management level

The results of this study indicate that UIS does not vary significantly across management levels (F = 0.26). If management level is an appropriate method of operationalizing management activity then this indicates that MIS are meeting the different requirements of the different management activities to the same degree and that UIS does not vary with management activity. These results are contrary to those of Franz and Robey (1986) who suggested that users at lower levels saw systems as more useful than higher level users, and Cheney and Dickson (1982) whose findings suggest that the level of information satisfaction differs across levels of management. One possible reason for this difference in findings is that the method of operationalizing management activity may not be appropriate when measuring UIS for a particular MIS. In the context of the overall operations of an organization it is true to say that the strategic planning is mainly carried out by top management, management control by middle management and operational control by lower management. However it must also be recognized that each level of management carries out all three types of management activities to differing degrees. The type of management activity that a user is involved in when using a particular MIS may not be related to the main management activity generally associated with that level of management. A more appropriate operationalization of management activity for studying UIS might be to find out the types of management activity for which the user uses the MIS and then assess UIS by these activities for that user. A second explanation of the findings could lie with the composition and size of the sample itself; another one is that managers at different levels see different parts of the MIS.

## UIS by organizational function

The results of the study suggested that UIS varies across organization function (F = 10.78, P < 0.002). The mean UIS score for administrative function (17.59) was over double that of the technical function (8.27). These findings are consistent with the observations of Hamilton and Chervany (1981) and Macintosh and Daft (1978). Several reasons can be advanced for these results.

## User commitment to and competence in development

Discussions with MIS staff in the various organizations highlighted the fact that administrative users had a unity of purpose and clarity when specifying their information requirements whereas technical users were less definite and more fragmented. There are at least two possible reasons for this, both of which hinge on the fact that MIS development have historically been associated with the development of systems that satisfy the record keeping and financial reporting requirement of an organization; the information requirements of administration users are largely determined by these same needs.

The first reason is the administration users' understanding of and experience with the user-MIS staff interaction. They are more aware of what is required of them to completely and accurately specify their needs. Many technical users commented that they had only recently been involved in developing information systems and they felt that they had yet to develop the communication skills and specification skills of many administration users. The second suggested reason is that the MIS staff's knowledge and understanding of administration requirements is based on experience. The information requirements of the technical users are largely related to productivity and performance measurement, areas that have only in recent years been included in many MIS. This by necessity requires information to be analysed and presented in a different format and these needs are not always understood by the MIS department.

An additional problem indicated by the MIS staff was the difficulty encountered in involving technical users in the development process for lengthy periods of time. 'Time means money', particularly to technical users.

Many of these issues reinforce the observations of Joshi (1990) that user perception of equity in the allocation of MIS resources is a major determinant of satisfaction with systems. Since the administrative users were seen to have strong historical ties with the MIS group, the technical users may have perceived a lack of equity in systems developed for them.

## Appropriateness of development methodology and management

Davis and Olson (1985) suggest a contingency approach for selecting an appropriate development methodology based on project size, degree of structuredness, user knowledge and developer proficiency. Beath (1987) similarly suggests a contingency approach to determining the appropriate approaches to managing user involvement in information systems development based on project difficulty which is determined by uncertainty, complexity, lack of specific knowledge and short term view (considerations of previous and future exchanges). This raises the question, for multifunctional MIS, of whether different methodologies for systems development and user involvement should be considered for use within the one project dependent on the user group involved and the nature of their task (organizational function). This proposition is supported by Franz (1985) who suggests that both the complexity of the system and the experience of the users and analyst 'may be viewed as factors which determine the best SDLC leadership approach to adopt to decrease the possibility of system failure'. Furthermore, as results presented later will show, the results of this study also support this proposition. The relationship between user leadership of the SDLC and UIS is very different for administrative and technical users.

## Scope of the system design

The scope of users associated with the data entry for a system is nearly always easily and clearly identifiable. This is not always the case with information users especially in the case of new systems development or large redevelopment projects which involve bringing the system on-line or integrating multiple organizational functional areas. There is a reasonable possibility that the needs of some information users may not be recognized until after implementation of the system by which time the information needs of their specific task(s) may not be able to be addressed and this may differ by organizational function. MIS personnel tend to show bias to the administrative functions, which they understand, in systems development.

## Training and documentation

A common complaint made by technical users was that training was rarely provided on inquiries or report generation and was directed at the data entry functions. MIS ownership usually rests with administration users. They tend to be responsible for issues of data entry, data integrity, transaction processing, batch runs etc. For most MIS the focus of training and documentation tends to be on these issues. The real information users, as opposed to data entry and system vetting users, are sometimes viewed as peripheral to the system.

The problem regarding training raised by the users, especially the technical users, is reflected in the UIS scores for the MIS examined. This effect of training on the success of MIS was proposed by Medsker and Medsker (1987). They indicate that effective training will minimize users' initial resistance and enhance the chance of user satisfaction.

Technical users also indicated during interviews that documentation was not directed to their needs. Many technical users expressed concern that they did not know how the MIS subsystem served the overall organizational requirements or linked in with other MIS subsystems. In some cases they saw the MIS as a 'black hole that consumed resources and gave back nothing'. This is consistent with the view expressed by Ackoff (1967) who states that for an MIS to be effective the management must 'understand how their system works' and 'how to use it'. Grimm (1980) suggests that the key to understanding a system is complete, well-written user documentation. This view is supported by the results of a study carried out by Doll and Ahmed (1985). The results of the study provide evidence that good documentation is important for user understanding and maintaining user satisfaction with operational applications.

The question of who is responsible for documentation and training needs to be addressed in many organizations. This could or should be seen as a joint venture rather than as the sole responsibility of the MIS staff.

The study also investigated the relationship between UIS and user involvement/user leadership in the SDLC. Table 2 presents the correlation coefficients between the UIS, user involvement in SDLC and user leadership and control in the SDLC.

As expected, these results indicate that the UIS will tend to be higher for higher levels of user involvement and leadership in the SDLC. These findings are consistent with previous studies (Franz and Robey, 1986; Baroudi et al., 1986). However, these results mask significant differences in the correlations across management levels for different functional areas. Figure 1 illustrates these relationships.

Discussions with users indicate that within the administration function the needs of top management were able to be defined by the lower levels of management or were well known by the MIS staff. This could explain why the correlation between UIS and user involvement was lower for top management in this functional area.

Within the technical functions the reverse was found to be the case. Top management indicated that their involvement was essential because their information needs were generally not known or appreciated by MIS staff or the lower levels of management. The lower management level users generally indicated that their involvement was only slightly related to UIS because in many cases the new MIS added to their workloads but did not appear to give any tangible benefits.

A positive relationship between UIS and user leadership in the SDLC was evidenced in both the administrative and technical functions and for all levels of management. These overall results of a positive relationship between UIS and user leadership in the SDLC is consistent with those proposed by Ginzberg (1978), Edstrom (1977) and Oliver (1988). However the relationship between UIS and user leadership is not consistent across the administration and technical functions as is evidenced in Figure 2.

A significant positive relationship exists for all levels of management in the administrative function. In the technical function, however, there is not the same relationship and in fact the relationship is negative for both middle and top levels of management. This discrepancy is consistent with comments made by the users about their involvement in the SDLC during interviews. Many technical users expressed doubts about the importance placed on their contributions to the development process and their requirements by other users in the development team and the MIS staff. They indicated that their requirements were often treated as secondary to the administrative users or at times not even included. They did not have enough power in the development process to ensure that their information requirements are satisfied.

Consistent with these findings, Lucas (1974) identified power as the key problem when designing MIS at the organizational level. Glennon (1978) confirms this by advocating that users should not delegate to others the authority and responsibility for decisions that are fundamental to the users' functional area.

Support for these propositions can be found in a number of other reported studies. Franz (1985) described a case where

other user departments complained that too much attention was given to the primary user department, which resulted in too many assumptions being made during system design about secondary users' requirements. The MIS specialist admitted that during design it had seemed too complicated to him to co-ordinate and involve four user departments.

Robey and Farrow (1982) describe how user participation and influence can lead to conflict. Franz and Robey (1984) describe a case study which shows that user control does not necessarily mean a broad base of participation, because the user leader was from the administration organizational function.

Baronas and Louis (1988) indicate that 'the active ingredient in user involvement is perceived control; user involvement is effective because it restores or enhances perceived control'. However, in the case of multifunctional MIS, user involvement alone is not sufficient to ensure success. For these systems, user involvement provides an area for bargaining and conflict resolution about design issues (Keen, 1981). Attention must be paid to the role of user control in the SDLC to ensure that ALL user groups are heard and their requirements addressed. Swanson (1985) states that

through recognizing and better understanding power, information analysts will be less guilty of ignoring the system's environment; the inclusion of power should help maintain a holistic view of the user's information needs.

## Conclusions

The primary objective of this research was to investigate the relationship between MIS success and the management activity and organizational function of the user, and between MIS success and user involvement in the SDLC. The investigation was conducted via a cross-sectional field survey of different MIS in a number of different types of organization. Management activity was measured by management level. Organizational function was described as either administrative or technical. User involvement was measured using two dimensions: the number of stages in the SDLC in which the user was involved; and the extent of user leadership/control over the stages of the SDLC. User information satisfaction was measured by an established instrument which demonstrated acceptable levels of reliability and validity.

The study results indicated that UIS does vary with the organizational function of the user but not with the management level of the user. This indicates that job-system fit, as measured by UIS, does vary when assessed by organizational function. If these results are validated by further studies this may have implications for the approaches used for developing systems for multiple organizational functions and the manner in which user involvement is managed. Attention should also be paid to appropriate documentation and training for both information receivers and information maintainers.

The study also confirms the commonly held belief that user involvement positively affects the success of an MIS. It was found however that the effect of user control over the stage of the SDLC varied significantly between administrative users and technical users. Further studies are necessary to validate this finding and to determine the causes for this difference. If the results are validated, the user–development relationship needs to be monitored to ensure that the needs of all users are addressed, rather than power being vested in the users from a single function or area within the organization.

The limitations of the study include the diversity of organizations and MIS investigated. The MIS investigated did not all have users from each management level in each organizational function.

In addition, the study focused only on management level, organizational function and user involvement; other factors that have not been considered in this study may account for or affect the relationship between UIS and user organizational function. These factors include the technical quality of the system, personal characteristics of the user and organizational context. The technical quality of the system was controlled for in some ways because all of the systems were key systems and they all had a reasonably high level of UIS overall. No unsuccessful systems were included in the study.
