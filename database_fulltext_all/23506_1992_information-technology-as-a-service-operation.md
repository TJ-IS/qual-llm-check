# Information technology as a service operation

TONY RANDS

Templeton College, Kennington, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

Research into service management has revealed concepts and ideas which may be usefully applied in other management settings. In order to evaluate their potential relevance to managing information technology (IT), several aspects of service management are explored, including the nature of service products, customer–supplier contact, the design of service delivery systems, and measuring and achieving service quality.

Some ideas have already been applied to IT, but others could also be relevant. These include examining contacts between users and IT specialists, using process flow charts to design IT services, and employing service quality measures in IT management. A general model is proposed for managing IT service quality; the application of this model at the strategic level of firms is examined.

## Introduction

## Applying service concepts to IT

With growth in the service sectors of western economies, an awareness has developed that providing a service is, in many respects, similar to supplying goods. Levitt (1972) argued that techniques developed in manufacturing were relevant to services, while Sasser et al. (1978) applied manufacturing thinking to designing service delivery systems, e.g. planning capacity levels. Possibly nowhere have techniques developed for manufacturing goods been applied more rigorously than in fast-food franchising, where McDonald's has become synonymous with a service based around production principles (Sasser et al. 1978).

While similarities were recognized between supplying goods and providing services, several writers, including Mills and Moberg (1982), cautioned against the wholesale use of manufacturing techniques in service industries, since major differences existed. For instance, while many goods may be stored, services, such as airline seats, often have no shelf-life, leading to simultaneous service production and consumption (Sasser et al. 1978; Mills and Moberg, 1982; Lewis, 1988, 1990). Also, whereas customers are usually exterior to goods manufacturing processes, they are normally integral to service processes and contribute to service production (Gronroos, 1984; Johnson and Morris, 1985; Haywood-Farmer, 1988; Larsson and Bowen, 1989; Wild, 1989).

These differences have led researchers to develop methods of analysis which address service environments. The spotlight has fallen on several aspects of services, including quality (Gronroos, 1984; Zeithaml et al., 1988; Brogowicz et al., 1990; Lewis, 1990), technology (Mills and Moberg, 1982), delivery system design (Shostack, 1982; Sasser et al., 1978), and management control (Johnson and Morris, 1985; Bruggeman et al., 1988). Now it appears these works have revealed new perspectives on management in other settings; recent papers by Kyu Kim (1990) and Galloway and White (1989) suggest the relevance of service concepts to managing IT should be taken seriously.

One reason insights from services may be relevant to management in other settings could be a growing acceptance that few products consist entirely of goods or services (Sasser et al., 1978; Shostack, 1982; Lewis, 1988; Hill, 1991). Johnson and Morris (1985) see a firm's products lying on a continuum of physical goods (what you buy) and processes (how it is provided). They position car manufacturers' at one extreme, orientated towards physical goods, with undertakers at the opposite, being service or process orientated. On this continuum, it may be argued IT provides mainly a service, rather than a physical, product for firms.

This suggests managing IT may be similar to managing other services which utilise equipment to provide at least part of the service product. However, this observation is not new, as, for a number of years, the service aspect of IT has been recognised. Rockart (1982) identified “Service and the perception of that service by management” as a critical success factor in managing information systems. Satisfaction, a measure used in service settings, is also employed to assess IT applications, while, in a study by Martin (1990), several firms said they would like to introduce Service Level Agreements. As mentioned earlier, what is new are recent papers which have approached IT management using concepts developed principally in service settings.

Research at Templeton College suggests the application of service concepts to IT marks an important departure from previously accepted methods of analysis, which will yield new insights and methods for managing IT. In order to illustrate the nature of the departure and its potential consequences, this paper firstly summarises concepts of service management, and then examines their possible application to IT, concluding with suggestions of research directions to investigate applications areas further. Aspects covered include defining service products, customer contact, designing service delivery systems and service quality.

## The service product

## Mismatches between customers' and producers' perceptions

There are other distinctions between service and physical products, besides possessing no shelf-life and involving the customer in production. Services are usually intangible, being the perceptions of customers about the processes of a service provider (Sasser et al., 1978; Johnson and Morris, 1985; Haywood-Farmer, 1988; Lewis, 1988). Also services are consumed, rather than possessed, by customers (Lewis, 1988), consumption on a package holiday being the use of facilities and goods, e.g. the airline and hotel food (Johnson and Morris, 1985).

The intangibility of services means that, in contrast to manufacturing goods where the product is usually well defined, differences often occur between what a firm believes it supplies and what customers feel they buy (Johnson and Morris, 1985). This means mismatches may occur between the service products perceived by producers and consumers (Zeithaml et al., 1988). As, in many circumstances, 'the service product is the service process itself' (Johnson and Morris, 1985), designing service products consists of developing their delivery systems (Sasser et al., 1978). To avoid mismatches between customers and service producers, therefore, customers' perceptions of service products should be understood when service delivery systems are designed (Sasser et al., 1978).

## The dimensions of service products

As services products are generally based on consumers' perceptions, attempts to define them have been based around customer needs. Sasser et al. (1978) saw customers as having a Substantive Need for the essential parts of a particular service, plus other needs which include Control, Trust, Consequence, and Self-fulfillment or Status. In order to satisfy these needs, services are generally composed of physical (what is provided) and functional (how it is provided) components (Sasser et al., 1978; Shostack, 1982; Lewis, 1988; Hill, 1991).

Surveys by Parasuraman et al. (1985) revealed that, in evaluating services, consumers used criteria which fitted ten potentially overlapping dimensions, which they later (Parasuraman et al., 1988) reduced to five. These dimensions, as well as derivatives of the original ten, have since been widely accepted as providing a basis for defining service products. The five are:

(1) Tangibles - physical evidence of service

(2) Reliability – ability to perform the promised service dependably and accurately

(3) Responsiveness – promptness and willingness

(4) Assurance – knowledge about client and ability to inspire trust and confidence

(5) Empathy - caring, individualised attention provided to customers

While these dimensions relate service products to customers' needs, reflecting both physical and functional components, Wild (1980) sought to link services to the design of their delivery systems; for this he identified three broad service categories of his own. These were the Specification of the service, its Cost and Timing. Within each category he defined narrower parameters. As, other than Cost, parallels may be drawn between Wild's narrower parameters and Parasuraman and colleagues' proposals, it would appear their five dimensions could provide the basis both for defining service products and designing delivery systems.

## Customer contact

## Classifying services organisations using contact

The recognition that customers were integral to service production, while service products were customers' perceptions about service processes, has led to several analyses of contact between service firms and customers. Larsson and Bowen (1989) have argued the nature of service products depends on how contact is managed. Contact often occurs through employees (Lewis, 1988), although, in many services, this has been reduced by substituting technology, for instance vending machines (Sasser et al., 1978). The aim has usually been to achieve consistency and reduce costs, but increased availability has also been a reason (Sasser et al., 1978).

Although technology might be employed in services, Sasser et al. (1978) contend interactions between customers and employees may be key to those services, as some human attributes cannot be substituted. Also Zeithaml et al. (1988) argue employee–customer contacts create quality. One reason contact with employees could be important is customers' contributions to producing services are often crucial to those services (Larsson and Bowen, 1989), and depend on contact with employees. Larsson and Bowen have called this 'co-production'. Although customers' input to service production may be important, Larsson and Bowen (1989) argue there has been a tendency in many services to seek to buffer customers from the core service technology. By doing this, customers are treated “only as consumers, not as producers” (Larsson and Bowen, 1989).

Due to its importance in shaping service products, customer contact has now been widely accepted as a basis for classifying service organisations (Chase, 1981). For instance, Haywood-Farmer (1988) and Schmenner (1986) use the extent of customer-firm contact and human involvement to distinguish between airlines, hospital, schools, doctors, etc.

## Task interdependencies between consumers and employees.

Instead of using the extent of contact to classify service organisations, Larsson and Bowen (1989) adopted the 'nature of the task dependency' between customers and employees required to produce the service. For this they applied a model of Thomson (1967) for analysing internal organisational relationships, which associates dependencies of parties to links between the tasks each undertakes.

Thomson defines three task dependencies, Reciprocal where the output of each has to be the input for the other, Sequential where the output of one party alone should be the input for the other, and Pooled where no direct relationship exists other than common goals.

Larsson and Bowen (1989) propose a grid with axes, Diversity of Demand and Customer Disposition to Participate, to categorise firms into four quadrants. A derivative is shown in Figure 1. In Quadrants II and III, where demand diversity is high, services would generally need to be customised, whereas in Quadrants I and IV, with low demand diversity, standard services may be offered. In each quadrant, Larsson and Bowen included the customer-firm interdependency generally needed to produce the service product (this is also shown in Figure 1).

## Designing service delivery systems

## Process flow charts

As in most services, the service process is inseparable from the service product (Johnson and Morris, 1985), it was mentioned earlier that designing a service consists of designing its delivery system (Sasser et al., 1978). Wild (1980) examines the relationship between specification, cost and timing of a service, and the productivity of machine, labour and material resources in its delivery system.

One method of designing a delivery system is to use process flow charts (Sasser et al., 1978; Shostack, 1982; Wild, 1989), which represent sequences of operations in the system. Flow charts usually incorporate processes followed by customers (Wild, 1989), as well as resources. The latter include the movement and preparation of materials, the deployment and maintenance of equipment, etc.

The inclusion of customers in process flow charts reflects their contribution to service production, and allows the service received by customers to be identified by the service provider. For instance, charts may show where customers have to queue, often for resources to become available, where bottlenecks exist and where customers contact the service provider. Flow charts may also incorporate service quality checks (Sasser et al., 1978). One limitation is a tendency to focus on physical rather than functional aspects of services; in an attempt to overcome this, service elements (Shostack, 1982) and 'fail points' may be included within flowcharts.

A simple flow chart for a hypothetical car hire firm is shown in Fig. 2. It includes processes undertaken by the customer, on the car and a number of clerical operations. The service product perceived by customers is likely to be processes followed by them, some in contact with the firm, and is represented by the dotted box in Fig. 2. Changing these processes, therefore, as well as processes to the car, will modify the service product. Technical parts of the service include the car's physical attributes, e.g. its size and economy, whilst functional parts include its cleanliness and availability on collection, attitude of staff, etc. By driving the car, the customer participates in producing the service, leading the firm to fall into Quadrant I in Figure 1.

## Front and back office activities

Although customer contact is important in shaping services, other activities, which may be undertaken remote from customers, are also necessary. In Fig. 2, several operations to the car fall into this category. To distinguish customer contact from other operations, they are usually known as front and back office activities respectively (Chase, 1981; Larsson and Bowen, 1989; Hill, 1991). One result of substituting technology for employee contact has been to increase the importance of back office activities (Sasser et al., 1978), for example servicing teller machines in the case of banks.

Deciding whether activities should be front or back office is normally integral to designing service delivery systems. Larsson and Bowen (1989) have suggested the division between front and back office work should be determined by 'the nature of the task dependency between consumers and employees'. In some situations, it may be unclear whether an activity ought to be front or back office. For instance, in Figure 2, Assign Car may be regarded as either front or back office, depending on the required customer involvement.

Deciding whether an activity should be front or back office often influences other factors, such as employee skills requirements. Each usually demands different attributes (Lewis, 1988), front offices requiring interpersonal skills, while back offices require technical skills (Zeithaml et al., 1988; Larsson and Bowen, 1989). If Assign Car in Figure 2 were front office, it would demand different personal skills than if it were back office.

## Coordinating mechanisms

Several procedures in Figure 2 may be performed exclusively by the customer or within the back office; hence Drive Car is undertaken by the customer, while Initial Check and Inspection are purely back office activities. However other procedures cross boundaries, either between customers and the firm, or between the front and back office. These procedures have the effect, therefore, of linking customers with front offices, and front to back office; Larsson and Bowen (1989) refer to these as coordinating mechanisms. In Figure 2, Pay Bill and Assign Car are coordinating mechanisms linking customers with the front office and front to back offices respectively.

Larsson and Bowen (1989) have argued that 'the coordinating mechanisms required in a delivery system are dictated by the task dependency between customers and the firm necessary to produce the service'. This means mechanisms for linking consumers to front offices and front to back offices should be determined in the light of the required service and the dependency between clients and employees. For example, with firms in Quadrant II in Figure 2, coordinating mechanisms linking customers to front offices should reflect a Reciprocal task dependency, and ensure the output of each becomes the input for the other. Meanwhile, for firms in Quadrant I, mechanisms should ensure outputs from customers become inputs to the front office.

## Managing service quality

## Service quality measures

Service quality is linked to customers being satisfied with the services they receive (Lewis, 1988), and has been defined as the degree of fit between customers' expectations and perceptions (Gronroos, 1984; Lewis, 1988; Zeithaml et al., 1988; Silvestro et al., 1990). However, Gronroos (1984) distinguishes between technical and functional quality, reflecting the physical (what is provided) and process (how it is provided) aspects of services (Sasser et al., 1978; Shostack, 1982; Lewis, 1988; Hill, 1991).

Gronroos (1984) argues technical quality is necessary, but not sufficient to achieve customer satisfaction, and suggests overall quality also involves attaining functional quality. Brogowicz et al. (1990) believe the two often cannot be separated, as high technical expectations may lead to high functional expectations. They argue that functional quality is easier for consumers to evaluate than technical quality, but may be more difficult for the supplier to measure.

In order to measure service quality, Parasuraman et al. (1988) developed a tool, SERVQUAL, which recorded consumers' views about twenty two aspects of services, each related to one of the five service dimensions described earlier. Consumers were asked for their 'expectation before and perception after' they received the service, but not their ranking of the importance of each aspect. Carman (1990) concluded SERVQUAL was reliable, but recommended consumers also be questioned about the importance of each aspect, consistent with services being composed of central and peripheral elements (Sasser et al., 1978; Brogowicz et al., 1990). Carman also questioned whether the five dimensions were generic, or could be amended for services other than retailing, where SERVQUAL was originally developed.

Several studies have been made using derivatives of SERVQUAL. Lewis (1990) assessed customers' views of some major UK banks, building societies and retailers. Meanwhile, Kierl and Mitchell (1990) adapted SERVQUAL to measure the service quality of six firms supplying basic industrial products, perceived by 85 senior purchasing managers. Their questionnaire included the importance of each aspect, and the results showed the gap profile across the service dimensions for the six firms. Kierl and Mitchell concluded with a map of how each firm fitted consumers' needs.

## Designing quality into delivery systems

The simultaneous production and consumption of services creates obstacles to inspecting their quality (Sasser et al., 1978). This means service quality may best be achieved by designing it into the delivery system (Johnson and Morris, 1985; Sasser et al., 1978). Sasser et al. describe a hotel, where complaints about oversights in preparing rooms, e.g. burned out bulbs, were not solved by regular inspections. They were eventually reduced by standardising procedures for preparing rooms.

In designing quality into delivery systems, a relationship between service level and service cost to customers should also be recognised. Sasser et al. (1978) identified service level-price combinations, acceptable to both customers and producers, and others where the service level was either too high or low for the price paid by customers. This made the services unprofitable for producers, or unacceptable to customers.

Previous discussions suggest a number of factors are important in achieving service quality. Among these are:

(1) Front-back office distinctions: a failure to make the correct front-back office distinction for an activity, e.g. Assign Car in Figure 2, may lead to poor service quality. Contact personnel may lack sufficient interpersonal skills (Zeithaml et al., 1988) to interact with customers.

(2) Coordinating mechanisms should reflect customer-employee dependency: coordinating mechanisms within a service delivery system should be compatible with the dependency required to produce the required service product (Larsson and Bowen, 1989). Mechanisms should ensure consumers contribute to producing services.

The latter suggests that, when the processes for linking clients, front and back offices are inappropriate to the task dependency required to produce the service, the resulting service is unlikely to satisfy customers' needs, causing low quality.

## Achieving quality in customised professional or specialist services

In Figure 1, Quadrant II contains firms which experience high demand diversity from customers with a strong disposition to participate in producing the service. Larsson and Bowen (1989) argue customers of these firms often have unique and complex problems requiring specialist expertise from employees to customise individual solutions. To produce this service, Fig. 1 shows task dependencies between customers and employees generally to be Reciprocal (Larsson and Bowen, 1989). This means contacts between employee and customers should be managed to ensure the outputs of each are the inputs for the other, reflecting the 'need for the customer to provide information for adequate problem solving throughout service production'. This close linking should lead to 'interactive service production' (Larsson and Bowen, 1989). Many professional and specialist services, for instance legal advice and medical care, fall within Quadrant II (Larsson and Bowen, 1989), because consumers require expertise, and Reciprocal dependency with employees is necessary to produce the customised service.

However, in professional services, power distribution between clients, professionals and service managers influences the way operating decisions are taken (Harvey, 1990), which in turn impact the quality and quantity of the service provided. A number of factors influencing power distribution are external. The professional's power, represented by autonomy, namely the “absence of ... pressure from clients, from the organisation or from any other person” (Harvey, 1990), is partly determined by the prestige of, and supply and demand for, the profession. Clients' power is partly shaped by competition for their business and their knowledge of the profession (Harvey, 1990). Meanwhile internal factors influencing power distribution include procedures which allow professionals to act as gatekeepers to the service.

For clients requiring a customised specialist service, therefore, a potential dichotomy exists between the Reciprocal dependency with professionals needed to produce the service, and power distribution between clients, professionals and service managers. This may allow professionals sufficient autonomy so client contacts do not reflect the Reciprocal task dependency demanded by the service. As a result, professionals may interpret clients' requirements differently from clients themselves, creating service mismatches.

This suggests that, to achieve quality, internal procedures of organisations providing customised specialist services should manage the power distribution in a manner which permits the Reciprocal task dependency, necessary for the service, to function. Front office activities should be the main focus for coordinating mechanisms (Larsson and Bowen, 1989), with links between front and back offices supporting these. Front office procedures should ensure a continuous transmission of information between clients and employees.

## Models for managing service quality

For firms to manage service quality and minimise gaps between consumer expectations and perceptions, Zeithaml et al. (1988) identified four sources for these gaps. They attributed these sources to mis-perceptions which occur during the specification, design and installation of service delivery systems.

The sources identified by Zeithaml et al. were:

Gap 1 – difference between consumer expectations and management perceptions of consumer expectations.

Gap 2 – difference between management perceptions of consumer expectations and the service specification.

Gap 3 – difference between the service specification and the service actually delivered.

Gap 4 - difference between the service actually delivered and what is communicated to consumers about the service specification.

Zeithaml et al. (1988) proposed a model for achieving service quality, which sought to manage these four gaps during the design and implementation of service delivery systems. The model, therefore, provides a framework for managing service quality. Brogowicz et al. (1990) later modified Zeithaml and colleagues' model to allow for, amongst other things, technical and functional aspects of service (Gronroos, 1984). The proposed framework for managing quality in IT services is a derivation of these models.

## Applying service concepts to IT management

## Earlier papers using service concepts

It was argued earlier that adopting service concepts could be an important departure from accepted methods for analysing IT management, which may herald fresh insights and new approaches. Galloway and White (1989) produced one of the first papers using service concepts, which considered IT development projects in terms of front-back office activities. They argued that, whereas Design, Programming and Testing are often treated as back office, they should also be front office. Skills required for contact with users are often not possessed by programmers and analysts in typical DP departments.

Meanwhile Kyu Kim (1990) examined inconsistencies between earlier methods of measuring User Satisfaction with information systems, and proposed that service concepts provided a conceptual base. Using these, Kyu Kim defined User Satisfaction as a function of the difference between users' expectations and perceptions of an IT application, and identified three gaps within the Software Development Cycle. These were gaps in determining information needs, designing and installing the information system, and delivering the information service. Kyu Kim's gaps are similar to Zeithaml et al. (1988), explained earlier, but do not correspond exactly.

By applying ideas derived from service environments, I believe both works have added to our understanding of IT management over more traditional approaches. Despite this, previous discussions suggest other service concepts might also be relevant, and provide additional insights into defining IT service products, managing contacts between users and IT specialists, and achieving quality in IT services. These are explored in the remainder of the paper.

## The IT service product

It was argued in the Introduction that, for firms, the consequence of deploying IT must be the receipt of services, as IT installations and personnel in themselves do not constitute the IT product. This may mean that determining the nature of the service product provided by a firm's IT resources should be linked to benefits it is able to gather through their use. However, benefits may be perceived at a number of levels; for instance, at the lowest are those derived from individual IT applications, whilst, at the level of the firm, lie overall benefits obtained from its complete range of IT resources, which includes people, software, equipment, etc.

## Components of the IT service product

This discussion suggests the overall service product provided to a firm by its IT resources could be perceived as the combined outcome from a portfolio of separate IT service components, possibly occurring at different levels, from specific areas to the level of the firm. It is proposed here that individual IT service components in the portfolio will tend to fall into one of the four quadrants suggested by Larsson and Bowen (1989), with the total IT service product the combined outcome of the service component mix. A suggested portfolio of service components comprising the overall service product provided by a firm's IT resources is shown in Figure 3. Should the mix of service components within the portfolio of Figure 3 change, so the total IT service product provided to the firm would be modified. Components comprising the portfolio which forms the overall IT service product include IT strategy formulation, equipment maintenance, and help desks.

In Figure 3, applications development has been included as a service component. This is because the product of the applications development process within the IT function should not be conceived as a delivered system, for users may feel this to be only part. They might also believe the product includes the way the system is delivered, e.g. its timeliness, difficulties in installation, etc., and other factors, such as training, helpfulness of IT staff, etc. These items are not physical and have parallels with the functional (the manner of delivery), rather than the physical (what is delivered), aspects of services (Gronroos, 1984). Thus the product of the IT Function's development processes may also be perceived as a service, similar to helps desks, composed of physical elements, i.e. delivered systems, manuals, etc., and functional parts, such as those just discussed.

In Figure 3, each service component has been assigned to the quadrant felt most appropriate. For instance, the application development service is placed in Quadrant II, as customizing is usually high and users often have a strong disposition to participate, while the help desk service has been placed in Quadrant III, as users may have a wide range of queries and not be disposed to participate in creating the service. On the other hand, if the end-user development support service provided were standard, e.g. confined to assistance with creating Lotus models, then this would fall in Quadrant I, as user disposition would be high. Finally, the network operation service has been put in Quadrant IV, as this is likely to be standard, with user disposition low.

## Service quality as a surrogate measure for IT effectiveness

It was suggested earlier that defining IT service products may need to be linked to benefits a firm is able to gather through using its IT resources, comprising equipment, people, software, etc. However, many uncontrollable and unmeasurable factors influence firms, often making it impossible to attribute performance improvements directly to the use of IT (DeLone, 1988). This creates an impediment to measuring IT's effectiveness and contribution, across all levels of firms, through benefits directly accredited to the use of IT resources. Instead surrogate measures have been developed (Bailey and Pearson, 1983; Miller and Doyle, 1988; DeLone, 1988), some of which relate to users' reactions. For individual IT applications, these reactions include users' feelings, e.g. user satisfaction (Bailey and Pearson, 1983; Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988; Ives et al. 1983), and the extent applications are used (DeLone, 1988).

Although this paper has claimed that service concepts provide additional insights into managing IT to traditional methods, it is recognized here that using service concepts is unlikely to remove this basic impediment to assessing IT effectiveness through direct measurement, caused by the influence of uncontrollable and unmeasurable factors on firms' performance. Thus it would appear that considering IT from a service perspective provides alternative surrogate measures of IT effectiveness to those mentioned earlier. In this case, the surrogate measure would be the quality provided by individual service components within the IT service product, typified by the portfolio shown in Figure 3. The implicit assumption would be that, if the mix of service components within the portfolio were consistent with the required IT service product, and the quality of each component were sufficient, then this would lead to overall IT effectiveness within the firm.

## Dimensions of IT service components

Each service component within a firm's overall IT service product is created by users' interaction with a range of different IT resources, e.g. equipment, software, staff, etc. which have been assembled to provide the delivery system for that particular service component. For example, users consuming the help desk service may mainly interact with IT staff located at terminals through telephones, whilst those using the network operation service may primarily interact with keyboards and screens, and only occasionally with operators.

With a range of different IT service components potentially available to firms, it would appear advantageous, therefore, if a consistent method of defining each component could be provided, which could be applied across the entire range and permit comparisons between service components. Also it would seem desirable for the method to reflect both their technical (what is provided) and functional (how it is provided) aspects (Gronroos, 1984). For in each service component, functional aspects may be as important as technical in determining quality. For example, help desk service quality may depend, not only on equipment installed, but helpfulness and competence of staff.

![](/api/attachments/Z2AF3C8Z/fulltext/images/cc7eacdaebe408bc0415341b91228e5fddf553e3a5472b6924baaae2c2f0bf40.jpg)

This need to reflect the technical and functional aspects of each service component implies that the chosen method of definition should regard IT service components as multi-dimensional (Parasuraman et al., 1988). With this in mind, it is proposed that, subject to reservations expressed by Carmen (1990), the service components of the IT service product should each be measured using the five dimensions of Parasuraman et al. (1988). A general model which incorporates these dimensions is given in Figure 4. Here a single service component within the portfolio which forms the IT service product is shown. User performance, stemming from the provision of the service component, is influenced by service quality, and provides benefits, small or otherwise, to the firm. The service component in Figure 4 is provided by its own individual component delivery system, a sub-system of the overall IT delivery system, consisting of physical resources (e.g. equipment, software, staff), service infrastructure, intellectual products, etc. In the case of the help desk service, the delivery system may comprise telephones, staff, equipment, documentation, etc. Meanwhile the delivery system for IT strategy formulation service may be comprised principally of IT managers and documentation.

I believe that viewing IT services as multi-dimensional using the five dimensions of Parasuraman et al. is a radical departure from earlier approaches to IT, which may significantly increase our insight into IT management. Traditional approaches have tended to treat IT in unidimensional terms, with the possible result that the impact of physical aspects on IT effectiveness has been over-emphasized. One example of this could be user satisfaction, which has often been measured using a single scale, with the result that several tools developed (e.g. Bailey and Pearson, 1983; Montazemi, 1988; Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988; Ives et al. 1983) possibly have a technical bias in questions put to users.

## User/IT staff contacts

Earlier descriptions of the perspective provided by service concepts suggest contact between users and IT staff may be key in creating the required quality for each service component of the IT service product shown in Figure 3. It is possible the following aspects of contact may be applicable.

(1) Task dependencies required to produce IT service components: Figure 3 has demonstrated that service components of the IT service product may be differentiated according to the degree of customizing required, and users' disposition to participate in service production. This suggests task dependencies between users and IT staff should match the quadrant to which each service component is assigned. For instance, providing the application development service demands reciprocal relationships between users and IT staff, with outputs of each becoming inputs to the other. The IT strategy formulation service would also appear to require reciprocal relationships. On the other hand, providing the help desk service for users with IT knowledge, e.g. end-users (Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988), may require sequential task dependencies between them and IT staff.

Whichever task relationship may be required to create a particular IT service component, Larsson and Bowen (1988) have highlighted the importance of managing consumer contact with staff, so consumers become part of service production. This would suggest that, however slight user/IT staff contact may need to be to produce a particular service component in Figure 3, e.g. the data base maintenance service, that contact should be managed so users contribute to service production.

(2) Front/back office IT activities: Galloway and White (1989) have already drawn attention to front and back office aspects of managing the IT function during applications development projects, namely the application development service in Quadrant II of Figure 3. Their analysis has highlighted the need to increase the inter-personal skills of analysts and programmers so contacts with users, namely front office activities, produce a satisfactory service during development projects. However Figure 3 suggests other service components would require a different balance between front and back office activities. For instance, in the case of the network operation service, many activities may be back office, with few front office.

(3) Co-ordinating mechanisms: Larsson and Bowen's (1989) proposition, that coordinating mechanisms linking consumers, front and back offices should reflect task dependencies required to produce a service, demonstrates how users, and front and back office IT staff should be linked to each other. For instance, to provide a satisfactory application development service where reciprocal task dependencies are necessary, the main locus should be procedures co-ordinating interactions between front office IT staff and users to ensure 'mutual adjustment' through 'large, loosely specified scripts' (Larsson and Bowen, 1989); meanwhile there should be limited decoupling between front and back office IT staff. On the other hand, to provide the help desk service in Quadrant III of

Figure 3, the main locus should be linking between front and back office IT staff.

(4) Managing user participation: Bowen et al. (1989) have highlighted the importance of service providers managing customer participation during service production. As the required user participation in producing each IT service component identified in Figure 3 is likely to be different, this means managers of the IT function must provide a range of user participation patterns, each consistent with its relevant service component.

In the case of the application design service, Figure 3 has suggested users have a high disposition to participate, and relationships with IT analysts need to be reciprocal. This may mean that, in order to manage user participation satisfactorily, the software development cycle used by the IT function should include processes for user role-making, as well as procedures to ensure users possess relevant skills (e.g. IT knowledge) to contribute towards the production of the required system (Bowen et al., 1989). Without these, reciprocal relationships may not be developed between users and IT staff.

(5) Managing user/IT staff power distribution: Power distribution between users and IT staff may be shaped by a number of factors, such as the nature of IT staff specialisms, IT staff shortages and turnover, resource allocation procedures which may allow IT staff to act as gatekeepers, users' reliance on the internal IT function to provide services, etc. It was shown earlier that the management of this distribution could be critical in the case of Quadrant II services.

The principal aim of this paper is to introduce service concepts in the context of IT, and it is outside its scope to pursue specific topics in depth. However, a hypothetical example of power distribution in IT services falling in Quadrant II of Figure 3 may illustrate its importance. Here, one of the IT service components is the application development service. If the power distribution in operation allowed IT staff providing this service, in this case specialist analysts and programmers, considerable autonomy, a dichotomy could exist as the required reciprocal task dependency may not function in practice. Instead of procedures coordinating users' interactions with front office IT specialists being the main locus, and designed to ensure 'mutual adjustment' through 'large, loosely specified scripts', those procedures could become secondary to back office activities such as programming. Moreover, the procedures could also be in the form of 'standardized, tightly specified scripts' (Larsson and Bowen, 1989). Such procedures, therefore, would be more appropriate to

Quadrant IV services than Quadrant II (Larsson and Bowen, 1989), as IT specialists, rather than users, would tend to dictate the specification, timing and other aspects of systems.

It may be argued that applying service concepts to IT can lead to increased conceptual clarity about contacts between users and IT staff compared with earlier approaches. For instance, in the case of user involvement, or participation (Barki and Hartwick, 1989), during information systems development, various studies have come to mixed conclusions as to whether its impact was positive or otherwise (Ives and Olson, 1984). However, an examination of the studies reviewed by Ives and Olson in the light of the service concepts, just discussed, reveals potential difficulties in their approach, which may have made mixed conclusions inevitable. For service concepts demonstrate that, on its own, user participation in systems development cannot guarantee improved systems, unless it occurs in circumstances which allow reciprocal relationships with IT specialists. Should circumstances create other relationships, for instance sequential, due, say, to the power distribution in operation or a systems development cycle which failed to define users' roles adequately, then user participation would not necessarily lead to the application development service in Figure 3 producing satisfactory systems. Mixed conclusions across the studies reviewed by Ives and Olson could have stemmed from a failure in those studies to identify whether the user participation they recorded took place in circumstances which permitted reciprocal relationships.

## Measuring and achieving IT service quality

Users and IT staff are likely to develop different perceptions about each IT service component, possibly leading to serious mismatches. It may be easier for users to assess functional rather than technical quality of an IT service, but difficult for IT staff to measure functional quality (Brogowicz et al., 1990); therefore mismatches may concern the perceived relevance of technical and functional elements for each service component in Figure 3.

The framework of Parasuraman et al. (1988) suggests quality measures for IT service components should be based around the five dimensions proposed earlier, thereby indicating both functional and technical performance. They should also incorporate differences between users' expectations and perceptions of each service they receive. Kyu Kim (1990) has used the latter thought to propose a measure for user satisfaction with information systems applications, namely the applications development service, but made no suggestions about service dimensions.

To achieve quality in each IT service component, service concepts suggest:

(1) The use of SERVQUAL-type tools to assess IT service quality: IT versions of SERVQUAL which measured quality along five dimensions could provide sounder surrogate measures for IT effectiveness than single scale measures. Various items would be included for each dimension in Figure 4, and users asked for their expectations, perceptions and, possibly, importance (Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988; Carman, 1990) for each item.

In the case of assessing quality of the applications development service, SERVQUAL tools could incorporate items already available from existing user satisfaction measures, e.g. Miller and Doyle (1987). However additional items may also be required, possibly linked to dimensions which highlight functional aspects of the applications delivery service, such as Empathy.

(2) Designing quality into IT delivery systems: Service concepts suggest that quality of all IT service components is best achieved by designing it into the delivery system of each (Sasser et al., 1978). This has already been recognized in certain areas, for instance the applications development service, where Rahman (1987) has argued that poor software quality cannot be avoided by final inspection alone; processes which build quality into applications must be incorporated into the systems development cycle. Rahman is supported by a report for the DTI in the UK (Price Waterhouse, 1988) which said 'it is more important to build quality through the development process, rather than identifying faults by testing the finished product'. However, this message is, apparently, not widely appreciated. A survey of over 400 firms by Vickers et al. (1987), covering a cross-section of industries in the US, showed less than $32\%$ had even formal quality standards for software development.

(3) IT service process flow charts: The use of process flow charts to design the delivery systems of individual IT service components may assist in identifying the service product in each case, by including processes covered by users, as well as IT staff. Process flow charts may incorporate quality procedures, e.g. as proposed by Price Waterhouse (1988) for IT applications, as well as coordinating mechanisms linking users to front offices, and front to back offices.

Figure 5 shows the process flow chart for a hypothetical application development service, based around the traditional systems development cycle. The service product, as perceived by users, is represented by the dotted box, and is composed of processes involving them, i.e. use system, training and accept system, etc. Processes and documentation used by IT specialists during the software development cycle have also been included. There are a number of coordinating mechanisms, for instance accept system.

## A proposed framework for managing IT service quality

In order to bring together the ideas which have just been discussed, a general framework is proposed, which may be applied to each IT service component in Figure 3. This framework seeks to achieve service component quality by managing the specification, design and installation of the delivery system for each service component. The framework is shown in Figure 6, and is an adaptation of the model by Zeithaml et al. (1988), referred to earlier, which was later modified by Brogowicz et al. (1990).

Figure 6 attributes gaps between users' expectations and perceptions of a particular IT service component to misperceptions which occur during the specification, design and installation of its delivery system shown in Figure 4. Perceptions of users' expectations by IT management provides the basis for service component specification, i.e. determining the required features of the service component delivery system. Following this, the service component delivery system is installed, and is composed of various IT resources, as shown in Figure 4. IT management, explicitly or implicitly, passes communication to users about the service component specification, which helps form the service image for that component.

Meanwhile, users' experiences of the service component delivery system influences their opinion about the perceived technical service and perceived functional service. Perceived service is judged partly from these components, but also from communication to users. Expected service is a combination of expected technical service and expected functional service, shaped by needs, word of mouth, past experience, service component image and the technology environment.

In Figure 6, service component quality is measured by the service gap, i.e. the difference between the expected service and perceived service, and is a combination of the technical gap and the functional gap. Sources for the service gap occur during the specification, design and implementation of the service component delivery system. In this framework, four gaps are identified. These are:

Gap 1 - difference between expected service and perceptions of user expectations within the IT function. Gap 2 - difference between perceptions of user expectations within the IT function and service component specification.

Gap 3 - difference between service component specification and the installed service component delivery system.

Gap 4 – difference between installed service component delivery system and communication to users.

This framework may provide the basis for managing the quality of different IT service components within the portfolio of Figure 3, for instance, help desks, end user development support, etc. A version of Figure 6 has already been applied to the application development service by Kyu Kim (1990), who traced differences between users' expectations and perceptions to three gaps, rather than the four suggested by Figure 6. The gaps identified by Kyu Kim occur during different stages of the systems development cycle, such as determining information needs, designing and installing the MIS, and delivering the information service (Kyu Kim, 1990).

## Conclusions

In this paper, several service concepts have been discussed, and their general meaning in the context of IT management examined. At this early stage of applying service management principles to IT, the discussion in no way constitutes proof that service concepts are relevant to managing IT resources and guaranteeing IT effectiveness. However, their use to explain why the surveys examined by Ives and Olson (1984) were inconclusive is encouraging for their application to IT. Also, Rahman's (1987) proposals for building quality into applications through the software development cycle coincide with the argument by Sasser et al. (1978) that quality cannot be inspected into services.

In order for the usefulness of service concepts to IT to be evaluated, further theoretical and applied research is required. Several directions are immediately apparent. Among these is a detailed study of IT service dimensions, possibly identifying dimensions relevant to different IT services at various levels of firms. Another avenue is research into the value of using SERVQUAL type measures for user satisfaction with firms' applications development service. User participation in systems development could also be usefully revisited, making allowances for task dependencies required to develop the system, and the impact of the coordinating mechanisms. When these, and other research projects, are completed, it may be possible to evaluate the potential value of these approaches to managing IT. After this new methods of managing IT may emerge.
