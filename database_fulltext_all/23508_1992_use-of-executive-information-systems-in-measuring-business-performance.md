# Use of executive information systems in measuring business performance

JAMES HOLOHAN

Noble Lowndes Irish Pensions Trust Limited, Dublin, Ireland

Much effort and research has concentrated on the need to formulate and implement business strategy, and the need to monitor performance against strategic plans. This paper, based on research carried out from July to September 1990, identifies the methodologies which organizations applied to define performance indicators presented on their Executive Information Systems and if these indicators helped steer the organizations towards their strategic objectives. Organizations applied either a critical success factor, an alternative top-down, or an evolutionary/prototype methodology to build their EISs. Unfortunately, not one organization which took part in the research modelled its EIS on its overall business strategy, thus limiting the use of their EIS to that of a glorified fire extinguisher rather than using it to help bridge the gap between formulating and implementing a business strategy.

## Introduction

Many eminent writers and commentators on managerial and business issues constantly highlight the need for top managers to formulate and implement strategy, and the need to constantly monitor performance against strategic plans. Various methodologies and tools have been devised to assist managers with these tasks and still many managers fail to achieve what is widely seen as their main function – strategy formulation and implementation. Executive Information Systems (EIS) is one such tool which has entered the arena over the last decade, but on their own they are simply just another managerial tool and if used in isolation they add no value to improving the manager's lot.

During the period July–September 1990, I carried out sponsored research on behalf of Business Intelligence, London, which formed the basis of my dissertation for an MBA at City University Business School, London. My primary objective was to identify the methodologies which organizations applied to define the performance indicators they present on their EISs, and if any of these methodologies linked closely with the organizations' overall business strategies. Of all the responses (questionnaires and interviews) 20 are current users of an EIS and seven intend to install as EIS within the next 12 months. From an estimated EIS user population in the UK and Ireland of between 170 and 220, the sample size for current users is reasonable.

The research involved sending questionnaires and conducting interviews with organizations in the UK and Ireland. From the sample, only 25% of current EIS users applied a Critical Success Factor (CSF) approach to develop their EIS. A further 25% applied an alternative top-down approach and the remaining 35% an evolutionary/prototype approach. Fifteen per cent declined to say which methodology they applied. Unfortunately, not one organization modelled its EIS on its overall business strategy and worse still, none of the participants in the research flagged this as a problem. Hence, the opportunity to bridge the gap between formulating and implementing strategy (which would have resulted in a strategic driven EIS) was missed.

## Executive information system (EIS) and the critical success factor (CSF) concept

## Senior managers and their work

Henry Mintzberg (1990, p. 163) stated that the classical view of a manager as the organizer, coordinator, planner and controller at best indicates some vague objectives which managers have when they work. Mintzberg points out that there is no answer to the basic question: what do managers do? and wonders how can we design planning or information systems for managers.

John Kotter (1982, p. 156) of the Harvard Business School goes one step further and suggests that a large gap exists between the classical view of a manager's function, tools and systems on the one hand and the manager's behaviour on the other. Kotter finds this gap disturbing as it raises many serious questions about the type of formal planning and appraisal systems in common use today.

With the advent of the EIS, many organizations have in the past few years attempted to provide managers with a planning and information system which helps them with their brief, varied and discontinuous day-to-day activities. The EIS attempts to do this in two ways. First, by enabling better information access and information management thus allowing managers to target what is most critical; and second, by bringing key issues to the surface quickly.

## EIS the tool

EISs have equipped top managers with a tool to help them obtain from many sources, the numerical and textual data they require to manage their business. EISs do not come with a predefined methodology to help the top managers define their information needs, thus enabling them to use the tool to its greatest effect. With or without an EIS, the problem of defining exactly what data the manager needs still exists.

If an EIS on a manager's desk has a low information value, it is highly unlikely that the system will be used and it is also unlikely the manager will invest the necessary time and effort to review his/her information needs so that it does provide significant benefits (Heygate, 1990, p. 55). To avoid this situation it is necessary for the manager to become involved in the development of the system. As with end-user computing, EIS is a technology which resides in the user community, hence that community should be responsible for its management. Many managers see or hear of their competitors using EISs for competitive advantage and they recognize the need to become involved in the management of this new technology, however, they don't know how (Porter and Millar, 1985, p. 149). The manager's main contribution is to identify and communicate what it is he/she wants from his/her system. If the manager fails to do this, the EIS will be viewed as a cost rather than a contributor to superior management performance.

## Brief history of CSFs

In the past, the information which managers received from information systems within the organization was usually determined by key personnel at the corporate or business unit level. These key personnel often felt that managers judged performance in the main by monitoring financial indicators. As financial data was routinely reported by the information systems, it was a summary of this data which made up the bulk of the information the manager received (Rockart, 1979, p. 82).

The CSF method was devised to enable managers to focus on their current information needs, both hard and soft. This method recognizes that each manager's information needs vary and that these needs evolve over time. The method is based on the concept of 'success factors' which although conceived by D. Daniel in 1961, it has been J. Rockart (1979, p. 85) who has highlighted the idea of CSFs as the key to designing an EIS. When Daniel introduced the concept he referred to organizations whose information systems produced vast amounts of information, but little of the information met managers' information needs. To help identify these needs, Daniel turned to the CSF concept.

Daniel focused on CSFs that are applicable to an organization in a particular industry. Anthony et al. (1972, p. 148) expanded on Daniel's work a decade later by concentrating on the design of management control systems and recognized that CSFs differ from manager to manager as well as from company to company. They emphasized the need for three criteria in such systems.

'The control must be tailored to the specific industry in which the company operates and to the specific strategies that it has adopted; it must identify the critical success factors that should receive continuous management attention if the company is to be successful; and it must highlight performance with respect to these key variables in reports to all levels of management'.

## Definition of CSFs

Rockart and Delong (1988, p. 64) have identified CSFs as:

'the limited number of areas in which results, if they are satisfactory, will ensure successful competitive performance for the organization. They are the few key areas where things must go right for the business to flourish. If results in these areas are not adequate, the organisation's efforts for the period will be less than desired'.

Not only does the CSF approach define information needs, but it also helps prevent the common practice of loading existing reporting methods in to the EIS. This latter approach does result in the provision of graphs and trend analysis – but not necessarily those required by the managers to run the business. The most important part of any presentation is not the visual aids, but the underlying ideas.

## Identifying CSFs and their Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

The CSF process is a flexible top-down approach designed to help managers and systems designers identify the information necessary to support the key business areas. Leadership and sponsorship for a CSF analysis must come from the business, not the Information Technology (IT) function. As with conventional IT projects, the sponsorship is often easy to obtain but commitment to the project by people from the business can be difficult to secure.

The exercise starts by clarifying the organization's mission, as the management team must be agreed on the business they wish to be in. Then the objectives of the organization are clarified as they represent the goals the organization wishes to achieve at a particular point in time. The next step is to formulate the strategy and only then do you determine the limited number of key areas of the business on which the managers should focus – these are the CSFs. Once the CSFs have been identified, measures to monitor them are devised – these are the KPIs. It is only now that the design of the EIS can commence.

Not only is the CSF process useful for group level managers, but it is also useful for other levels of general management such as divisional and business unit. The process helps each general manager to focus attention on their specific CSFs and to develop measures to monitor them. This in turn will help reduce the masses of information the organization gathers as a whole, thus reducing the costs associated with data collection.

An organization can have conflicting KPIs in different parts of the organization – production and sales, for instance. This can compromise the achievement of the CSFs. Rockart and Short (1989, p. 7,8,9) argue that IT can provide a new approach to help solve this problem, which is effectively managing interdependence. An organization can no longer hope to survive by employing strategies which are aimed at optimizing operations within functions, product lines or geographical organizations. The activities within each of these dimensions are far from independent and the CSF approach can help highlight conflicting KPIs, thus enabling improvements to take place which will optimize the flow of goods and services through the organization's value chain.

## Summary of CSF philosophy

Unlike some methodologies, the CSF process is a business driven rather than a data driven approach to designing information systems. The business person can relate to CSFs as they are an extension of objectives and goals. As CSFs are the few key areas where things must go right for the business to flourish, they should be constantly reviewed and continue to receive careful attention from management. Not only should measurement of performance receive constant management attention, but reviews of the measurement criteria should also receive management attention, to assess their validity in a changing business environment (Index International, 1986).

When an organization combines the CSF approach with the introduction of an EIS, the redesign and enhancement of current planning and control systems often takes place. This can lead to changes in the organization's measurement system (Rockart and Short, 1989, p. 15). The result is a strategy-driven reporting system which enables managers to monitor achievement or otherwise against the organization's strategic objectives.

By applying the CSF approach the gap between senior managers and other staff members has narrowed. By identifying KPIs for each CSF, individuals have a clearer picture of the broader goals and objectives of the organization. The top-down approach has also helped form a common focus throughout the organization and helps align and goals of individuals, departments, business units, divisions and the group. The CSF approach is based on the need for simplicity, whereby a manager can concentrate on those few variables which capture the strategic essence of the business.

It is important to note that the CSF process deals with information required for management control and not with information required for strategic planning (Rockart, 1979, p. 88). Strategic planning takes place prior to the CSF exercise. The EIS makes possible the ability to provide a continuously updated strategic plan, a facility which is sure to capture and hold management's attention.

## People with day-to-day responsibility for the EIS

The sponsor can seldom devote the time required to develop the EIS and therefore delegates this responsibility to a ‘trustee’ who communicates the executives needs and concerns to the development team. Although Rockart maintains that this approach often leads to unused or low-value systems, those interviewed, whose company took this approach, strongly denied that this was the outcome. They cited the various prototype techniques used to elicit top managements’ needs during the development of their EIS as the way to overcome this problem.

From the research it became clear that people who are given day-to-day responsibility for the project are often those who can bridge the gap between IT and the business; these people come from both the IT and business functions. They typically had job titles such as 'Executive Information Manager' or 'Corporate Information Manager'. Of the 14 people who had such job titles, six had a finance background, three had an IT background but had moved into an end-user computing role over the last couple of years and the other five people came from mainstream business areas.

## Type of package

The type of package used (proprietary or in-house) had no bearing on whether a top-down approach to development took place or not. It is interesting to note that six (30%) (see Figure 1) of the sample have developed an in-house EIS package although there are many proprietary EIS packages available in the market place. One such organization is an airline which has two EISs. One of the main reasons for building their second EIS in house was to keep the same user interface they had with their first EIS, as the users of the second EIS were at one time the main users of the first EIS.

An organization in the computing industry also built its EIS in-house. A requirement of its system was to integrate with, and have the same user interface as a well-known office automation package. This was requested as the users were familiar with the package, having used it in their day-to-day work for a number of years. This requirement could not be met by a proprietary EIS package.

## Number and type of users by management level

For the majority of cases the EIS was initially built with the information needs of the sponsor in mind. The result was an EIS which focused primarily on information which the sponsor deemed important. Through using the EIS and requesting enhancements/additions the information which became available to the sponsor expanded, thus improving his/her visibility of events at the operational level. This in turn led senior and middle managers to request the EIS, so that they could view the information their superior viewed about their operations.

The most notable case is that of the airline which has the two EISs. The first system which was implemented in August 1983 has a user population today of 321, which is comprised of one director, 120 senior managers and 200 middle managers. The number of directors who use the system today compared to when it was first implemented has diminished and the number of senior and middle manager users has increased. The company predicts that the user group will consist of 150 senior managers, 220 middle managers and no directors in 12 months time. The second EIS which was implemented in May 1990 has a user group consisting of two directors and five senior managers. The company predicts that in 12 months time the user group will consist of 12 directors, 28 senior managers and no middle managers. The company hopes the system will not expand beyond this user base.

Does this show a trend for the future? Will the evolution of EISs consist of a ‘hand-me-down’ approach, or will it concentrate on having one (but constantly improving) EIS in the company?

## Methodology applied to define performance indicators

The main part of the research was to determine the methodology used to define the performance indicators which are presented on the EIS. Three methods were applied, the CSF approach, other top-down approaches and an evolutionary/prototype approach.

The most popular indicators presented on the EIS are financial and quite a number of organizations present indicators which are specific to their business. Only four organizations have external information on their EIS and hence very few use their EIS to monitor and control strategic plans, although some do use the system to monitor indicators (mostly internal) which are of strategic importance.

## Methodology

As already stated, there is no standard methodology for developing an EIS and systems quite often evolve through a mixture of top-down and prototyping approaches. This is reflected in the research findings, as each of the ten organizations which applied either the CSF or an alternative top-down approach, developed their EIS by applying iterative/prototype development techniques in conjunction with their top-down approach.

A further seven organizations applied an evolutionary/prototype development method. The terms evolutionary and prototype were used by respondents to describe a system which was put together quickly with a limited amount of data after consultation with the prospective users, so that quick results and feedback could be obtained. The users constantly request amendments, additions and deletions. No EIS was developed applying the 'throw away' prototype method.

Three organizations (financial, computing and industrial) declined to comment on the methodologies they applied. Of the three, two were sponsored and developed by the IT department. One of these systems is used solely by technical staff to monitor systems availability on a daily basis, while the other is used to monitor basic financial information only. The third organization had its EIS sponsored by the finance director and the system contains financial and elementary sales indicators.

## Evolutionary/prototype approach

Seven organizations refrained from using a formal top-down approach to identify performance indicators and went straight into an evolutionary/prototype approach. Table 6 shows who was involved in the process (excluding developers) and a breakdown by percentage of indicators used is also supplied. Organizations 1, 2 and 6 obtained performance indicators for their EISs from the subordinates of the intended EIS users. According to Rockart this often results in an EIS which does not contain the information needs of the intended EIS user and the EIS ends up being used by the subordinate. For organizations 1 and 2 who are both in the financial sector, the people for whom the system was developed do use it. For organization 6 (the airline who implemented the system in 1983), Rockart's viewpoint is amplified as only one of the original intended users still uses the system today, whereas many subordinates use it in their daily work.

Organization 2 has just installed its EIS, so it is too early to comment on its usage. The people responsible for the development (assistant to directors) of the EIS for organizations 1 and 6 identified their main problem as marketing the system to its intended users. However, the scale of the problem has decreased after many amendments, requested by the top managers, were made to the system. It is possible that these amendments may not have been necessary if the top managers, rather than their subordinates, had defined the indicators at the outset.

## Outcome of the CSF approach

All five organizations which applied the CSF approach monitor the same key financial ratios today as they did prior to implementing their EISs. All five organizations identified indicators which they never used before to monitor performance.

Two organizations which identified new sales indicators (competitor sales and salesperson performance), attribute this finding to the inclusion of sales personnel in the CSF process. Likewise, the airline was the only organization which had a marketing representative in its CSF process and the result was the identification of marketing indicators which show the market share held by the airline on each of its major routes.

The three organizations which monitor business specific indicators believe the CSF process was the driving force behind their identification, as these indicators were not monitored prior to the implementation of their EISs. These indicators are too numerous to mention but include such measurements as the number of vehicles grounded and late arrivals.

The same can be said for the four organizations which monitor soft data. Once again the CSF approach was identified as the main driving force which led to the identification of the soft indicators. Soft indicators were identified as those which contribute to the measurement of quality and service, i.e. complaints received and number of deliveries made on time.

Albeit that three organizations claim they use their EIS to monitor strategic information, it is disappointing that they only monitor indicators which reside inside the organization. This strategic information consists solely of the organization's financial position, which is monitored to ensure the organization has the ability to support the commitments specified in its strategic plans, i.e. capital investment and borrowing.

No organization identified from the CSF process the need to gather information on its external environment. No explanation was given as to why this way so – either strategic planning is never carried out, or those who are responsible for strategic plans had no input to the CSF process.

The CSF approach did highlight the need to look ahead rather than look behind, as three of the four organizations who compare their sales indicators do so against forecasts. Although each organization maintained that the CSF process highlighted the need to look forward, the commitment to do so came from the top, i.e. from the top managers involved in the CSF process. Without this top management commitment it is unlikely that this forecasting would take place.

## Outcome of alternative top down approach

All five organizations which applied an alternative top-down approach monitor the same key financial ratios today as they did prior to implementing their EISs.

Only three organizations (financial, utility and food processing company) identified new performance indicators as a result of applying a top-down approach. The financial organization whose EIS was implemented at a time when new measures were being applied to its salespeople, uses its EIS to monitor these new indicators. The utility organization monitors new indicators which arose due to its imminent privatization; and the food processing company, whose EIS was sponsored by the commercial director, monitors one new indicator – product profitability. Each of these additional indicators are the result of changes in how the organization operates rather than the result of the top-down approach applied to implementing the EIS. However, the top-down approach did give a structure and method to those who identified the need for these new indicators.

Only the computer manufacturer claims to use its EIS to monitor strategic information, but once again it only maintains indicators which reside within the organization.

The top-down approach highlighted the need to compare sales and human resource indicators against forecasts as well as budgets and previous periods. The belief that forecasting would take place only if the commitment came from top management was amplified, as those who forecast sales and human resource indicators had top managers from the sales and human resource functions involved in the top-down process.

## Outcome of the evolutionary/prototype approach

The most striking difference between those organizations which applied an evolutionary/prototype approach as opposed to a formal top-down approach is the absence of new indicators. New indicators were incorporated, but only after requests for amendments were made by users after delivery of the system. The airline was the only organization to incorporate new indicators in its EIS prior to delivery of the system and all were sales related. It would be incorrect to attribute the inclusion of these new indicators to the evolutionary/prototype process, as the airline was embarking on a marketing and sales business focus.

The underlying feeling is that each organization sought tighter financial control, as all seven organizations monitor key financial indicators. An EIS which could show trends and exceptions quickly, and provide improved presentation and analysis techniques for current financial indicators, would achieve these aims.

The majority of organizations favour comparisons against previous periods and budgets than they do against forecasts. Whether the evolutionary/prototype approach identified the need to forecast or not is unclear, but what is clear is the tendency for organizations to direct themselves on the basis of comparisons made between todays results and previous results, rather than looking ahead for new opportunities.

From the study it would appear that the CSF approach is best suited to developing an EIS which requires corporate wide indicators. However, when an EIS is implemented for one particular function, an alternative top-down approach will suffice. The evolutionary/prototype approach results in numerous requests for the EIS from senior and line management, and it will be interesting to see if the same happens in approximately 5 years time to those organizations which applied the CSF approach.

## Conclusion - EIS practice versus performance monitoring theory

An EIS provides managers with a tool to monitor business performance in two ways: (1) by enabling better information access and information management, thus allowing managers to target what is most critical; and (2) by bringing key issues to the surface quickly.

An EIS can help managers improve their methods for monitoring and controlling. It can provide more timely and accurate information, thus increasing control which in turn reduces uncertainty. It can also make possible the ability to provide a continuously updated strategic plan, but only if its information contents are derived from a previously formulated business strategy.

Unfortunately, not one organization which took part in the research modelled its EIS on its overall business strategy and worse still, not one person flagged this as a problem. Only four organizations monitor strategic information, all of which resides inside the organization. Strategic planning takes place prior to applying a top down methodology for developing an EIS. The sole concern of the CSF approach is to identify the information needs of management to control and monitor the strategic plans. By not linking CSFs (derived from any top-down approach) to overall business strategy, those organizations which took part in the research missed the opportunity to bridge the gap between formulating and implementing strategy, which would have resulted in a strategic driven EIS.

All organizations which applied the CSF approach discovered new performance indicators and all attributed this discovery to the approach taken. However, these new indicators were not based on industry structure or competitive strategy, but on temporal factors only. The CSF approach did help organizations to identify the need to look ahead and many of the new and old performance indicators are now compared against forecasts as well as historical and budget figures.

Rockart (1979, p. 93) points out that CSF reviews are required on a regular basis so that adjustments can be made due to the constantly changing nature of the business environment. Not one organization which took part in the research has had more than one CSF exercise, but many organizations have added, amended and deleted performance indicators by applying an evolutionary/prototype approach to help meet the demands placed on the organization by the changing business environment.

For those organizations which applied an alternative top-down approach, all failed to perform a review of the whole organization and concentrated mainly on one or two functions. Those organizations which identified new performance indicators attribute this to major changes in how their organizations operate rather than to the top-down approach. Like those who applied the CSF approach, the new performance indicators which were identified were not based on industry structure or competitive strategy, but on temporal factors only.

When the evolutionary/prototype approach is applied in isolation to a top-down approach, the results are typified by the identification of performance indicators which are currently monitored. That is not to say that these systems add no value for top management, as they provide a more friendly reporting vehicle through the use of graphical and trend analysis. However, none of the indicators are derived from linking the development approach to the overall business strategy. This approach has enabled organizations to establish tighter financial controls and clean up current performance indicators. In such cases the EIS serves as another glorified fire extinguisher in the ever present world of those managers who fail to formulate and implement a business strategy. The organizations which applied the evolutionary/prototype approach are by and large those with many middle managers as EIS users.

The research did show that the CSF approach is the best method to develop an EIS which is required to contain corporate-wide indicators; but, to help monitor and control strategic plans the CSF approach needs to follow a formulated business strategy. The opportunity now exists for those who will apply the CSF approach in this way, to bridge the gap between formulation and implementation of business strategy which is so widespread in business today.
