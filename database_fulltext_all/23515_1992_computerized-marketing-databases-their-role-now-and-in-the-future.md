# Computerized marketing databases: their role now and in the future

ROSS CAMERON and DAVID TARGETT
University of Bath, Bath, UK

The application of IT to the marketing function for strategic purposes has been the subject of much debate. At the same time the use of computerized marketing databases has experienced rapid growth which is likely to continue. This paper reports a research survey to find out about the use of CMDs and, specifically, whether they are being used strategically.

## Introduction

Debates about the strategic role of information technology continue undiminished and few can be unaware that IT is now firmly on the general management agenda. Managers have been provided with frameworks, matrices and checklists to help explain IT's strategic role. What is not clear is the extent to which managers and organizations have changed attitudes and behaviour. How do theory and practice compare? Recent research (Brady et al., 1992) has revealed significant gaps between the two. This article looks at practice along one dimension of the 'strategic IT' debate, the application of databases to marketing.

Zuboff (1988) has led the way in pointing out that recent large scale computerization of business processes has spawned vast amounts of information, much of which was previously unavailable, as a by-product. In her words IT does more than automate, it can ‘informate’. Porter & Millar (1985) have suggested ways in which information can be used strategically, in some cases to secure competitive advantage. They recommend ‘taking a hard look at the value chain’, proposing the marketing function as an area for attention.

The use of IT in the marketing function is a sign of increasing IT maturity and development in organizations which are looking at IT in a new way. These organizations are using IT to increase revenues, in contrast to the use of IT for cost reduction purposes. This ‘emerging view’ of IT is a central theme of the MIT90s Research Programme (Scott Morton, 1991).

At the same time organizations, attempting to become more market driven, are increasingly searching for ways to improve and transform their marketing functions. They are looking to IT for increased accuracy, sophistication and cost effectiveness of marketing initiatives. More importantly they are looking to IT for radical changes in the scope of their marketing: better segmentation, customer targeting and service enhancements, for example. Synott (1987) has written specifically about these issues and the strategic use of IT in marketing in general.

The main concerns, however, were highlighted some time ago by Stone and Shaw (1978). They made the point that as market maturity increases and leads to increased fragmentation, mass marketing initiatives are not good enough. Organizations need to be able to target markets and position products and messages in accordance with the needs of specific market segments.

These different strands are brought together in computerized marketing databases (CMDs) which are able to store, analyse and distribute marketing information. They allow organizations to move to the customized marketing initiatives proposed by Stone and Shaw. The raw data on which CMDs operate may have been collected from a variety of different sources. Banking systems, electronic point of sale (EPOS) and sales order processing systems are prime examples. Potentially, CMDs have the capability to use Zuboff's 'by-product' information to achieve some of the marketing improvements described above.

This paper reports on a survey which investigated the current usage of CMDs and asked how this might develop in the future. The findings should enable organizations to employ CMDs more effectively and suppliers of CMDs to identify profitable marketing opportunities. They are based on a research project undertaken with marketing and IT managers of leading companies in three sectors: Finance, Manufacturing and Retail.

After explaining the methodology, the paper describes the findings, broken down into five sections.

(1) General information For example, what proportion of organizations use a CMD and think it is satisfactory?

(2) Current usage For example, what information do CMDs contain and what are their applications? In particular respondents were asked whether their CMD was being used for strategic purposes rather than as a list of addresses.

(3) Integration For example, are CMDs used across different functional areas? The MIT90s research programme (Scott Morton, 1991) stressed the role of databases as integrators.

(4) Management For example, are the benefits of CMDs quantified? Respondents were also questioned about what is currently a major issue; that of the use of facilities management/outsourcing for CMDs.

(5) The future. For example, do current users intend to upgrade in the near future and do current non-users intend to acquire CMDs?

## Methodology

The paper is based on a postal questionnaire survey and a series of personal interviews undertaken with Marketing and Information Technology executives. The postal questionnaire was sent to approximately 100 organizations. The research aimed to question one marketing executive and one IT executive in each organization approached.

Telephone contact was made with respondents prior to the questionnaires being sent to ensure that they were addressed to the most appropriate person to comment on the firm's marketing database, and use of marketing information. This approach must also have assisted in achieving the survey's high response rate.

Marketing and IT executives in other organizations were approached and asked if they were willing to be interviewed. The result was as follows:

## Finding 2: proportion of organizations using CMDs

The saturation of CMDs is very high. As Figure 2 illustrates, the vast majority (82%) of responding organizations had at least one CMD. Furthermore, as is reported later, all the organizations that do not currently have a CMD intend to acquire one within the next three years.

Ownership of CMDs is very high in the Finance sector (93% saturation), but relatively low in the Manufacturing sector (62%). Usage of CMDs is also relatively low in the Retail industry where current and future usage is largely dependent upon the nature of the markets served. For example, few retail organizations dealing in FMCGs said they had a CMD focusing on consumers. The first reason was cost.

## Finding 3: level of satisfaction

Marketing executives from the Finance industry expressed a high degree of dissatisfaction with their CMDs, which contrasts sharply with executives from the Retail and Manufacturing industries.

Figure 3 relates to all three industries but within this, 57% of Finance organizations stated that the range and depth of information contained on their CMD is either ‘unsatisfactory’ or ‘very unsatisfactory’. In contrast, all organizations in the Retail and Manufacturing industry indicated that they are all either ‘satisfied’ or ‘very satisfied’ with their CMDs.

One major cause of dissatisfaction with Finance CMDs is that they were developed for accounting and not marketing purposes, as the following comments indicate:

'Our database was not built with marketing in mind, It's account driven, not customer driven ...

Our database was designed with accounting requirements in mind, in the '60s before the move to marketing became important ... This has enabled building societies which have grown in the past five years to seize opportunities to service customers better because they have been able to purchase “off the shelf” databases.’ (Finance, Marketing executive)

## Finding 4: areas for improvement

Many executives felt that the information on their CMD was not comprehensive enough. 80% of marketing executives in the Finance industry stated this to be the case. One example of this lack of ‘comprehensiveness’ cited by some Finance executives is in information on the totality of the company’s relationship with the customer. Nonetheless, ‘comprehensiveness’ means different things to different organizations.

'We need more dynamic information – when they moved, where to etc. This is important information. Also we need far more personal details. The fields are there, they just need filling out.' (Finance, Marketing Executive)

Many organizations also stated that it should be easier to target prospects by segment. This corresponds to the findings discussed later that CMDs are increasingly being viewed in terms of improving external effectiveness in addition to merely internal efficiency.

A number also stated that they would like their CMD to be easier to update:

'The big trouble with databases in marketing isn't building them, it's affording to keep them up and using them.' (Retail, Marketing executive)

## Finding 5: information contained on CMDs

Consistent with the perception that a CMD is more than simply a list of names and addresses, the information contained on organizations' databases generally consists of not only customer details but also information on account sales history. It is this latter information that would allow an organization to develop customized marketing initiatives. This is especially the case in the Manufacturing and Retail industries.

Few organizations (13%) have a CMD containing information only on business accounts. ## Finding 7: forms in which information can be produced

The business and marketing activities of some organizations are limited by their inability to produce information from the CMD in the desired manner. This presents an opportunity for future suppliers of CMDs. For example, less than a third of organizations with a CMD are able to produce information on the customer account profitability, information thought to be of critical importance.

Furthermore, less than a half of organizations with a CMD can produce information by the salesperson managing the area/account, although it should be noted that this is not necessarily relevant for all organizations.

Many organizations in the Finance industry are unable to produce marketing information in many of the forms addressed in Figure 7. Some are unable to produce even the most basic types of information.

## Integration

## Finding 9: integration

Many organizations do not possess one database which can be accessed by all personnel in the company, as shown in Figure 11. While this is the case for all industry segments, the Finance sector highlighted particular inadequacies in this regard. Indeed 64% of Finance companies surveyed with a CMD do not have a database that can be accessed by all functions. Nonetheless, it is important to note that such a fully integrated CMD may not always be desirable for Finance companies.

'We need Chinese walls. As desirable it may be (CMD integration), it's not allowed by law. Our clients are each other's competitors.' (Finance, IT executive)

However, this differs between organizations. The above comment came from a Commerical Finance company, unlike the following from a marketing executive of a Consumer Finance company:

The system currently operating is such that we need to cross check with other product divisions to check what type of services are currently being offered ... Initially we used the information for internal purposes only, so it wasn't a problem. Now we need to use it for outward looking marketing and it's not integrated enough to do this.' ## Finding 10: functions that can access the CMD

While the Marketing, Sales and Accounting Functions can gain direct access to most company's CMDs, lack of access to the CMD by other functions illustrates the degree of isolation between functions in many organizations. This is presented in Figure 12.

![](/api/attachments/CDADRC2T/fulltext/images/13cdfafa0e52b939aff989cd7ed2501f4cf906cfeb07602e990f4cbd632b9f1c.jpg)  
Figure 12 Functions that can access the CMD

Some Marketing executives indicated that the CMD is of primary use to sales personnel, and as such they must have access to the information.

'The trick is to actually make use of the information. A lot of companies spend time and money producing information for strategic purposes..... but if that never actually works its way to the sales force then you're not achieving anything in the short term.' (Manufacturing, Marketing executive).

In the case of those organizations that do not have complete functional accessibility to the CMD, the functions that cannot access are the Manufacturing and Product Design areas of the company. This appears generally to be the case for organizations in the Manufacturing industry, where functions such as Operations and Product Design seem rarely to have access to information contained on the CMD.

## Finding 11: locations that can access the CMD

Country-wide access of the CMD is uncommon, in particular with Retail and Manufacturing organizations. Only 30% of organizations have a CMD which can be accessed by all locations or branches of the organization in the UK. These were mainly in the Finance sector.

Nonetheless, many organizations indicated that country-wide access of the CMD is not particularly desirable anyway. Outside the Finance sector, 71% of organizations indicated that such access is unnecessary.

## Future intentions

## Finding 12: organizations intending to upgrade within a year

This finding addresses the future CMD behaviour of organizations that currently possess a CMD. 37% of respondents that currently have a CMD stated that they will upgrade their CMD within a year. This future demand for CMDs will come from all sectors, but to a lesser extent Finance than the other two sectors.

37% will not upgrade and 26% do not know.

## Finding 13: future purchase intentions

This finding addresses the future behaviour of organizations that currently do not have a CMD. All organizations that do not currently have a CMD stated that they intend to acquire one within three years.

## Finding 14: applications for future CMDs

This finding also addresses the future behaviour of organizations that currently do not have a CMD. As shown in Figures 13 and 14, these organizations will put CMDs to both internal and external uses. ## Management issues

## Finding 15: quantification of CMD benefits

As is often the case with IT investments (Farbey et al., 1992), most organizations have made no attempt to quantify the benefits of their CMD. Indeed only 21% of organizations with CMDs have tried to quantify the benefits it has delivered.

Furthermore, the criteria on which CMD benefits have been assessed indicates that such assessments have been somewhat unsystematic and approximations only. All companies that have assessed the benefits of the CMD have done so in terms of the order generation that the CMD has led to. Some have also attempted to make an assessment for improvements in ‘general operating efficiency’ resulting from the CMD. Aside from these however, no other factor was used by many organizations in benefit quantification of CMDs.

As would be expected from end users of IT, who see IT as being the means to other ends, marketing executives perceive the major benefit of FM for CMDs as being that it would enable them to concentrate on the core business. ## Finding 20: reservations about FM for CMDs

Executives have a number of concerns about outsourcing their CMD. Most could be overcome through the building of a professional relationship between key executives of the organization and the FM organization. This would develop the trust so necessary in allowing a third party to manage something as fundamental to the organization as the CMD.

As is the case with more general FM services, major concerns of Marketing executives relate to security and costs.

'The Database is too linked to the business itself to remove it and take it external.' (Finance, IT executive)

A number of executives stated that the third party would not know the business well enough to be able to maximize the use of the CMD. They prefer to rely on the greater level of expertise lying within the company.

'The third party may not know the system as well, therefore they can't see all the applications that the system could be put to -- only the bank would know when an application possibility appears, and whether it is worth pursuing.' (Finance, Marketing executive).

'We have a dedicated management services division that takes care of our mainframe and the expertise is there. There's no need to go outside. Also the marketing database integrates with the other functions as well. We'd lose this if it went outside.' (Manufacturing, IT executive)

## Other concerns about FM of CMDs include the following.

'You must have the people who you are designing and building the system for as an integral part of its growth or they won't be prepared to use it.' (Retailing, Marketing executive)

'To try to outsource the development and then integrate it back into our system is very difficult, and the implementation side of any project is often as difficult as the building in the first place.' (Finance, IT executive)

'There are management companies we've spoken to about running a marketing database for us ... but our finance is allocated elsewhere.' (Retail, Marketing executive)

## Discussions and conclusions

The use of CMDs is likely to continue to grow strongly, despite the high proportion of organizations that currently have a CMD (82%). Most organizations are looking to upgrade their CMD in the coming year (37% of organizations currently with a CMD will upgrade) and the 18% of organizations currently without a CMD will acquire one.

Although on the whole organizations expressed a high level of satisfaction with their CMDs, there are major areas needing attention, particularly in the Finance industry. The following improvements are needed: greater comprehensiveness, the provision of information to enable the organization to target prospects by market segment, and easier methods for updating the CMD.

The real question is whether CMDs are helping to 'informate' organizations, in Zuboff's terms: are marketing functions capitalizing on the potential offered by the 'by-product' information generated by major information systems? The answers given by respondents provide some evidence that this is so.

\- Most organizations see the purpose of CMDs as being to generate revenue and improve effectiveness rather than automating a clerical function.

\- Some organizations have moved towards integrating CMDs across functions and locations.

\- Decisions about CMDs are handled at a strategic level, involving senior management.

\- The scale of CMD activities is sufficiently large to warrant consideration of outsourcing.

Whilst progress has been made it is equally clear that there has not been a radical transformation of marketing. A number of barriers to further progress still exist and the problems lie with both users and suppliers.

On the supply side respondents complained of the inability of CMDs to do some of the tasks they wanted, customer profitability for example. This may be just a question of the inevitable lag before old systems are updated. Or it could be that some suppliers have still to catch up with current thinking and they are not yet taking the leadership role that could be theirs. Whichever is true, new CMD designs with improved capabilities are needed and it is clear from the section on ‘future intentions’ that suppliers will have to provide them if they are to benefit from projected growth in the CMD market. Particularly, if they are to take advantage of outsourcing opportunities suppliers must develop professional relationships with the key decision-making executives of their client organizations.

On the user side, respondents complained of data not being provided ('the fields are there they just need filling out') and of old-fashioned design ('it's account driven not customer driven'). The answer may be that whilst the respondents were familiar with CMDs and well aware of new ways of using information, elsewhere in their organizations management thinking has not yet adjusted to new developments.

It would seem that there are a number of prerequisites to the successful use of CMDs in strategic marketing. First, an impetus is needed in an organization to spark a re-adjustment of management thinking. A notable marketing success on the part of the organization or the threat coming from the success of a rival are examples. Second, more publicity should be given to successes that have already been achieved to promote a greater awareness of what is possible. Perhaps marketing journals should devote more space to the role of IT in strategic marketing. Third, the education of marketing executives should evolve to include more on IT so that a better understanding of how to ‘informate’ the marketing function is created.

## Biographical notes

Previously at the London Business School, David Targett joined the University of Bath in 1990 as ICL Professor of Information Systems. Before becoming an academic he was an industrial engineer with British Leyland.

His work has been concerned with the impact of information systems on general management. A current research project is investigating competition and collaboration within value chains. He is also working on another major funded project to investigate the way organizations evaluate and take decisions about IT investments.

He has regular consultancy contact with organizations in many sectors of business including retailing, banking, transportation and the whisky industry. His paper 'Designing an Executive Information System for a Bank' won the Pergamon Prize. The most recent of his eight books is How to Assess Your IT Investment (Butterworth-Heinemann) and is to be published at the beginning of 1993.

Ross Cameron obtained his Bachelor of Commerce degree at the University of Melbourne, Australia. He then worked as a project manager for BIS Shrapnel, which is Australia's largest business-to-business marketing and economic forecasting consultancy.

He came to the University of Bath, UK, in 1990 to study for an MBA degree, which he obtained, with distinction, in the following year. While at Bath his dissertation was concerned with the strategic use of IT, particularly in connection with marketing and, as a result, he has been the co-author of papers which have been published in academic and management journals. He has now returned to Australia where he has started a successful marketing consultancy company.
