# A vision of the future? An innovative database micromarketing system

S. FLOWERS

Department of Business Management, University of Brighton, UK

The increase in the scale of retail grocery operations to the supermarkets of today has been at the expense of the detailed knowledge of local customer preferences that small retailers could possess. This article examines the features of a technological solution to this problem and describes how the Vision System, an interactive multimedia point-of-sale shopper loyalty and promotion system, is revolutionizing retail marketing in the United States. The implications of highly-targeted marketing at the individual household level, micromarketing, are examined and it is suggested that supermarkets using such systems would achieve a significant competitive advantage. It is also suggested that the widespread adoption of Vision-type systems would have a profound impact upon manufacturers and the marketing industry as a whole.

## Introduction

One feature of grocery retailing in the UK since 1960 has been the growth in the market share of multiples at the expense of independents and cooperatives. Between the years 1960–1990 the multiples market share (of grocery sales) grew from 25% to over 70%, with independents falling from over 55% to under 20% over the same period (Nielsen, 1992). The last thirty years has also seen the creation of ever larger retail units and the industry has largely changed its focus from the local independent shop to the out of town hypermarket. The transition has also been accompanied by the depersonalization of grocery retailing and the centralization of marketing and distribution systems. Whilst these centralized systems are arguably very efficient, marketeers are faced with an inevitable loss of personal contact with, and thereby knowledge of, the customer. This has led to a situation in which extensive market research is undertaken in an attempt to regain the detailed knowledge of customer preferences that has been lost both as a result of centralization and the large scale of retail grocery operations.

Two important priorities of retail grocery multiples are the need to recapture some of the benefits, in the form of a detailed knowledge of customer behaviour and the opportunity to develop shopper loyalty, that the personal relationship of the independent shop provided. Indeed, the strategy of the future could be summed up as 'local and loyal', with each store requiring a catchment marketing policy (Leighton, 1992). A sophisticated interactive multimedia marketing system that is designed to address exactly these priorities is the Vision System created by Applied Promotion Technologies (APT), a joint venture involving Proctor and Gamble, A.C. Nielsen,

CheckRobot, Schlumberger, Central Bancorporation and GTE Interactive Services, in the United States. The effectiveness and consumer acceptance of the Vision System was tested in a year-long trial, ending in May 1992, involving the issue of 120,000 smartcards (Smith, 1991). Thirty stores in five supermarket chains, nine manufacturers (representing over 450 brands) and one bank. Whilst detailed results of this trial are yet to be published, it is intended that the commercial version of the system will begin to be implemented from September 1992, commencing with a programme involving 200 Super-Valu supermarkets (Amann, 1992).

## The Vision System

The Vision System has two main components, 'Vision' an interactive multimedia marketing system designed to provide targeted promotions and advertising at the point of sale, and the 'Value Club', a smartcard based database marketing and frequent shopper (customer loyalty reward) system. The promotional 'Vision' part of the system uses a touch-sensitive screen at the checkout to reinforce promotions with advertisements or promotions like instant credits, electronic or paper 'money-off vouchers', or recipes according to the products that are purchased. This part of the system consists of a high-resolution colour touch-screen monitor and thermal printer located in each of the supermarket checkouts.

The frequent shopper 'Value Club' part of the system uses the smartcard to store the purchase history and record the points awarded for shopping in a particular store or buying particular brands or products. The points collected can be redeemed against a variety of catalogue goods. The complete Vision Value Club System operates by combining the personal and transaction history information read from the smartcard with the details of current purchases picked up from the bar code scanner in the supermarket checkout. This enables short promotional or advertising videos to be displayed on the high-resolution monitor according to the products that have just been purchased.

The monitor is contained within a purpose-built terminal that is 41 cm long and 23 cm high and includes a cheque writing stand. In addition to the touch-sensitive monitor the terminal includes a printer, a smartcard reader, and a magnetic stripe card reader for use with credit cards. The high-resolution monitor uses a split-screen display with a continuous 'electronic cash register tape' containing price and product description for the last five items scanned on the right hand side of the screen. It is this feature that is designed to attract and hold the consumers attention and provide an audience for the promotional and advertising material displayed on the left hand side of the screen. The right hand side of the screen also displays the name of the customer, and the running total of the Value-Club points collected so far. This is illustrated in Figure 1.

One of the major innovations of the Vision System is the way in which advertising and promotional material (e.g. buy two, get one free, money-off credits) are interactively linked. An important benefit of the system from a marketing point of view is the way in which consumers are interactively involved with the system using its touchscreen facilities. The aim is to encourage consumers to be more attentive to the Vision System display and thus to the product or brand messages shown, with the intention of increasing the impact of the advertisements carried.

When joining the Vision Value Club, in addition to providing such things as their own and their partners name, address and date of birth, applicants are also asked to provide details about the number, date of birth and sex of their children, the type and number of pets they have, and their households total pre-tax income. In total information relating to nine separate demographic factors is requested (although consumers can decline to provide any information other than their name and address when they enroll).

This information is stored on the smartcard and promotions can be triggered according to the good that has just been scanned, the households previous purchase history or else some other demographic factor like a child's imminent birthday, or ownership of a dog or cat (Smith, 1991). Information on products that are complementary to, or competitors of, the products just purchased can also be shown and customers may be prompted to touch the screen to respond to offers for an electronic money-off coupon to be added to their smartcard or else for a recipe to be printed out. The details of household purchases are periodically transferred from the smartcard to a central database, enabling a detailed purchase profile to be built up.

The advertising or promotional videos are very short, between five and seven seconds long and, in order to prevent delays at the checkout, will stop (even in midstream) as soon as all the goods to be purchased have been scanned and the till opened. Any offers or credits will however still be put onto the smartcard. Promotions are currently only permitted by APT on a category exclusive basis (i.e. only one brand of toothpaste can be promoted at a time) and it is estimated that between two and four videos would be initiated by the end of a typical shopping trip (Amann, 1992).

Whilst an early version of the Vision System used laserdisks as the storage and distribution medium for the video clips, they can now be sent direct to the participating supermarkets over a Hughes Network Systems satellite communications link and stored on conventional hard disk systems.

The Vision System currently uses microcomputers to control groups of checkout lanes with consumer and transaction data being held by an in-store central processor. The data collected is transmitted periodically from the central processor to ATP and a central clearinghouse where charges for promotional fees and redemptions are calculated. The hardware, software and system maintenance are all provided without charge, with the retailer paying only a portion of the installation costs.

The Vision System also offers a number of other services including Vision Plus, where credit-card facilities are added to the smartcard, and the VisionInfo terminal, a free-standing version of the checkout terminal designed to operate as a dedicated promotional system.

## Micromarketing potential

The most important and innovative feature of the Vision System is the way in which detailed information on household purchases is collected. The use of a smartcard to collect and record information locally is a refinement of the current approach to database marketing which relies heavily upon centralized database facilities. The use of a smartcard, which becomes in this context a form of distributed database, to record information on actual purchases over time effectively redefines the way in which fast moving consumer goods are marketed.

Much current advertising and promotional activity is generally aimed at specific geodemographic or psychographic groups rather than individual households. This relatively unfocussed marketing effort is the result of a lack of hard data at the household level and can cause resentment (as with junk mail) when poorly targeted. The smartcard component of the system makes it possible to target point of sale advertising or promotions at particular households based upon current purchases, purchase history or a combination of both factors (Smith, 1991). Manufacturers can target promotions at consumers who purchase a competitors product to encourage brand-switching, or manufacturers of nappies could target an advertisement at households who purchase baby-food, for example. It also becomes possible to market to high-earning households aged between 25 and 35 who have enrolled in the Vision Value Club, have two children and a dog and regularly spend over a predetermined weekly amount in a participating supermarket.

## Implications for database marketing

In the current implication of the Vision System the data collected on consumer purchases is owned by the retailers, whilst information relating to the results of product or brand promotions is owned by APT itself, with manufacturers able to buy rights to any or all of this information. Periodically, information on household purchases is uploaded from the smartcard enabling a centralized database of consumption behaviour to be created. A database containing such detailed information on household consumption patterns would be of great use to both retailers and manufacturers.

The creation of such a highly detailed database providing time-series consumer behaviour at the household level also raises the possibility of database micro-marketing. Given this mass of data relating to actual purchases direct mail could be used to target known purchasers of certain goods, and buying patterns across categories or between individual products could be examined. Given the ability of direct mail organizations to build databases from information obtained from a wide variety of sources, the addition of such detailed data would confer a significant competitive advantage to those companies that could obtain it.

## Implications for the market research industry

In the face of a new source of highly detailed accurate data, as would be obtained from Vision-type systems, the role of market research organizations in tracking consumer behaviour in stores would be redefined. The role of consumer panels like A.C. Nielsen Homescan and AGB Superpanel in the UK that are used to sample consumer behaviour, are likely to be replaced by the more detailed and accurate data from systems like Vision. The role of the market researchers may expand however in creating and selling systems that will enable organizations to make effective use of the huge volume of data that will become available.

Market research organizations would be in a position to assess far more accurately than is currently possible the effectiveness of an advertising campaign by a direct and far more precise comparison of a supermarket in which a marketing push took place and a control supermarket. It would also become possible to precisely track purchase behaviour over time and assess the impact of such factors as modifications to product packaging and the effect of a given promotional technique on specific target groups.

## 'Fast-cycle' marketing

With the ability to receive sales information quickly and transmit promotional and advertising videos direct to the retail outlets, manufacturers are moved much closer to the consumer. It becomes feasible for manufacturers to closely monitor the impact of a promotion, 'fine-tune' the advertising or promotional videos, and deliver the new version direct to the supermarkets within days. The adoption of such 'fast-cycle' marketing methods would impact greatly upon the traditional advertising media of radio, television and newspapers, if manufacturers moved towards the use of such highly targeted in-store methods of marketing.

## Market segmentation

The availability of detailed information on actual household purchases could potentially transform current retail grocery marketing operations from a highly centralized to a locally dispersed activity. As discussed by Shaw (Shaw, 1991) such detailed microdemographic information would enable individual supermarket branches to closely reflect the demands of the community in which it operates, and in this way enable a supermarket chain to establish a segmented market on a shop by shop level. Such an approach, if successful, would confer a significant competitive advantage upon the supermarket chain as a whole.

A move towards a localized distributed marketing approach would require the current centralized focus of many large retailing operations to be modified. There would need to be the creation of localized marketing teams using the local segment of a fully distributed database to plan and coordinate their marketing effort. This approach, in line with developments in the use of computers generally, would in some way recreate a high-technology version of the corner shop, where the retailer closely understood customer needs and behaviour and could use this knowledge to maximize sales. However, although the personal service element of small shops cannot be recreated within the context of a large supermarket, the detailed information of consumer behaviour can be used to create highly-targeted advertising and promotions that can move customers to more expensive brands and thus build sales.

The ability to micromarket at the household level would not only enable marketing effort to become more highly targeted, but the advertisements and promotions for a particular product could be differentiated to maximize customer response. It would become possible for the same advertisement to contain different actors and the script to be varied according to the micromarket to be targeted. The cost of such a differentiation of marketing effort could in part by met if its success enabled a reduction in other less focussed forms of advertising and promotional activity.

## Vision and customer loyalty

One of the primary aims of the Vision System is to build brand or store loyalty. Using the Vision System manufacturers could precisely target heavy buyers of a product with 'money-off' coupons or other special offers and thus encourage brand loyalty. Supermarket chains are also able to reward loyalty with Vision Value Points awarded according to the amount of money spent. Indeed, the UK-based supermarket chain Asda recently undertook a trial of a similar shopper-loyalty system that instantly issues money-off coupons in reaction to the goods scanned (Bidlake, 1992). A problem with earlier shopper loyalty schemes like Green Shield Stamps was that they eventually became a generic benefit obtainable from a large number of stores. Vision-type systems are not such a generic benefit since precise targeting of promotions will enable localized flexibility and guard against a loss of differentiation (Shaw, 1991). A study of grocery buying consumers' usage and attitudes towards the Vision System (Foskit, 1991) found that it encouraged shopper loyalty and may be a factor in promoting brand loyalty. It was also found that the smartcard component of the system was a significant factor in promoting store loyalty.

A market that shares some of the competitive characteristics of retail grocery, highly competitive market, largely undifferentiated product range, extensive non-price competition, is the retail petrol market. Customer loyalty schemes are a prominent feature of this market and interest has been shown in using the Vision System in this context (Amann, 1992).

## Integration with sector value chain

Using Porter's (Porter, 1985) value chain concept it can be seen that the Vision System enhances and integrates with the value system operating within the retail grocery sector. The collection of detailed demographic and consumption data at the point of sale can be used by product manufacturers, marketing organizations and retailers to learn more about customer behaviour. This knowledge can be used by manufacturers and marketing organizations to create finely tuned national promotional campaigns that utilize a more complete understanding of consumer behaviour as the basis for their success. A new wave of highly targeted, and thus potentially more successful, direct marketing campaigns could also be created using the same detailed information.

Individual supermarkets will, in turn, be able to use the information collected to learn more about their local customer base and be in an enhanced position to built a 'local and loyal' (Leighton, 1992) marketing strategy. The consumer, upon whose cooperation everything depends, also receives increased value from the points, gifts and other promotional offers that the Vision System provides.

## Privacy issues of micromarketing

The growth of database marketing and the direct mail industry has sensitized many people to the issue of data privacy (Lamidey, 1992). The Vision System, whilst having the capability of collecting far more detailed information on household behaviour than was previously possible, is itself marketed primarily as an interactive advertising and promotional system, with consumer data viewed as a secondary benefit. Indeed, customers only have to give their name and address to enrol in the Vision Value Club. However, the use of modern database profiling techniques means that even a name and address, especially when coupled with a detailed purchase history, is enough to be of great use to database marketers. This may in itself prove to be a significant disincentive for consumers to enrol in the scheme.

Whilst in the US there are few laws on the collection of data by commercial organizations, at the time of writing the EC is considering a draft directive on data protection that is re-examining the whole issue of the commercial use of personal data. One clause deals with an ‘opt-in’ requirement for individuals to provide their ‘informed consent’ to permit the use of their personal data. In addition individuals should be informed at the time of collection the use to which the data is to be put and given the name and address of the controller of the database. Even if the draft directive is implemented in its current form the use of Vision-type systems in Europe would be possible.

A major difference between the Vision System and the manner in which many marketing databases are currently compiled is the way in which the issue of data use is addressed. Although the law has recently changed, it is likely that the majority of data that exists on marketing databases has been collected without the knowledge or consent of the data subject, and it is this situation that has led to widespread concern about the methods and motives of direct marketers. The Vision system, in contrast, recognizes these legitimate consumer concerns and gives the consumer an incentive, in the form of points, to provide information. Because of this apparent openness and the financial incentive to participate it is by no means certain that such schemes would be met with significant consumer resistance.

## Conclusions

It seems likely that in the search for a competitive advantage supermarket retailing is about to enter a new phase with the adoption of innovative Vision-style systems that can deliver highly-targeted marketing materials. The widespread use of such micromarketing systems would not only have a profound decentralizing effect on supermarket marketing and information management activities, but also on the future role and structure on the advertising and market research industries that have grown up around them. The supermarket of tomorrow could use Vision-style systems as a basis for a 'local and loyal' marketing strategy that is a modern interpretation of the corner-shop ethos of yesteryear.

Vision-style systems could also enable manufacturers to move closer to their customers and adopt a 'fast-cycle' approach to marketing that could bypass the traditional media of newspapers, radio and television. The highly detailed information available from such systems could in turn generate a second-generation direct mail industry that can precisely target marketing effort. If there is no significant consumer resistance to the introduction of such systems on privacy or other grounds, it seems likely that the age of micromarketing will soon be upon us.
