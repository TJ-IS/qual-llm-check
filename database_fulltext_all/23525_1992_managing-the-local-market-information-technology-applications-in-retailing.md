# Managing the local market: information technology applications in retailing

JONATHAN REYNOLDS

Oxford Institute of Retail Management, Templeton College, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

The need for UK retailers and other consumer service companies to be taking better informed decisions within an increasingly uncertain market environment and in response to a series of other key drivers has had a number of consequences for the management processes within these organizations. Not least, it has served to formalize processes having to do with the geographical aspect of the market environment, a perspective of particular importance to the consumer services sector. This paper reviews the combination of pressures which has led to the introduction of more sophisticated techniques of local market profiling and segmentation within UK organizations, and in which information technology and the management of information has begun to play a significant role.

## Introduction

The retail industry in the UK is one in which the ready adoption of new ideas and methods has usually proved a characteristic means of evolution. Social and economic circumstances have channelled these forces into the dynamic and competitive form of retailing, capable of profitable replication into local markets throughout the country, which we recognize today (Brown, 1987; Staniland Hall Associates Ltd, 1992). However, such businesses have generally been conservative in their attitudes towards information technology (IT), initially suspicious of the real benefits which IT could offer a 'people business'. Indeed, it was because of this initial caution that retail businesses avoided many of the mistakes made by earlier adopters. Much adoption of IT among retail businesses has been largely cautious, incremental and supportive of the conventional operational functions of the organization, rather than being radical or integrative (Scott Morton, 1990).

Curiously, however, and until recently, one aspect of the retail business has tended to lag behind even this gradual process of contemporary IT application: the functions of the organization dealing with the management of the branch portfolio and the store location decision-making process. The lag is superficially curious because of the prime importance given to locational issues by consumer service organizations and the early history of store-location theory and modelling (Davies, 1976, Davies and Rogers, 1987). Contemporary investment in marketing information systems has not generally tended to benefit those responsible for managing geographical markets for goods and services (Piercy and Alexander,

1988). It has to be recognized that in this area above all others, the ‘well-wetted finger in the air’ has been an enduring characteristic of UK retailers’ decision-making behaviour and, furthermore that:

“the culture of retail decision-making is especially receptive to answers and advice; however, it does not appreciate esoteric analytical problems which invariably surface in any detailed research and modelling approach.”

(Penny and Broom, 1988)

This paper attempts to review by example some of the ways in which IT is now playing a part in supporting the key locational decisions being taken by the UK retail industry, exploring the reasons for the change, and assessing the extent to which emerging geo-demographic information systems are perceived to assist in improving the effectiveness of decisions in relation to the branch portfolio. In order to combat the problems associated with the use of company-confidential information, use is made of data derived from a series of long-term research projects which monitor the characteristics and effects of new forms of retail development.

The paper begins with a justification for the increase in attention to demographic analysis and the use of spatial marketing techniques as part of the store location decision-making process, through a study of retail dynamics. It continues by considering the extent to which retail companies have acquired demographic data for use in spatial marketing and locational analysis. It concludes with some predictions on likely future issues which will confront practitioners in this area.

## The pressures for change

This section of the paper briefly sets out the dimensions of change within retailing which provide much of the justification for the development of advanced techniques in local area analysis.

The most consistent trend relevant to this discussion has been the increased concentration of the industry and the preoccupation with corporate growth. This has brought with it an increase in financial power and economies of scale in the development and management of retail outlets. The share of trade taken by the large multiples (those firms operating more than ten outlets) approached 61% by 1989 (CSO, 1991). While this figure nears some kind of equilibrium, with the number of independent outlets remaining relatively stable through the late 1980s, concentration of retailing within the multiple sector continues to increase. More, very large companies, control a higher proportion of outlets (Howard and Davies, 1988) as well as of turnover. The top 10 UK retailers accounted for some 46.3% of the top 600s sales in 1989–90 (Corporate Intelligence Group, 1992).

The management of scale presents different problems for different types of operator. Breheny (1986) for example distinguishes between those types of retailing which are product-dominated (such as specialist fashion shops, jewellers and financial services outlets) and more general outlet-dominated types (such as food superstores and department stores). This kind of distinction may be increasingly difficult to maintain. Table 1 lists the ten largest retail outlet portfolios in the UK, alongside their position in the top 100 companies in terms of turnover. The lead placing for Sears conceals the diversity of trading names operated: 26 individual fascias. Such companies tend to operate high margin specialist chains and are actively seeking ways to segment their market places. Table 2 illustrates the converse approach to retail operations: stores, in this case large solus stores in the grocery sector, dependent for their profitability on identifying and drawing upon a large local market.

For both sub-sectors, overall levels of capital investment and therefore commercial risks, are very high. The rate of investment in UK retailing by the retail sector itself is massive: at the peak of the recent consumer boom in 1987, this amounted to over £3.3 billion, an increase of 60% in constant prices over investment levels in 1982 (HMSO, 1988). Indeed, the property portfolios of many of the UKs largest retailers are larger than those of many of the biggest UK property companies. The grocery retailing sector in particular has been making property investments at a phenomenal rate in an attempt to forestall market saturation effects (Treadgold and Reynolds, 1988). Figure 1 shows that such companies were spending at between 1.3 and 2.2 times the level of their pre-tax profit between 1986 and 1991. It is not difficult to see the rationale, therefore, for a considerably more scientific approach to the spatial distribution of retail investment and the identification of appropriate investment opportunities. The greater need to justify such capital investment programmes externally has also played a part. A recent disenchantment with the retail sector and with retail property on the part of UK financial institutions has resulted in the requirement for more stringent analysis of investment opportunities.

If further justification were required, the repercussions of the ‘demographic roller coaster’ (Myers, 1987) provided it, alongside the more subtle changes in the attitudes and expectations of the individual consumer. The impetus for change in the retail industry began to come increasingly from those who were using it. Further, the kinds of movements in urban populations which were occurring, combined with increases in personal mobility, were causing retailers to be much more aware of the broader spatial dimensions of demographic change. (See, for example, the trends discussed in Champion et al., 1987).

Retail businesses were forced to look beyond simple extrapolation of gross demographic trends and start to consider a greater range of variables. There are clear demographic determinants of household spending – particularly demonstrable in terms of household size, marital status, level of education, and age structure. For example, the level of education of the head of household and spouse can be shown to be strongly correlated with levels of expenditure on clothing, food, housing, car-related spending, recreation and loosely defined ‘social welfare’ activities (Ketkar and Ketkar, 1987), with households having higher levels of educational qualification spending significantly more on these commodities.

## The store location decision process and the role of spatial marketing techniques

This section conceptualises the store location decision process and outlines the role which spatial marketing information sources, techniques and systems may have within it. It examines some of the main features of the phenomenon's growth: the development of in-house expertise and the appearance of commercial companies offering external data, advice and consultancy.

"data and methods are available to provide invaluable information to assist retail management with their decision making at an extremely small fraction of the cost of their aggregate investment."

## (Beaumont, 1987)

Beaumont sees store location analysis as an integral component of an overall marketing strategy plan and as the task of matching demand and supply relationships over space, and while there is no single solution to the range of problems posed, Breheny (1986) has set out a framework for retail location problems which attempts to conceptualise the process (Figure 2).

Figure 2 The store location process (source: Breheny, 1986)

The immediate applications of spatial marketing techniques within this overall process fall into two broad sets: perspectives which are market-oriented and those which are store-oriented. Some of the tasks within these broad groups can be summarized:

## (1) Market-oriented

(i) Market identification – the general search for distinctive market characteristics over a wide area, for example, ‘households with access to two or more cars with high educational qualifications’.

(ii) Market profiling – the profiling of a local market area according to its demographic and socio-economic characteristics.

(iii) Market profile comparisons – the comparison of such profiles to achieve a ranking of market opportunities.

## (2) Store-oriented

(i) Site screening – the identification of an initial set of priority locations for a store development programme.

(ii) Catchment area definition – assisting in the designation of trading catchment areas around a store, with the help of customer survey data or expenditure estimates and local demographics.

(iii) Store portfolio evaluation – the comparison of trading catchment areas for a company's existing outlets.

## The growth of in-house expertise

The extent to which such formal processes have been adopted within retail organizations in the UK is relatively poorly documented. One survey of 200 retail companies attempted to gain an understanding of the criteria used in store location decisions (Simkin et al., 1985). Among the more significant results were

(1) the larger the average store size, the higher was the quality of research;

(2) the more stores operated, the poorer the quality of research (reflecting the large number of small store high street multiples with relatively high portfolio turnovers);

(3) the further a company's ideal pitch lay away from the high street, the more thorough the market assessment/store forecasting undertaken;

(4) the use and assessment of demographic information played a major part in the locational analysis carried out by all sectors of retailing and services with the exception (at the time) of financial services outlets.

The survey also identified the large number of companies who had formalised their market-oriented and store-oriented analyses through the establishment of in-house research and intelligence units. Three quarters of all respondent companies claimed to run such a specialist unit, although it was notable that those companies operating fewer, larger outlets again tended to dominate the scene. Only some 17% of the fashion retailers surveyed, for example, compared with 100% of discount warehouses, supermarkets and department stores. Piercy and Alexander's study (1988) of marketing and market research activity within UK retailing confirmed this variability.

More recently, Rickman (1992) has undertaken an analysis of the usage of marketing intelligence systems in the retail sector, with a specific focus upon their use in local market contexts. Survey response was poor largely because of retailers' notorious secretiveness. Nevertheless, the most widespread applications occurred in food retailing, where the most sophisticated organizations are now beginning to integrate information drawn from geo-demographic, purchasing, competitor and point-of-sale sources. It is unsurprising that the food superstore companies, with their heavy investment in large individual outlets, dependent upon the identification of relatively unsaturated local markets, took an early lead in the development of specialist research units and in the exploitation of IT in the management of local marketing information.

The experience of Tesco is well reported (Penny and Broom, 1988) and dates from the early 1980s, when the increasing centralization of decision-making and the growing professionalism of senior managers employed within the company led to the setting up of a site research unit. The unit is now almost completely self-sufficient and co-ordinates an extensive site search and sales forecasting system which culminates with the opening of between 20 and 25 superstores a year.

"Our approach was not to replace the previous method of decision-making but to provide a research analysis alongside the existing and somewhat colloquial 'gut-feel' of the retail directors."

(Penny and Broom, 1988, p. 108)

Three levels of analysis are provided to senior managers by the unit.

(1) Identification of areas offering market potential: the unit targets regions of the country on a regular basis to identify broad areas of undersupply.

(2) Site screening of development opportunities: the unit is presented with over 600 potential sites for stores in any year by its own estates department and through external agencies. In order to optimize the time spent by experienced site appraisal personnel, sites are screened to select those meriting further examination.

(3) Sales potential forecasting: the unit provides detailed sales forecasting for priority sites (approximately 50% of sites screened) as the basis for capital investment appraisal. These estimates have a requirement for error, because of the nature of food margins, of less than +/- 5%. An iterative process, which includes the detailed monitoring of actual store performance, is used to adjust and revise each level of analysis.

In technical terms, the unit relies upon a location modelling system, developed in an incremental way to satisfy the company's particular needs. Four data components feed this system and are used to calibrate a customised forecasting model. First, the unit has purchased an extensive subset of the 1981 population census for itself. Secondly, a major customer survey is undertaken once a year, with the aim of capturing the demographic, socio-economic and expenditure habits of up to 10% of customers passing through Tesco stores during one week. In combination with a data base of competitor activity and a digitised road network, these components form an integrated system which is now capable of interrogation by non-technical senior management.

## The growth of the demographic data companies

For those companies without the resources to set up a in-house unit, or who require more specialist analysis on a less frequent basis, accessibility to demographic information has been greatly assisted by the growth of a number of commercial data companies, offering a range of new products and services derived from existing and newly-developed sources of information. It is from these companies, for example, that many retailers obtained their first experiences of the use of cluster analysis as a means of simplifying complex distributions of demographic and socio-economic data. The early 1970s in the US saw the Los Angeles-based Urban Decision Systems offering the first set of systems of this kind. The extent and growth of demand in the UK is evidenced by the fact that six companies currently offer a range of such services at present, and have no difficulty co-existing in the market place (Sleight, 1992).

Figure 3 outlines the broad data framework within which such companies operate. The relative importance of small area population census statistics as a data building block is clear, but in the kind of geo-demographic information systems which companies such as CCN and Pinpoint Analysis Ltd are now developing, such census data is only one component in a much more complex configuration of data sources. Specially commissioned surveys and client data, such as CCNs use of the National Shopper Survey, plays an increasingly important role in tailoring analyses to retailers' specific requirements.

Rogers (1986) sets out the three general advantages which demographic data companies have over traditional sources of market information for retailers:

(1) customized small area calculations - the calculation of aggregated small area statistics for idiosyncratic trading or search areas; for example a radius search around a store, a drive-time catchment or fixed size grid square;

(2) updated estimates of selected demographic variables – the ability for such companies to provide selective updating of key census variables (particularly those susceptible to extensive degradation during inter-censal periods);

(3) added value products and services – all companies currently operating in the UK offer some form of additional ‘value-added’ service, whether they be links to customized survey data, bespoke clustoring algorithms or more advanced modelling and system-building capabilities.

All the companies, however, offer cluster analysis of geo-demographic variables as a core marketing and store locational tool to retailers. The trend, moreover, is increasingly towards the development of targeted discriminators, and to some extent a rudimentary segmentation of the market place has already occurred, building on these links. CCN Systems, for example, has linked its MOSAIC clustering product with a proprietary credit scoring data base. Pinpoint Analysis has developed a financial discriminator, FiNPiN, derived from an extensive survey of households' propensity to consume financial products and services.

The benefits of clustering systems fall into two areas. First, they achieve a considerable simplification of large and complex data sets in a statistically robust manner (subject to certain technical caveats). The population census is, of course, one of the largest, consisting of a matrix of 4000 counts by 130 000 enumeration districts. However, some tailored surveys – particularly some of the larger product-oriented surveys – are far from negligible in size. The TGI (Target Group Index) for example reports detailed buying behaviour of 24 000 households. Computerized Marketing Technology's National Shopper Survey deals with some 10 million consumers annually.

Second, such integrating systems can provide an important element of consistency between different data sets, provided that the interface between the individual sources is properly developed. Composite information and analysis can be presented in an intelligible and readily useable form, provided that the user has confidence in the methodology employed. As with all statistical techniques, cluster analysis and related spatial marketing techniques assume an intelligent user who has knowledge of the assumptions under which analysis is conducted and of the limitations inherent in the methodology.

Particularly important has been the link of demographic data to consumers expenditure information – “... the holy grail in site selection...” (Ody, 1988). Attempts to link Population Census data bases with other government data sets, such as the Family Expenditure Survey, have been moderately successful to date, but are in the end limited by the inherent inaccuracies, small sample size and the infrequency of data collection (not to mention the extensive delay between data collection and publication).

Also criticised has been the hitherto generic nature of such systems, designed to be ‘all things to all people’ and therefore satisfying no-one’s specialist requirements. The danger in relying upon classifications of neighbourhood characteristics which are not designed for application in specific markets has been countered with the development of segmented, sectoral discriminators and tailored cluster solutions, taking into account a client’s specific needs, although not without cost.

Several technical caveats have also been raised which are peculiar to this form of marketing technique, summarized as the modifiable areal unit problem (see for example the discussion in Openshaw, 1985). Most notable is that, since such classifications attempt to apply aggregate labels to what are in reality heterogeneous areas, the labelling does not properly represent the variety of neighbourhood characteristics present. Nevertheless, this can be countered by the argument that such systems are seeking only to incrementally improve market-oriented or store-oriented accuracy.

## Geographical information systems

To the debate over the merits of sources and techniques has come the inevitable systematizing of local market information, through the use of systems first developed in the area of physical resource management (Rhind, 1987). Such geographical information systems (GIS) seek to offer integrated mapping and analytical capabilities which have not always paralleled the quality of the data employed.

"As a general comment, further enhancements to any of the techniques employed [in store location decisions] cannot be justified until the quality of the available data is improved. The proliferation of computer hardware and software does little to facilitate the sound application of techniques in store location analysis; the key determinants are the availability of the necessary input data and experienced staff."

(Beaumont, 1987)

Such concerns are not new. Also not new in the adoption of such systems is the initial pre-occupation with machines and software and their display and presentational capabilities, at the expense of their effectiveness. Information systems, of which GIS are only the lastest examples, are only valuable insofar as they relate to people and procedures. There are a series of perceptions of the costs and benefits associated with such systems which must be assessed. How realistic are these perceptions as far as retailers are concerned?

While there is a reasonably common understanding of the general benefits which GIS applications can offer the retail organization (Table 3), once developed and integrated, there is a less consistent understanding of the market place itself and of specific system design and performance. Such insights have, it is to be hoped, taken us beyond the description of the industry as,

“hype-ridden ... based in part on often unfounded, sometimes incorrect and frequently exaggerated claims.”

(Openshaw and Coombes, 1988)

In terms of productivity, such systems are seen as offering the store location analyst the possibility of evaluating more alternatives, obtaining greater responsiveness than with several discrete or manual systems, and allowing for the results so obtained to be more rapidly and effectively communicated to strategic decision-makers.

The more rigorous requirements of information and analysis systems, centred around spatially referenced information, are perceived as providing a useful potential for consistency between data sets and departments, whilst the presentation of analyses achieve a greater professional quality. Properly managed, such systems can also ensure a continuity which permits the business to weather the already high levels of staff turnover in specialist market research and store location planning departments.

The retail industry is nevertheless less concerned with benefits and more with costs. The system requirements of GIS applications are of less immediate interest than the constraints affecting data capture and data quality. The fall in scale and cost of information technology (IT) generally, and multiple retailers' existing experience with point-of-sale and similar applications means that even medium-sized businesses have some degree of comfort and experience in the installation of new systems (although there appears a temptation to treat GIS as 'standalone' platforms and starve them of the interdepartmental links which they require to be effective). However, the quality, compatibility and implementation timescales are of more concern; implementation particularly so, given the conventionally short term view of the industry. There is still a relatively low level of expertise among IT professionals within retail and consumer service businesses of the spatially-referenced display, data manipulation and output requirements of geographically-oriented information systems.

More interestingly, less quantifiable but equally significant costs are seen as accruing through the heightened expectations which some systems are capable of facilitating among senior management (through attractive visual displays, for example), which may eventually begin to generate a greater workload than the original procedure. (Retailers draw upon their experience of space management systems to come to this view.)

The final implication of the adoption of GIS is also derived from early experience of other forms of management information systems – notably with marketing information systems (Jobber and Watts, 1987). It is that retail organizations themselves may not be amenable to the introduction of such an integrating tool as a GIS. Not only may they not possess the requisite flexibility to permit such systems to work optimally – they may not even desire it. As proponents for GIS seek for it to become more strategic and less tactical so they will come up against an increasing number of organizational obstacles which will need to be overcome.

## Applications of spatial marketing techniques

Subjecting real sets of local market data to analysis is one way of exploring the scope and potential of geographical information systems within the consumer service sector. Understandably, much of the raw data needed for such analysis is necessarily confidential to the companies concerned. However, the Oxford Institute of Retail

Management, working with a number of retailers, developers and geodemographic data companies, has built up some extensive sets of information relating to local UK markets which illustrate both some of the new challenges which retail companies face, together with the assistance GIS can offer to other parts of the retail business. Two of these cases are shown below.

## Understanding new shopping patterns

Major shifts are occurring in the relative importance of the UKs shopping centres, as the result of new development now in use as well as a consequence of more complex expressions of consumer patronage (HMSO, 1992). For example, in the last two years retailers and consumer service companies have been faced with the prospect of deciding whether or not to take units within the newly-emerged regional-scale shopping centres on the edge of four major conurbations. Derived from North American experience, these schemes provide in effect a High Street out-of-town, comprising up to 300 shop units with additional leisure and catering facilities. Little is known about the ways in which these and similar but smaller centres function within the hierarchy of UK shopping centres.

The illustrations within Figures 4 and 5 report further results from the Institute's project modelling the impact of the 1.2 million sq ft Meadowhall Shopping Centre on the Sheffield region (Howard, 1991). The project uses a commercial GIS to capture and analyse a wide variety of spatially-referenced demand and supply data connected with the centre's activities. Figure 4 shows the clear early and extensive attraction of the centre, based upon in-centre interviews. Fully one-third of all spending and a quarter of visitors are coming from beyond 45 minutes from the centre. The draw along motorways M1, M18 and M62 is equally pronounced. Preliminary analysis of these returns generated by the system shows that Meadowhall's shoppers in this opening phase are younger and more prosperous than those in other centres. Age profiles are weighted much more strongly to the important (for retailers) 25–44 year-old cohorts than in surrounding centres. Shoppers are visiting in family or social groups far more than would be the case in traditional centres.

Figure 5 applies a generic geodemographic discriminator (held internally by the GIS) to provide a rapid 'snap-shot' of customer characteristics compared with the background population from within 45 minutes drive of the centre. Meadowhall is clearly attracting households from neighbourhoods characterised by high proportions of young but established families and aspiring council tenants. Similar profiles conducted for Sheffield shoppers reveal a considerably older profile and reinforce the belief that such new centres bring about a polarisation in retailing opportunities. Such analysis provides a significant guide to the likely level of future retail investment in centres of this kind for particular retailer types.

## Examining local labour market vulnerability

By definition, the retail sector is tied much more closely to local markets than many others – certainly more so than are business services, but even some other consumer service sectors (such as hotels, catering and leisure services), and is consequently much more vulnerable to small area trends in labour availability (Reynolds and Brue, 1990). Given the shifting environment for the recruitment and retention of labour, if a company was better able to identify those stores within its portfolio (and perhaps even outside its portfolio) where the labour market was tighter, recruitment policies and resources could be appropriately targeted.

Recent shifts in rates of unemployment (amongst other variables) also act as a useful index of economic confidence at the local level. In the absence of more disaggregated and contemporary indices of prosperity, they can offer insight into those local markets which are likely to prove less buoyant than the average over the medium term. Figure 6 assembles a range of analyses undertaken to demonstrate the potential for prioritising local markets in relation to changes in rate. The data is drawn from the National On-Line Manpower Information System at the University of Durham; a source being used by a number of retail organizations to provide 'live' updates of local conditions as one input to sets of data within an integrated GIS. The analysis produced is of use not just to store planning and marketing functions in the business, but is relevant also to the needs of merchandisers and personnel.

## Conclusion

This paper has sought to outline and demonstrate the role of spatial marketing techniques and systems within the locational decision-making process of retailing and related companies. It concludes with a consideration of future expectations in this area.

We can anticipate that the process of integrating spatial data sets of relevance to retailing and marketing will continue. In addition to the growth of geographical information systems of holistic location modelling (such as the CACI Shopping Centre Planner); and the technical advances being made to satisfy some methodological criticisms of spatial data processing techniques (such as the advances in fuzzy spatial retrieval algorithms – see Openshaw and Coombes, 1988); more data building blocks will be incorporated, partly through the development of more sector-specific discriminators, powered by new or existing market surveys. Small area statistics from the 1991 population census will also act as a trigger for data suppliers to develop new generic geodemographic products.

Despite this, of the 2800 members of the Population Association of America, only 700 are applied demographers. In the UK, whilst there is growing expertise within both private data companies and within the retailers' own research units, much academic training in applied demography and geographic information systems is relatively informal and dispersed and, with one or two exceptions, with the greatest concentrations in geography and town planning departments.

Finally, but linked with this educational requirement is the need seen amongst retailers to de-mystify the techniques of spatial marketing:

## Biographical notes

Jonathan Reynolds is Fellow in Retail Marketing at Templeton College, Oxford and a member of the Oxford Institute of Retail Management. He joined the College in 1985 to work with Tesco plc on the application of new technology to retailing, following a period spent with the University of Edinburgh and Coca-Cola Northern Europe. He now teaches and researches in the areas of retail marketing, retail planning and development, retail employment and marketing information systems. He has published articles on store development, structural change in retailing and retail employment. He was most recently the author of Retailing in the 1990s: Threats to Profitability and, jointly with Nord Brue, of Recruitment and Retention in Retailing, both published by Longman, Harlow. He is currently working on the application of geographical information systems to retailing and marketing, and the monitoring of longer term technological trends in the consumer service sector.
