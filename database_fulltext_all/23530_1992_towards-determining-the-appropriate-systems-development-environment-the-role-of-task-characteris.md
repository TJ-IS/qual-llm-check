# Towards determining the appropriate systems development environment: the role of task characteristics

BHARAT LAKHANPAL

California State University, Fullerton, CA 92634, USA

The growth of user developed computer-based applications (UDA) presents today's organizations with a means to augment traditional systems development by Information Systems professionals. In order to effectively use the resources offered by these two development environments, organizations need to answer the question: given a systems requirement, which development environment should be used to ensure success. This paper hypothesizes that the answer to this question depends upon the characteristics of the task? Drawing upon previous systems analysis and design research, we examine the effect of a task's degree of structure, interdependency, scope and periodicity, on the appropriate development environment. Results from a preliminary empirical test indicate that these task characteristics are useful in suggesting the appropriate environment for successful development of an information system.

## Introduction

The end-user computing (EUC) phenomenon has been in existence for several years and can be found both in the practitioner literature (Canning, 1984; 1986; Johnson, 1984; Bucken, 1990) as well as the research literature (Hammond, 1982; Benson, 1983; Leitheister and Wetherbe, 1985; Rivard and Huff, 1988). EUC has a strong intuitive appeal. It purports to place the power to develop computer-based systems in the hands of the ultimate user and has the potential to ease the pressure on the organization's application development backlog (Bucken, 1990).

Early EUC research generally described the EUC phenomenon, the end-users, EUC activities and issues in managing EUC (e.g. Benjamin, 1982; Hammond, 1982; Benson, 1983; Rockhart and Flannery, 1983; Johnson, 1984). Some of the more recent studies, however, have examined deeper issues such as factors associated with EUC success (Rivard and Huff, 1988), problems encountered in implementing EUC (Leitheister and Wetherbe, 1985; Guimaraes, 1984), and the effect of an application's characteristics on the likelihood of the system being developed by the end-users versus Information Systems personnel (Shomenta et al., 1983; Sumner 1985). Further, earlier research consisted mainly of conceptual studies (e.g. McLean, 1979; McCracken, 1980) and case studies of a few organizations. Though much of the research continues to be conceptual or case-study-based, some recent studies indicate a move towards empirical examination of the issues involved (e.g. Sumner, 1985; Cheney et al., 1986; Rivard and Huff, 1988).

The existence of the two environments, namely the traditional environment and the EUC environment, expands the spectrum of systems development resources available to an organization. The ability to bring more development resources to bear upon the systems development activity, however, also brings with it the question: given a system requirement, which development approach should be followed to ensure success? The choice of the approach could depend upon several factors, including organizational factors (Cheney et al., 1986), end-user factors (Rivard and Huff, 1988) or the characteristics of the application task itself (Shomenta et al., 1983; Sumner, 1985).

The purpose of this study is to explore and examine the characteristics of an application task as determinants of the development environment that will lead to a successful system. The premise in this research is that certain applications have characteristics that enable successful development by end-users while others may be more successfully developed in the traditional environment.

## Prior research

The meaning of EUC is generally understood as computing by end-users (of the information system) themselves, and it clearly stands out as an alternative to the traditional computing arrangements. However, several researchers, through closer examination, have found that the EUC concept is not a uniform and monolithic one. Several modes of EUC have been observed (Rockart and Flannery, 1983) and these modes correspond to several categories of end-users, including non-programming end-users, command level users, end-user programmers and functional support personnel. Two aspects of EUC, namely, information system use and information system development are found in varying degrees in all EUC modes. At one extreme, end-users could be pure users of the information system doing no development work, or they may be users as well as developers of the information system (Figure 1). This study deals with the development aspect of EUC, which has been termed 'User development of computer-based applications' (UDA) in the literature (Rivard and Huff, 1988).

Several researchers have examined UDA. Martin (1985) forcefully posited the benefits and inevitability of UDA. In defining EUC as 'development of information systems by the end-users', Leitheister and Wetherbe (1985) and Davis and Olsen (1985) clearly emphasized the development aspect of EUC over the use aspect. Rivard and Huff (1988) posit that user-friendly tools make UDA easier and its success depends upon several critical success factors. They developed a framework of UDA success consisting primarily of end-user related critical factors which was empirically tested using data from end-users in ten organizations. The data from end-users in these organizations indicated that several factors significantly impact UDA success. Cheney et al. (1986) similarly studied the effect of organizational factors on UDA success and found several significant impacts. Figure 2 summarizes the research thrusts in these two papers.

Sumner (1985), on the premise that the application's characteristics determine the appropriate development method (for ensuring successful systems), explored the likely development environment (UDA versus traditional development) for hypothetical applications having specific characteristics. MIS directors at 21 firms were presented with hypothetical applications and were asked to select the development approach which they felt would most likely to be used in their organization. Responses in this survey indicated that certain applications are likely to be developed by end-users while others are likely to be developed by information systems personnel in the traditional manner. Figure 2 places Sumner's research in perspective and provides a comparison with the Rivard and Huff (1988) and Cheney et al. (1986) papers.

Sumner's research was an important first step in empirically studying the relationship between application characteristics and the appropriate development environment. However, the study used hypothetical applications and elicited the MIS director's opinion regarding the most likely development environment. In this paper, the issue is examined through data collected on actual applications in use within an organizational context, using application task characteristics drawn from previous systems analysis and design research. Figure 2 illustrates the emphasis of this paper as compared with previous studies.

## Theory and hypotheses

This research hypothesizes the characteristics of an application task as determinants of the environment (UDA versus traditional) in which the system will be successfully developed. When certain characteristics are present in an application task, it is expected to be a successful end-user developed system. On the other hand, if certain other characteristics are present in an application task, it is expected to be either an unsuccessful UDA system or a successful traditionally developed system (by Information System Department personnel).

The hypotheses that were tested in the study are described below.

## H1: degree of structure

Application tasks with a low degree of structure are successfully implemented in the UDA environment while those with a high degree of structure are successfully implemented in the traditional environment.

A high level of structure in a task implies that the knowledge required for performing the task is standardized to a high degree. On the other hand, lesser level of structure implies that there is no routine decision procedure. In the latter case, there is ambiguity in problem definition and uncertainty about system design considerations (Naumann et al., 1980; Davis and Olsen, 1985). The concept of a task's degree of structure is applied to the issue of determining whether the traditional development environment or the UDA environment would be more appropriate for its successful implementation. For tasks with a high degree of structure, input, processing and output requirements are stable (Naumann et al., 1980) and can be defined with greater certainty. This makes information systems development more desirable in the traditional environment. Further, the Information Systems Department is likely to be aware of similar systems that may be in operation in other parts of the organization, facilitating adaptation of the existing applications to new tasks with similar standardized requirements. Conversely, tasks with low degree of structure will be more appropriately developed in the UDA environment since the user is more likely to be aware of his or her real information needs.

## H2: interdependency of tasks

Application tasks with independent task interdependency are successfully implemented in the UDA environment while those with sequential or pooled task interdependency are successfully implemented in the traditional environment.

Based upon Thompson's (1967) work, Hackathorn and Keen (1981) have suggested that the sub-task interdependency of a given task has important implications for development choices for designing decision support systems. They posit three types of sub-task interdependencies, namely, independent, pooled and sequential. Tasks exhibiting independent interdependency consist of sub-tasks that can be performed without interaction with other tasks. Sequential interdependency implies that sub-tasks can be performed in a certain sequence only – outputs from one sub-task are inputs into another. Finally, pooled interdependency implies that two or more sub-tasks must interact in order for the overall task to be accomplished.

The concept of the interdependency of an application's sub-tasks is also useful in suggesting an appropriate systems development environment. The UDA environment will be more appropriate for developing applications that exhibit independent task interdependency since the task itself does not impose any need for interaction with other tasks. Applications that exhibit sequential and pooled task interdependency on the other hand, will be better developed in the traditional environment since the task itself requires interaction with other tasks creating a need for coordination and control.

## H3: scope

Application tasks with small scope are successfully implemented in the UDA environment while those with large scope are successfully implemented in the traditional environment.

Large projects often involve many end-users and entail significantly large amounts of resources. An end-user cannot effectively determine the requirements of other users who may be affected by a large project. Further, questions of trade-offs between the requirements of different users may be involved. Finally, from the organization's point of view, it may not be advisable to entrust projects with high development costs and resource requirements to a single or few end-users. Therefore, larger projects will be more suitable for development in the traditional environment than the UDA environment.

## H4: periodicity

Application tasks that are performed infrequently or on an ad hoc basis are successfully implemented in the UDA environment while those performed routinely/frequently are successfully implemented in the traditional environment.

The periodicity with which a task is performed can vary substantially. While some tasks are performed frequently/routinely, others are performed either infrequently or on an ad hoc basis. Routinely performed tasks will benefit from systems development in the traditional environment because of the economies of scale that can be exploited by large Information Systems Departments. Further, standards and efficiency measures can be developed and enforced by the professional ISD personnel. On the other hand, infrequently performed tasks can be developed more appropriately in the UDA environment since lower throughput does not allow exploitation of economies of scale.

## Methodology and findings

We conducted a preliminary test of the hypotheses to gain insights into the effect of differences among tasks in terms of degree of structure, type of interdependency, scope and periodicity on the appropriate development environments. Twelve applications in a financial services firm were selected for study. All 12 applications were generally considered to be successful. Six of the applications had been developed and implemented in the UDA environment by end-user developers while the others had been developed and implemented by Information Systems Department personnel in the traditional environment. All six applications in the UDA environment were developed using an electronic spreadsheet program and were considerably complex from the modeling point of view. The six applications in the traditional environment were developed using a high level programming language.

In each of H1–H4 we tested the null hypothesis:

$$
\mathrm{H} _ {0}: \mu_ {1} - \mu_ {2} <   = 0
$$

against the alternate hypothesis:

$$
\mathrm{H} _ {\mathrm{a}}: \mu_ {1} - \mu_ {2} > 0
$$

where $\mu_{1}=$ the population mean (of each characteristic covered in the hypotheses H1–H4) for applications developed in the traditional environment, and $\mu_{2}=$ the population mean (of each characteristic covered in the hypotheses H1–H4) for applications developed in the UDA environment.

The null hypothesis, therefore, states that in terms of the tasks' degree of structure, interdependency, scope and periodicity, there is no difference between tasks for which systems have been successfully developed in the UDA environment and those that have been successfully developed in the traditional environment. The alternate hypothesis, on the other hand, states that, as compared with the UDA environment, tasks for which systems have been successfully developed in the traditional environment have a greater degree of structure, have a higher level of sequential sub-task dependency, are larger in scope, and are more routinely/frequently performed.

The characteristics of the tasks underlying each of the 12 applications under examination were rated through end-user responses to a structured questionnaire. In the case of applications developed in the UDA environment, the end-user was also the developer while in the case of applications developed in the traditional environment, the end-user had no development role and only used the system which was developed by Information Systems Department personnel. The response to each characteristic was elicited on a 10 point scale. For example, for 'degree of structure', a score of 1 denoted 'unstructured' while a score of 10 denoted 'structured'. Scores between 1 and 10 represented the end-user's response indicating their assessment of the varying degree of structure. The scores for the other 3 characteristics were obtained similarly.

The data obtained from the questionnaires was used to test the hypotheses by conducting a difference of means significance test. Table 1 shows the mean, standard deviation, and the value of the test statistic for the 4 task characteristics. The one tail t-test shows that the difference between mean scores is significant for degree of structure and sub-task dependency at the 99.5% level and for scope and routineness at the 97.5% level. The null hypothesis was therefore rejected for each task characteristic in the framework, implying that there is a significant difference between characteristics of tasks for which systems have been successfully developed in the traditional environment and those that have been successfully developed in the UDA environment. Where successful systems were developed in the traditional environment, the tasks had a greater degree of structure, sequential or pooled dependency in their sub-tasks, were larger in scope, and were more routinely/frequently performed.

## Conclusion

It is clear from Table 1 that, compared with systems developed in the traditional environment, those developed in the UDA environment had lower scores for all the factors included in our framework. As hypothesized, it was found that successful systems were developed in the UDA environment for tasks that had lower structure, had lesser interdependency among their sub-tasks, were smaller in scope, and were less routinely/frequently performed. In comparison, in the case of tasks for which systems were successfully developed in the traditional environment, scores for the same characteristics were comparatively higher. The difference between the scores for systems developed in the two environments was most striking in the case of two of the four characteristics, namely the degree of structure and task interdependency. This would seem to indicate that these two characteristics are more powerful determinants of the appropriate development environment than the scope and the routineness of the task.

The findings of this study have important implications for managing the systems development resources – whether they be Information Systems Department personnel or end-user developers – of an organization. Using these results, one can answer the question ‘which development environment should be used for a given application task?’, leading to a better likelihood of developing successful systems in a timely manner. These results, however, are preliminary. The findings from this research can be strengthened in the future by studying a larger number of successful systems in both environments and by having more respondents evaluate each application. Furthermore, instances of unsuccessful systems should be studied in a similar manner for additional insight.
