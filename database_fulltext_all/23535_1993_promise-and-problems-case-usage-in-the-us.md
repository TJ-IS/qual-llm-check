# Promise and problems: CASE usage in the US

GEOFFRY S. HOWARD

Department of Administrative Sciences, College of Business, Kent State University, Kent, OH 44242, USA

ARUN RAI

Department of Management, College of Business and Administration, Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA

A mailing to 2740 information system departments (ISDs) in the United States revealed that Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) technology has been very widely purchased but has not yet been placed into everyday use except in rare instances. Four distinctive categories of organizational usage of CASE are proposed, and the count of responding companies in each of these categories is given. Diagramming aids were found to be the most used CASE tools, with reverse engineering tools least used. The authors model innovation as a two-phase process and present factors from the study that are most often found in systems departments that are successful in each of these innovation phases. CASE implementation advice is offered.

## Introduction

Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) may be just a passing fad or it may be the breakthrough that will revolutionize the information systems industry. Some companies have reported stunning success with CASE, and there is preliminary evidence that CASE products of varying capabilities are being very widely bought and applied. Others have tried CASE and given up, saying that the cultural shift needed to diffuse the technology was an impossible challenge. Because of CASE's uneven success, many cautious managers have delayed exploration or extensive implementation, and are awaiting convincing evidence that they could suffer competitively by bypassing CASE. Accurate information on actual CASE usage and accompanying organizational characteristics of Information Systems Departments (ISDs) is needed to guide these implementation decisions.

## The depth and breadth of CASE usage

To fill this need for accurate information about CASE usage, we conducted a national U.S. mail survey. CASE use was measured along two dimensions: depth of CASE injection and breadth of CASE diffusion. This two-step injection and diffusion process consists of a period of product acquisition, experimentation and evaluation (injection) followed by a period of conversion to widespread everyday routine use of purchased aspects of CASE (diffusion). Injection 'depth' refers to whether a particular ISD has acquired CASE products with only a few technologically shallow capabilities, such as data flow diagramme drawing aids, or with technologically sophisticated capabilities such as full-life cycle integrated CASE software. ISDs with more sophisticated CASE tools are said to have attained high injection depth of CASE technology. The second dimension, diffusion breadth, measures the proportion of analysts and programmers in an information systems department who are using CASE on a routine daily basis. If essentially everyone in the systems organization uses CASE on all projects the diffusion breadth is said to be very high.

Depth and breadth are independent of each other. An ISD may buy very sophisticated CASE tools for internal experimentation and evaluation (high depth), yet decide against ever putting them into general everyday use (low breadth). Alternatively, firms may acquire very limited CASE tools, such as process and data model diagramming software (low depth), yet broadly deploy those tools so that all programmers and analysts in the firm use diagramming software daily (high breadth). This means a particular information systems function will, at any point in time, fall into one of four categories (low/high depth, low/high breadth) with respect to CASE usage.

## Actual CASE use in the USA - a survey

Our CASE usage survey was conducted, among other purposes, to determine the proportion of information system departments in each of the four CASE usage categories. This information provides a conveniently summarized portrait of how extensively CASE is actually being adopted. The postal questionnaire was developed using inputs from a series of CASE-usage interviews conducted with senior managers in major corporate data centres. A list of 13 primary functions of CASE tools emerged from the interviews. Respondents to the postal questionnaire were asked to grade the sophistication of CASE tools that they possessed in their departments for each of these 13 CASE functions. (0 = No case tool; 1 = very low sophistication through to 5 = very high sophistication). Thus, an ISD could achieve a maximum score of 65 on the depth dimension. Those with a score of 32.5 or greater were classified as having 'high depth' of CASE sophistication. To gauge diffusion breadth, respondents were asked to indicate on a scale of 0 to 5 the approximate percentage of their staff and projects that used a CASE tool for each of the 13 functions. Again, ISDs receiving a score of 32.5 or more on the usage dimension were classified as 'high breadth' instances of CASE diffusion. This response-matrix measurement technique has been successfully used in similar types of studies (Nilakanta and Scamell, 1990).

A questionnaire was sent to the senior manager in each of 2740 information systems departments in the US. These companies were randomly selected from a purchased mailing list, and each met one of three qualifying conditions: Fortune 500 firm; (IS) budget exceeding \$250 000; or annual sales greater than \$50 million. Quick feedback on the results of the study was offered as a participation incentive. A total of 405 usable questionnaires were returned, and tests on these returns showed that they were statistically representative of the geographical and industry distribution of systems divisions in the nation as a whole.

## CASE usage: results

The data from the mailing clearly reveal that CASE technology has not yet extensively penetrated the IS industry. Figure 1 shows the number of ISDs in their four usage categories.

Of the 405 ISDs represented, 92 (22.7%) reported that absolutely no CASE tools had been acquired, even for experimental use, resulting in injection depths and diffusion breadths of zero. Limited CASE capability had been purchased by 275 (67.9%) of the responding firms, but they were not yet using CASE broadly throughout their organizations, placing them in the low/low 'explorers' category. An array of sophisticated CASE tools, covering the majority of the 13 CASE functions, had been purchased by 30 (7.4%) of the responding departments (high depth), but they had not broadly deployed any of these tools into actual routine use. Firms in this category are referred to as 'researchers' because they are investing heavily in experimentation and evaluation of CASE products but are not yet using them widely. Only two ISDs were 'pragmatists', who have chosen a small number of CASE functions with obvious immediate usefulness in their particular environments and pragmatically adopted them throughout their organizations. The remaining six firms 'embracers' have fully adopted CASE technology – they have acquired a sophisticated array of products (high depth) and deployed many of those products for general and routine use (high breadth).

It is clear from these results that CASE is still predominantly an experimental technology. Two-thirds of the firms surveyed appear to be conducting limited exploration of CASE to determine ways in which it can be best used. Only a very negligible number of companies have substantially replaced conventional system development techniques with CASE methods. Yet the remaining 22.7% of firms with no CASE whatsoever may be in jeopardy of falling into an uncomfortable minority group of companies that have no organizational experience with the technology. Those who have succeeded with CASE report that very long lead times are needed to absorb the new technology, and that delaying now in anticipation of a quick implementation after the technology matures is a dangerous tactic.

## CASE usage by type of tool: results

Considerable variation was found among firms in the degree of use of the 13 primary CASE functions. Figure 2 summarizes these results showing the standardized injection depth and diffusion breadth for each CASE function for the 313 companies reporting non-zero CASE usage.

Project management CASE capability has been injected into more ISDs than any other CASE feature, followed by screen and report layout capability, diagrammers (entity relationship and data-flow diagrammes), and prototyping tools. Tools for the back end of the life cycle (database schema code generators, procedural code generators, data normalizers, and test code generators) all turned out to be grouped together in the middle of the usage range. Reverse engineering mainframe tools (programme structure analysers, database schema analysers and code restructurers) are the CASE functions least likely to be found in corporate data centres.

Acquiring CASE tools for experimentation and evaluation does not, of course, guarantee that they will be used. In terms of actual routine usage (diffusion breadth), screen and report layout tools ranked first, followed by project management, diagramming, and prototyping tools.

These usage results are not surprising. The most acquired and most used CASE functions are available in CASE tools that are not particularly expensive, target specific tasks rather than offering full life-cycle support, do not require fundamental changes in a systems group's approach to developing systems, and do not require in-depth retraining of programmers and analysts. In contrast, the least used functions (reverse engineering) require substantial monetary investment, training time, and methodology change in order to be effective. This does not mean that the lesser used CASE functions are technologically and organizationally unsuccessful. The data simply reflect the longer time lags connected with their adoption.

## What makes for innovation?

Innovation is a way of life in the information systems business, but much more so in some firms than in others. This leads to the question of why some information systems departments seem to be aggressive innovators, finding and successfully employing new technologies as soon as they emerge, while others change much more slowly. This is a particularly important question for IS managers who are trying to change their departments from a tradition of laggardly assimilation of technology into aggressive leading-edge innovators.

## The injection-diffusion model of invention

Figure 3 shows a generalized model of innovation that can be of help in understanding the process of assimilation of CASE into information systems departments.

At the left is a particular innovation, such as CASE, that pre-exists in the world outside the ISD. That innovation consists of a number of various aspects. We identified 13 such aspects (or functions) of CASE tools. When the department decides to explore an innovation such as CASE, it will select certain of the 13 functions that it feels it needs and acquire the CASE tool or tools that support those functions. When these CASE capabilities cross the dashed line and the innovation actually enters the department, the Injection stage has begun. The injection depth is the aggregate sophistication of CASE tools possessed by an ISD for the 13 system development functions.

Figure 3 An injection-diffusion model of innovation

In the injection stage, the CASE tools undergo evaluation and experimentation, typically by a small number of people. If some of the CASE functions that were bought show promise, management may opt to propagate them throughout the systems department, replacing previous techniques. CASE tools for drawing data and process models could, for example, be adopted throughout the ISD, so that substantially all programmers and analysts would have workstation access to these tools. Pencil and template approaches to diagramming would be essentially outlawed. In this instance, the diffusion breadth of diagramming tools would be considered high, because nearly all of the programmers and analysts would have had begun using them on a routine basis. To achieve a very high diffusion breadth of CASE technology, ISDs would have to widely propagate the use of appropriate CASE products for most or all of the 13 functional aspects identified earlier.

## Factors relating to high case usage

Obviously, then, a manager who is trying to move a data

processing organization into CASE usage, and to generally change the department toward more innovative behaviour, is (in the parlance of the model) seeking to increase the injection depth and diffusion breadth of CASE. In order to better understand how to do this, we included items in the mail questionnaire to assess variables that seemed to relate to innovative behaviour. The goal was to determine what factors correlated with CASE injection depth and, separately, with CASE diffusion breadth.

## Selection of theoretical factors

Selection of the factors that were candidates to relate to depth and breadth was guided by the existing body of research in innovation, as was the construction of the overall model. Zaltman (1973), Duncan (1977) and others have determined that innovation adoption consists of two distinct stages: initiation and coordinated implementation. Thus, our injection-diffusion model is broadly consistent with existing literature. Utterback (1971), Duschesneau et al. (1979), Kimberly and Evanisko (1981), Ettlie (1987), and many other theorists and empirical researchers have identified variables that are likely to relate to injection depth and diffusion breadth. Based on this prior work, we selected seven variables likely to relate to injection depth and six expected to relate to diffusion breadth.

## Measurement methods

Items were designed into the questionnaire to assess the strength of these variables in each of the responding ISDs. Several scaled-response items were written for each variable. These questions were answered along a seven point ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ scale. For example, one factor we measured was the extent of in-house ‘championship’ for CASE. Figure 4 shows the questions from the survey instrument that measured this variable.

A total CASE ‘championship’ score was obtained by reversing the results from numbers 19 and 38 (these items were inversely worded) and summing the total points for these four questions. Each ‘strongly disagree’ response was worth 1 point, ‘disagree’ 2 points, ‘disagree slightly’ 3 points, etc. Thus the CASE advocacy score for a particular ISD could range from a low of 4 to a high of 28 points.

In general, there were four questions such as those above for each variable that was being measured. The questions for each variable were intermixed (note the question numbers in Figure 4) and some of the items were intentionally reverse-worded (such as numbers 19 and 38) in order to minimize attention lapses and the respondent's subconscious tendency to mark answers in patterns.

## Validity of results

Several statistical checks were done to assure that the questions were actually measuring the underlying variables of interest. Since all the questions for each variable sought to ask the same thing with slightly different working, it would be expected that the responses from a particular company or, for example, the four CASE advocacy questions, would show a high degree of internal consistency. Cronbach's coefficient alpha indicates internal consistency reliability, with an alpha of 1.0 considered perfect. All alphas for all variables except one exceeded 0.6, and most were greater than 0.8, indicating that all the items for each variable appear to be measuring about the same construct.

Also important is the issue of whether the four ‘championship’ questions really measured something different than, for example, the four ‘functional differentiation’ questions. Factor analysis was used to help make this determination. The rationale underlying factor analysis is that, over a large number of respondent questionnaires, responses to groups of like questions should cluster together, and that those cluster scores should be statistically different for different clusters. A principal components factor analysis with a varimax rotation confirmed this to be the case, providing strong statistical ecidence (most loadings exceeded $|0.7|$ ) that the questions were measuring the variables that we intended for them to measure.

## High CASE usage factors: results for injection depth

Having verified the correctness of the measurement techniques, we can determine which of the variables were actually related statistically to the depth and breadth of CASE usage. Multiple regression techniques were used, with depth results as shown in Table 1.

CASE tools are intended to be methodology companions – not replacements for the rigour of a structured development methodology. Hence it is sensible to assume that ISDs that espouse structured approaches to system development use rigorous methodologies and aggressively train their programmers and analysts in those philosophies. Because CASE tools are explicitly designed to support structured methodologies, it would be highly likely that such ISDs would have many aspects of CASE under evaluation (high injection depth). Thus, the data show that departments steeped in structured methodologies are more likely to achieve high CASE injection depths.

Championship for an innovation has, in other studies, been found to be significantly related to adoption of that innovation. When a respected and visible ISD manager or technical expert becomes 'sold' on CASE, there is good likelihood that CASE will be brought in for evaluation, even though the majority of the staff may be initially sceptical. As Table 1 shows, our data strongly confirmed the importance of internal advocacy/championship to achieving deep injection of CASE into an ISD. The results also confirmed that size is important – larger departments (based on employee counts) were found to have higher case injection depths, ostensibly because they have deeper pockets and can afford the risk of purchasing CASE tools for experimentation and evaluation.

Before companies can make the decision to import aspects of CASE, they must, of course, be aware that CASE exists in the first place. This awareness requires exposure to external (outside the ISD) sources of information about CASE. This exposure comes about through vendor presentations, attendance at trade shows and technical seminars, trade journals and via personal contact with information system professionals in other firms. Our results confirmed that departments which have more contact with external sources of information achieve higher degrees of CASE injection depth.

Performance gap refers to the difference between where an ISD presently is (in backlog, cost performance, and user satisfaction) and where it would like to be. When there is a large gap, the department can be expected to seek ways to improve its productivity. CASE has been broadly touted in the trade press as able to improve systems development and maintenance productivity dramatically, so this will be an obvious stimulant for a company to give CASE a try. The data confirmed that ISDs with larger performance gaps are more likely to have high CASE injection depths. These groups are experimenting with CASE (and probably other innovations) in a quest for ways to close the performance gap.

Functional differentiation is the degree to which an information systems department is subdivided into specialized areas such as methodology development, expert systems, new systems, object-oriented approaches, systems maintenance, client-server development, and so forth. The opposite of differentiation is an ISD in which everyone works on a mix of tasks and there is very little deep skill specialization. Functional differentiation and the deep specialization that accompanies it have been found to lead to innovation (Nyderbrand (1973)). This is because specialized groups can focus intense attention on a narrow area and quickly advance in that area beyond the routine to the fringes of knowledge. Innovation occurs at the fringes, and these specialized groups have the necessary awareness to discover and evaluate innovative elaborations that might be of interest to them. Our results confirm that departments organized into functionally specialized subgroups are more likely to be experimenting extensively with aspects of CASE tools.

Finally, some researchers have found a link between environmental instability (threat of layoffs, significant changes in their job responsibilities, etc.) and high levels of innovation activity (Tomeski (1975)). Our premise was that ISDs threatened by decentralization or outsourcing to contractors, for example, would innovate in order to survive. The data did not support this premise, though, possibly because innovation was perceived as too long a process to effectively counter an immediate destabilizing threat.

Results in Table 1 indicate seven clear factors that are present, to various degrees, in data centres that are experimenting extensively with CASE.

## High CASE usage factors: results of diffusion breadth

Experimentation alone is not enough. The payoff comes when innovations are implemented – to control costs, improve user satisfaction, and achieve competitive advantage. As was done for the depth variables, multiple regression analysis was used to identify the variables that were statistically related to achieving broad diffusion, or implementation, of CASE technology. Results are shown in Table 2.

Our analysis confirmed that methodology training is significantly related to breadth of CASE diffusion. Training is a mechanism to provide the necessary technical skills to overcome resistance to change grounded in technical inertia and perceived complexity of CASE. It is also a vehicle to educate programmers and analysts on the relative advantage of CASE over traditional systems development work. Our results conform with past studies in IS implementation research that have found positive associations between perceived relative advantage of an innovation and its usage, and negative associations between the perceived complexity of a technological innovation and accompanying usage levels (Kwon and Zmud (1986)).

CASE championship was found to be one key to achieving innovation breadth just as it was for depth. An important distinction, though, is that here, championship must flow from high level management. This is because only top managers (either within the department or corporate general managers) have the power to overcome the three generic obstacles to implementation of innovations: insufficient worker motivation, technological intimidation, and resource shortages.

ISD staff members must be provided with a reason to learn and use CASE – they must identify their personal success with its success. One way to do this is through awards, promotions, bonuses and special recognitions, all of which require resources that only senior managers can provide. Motivation also requires positive attitudes towards the innovation, and staff members can not be expected to be positive about CASE if the senior management is not openly enthusiastic.

Even when attitudes are positive, staff are liable to be intimidated by the technical complexity of CASE tools. Extensive availability of in-house technical consultants to answer questions and solve bugs is the solution to the technological intimidation problem. This also requires resources that will only be forthcoming if top managers vigorously advocate CASE.

Other resource shortages that can torpedo CASE implementation include insufficient funding for the many replications of software and hardware required to make CASE easily accessible throughout a department, and a lack of money to fund the productivity dip that accompanies the implementation of CASE. Learning CASE will take programmer and analyst time away from everyday work, and CASE-driven productivity gains will not occur until workers attain proficiency. These effects will combine to cause an initial decline in productivity and the top management CASE champions must be willing to fund this decline.

Implementing innovations costs money. Without vigorous top management CASE championship, that money is unlikely to be available, and efforts to broadly diffuse CASE will probably be doomed. Both our data and conventional management wisdom support this claim.

Our data also show that larger ISDs are more likely to broadly diffuse CASE. Size and resources usually accompany each other, and smaller data centres may simply be unable to muster the funds to buy sufficient hardware platforms or copies of CASE software, to train and maintain an in-house CASE technical support staff, and to absorb the initial implementation productivity lags.

Job rotation was found to promote broad CASE implementation. Programmers and analysts who are shifted periodically from, for example, maintenance groups to new technology groups to methodology groups tend to be more receptive to change, have a broad view of what is best for the systems department and for the firm as a whole, and feel a greater sense of job security because their continuing employment does not rely on one skill. Conversely, those who remain in the same job role year after year often stagnate, and are likely to resist the uncomfortable feeling that accompanies a major change in work practices such as CASE.

We found a positive relationship between institutional leadership and CASE diffusion breadth. ‘Institutional leadership’ means that top corporate management has set and communicated clear goals for information systems, and clearly understands the link between an effective systems group and corporate success. In this environment, all the ISD staff members are more likely to feel part of the corporate team, perceive that CASE is a stepping stone to corporate jobs, and support the CASE innovation as it is factored into everyday usage. Positive, active corporate leadership can provide a climate in which innovations such as CASE can flourish broadly.

Finally, ISDs experiencing high degrees of environmental uncertainty (threats of being replaced by contractors, decentralized with all applications shifted to PCs, etc.) fared badly at trying to widely disperse CASE internally. A stable, secure and predictable systems department is fertile territory within which innovation can flourish, and ‘emergency innovation’ methods fare badly.

Results in Table 2 indicate six factors expected to be present in various combinations in data centres that are succeeding in broadly implementing CASE: strong top managerial championship of CASE, large size, frequent job rotation, clear institutional leadership, and environmental stability. These and the preceding seven CASE depth factors provide a theoretical portrait, confirmed by data from the US national survey, of ISDs that appear to be ideally configured to achieve success with CASE.

## Conclusions

Our survey reveals the following:

(1) An overwhelming proportion of ISDs are actively experimenting with CASE tools.

(2) Almost no ISDs have broadly implemented CASE as their predominant standard systems approach.

(3) The most used aspect of CASE is diagramming tools; reverse engineering is the least used aspect.

(4) Data centres that have acquired substantial CASE capability (high injection depth) have the following factors in common: a well entrenched systems methodology, strong CASE championship, large size, extensive external communication links, a noticeable performance gap, functionally differentiated programmer and analyst workgroups and operate in uncertain environments.

(5) Data centres that have succeeded at broadly implementing CASE (diffusion breadth) have the following factors in common: entrenched systems methodology, top management CASE advocacy, large size, a regular practice of programmer and analyst job rotation, strong and clear institutional leadership, and a low degree of environmental uncertainty.

CASE products have sold well, indicating an industry-wide awareness of the technology and faith in its long-term potential. Our results show, though, that CASE has been very slow in making the transition from experimental use to broad, everyday practical use. In many cases this slow implementation reflects proper managerial caution. Successful CASE implementation requires considerable training and consulting resources, application of cold, hard principles of basic project management, careful establishment of a methodological groundwork, and a crystal-clear appreciation of what CASE will and won't do within your computing environment.

CASE failure stories abound. Each failure can usually be traced to a violation of one of the above basic implementation principles. Given the newness of CASE and the long lead times required to establish the proper conditions for implementation success, it is not surprising to hear failure stories from early users, few of whom could possibly have taken enough time and trouble to prepare properly for CASE implementation. Our hope is that the bulk of the ISDs who are experimenting with CASE but have not yet implemented it are holding back purposefully, systematically aligning their methodologies, marshalling training and consulting resources, and planning realistic and attainable implementation milestones. These actions, coupled with a vivid awareness of the six 'diffusion breadth' factors, will maximize the odds of success.
