# Software standards: an information requirements framework

THEODORA N. NGOSI and JOHN O. JENKINS

School of Informatics, Department of Business Computing, City University, London EC1V 0HB, UK

This paper discusses research findings on information requirements for developing international Information Technology (IT) standards. Using the software standard development domain as a case study, the objective is to examine the standard development processes and the problems associated with IT standardization, and based upon the findings, an information requirement framework is provided.

## Introduction

Standards provide harmonized applications of IT products, facilitate the interchange of information, and provide appropriate degree of interworking amongst various systems. A ‘standard’ is a comprehensive document that describes one or more sets of characteristics for a product or process, for example performance and safety requirements.

The standardization process is based on shared knowledge about the product or process to be standardized, the rationale of which is to enhance existing practices and/or to effect variety reduction. The development of an IT standard involves the following steps:

(1) the need for the standard is identified;

(2) requirements are studied;

(3) draft standard is prepared and authorized;

(5) draft is reviewed by technical experts, by consensus;

(6) standard is approved for publication.

The main concern of this process is that, Standard Development Organizations (SDOs) can provide only the means necessary to manage standard development projects (DGXIII, 1990). Specifically among the standard developers, the ways in which technical information is exchanged shows excessive specialization which reduces flexibility in the way this information is subsequently used.

Considering this evidence, it will be established that the exploitation of well-tried methods in Information Systems (IS) would provide better tools to support the information required for the standardization, consequently this would improve the development processes. This is the significant hypothesis in this research.

## The IT standard development environment

Referring to Figure 1, the international IT standard development community relies on groups of voluntary standard developers, the experts, who provide the technical knowledge and document the technical concepts.

The developers are selected from differing but associated industries, trade associations, academic and government institutions and they work in groups subordinate to acknowledged technical committees. In most cases, regularly organized committee meetings consolidate the work that takes place at national levels and/or within ad hoc research programmes. Membership on these expert groups is open to any individual or organization directly affected by the activity and those willing and able to participate.

Developers communicate at national and international levels through accredited SDOs. The SDO formulates the rules to support the standard development procedures. The standard is developed in a public forum providing opportunities for all interested parties (representative committees, comprising manufacturers, users and governments).

## The standard development process

Standard development processes always include extensive knowledge acquisition procedures involving collaboration between the developers and industry. The resultant procedures form part of the requirements for formulating projects which eventually lead to the production of the standard.

Predominant procedures involve producing the standard through a nominated SDO or an accredited body. The most common processes involve the formulation of the set of requirements to be standardized. Discussions take place in committees, specifically to refine the technical proposals and to consolidate the knowledge acquisition procedures. Once written, the standard is approved for publication. Consensus among appropriate interested parties is the method commonly used to reach agreement on the adoption of the standard, although in some cases, voting rules apply.

## The five-stage model

## Stage 1 - proposals

These are often initiated outside the standard development process by individuals or organizations directly or materially affected by the activity. In ‘perfect’ circumstances, a proposal is formally submitted in writing to the SDO identifying the need for the standardization. A formal vote is taken by interested parties in support of the proposal. If the idea has sufficient support, the task is assigned to an appropriate technical committee, or, if none exists, one is set up consisting of volunteer standard developers who can work on the project. If not, the idea is dropped.

## Stage 2 - study and discussion

Once the proposal is approved by the SDO, various discussions take place within the entrusted committee to formally assess and study the identified project requirements. Appropriate technical information is gathered and studied, the outcomes of which are draft technical specifications which will subsequently be used to invite widespread and appropriate international support, expertise and technical comments. Draft technical specifications are reviewed through formal ballots to ascertain the application of the technical concepts being standardized.

## Stage 3 - writing the standard

When various concepts have been consolidated the entrusted committee drafts a formal technical document, the draft standard is written, which is given an identity for example: IEEE Standard 1074 (draft 0.1) 19xx : Guide for developing Software Life Cycle Processes

The SDO would be requested to formally authorize the existence of the document. At this stage formal committee ballots, consultations with interested parties and industry take place to assess the impact of the draft and its potential useability, once published.

## Stage 4 - consensus

Consensus potentially plays its formal role in the writing stage where the drafted concepts are reviewed and amended, and these need a majority approval of the committee. Consensus procedures therefore require the resolution of substantial objections from the committee, interested parties and where appropriate, public comments are made on the basis of safety and conformity to industry practices. When the draft standard is in a 'stable' state, the final formal text is validated through a relevant standardization body on the basis of its declared procedures. It is then further reviewed by technically qualified experts. The acceptance of technical and/or general comments is the means to (virtually) guarantee agreement for the adoption of the standard.

## Stage 5 - publication

Based on the result of the draft international standard votes, the final text is prepared. The standard would be approved and its publication would be announced. The standardization process is completed. The technical committee would then be disbanded.

## Review and maintenance

This is an integral process which provides feed back on the standardization that took place. As the technology changes, periodic reviews on published standards are required to maintain up-to-date concepts. Any such cases will be identified and fed back into the standard development process and the standardization is initiated again, from proposal to publication.

## Research methodology

Based on the five-stage model procedures, the objective of this research is to analyse the standard development process with emphasis on providing appropriate IS solutions to improve this process. Against this background, preliminary studies have been carried out to support the IS requirements.

Initially, pilot studies were carried out with widespread IT standard developers and SDOs. The studies aimed to identify and establish unbiased, experience-related requirements for developing the IT standard. Questionnaires were used to collect generic descriptions of IT standard development requirements, and based upon the responses, general statements for information requirements were collected. Resultant statements were categorized under the main headings: general, standard development process and developers' requirements. The problems were analysed separately. An example of the information requirements question and resultant responses is shown in Table 1.

From the identified requirements, it was established that the accepted international approach for the development of the standard comprises interactive procedures and these denote the developers' iterative processes. Further, as a basic approach, there is a need to establish characteristic requirements that support these processes.

## Software standard development survey

Subsequent surveys were carried out specifically with software standard developers selected from IEEE and ISO/IEC JTC 1. In this case, the requirements gathered from the pilot studies were further examined under two main categories: software standard development processes and the developers' requirements, as well under specific categories of technical information, document development, project management and technology options.

## Data collection

Questionnaires were mailed to IEEE with a covering letter requesting distribution of the questionnaires to appropriate software standard developers. From a compiled list of names, the same questionnaires were mailed to ISO/IEC JTC 1 Sub-Committee (SC) 7 members assigned to Software Engineering development and system documentation standards.

The questions were designed to identify fundamental software standard development processes, to establish significant problems and to identify the requirements necessary to develop the software standard. In a separate questionnaire, and in order to assess the feasibility of implementing IS in standard development processes, developers were further asked to consider the processes that would be improved by IS.

## Criteria for participation

As shown in Table 2, the selection of candidates for participation in this survey and the ultimate selection of responses was based on established criteria including:

(1) candidates should have had relevant past experience in accomplishing one or more stages of developing a software standard, for example drafting technical specifications;

(2) they had to be registered as participants within IEEE and/or ISO/IEC JTC 1;

(3) they were expected to be participants nationally, within selective major standards sectors such as North America, Japan and Western Europe;

(4) they should have participated in a committee which had completed developing an adopted software standard.

Table 2 also shows further qualifications for participation, these were to be indicated on the questionnaires concerning:

(1) technical background and experience in identified software standard development stages such as requirements setting; design and configuration;

(2) general participation commitment in terms of the time spent on standards activities, that is man-days/years.

## Selection of responses

Three-hundred and fifty (350) questionnaires were distributed. The responses were collated over a period of five months, during which some of the candidates were interviewed. Having established the participation criteria, these supported subsequent selection of responses.

Two-hundred and sixty-nine developers (269) responded to this survey (76% response). One hundred (100) respondents who fulfilled the established participation criteria were finally selected. A summary of the selected responses is given in Table 2.

## Hypotheses tested

Using the research objectives and the resultant responses, hypotheses were established to support subsequent analyses of the identified requirements.

H1: the introduction of IS in some standard development processes would improve information flows between developers and SDOs.

This is the key hypothesis in this research. The assumption is that, the use of IS would enable control of technical information and make it easily accessible to developers particularly in the study and consensus stages where there is a greater need to coordinate technical information.

H2: IS could improve standard development processes: IS would provide better coordination of the key processes.

Based on the analyses of the established requirements, H2 is a derivative of H1. Developers indicated that the five-stage model ‘prescribes’ that information exchanges should be well coordinated. As such, the basic assumption in H2 is, IS solutions should improve the control of technical information thereby providing appropriate monitoring of processes and feed back on key processes. This is the key coordination required.

H3: information requirements versus standard development process: as a significant requirement, technical information has greater importance within the process than any other established requirements.

One of the main concerns of this study is to provide requirements frameworks for the development of the software standard. To achieve this, requirements were evaluated according to the established developers' rating scores. Technical information for example was considered of significant importance since it initiates the development of the standard. It is hypothesized here therefore that appropriate information requirements provide practical standard development processes.

H4: the technology options used should eliminate problems such as fragmentation, duplication of effort and delays in producing the standard.

H4 assumes that the selected IS strategies should broaden the scope for improved processes subsequently eliminating problems such as fragmentation of activities.

## Statistical tests

Two types of statistical analyses were performed to test the hypotheses: the standard normal distribution test denoted by 'z' and the rank correlation coefficient test denoted by 'r'.

## The 'z' test

The developers' responses established two samples: 32 information requirements and 23 process requirements. From these samples, the means and variances were generated. Using 'z' in a two-tailed test at significance level 0.05, H1 and H2 were tested against a generated hypothesis. H0: ISs would not improve standard development processes.

The assumption of the test is that if the two samples are independent and randomly selected normal variables, then the difference of the two sample means will possess a normal or an approximation of a normal distribution; as the distribution deviates from zero (0) it should be 'bell shaped', indicating that H1 and H2 are supported. Significantly, this test provided evidence that developers possessed some agreement on H1 and H2.

## The 'r' test

In the questionnaires, developers were requested to rate the identified requirements. The rating scales were consequently used to assess the developers' perceptions to the order of importance placed on each of the identified requirements. As shown in Table 1, the rating scales were between 0 and 5 where: 0, 1 were of low importance; 2, 3 were medium; 4, 5 were the high rating scales.

From the established sample of requirements, the rank coefficient test was used to determine the association of the different rating scales for a sample of rated requirements: 24 medium-high rated developers' requirements; 19 medium-high rated process requirements. The sample means were generated and in a two-tailed test, 'r' was tested at 0.05 significance level. Significantly, H3 was supported providing evidence that information requirements were of greater importance.

The test was further used to establish the correlation of 14 rated technology options against the rated developers' requirements. Evidence was provided that developers agreed with the introduction of IS, and this further supported H1 and H2.

## Discussion of findings

## Primary definitions

## Technical information

This is a basic essential element necessary for the completion of the standardization task. This information consists of technology specific information such as R & D activities, industry practices and standards, reference models, technology profiles and applications. Developers acquire technical information from industry, R & D, academic collaboration and through industry experience.

Reference models, for example, provide a framework for the development of a set of standards addressing the provision of a function or closely related functions (Cargill, 1989; ISO/IEC JTC 1, 1990). The Software Life-Cycle Model for IEEE standards is one such example which takes into account specific guidelines which outline the target industry functions and the respective technology applications. Such models are references which form part of the essential requirements for producing and/or targeting standards, at the same time they address practical methodologies and evaluation frameworks on which to draw upon subsequent technical information.

## Process information

This comprises a combination of the information that initiates the standardization activity, the activities that take place and the information that results from these activities. Because some of the standardization activities depend on technical information, they are defined as technical procedures. For example, consensus is a technical procedure which supports technical interactions such as writing of technical concepts. The information that ensues from this procedure are among others, technical comments. In this case, technical information drives the process, the outcome of which is the process information that would be used to determine the progression of that activity.

## Summary of general statements: identified problems

Resultant responses raised diverse problems associated with the development of the software standard and generic IT standardization. The following problems were the most significant:

## Technology drive

The IT market, especially the software industry needs relevant standards which match an analogous market-driven industry (ISO/IEC JTC 1, 1990). Standardizing IT requires greater involvement of technical experts who would attempt to match industry demands, however, there is little industry commitment. Further, competition within the IT industry, monopolistic attitudes and technical barriers are often the factors that impede IT standard developments (Lecraw, 1984).

## Targeting of IT standards

Similarly, as a significant problem, where current standards do not focus on relevant technology structures or appropriate approaches applicable to technological developments, the targeting of IT standards leverages on the development of the IT industry and subsequent standardization of the technology (Sirbu and Zwimpfer, 1985).

## Availability of conceptual information

The progression of IT developments demands specific requirements and appropriate frameworks for fitting various standards. As a means of communication between IT users and standard developers, there is a need for requirements and/or base reference models defining what users want from their applications (Cargill, 1989). However, the IT industry is not always forthcoming with information that would enable standard developers to develop and target standards appropriately.

## Duplication of effort

There are multiple standards making committees and bodies covering different aspects of related work, often competing for prestige. Competition often results in fragmented communications between developers and industry, which makes it difficult to coordinate essential technical information. Consequently, most projects are mismanaged in terms of costs and time. Such styles of operation duplicate and fragment standard development processes. It becomes almost difficult to assess, during development stages, whether there are overlapping projects.

## Developer-developer coordination

Such coordination is a primary input in the development of the standard whereby numerous developers, in disparate locations, gather the technical information that would be used in the study stage. Additionally, the writing stage relies on communications between appropriate developers who draft the technical specifications. Coordination of technical information therefore requires visible control and reporting structures and these should provide accountable linkages within the process.

However, the present coordination structures cause most of the delays experienced by developers. As a direct result of slow and sporadic communications or unstructured coordinations, delays are reflected on the time taken to complete the standardization.

Based on this evidence, the typical timescale for the completion of the standard should take 36 months or less depending on various conditions such as the time taken to study the requirements and consensus. IEEE for example takes 60 months to complete a software standard; ISO/IEC JTC 1 has an average timescale of 82 months, 40 months for consensus to circulate a draft international standard and 42 months for the publication processes (ISO/IEC JTC 1, 1991).

## Management of standards projects

The five-stage model (Cargill, 1989) is often perceived as a restrictive factor in terms of timeliness. Further, it does not distinguish the different approaches required for different IT standards environments. Rather, this model considers that standardization concepts and processes are the same across a wide spectrum of IT areas. For example, the development of a software standard would require different and distinct approaches and information compared to say the standardization of computer keyboards. Yet, these two areas use the five-stage model as the predominant basis for achieving different results, with consequences on timeliness and coordination of processes.

## Technology options and IS requirements

Using H4 as a basis for evaluating the feasibility of introducing IS, 61 % of the surveyed software standards developers recommended IS strategies as a solution, specifically to coordinate key processes such as control of technical information, coordination and consensus processes. IS would provide accountability in the way developers produce the standard and it would eliminate some of the problems identified in IT standard developments, specifically, fragmentation, delays and the lack of coordination. Table 3 summarizes the developers' technology preferences, where such options should aim to improve developers' communications by making information easily available for selective processes. 'Networked information bases' and 'decision support systems', for example, were rated the most important.

## Towards a solution and conclusions

This study has clearly indicated that there are significant problems that affect standard development processes. Without organized technical information, the degree of interaction within these processes hinders the coordination of information and the activities that take place, subsequently it leads to fragmented processes. Significantly, developers established that the critical factor that would improve standard development processes is the introduction of IS in key processes such as study of the project, consensus and writing the document.

The developers' requirements summarized in Table 4 demonstrate that at each stage of the development of the standard, requirements should be evaluated to provide some framework which considers what information or activity is appropriate to complete the task. The emphasis given to the selected requirements should consist of key characteristics that are indicative of the inputs required, how the inputs are to be used and what outputs are expected. Such categorizations provide guidance for gathering, preparing, selecting and organizing appropriate requirements to be addressed.

Accordingly, the rating scales in Table 4 were used to indicate on a given scale, the importance of the identified inputs. Using the rating scales, it was subsequently established that, what becomes essential for the development of the standard is appropriate technical information, the use of that information becomes an analogous approach associated with the process; the standard development process is therefore driven by the technical information. There are overlapping requirements within the process.

In the search for appropriate requirements, developers further indicated the following requirements:

(1) acquisition and control of technical information is essential: information should be readily available and accessible;

(2) coordination of the information to developers should include methods of control and selecting;

(3) key procedures should provide accountable linkages for consensus and provide the feed back mechanisms;

(4) administrative environments are essential for collation and the distribution of documented texts and summaries arising from ballots.

Using the developers' requirements established in Table 4, an Information Requirements Framework (IRF), in Figure 3 has been prepared. This considers the developers' essential information requirements. The IRF also emphasizes the systematic approach to setting requirements for the development of the standard and this framework will form a basis for potential systems planning. The IRF concepts are summarized below:

## External inputs

External inputs comprise industry involvement. This should provide the technology concepts to be standardized and participating parties.

## Primary inputs

These are part of the formal standardization process where the vital requirements include participation from interested parties; setting up of SDOs that would direct the progression of the project. Included in this category are the primary requirements for the development of the project:

(1) major technical requirements comprise the technical information for example, technology specific requirements, reference models and/or technology profiles. Reference models and functional profiles are a subset of the standard development process and these form part of the technical guidance required for the standardization.

(2) secondary technical requirements include user inputs. Once the standardization requirements have been set, the user groups corroborate information with developers and this includes technical literature, industry standards and industry practices.

## Process inputs

These are the pivotal requirements comprising the five-stage model as a primary guidance to the methodology of developing the standard. Subordinate to the process, there are key requirements for independent management environments to control, monitor and enhance the processes that take place for example, consensus, administration, coordination of information and the management of the project. Such environments provide a base for evaluating key processes, coordinating international approaches and feed back mechanisms.

## Requirements for key processes

(i) Consensus environment: as an independent process this requires effective control of information resulting from the exchanges that take place; the coordination of that information, specifically to developers. This environment will establish new concepts derived from the standardization and also provide feed back on the direction of the project. Information filtered from this environment therefore requires supportive peripheral environments which coordinate subsequent activities.

(ii) Administrative environment: should support consensus environment for example for text preparations and formal documentations. In most cases developers communicate through written documentations which are subsequently distributed to participating parties. This environment is therefore an integral requirement for information control, preparation of texts and specifically, coordination of incoming and outgoing information.

(iii) Information coordination is a basic element for the interaction of technical information and processes. The requirements include the control and distribution of technical information and, the coordination of activities. For example, from balloting procedures comments are consolidated and subsequently distributed to various developers for analyses. Such coordination processes require control environments which may consist of administration bases which indicate where the information should be directed once the documentations are prepared.

## Strategic environment

When the standardization is completed, some of the output information is collated for use in formulating standards development strategies such as project management and international policies. This environment therefore is linked to key processes; its purpose is to control the standard development process as a whole and to provide direction as to what should take place.

Developers identified that within the existing IT standards development environment, the problems that affect the development processes are as a direct result of undefined coordinations and in some cases, there is lack of accountability on what actually takes place, consequently there are inconsistencies in managing the process. Highlighting some of these problems has instigated that solutions be established for improving the processes. It was also established earlier that the standard development process comprises interactive relationships including the developers' iterations, the coordination of information and processes. In the attempt to map these requirements relationships the IRF has considered only the tangible entities for example, what the developers want, what takes place within the process and the feed back mechanisms.

However, this IRF is not the ultimate solution. In this framework there are overlapping areas which could be improved and if extended, these would create other requirements and frameworks. For example, the development of reference models is an integral standardization process with analogous, but different information requirements and approaches. Such activities could be further analysed to formulate subordinate activities which have parallel frameworks connected to the IRF, thereby producing wider requirements definitions and new concepts relationships.

As such, conceptually, the IRF could be improved by widening the scope of each of the entities described in the ‘requirement setting boundary’ so that it becomes feasible to develop characteristic IT standards development requirements. As an appropriate method for requirements gathering in this study, this IRF addresses information requirements for software standards, and it can also be applied to other classes of IT standards. However, further research is required to add to the findings of this study, the benefits of which would provide effectual standard development requirements which would be used in IS planning.
