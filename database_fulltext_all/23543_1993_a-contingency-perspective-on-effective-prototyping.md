# A contingency perspective on effective prototyping

OSCAR GUTIERREZ

Department of Management Science and Information Systems, University of Massachusetts at Boston, Boston, MA 02125-3393, USA

Current demands on prototyping emphasize increasingly complex and dynamic applications that require sophisticated social mechanism and process enablers. However, much of the emphasis placed today in systems development under prototyping focuses on the supporting technology. The imbalance between product and process perspectives under this approach is explored. A view of prototyping effectiveness is presented in terms of non-linear problem solving, adequate technical and procedural solutions, and organizational support. Implications of this view are presented on the selection of prototyping techniques and on project management concerns. Future developments in prototyping practice are explored.

## Introduction

Most of the literature addressing prototyping for systems development discusses programming in very high level languages and methodological issues. The focus remains largely technical, even though prototyping's emergence as a new development strategy occurred for largely non-technical reasons (Earl, 1978). Although we now understand the most obvious contexts for prototyping, we have not extensively considered its use and impact in less immediately obvious contexts. Those are in looking beyond current information requirements to the longer term behaviour of humans in an information system, particularly in continuously changing conditions. There is an increasing gap between technical development and support for prototyping and our knowledge of and response to the prototyping context itself. We have seen the emergence of innumerable excellent tools, but we have not yet focused on the managerial frames of reference that may best exploit these tools. As increasingly complex managerial demands move us into different categories of problems, and we see low cost information technology proliferate throughout organizations, we also see a fundamental emerging shift in systems development attitudes.

This shift in attitudes moves away from the assumption that systems development goes through a series of long chronological steps. It also rejects beliefs ingrained in the established linear model that consider the interests of specialists and systems users as different worlds and cultures. The alternative systems development model from which most of the seminal prototyping literature originates (Earl, 1978; Brittan, 1980; Naumann and Jenkins, 1982; Dearnley and Mayhew, 1983), is fundamentally an evolutionary one which is based on practice or experimentation. It is one which perceives projects in terms of unified partial worlds of specialists and system users. In systemic terms, this model represents an ongoing process involving analysis and problem reformulation, practice, and gradual implementation.

The most visible rationale for prototyping is that cooperative experimentation will elicit a more reliable product, and will do so in the long run more efficiently (Brittan, 1980; Floyd, 1984). A less visible rationale deals with the psychology and philosophy of prototyping (Jorgensen, 1984; Kauber, 1985), which acknowledges that prototyping is itself a test for effective organizational response to complexity and instability: the process of prototyping is more important than its product. In either vein, this is a powerful technique that requires sophisticated social behaviours accompanying participation at different managerial and user levels, and commitment on the part of the developers to adopt an approach which benefits users. These characteristics place pressures on the technical specialist, who frequently has not been hired on the basis of, trained in, or evaluated for such sophisticated behaviours or commitment and may therefore not be fully equipped to make the most of prototyping's promise. These characteristics also challenge the organization, as prototyping's departure from traditional life cycle methodology requires new forms of support.

In the sections that follow, current organizational perspectives on prototyping are discussed. An argument is made that prototyping effectiveness can only be achieved by considering issues from both a product and a process perspective. The paper then considers implications of this broad view concentrating on the selection of prototyping techniques, and on project management concerns. The paper concludes with a discussion of further developments in prototyping practice.

## Organizational perspectives on prototyping practice

Prototyping effectiveness cannot be determined on the sole basis of technology adoption. To be effective we see two perspectives that in practice must complement with each other: product driven and process driven development. We recognize that the former is currently the dominant approach.

## Organizational perspective: product driven development

In the product driven perspective, the available prototyping tools are what determine the form of the prototyping process. Very high level development tools, such as relational systems and non-procedural languages have been used with great success in a number of contexts (Langle et al., 1984). However, from this perspective, the benefits of prototyping accrue mainly in the specialist's domain, primarily in terms of ease of development and reduced development time.

Our concern in using only a product approach is that these tools focus development concerns mainly on the rapidity on assembling system elements into a working product. Hence, developers do not modify to a significant degree their current development practices. A product focus will thus tend to neglect issues of the complexities of the problem, the problems that may occur in the interactions between the interested parties, and the knowledge gained during the evolution of the prototype. In the long run, this focus on speed may weaken prototyping's potential by serving to view the initial 'fast' prototype as an end in itself. As the prototype then begins to take the form of a final product, the technique 'dissolves' thus possibly embedding serious technical omissions and design flaws in the product. The intricacies and technical complexities of both the problem being solved and the need to regain project momentum, may force developers into a position where it is difficult to replace the prototype and build a robust new system.

## Organizational perspective: process driven development

A complementary but less commonly employed view of prototyping emphasizes the organizational, human processes this technique both encourages and requires. This view treats prototyping as a vehicle by which users may more readily engage, adapt, and commit themselves to the information system, and by which developers may themselves come to grips with the human and technical needs of the user. The issue of collaboration between developer and user has been discussed in the literature as the most significant social enabler for prototyping (West, 1986).

This view also treats prototyping as a means to conduct organizational experimentation. Hedberg and Jonsson (1987) for example, propose a type of intelligent information system that uses probabilistic thinking and allows organizational members to debate the validity of reported information, to experiment and to test various solutions. Prototyping used in this context becomes more than a methodological response to elusive system specifications; it expresses and furthers the organization's need to encourage flexible, cooperative problem solving. Support tools in such an atmosphere would focus on helping individuals learn together, not simply design or decide together.

The implications of complementing the dominant product driven view with this process driven perspective, pose requirements that cannot be effectively satisfied by developers or project leaders alone. Effective prototyping requires the allocation of a dedicated and tailored set of resources, with broad managerial strengths to support its mission.

## A view of effective prototyping

Questions regarding prototyping effectiveness have been addressed in a product driven perspective, particularly at a technical level (Naumann and Jenkins, 1982; Janson and Smith, 1985). But when analysing effectiveness from our process driven alternative view, the evidence is scant. There is a need for mechanisms to support prototyping from a process viewpoint. To establish these mechanisms means development of alternative project management and problem solving skills, and involving a broader methodological and managerial framework to sustain and control the process.

Effective prototyping, in this view, exists when we are able to combine product and process driven perspectives. In summary, Figure 1 shows that there are several issues which arise from this integration: (a) the need to adopt a non-linear problem solving perspective to systems development; (b) the need to find both effective technical and process solutions to deal with different types of problems; and (c) the organizational need to adopt experimental practices.

## Non-linear problem solving approach

To more effectively deal with the increasing complexities of current managerial demands, prototyping techniques need to be applied in a non-linear problem solving context. Classic paradigms of effective problem solving propose a step-by-step process leading from awareness to implementation, where the goal is to capture the correct solution from the many possibilities. But we have come to recognize that effective problem solvers are more flexible and do not necessarily adhere to such an optimizing scheme. The traditional linear systems development life cycle with its waterfall modalities is a professional instance of the classical view of human problem solving. This model, still pervasive in practice, does not inherently accommodate change that may occur rapidly and unpredictably.

Prototyping provides a model consistent with our evolving views of effective problem solving. Cerveny et al. (1990) refer to this evolving model as concurrent problem solving. The term ‘concurrent’ is used to indicate that this model is inherently iterative, where the rigidity of the sequential process is replaced with a recursive overlap of phases in shorter time scales, where current conditions are compared with some ideal position, and an approximation to the ideal is made by the application of some operator (i.e. a working model). Operationally, prototyping techniques foreshorten the time and space between hypotheses, test, assessment, and re-try; they encourage the constructive use of error. By placing the developer in ongoing contact with the end user, these techniques encourage both participants to anticipate new problems raised by potential solutions.

The reasons for applying prototyping techniques are ingrained in the need to cope with risk and to resolve behavioural and systemic uncertainties. For example, Schach (1993) discusses a spiral model whereby each major development phase is preceded by risk analysis. Similarly, Land (1982) argues that the only way the designer has of reducing the uncertainty regarding the user's response to the system is to choose an experimental approach to systems design. A requirement to deal with this dynamic environment is a formal mechanism to survey a problem's present conditions. A non-linear problem solving approach provides that. It implies awareness of multiple perspectives and demands sustained suspension of judgement in the application of the technology to review different alternatives.

The shift from linear to concurrent or non-linear problem solving is consistent with the vision of a process perspective. It moves away from assumptions that the sets of goals and measures of current status are known and have been spelled out. Instead, concurrent problem solving is conducive to learning and insight. It does not force a deterministic breakdown on the problem's intended solution.

## Effective technical and procedural solutions

Developing effective technical and procedural solutions demands a proper fit be found between the type of problem, the technology, and managerial procedures. Finding this fit is more critical under prototyping than under a linear strategy because of its pragmatic nature. A number of authors (Dearnley and Mayhew, 1983) have pointed to the potential waste of time and money when a prototype is inappropriate or untimely.

A problem type is defined as a set of technical and social conditions that together form a problem situation with distinct characteristics and for which a computer-based solution is required. Land and Somogyi (1986) provide a classification of problem categories that can be used to identify different system development situations. This classification called Specification, Practice, Evolution, Uncertainty (SPEU) is suitable for product and process driven analysis. The SPEU problem taxonomy is summarized in Table 1.

An S-type (specification) situation is characterized by the existence of a complete and authoritative view of the environment. These situations place major emphasis on transformation processes. Solutions are evaluated by the extent to which they satisfy the specification.

In P-Type (practice) situations, satisfaction of the specification is not enough. A P-type problem is characterized by the impossibility of obtaining a complete abstraction of the environment, due perhaps, to changing conditions. By necessity the developers undertake an iterative process of reformulation and practice. Emphasis is placed on verifying the specification and on evaluating the efficiency of the solution.

An E-type (evolution) situation is characterized by a need to predict the effect that the system will have on the environment. However, since the prediction is not perfect, the design function must evolve. The developers need to apply techniques to simulate the operation of the object system. They also need to apply tools to enable the model to rapidly accommodate change.

Finally, U-type (uncertainty) situations are characterized by the absence of a model of the application. This is perhaps due to the presence of factors that inhibit a reliable abstraction of the environment. The developers apply tools and techniques that allow the exploration of the object system in order to resolve uncertainty. Emphasis is placed on analysis, as specifications cannot be produced until a more clear view of the environment can be determined.

The decision as to whether to prototype or not involves considering the point in the development when prototyping would be most appropriate, and the particular tools, techniques and procedural dynamics available for a given problem context.

Prototyping is normally placed at the beginning of the systems development cycle. However, different techniques can be used to effectively attend divergent stages; i.e., testing a chosen design, demonstrating elements of a new application, implementing a new system (Janson and Smith, 1985). If the problem is near totally specifiable (S-type), the solution is left to the discretion of the specialist who will define and select the most efficient means of dealing with the problem. The need to experiment in S-type problems is more evident when the development approaches completion. An experimental task in the form of a pilot process may be applicable. In this case, production or operational prototyping (Janson and Smith, 1985; Bersoff and Davis, 1991) may be adopted. Historically, software engineering efforts have concentrated in satisfying the needs of S-type problem situations (Land and Somogyi, 1986). Tools that support prototyping techniques for S-type problems are designed for the specialist. Under partial specifications such as in P-type problems, developers should adopt methods to modify their designs and test them rather quickly. Changing conditions or an incomplete specification force the developer to adopt iterative methods to modify the design and to put it into practice in a short time scale. Here, short cuts are taken so that efficiency and completeness are sacrificed for testability and modifiability. Techniques such as scenario-based design (Hopper and Hsia, 1982) and experimental prototyping (Floyd, 1984) help simulate system events by providing a model of certain system components. Commercial tools currently being developed aim at P-type characteristics. These include database query facilities and most fourth generation languages. They have been widely accepted in industry for their promotional productivity advantages and have made a significant impact in end-user computing. Although limited in applicability and efficiency they are appropriate for experimental prototyping and scenario-based designs. The user is normally presented with a system already defined and partially functional. His involvement is restricted to operation and evaluation of the experimental system. Prototyping in this scenario means anything from screen and report format painting to development of a limited working system for user approval and subsequent enlargement into a production system.

With evolving and uncertain problems, specialists must work jointly with users in defining a working model of the application. In E-type problems it is because the user is an integral part of an evolving problem being modelled. In U-type problems it is because the uncertain nature of the user's requests make it almost impossible to formulate an initial abstraction of the object system. Here the critical activities are on the analysis of the problematic situation. Techniques such as game playing (Quade, 1985), system simulation (Bratley et al., 1987), and exploratory prototyping (Floyd, 1984) may be of use. They act as catalyst to elicit ideas and to promote cooperation.

It is thus in E-type and U-type environments that a need for different techniques and tools becomes more evident. Put simply, support is needed to focus more on elicitation of information needs, and analysis and conceptual designs with less emphasis placed on physical designs, coding and testing. Konsynski (1989) argues that the building of information systems has evolved to a point where the next evolutionary step is compter-aided support for end-user and analyst problem specification, analysis, and solution construction.

From a process perspective, as developments move from specifiable to uncertain problem types, the techniques used must rely less on available tools and more on the facilitation of social interactions. This process cannot be supported without de-emphasizing one perspective and replacing it instead with effective procedural mechanisms. A starting point may be to look at the individual developer responsible for prototyping applications. The skill requirements of such individuals are different from traditional systems developers (Naumann and Jenkins, 1982). Specialists that abide by sequential strategies, develop a requisite activist style. It is assumed that these engineers will be creative with the tools and techniques that they have at their disposal. By applying rigid software engineering practices, they develop steady, clear, and incremental thinking abilities, most of which are actionoriented because they are pressured to act upon concepts and ideas.

The skill requirements for prototyping are based on the freedom to experiment. In such conditions individuals must develop an ability to avoid reliance on impulse. They must be insightful, reflective and able to resist pressure for an immediate resolution. Humans tend under pressure to grab the first answer that seems to work. We tend not to seek out disconfirming data. Prototyping, especially viewed from a process perspective, requires the developer and user to actively search for disconfirmation. At present this view is, however, different from what happens in prototyping practice since experimentation is something that has yet to make impact in MIS shops.

## Organizational experimentation

To benefit from a more experimental approach, MIS shops must be supported by an organizational willingness to undertake evolutionary developments. An evolutionary method for prototyping requires three elements: (a) that projects be based and initiated on an open premise that is to be tested; (b) that the organizational members with information needs be integral participants in the effort because requirements change and evolve; and (c) that a supportive managerial structure be present.

We argue however, that to achieve an effective level of user participation in experimental systems development, a more in-depth commitment is necessary than a methodological solution alone. The organization must be actively involved in self-evaluation or continuous improvement practices and be supportive of or engaged in deliberate pro-active change. These are characteristics that nurture and enable innovative behaviour. In organizations that have used experimental approaches to successfully develop new processes or structural forms, prototyping techniques would represent a suitable form of organizational technology. Beath and Ives (1988) argue that even if the organization as a whole does not display such proactive characteristics, the Information Systems function may. They go further by suggesting several nurturing organizational devices to enable innovation. Some of these devices are: organizational training on the norms or policies in the IS function applied to the management of change; explicit allocation of slack resources for learning and experimentation; network-forming devices like job mobility and teamwork; and specialization. Such commitment does not have to come from the top of the organization, but should be nurtured when it occurs. Users and developers gravitate towards these types of experimental solutions based not only on a problem-technology fit paradigm. Other factors involved are time pressures, scope or impact of the system, and project visibility.

Experimental behaviour in the use of information systems has been proposed on the basis of analogy with research paradigms (Hedberg and Jonsson, 1987): in this view, strategies are equivalent to the theory to be tested; plans are the models which represent the hypotheses and predictions about the theory. As plans naturally do not represent fact, they carry a high probability of error and should be considered as merely tentative solutions to the problems being addressed by the strategy. Finally, the programs of activities are the experimental tests that make use of the models and provide evidence to verify, evaluate, and define corrective action.

This form of organizational experimentation is not without problems. There are social, ethical and methodological spin-offs that must be addressed by management. For example, consider when a prototype is withdrawn after it has been in place for a determined time period. This may create dependence on temporal technology and may alter power structures on a selected group of users who may make demands contrary to the established strategy. The issues surrounding prototype replacement (Alavi, 1984; Bersoff and Davis, 1991) are symptomatic of the type of organizational problems that may be encountered when an experimental approach is adopted for the first time.

## Implications

## Impact of problem types

The SPEU classification can be seen as a continuum that spans from well defined problem conditions, to those that represent situations where there is considerable uncertainty about what is involved in the problem and how to represent it.

In situations that conform more closely with S-type characteristics, the specialist is the user of the technology who normally exhibits enormous tolerance to tracking down and fixing the ‘unexpected behaviour’ of a system. The main concern is the development of an efficient product that will soon go in operation. Prototyping in this context is an experiential exercise, which means that there is a working model that can be put in real life operation. And the focus of the developmental exercise is on successive refinement and assembly of system components.

On the other hand, in conditions that reflect the U-type extreme, the problem owner is, by necessity, the managerial user of the technology. He or she does not have the same interest as the professional user and exhibits a continuous need for assistance. His or her main concern is the analysis of the problematic situation, and he or she carries out this process by focusing on experimenting and searching out alternatives or explanations. Prototyping under these conditions is more of a conceptual process of formulations and representations.

The implications of this problem categorization are twofold: (a) time and effort requirements to undertake needs and means assessments, and (b) the need to balance the emphasis placed between product and process models.

Firstly, prototyping for S- or P-type conditions may be entirely different to prototyping for E- or U-type problems. Successful systems developers recognize that there cannot be a single prototyping technique. Due to the great variety of situations, developers are faced with a selection problem. This involves firstly, an assessment of problem conditions and system issues, as well as an assessment of available means from which the selection can be made. Secondly, it involves finding a fit between the problem requirements and the resources that can be provided by available technology (Modha et al., 1990). A number of contingency models exist to deal with the selection of information system methodologies and techniques. However, as Modha et al. (1990) argue, current selection models often ignore the process issues and lead to an exclusive focus on tool selection.

A second implication of this categorization is that, prototyping should not always be regarded only as an experiential exercise. For the most part, experimental techniques are currently utilized to learn about the requirements of the application through practice, instead of through design. This empirical approach is characteristic of the bias toward the dominant product perspective because of the over-reliance on the tools and the emphasis placed on the resulting product. This approach has proven to be suitable for specifiable requirements, but loosely defined problems require a process enabler to facilitate conceptual formulations. With the appropriate environment, and through adequate process models, the user should be able to interact within a team and ultimately with a computing system to experimentally conceptualize a model of the application, assess alternative designs and articulate the specifications.

## Project management

The practice of project management under conventional development methods is to schedule deliverables based upon specific objectives that are normally determined at the outset of the project. Under prototyping, project management needs to reflect volatility of the product as the objectives themselves change. Because prototyping embraces characteristics of evolutionary development, managerial support must stress disciplined adaptive practices that stretch over the development's duration. Applications development under prototyping will not normally exhibit the requirements and technical sophistication of projects that involve multiple teams and multiple locations. It is therefore necessary to adopt practices that lend themselves to a more flexible form of planning. Two related project management imperatives emerge: (a) the need to maintain an equilibrium between what is necessary to provide control mechanisms and what is necessary to facilitate the requisite flexibility and adaptability; (b) a need to furnish mechanisms to sustain the prototyping discipline until completion. This balancing act requires attributes not normally found in conventional project management. We consider some relevant attributes below.

First, project management must allocate resources for exploration of scenarios. These are the finite number of assumptions that will be tested and for which information will be collected for a specific deliverable. For example, the tasks in the human-computer interface design must reflect consideration of alternative user proposals coupled with alternative technical solutions. When the evidence provided by different scenarios converges, the deliverable's structure can more accurately be specified and resources for the development more realistically allocated. Also, more detailed tests for the deliverable can be formulated. Scenarios must therefore address only a finite number of design elements that can be tested in all viable alternative designs. There is a danger with this approach in that the groups involved in the development of the prototype may converge on a solution before they have fully considered the range of available possibilities in the context of the whole set of requirements (Cooprinder and Henderson, 1991). This issue for project management is, therefore, to spell out an explicit schedule of experimental trials for the specific aspect of the requirements being considered. This issue obviously brings up added complexities to the Software Configuration Management (SCM) task. For example Bersoff and Davis (1991) indicate the four areas which under evolutionary prototyping, SCM is challenged: configuration identification, control, status accounting, and auditing. Bersoff and Davis argue that the solution to these challenges lies in sensible decision making, judicious and skillful use of current practices, and in developing new ways to manage change in the development process.

The second attribute concerns explicit and continuous data collection processes to verify how design alternatives satisfy pre-established evaluation criteria as the development progresses. This involves, for example, setting up the time span for the tests, number of runs, testing conditions, and the nature of data collected. The objective of continuous data collection, then, is to describe in relevant dimensions how the system operates under given temporal premises. However, the issues with this particular project management attribute are that we do not know what type of data must be collected and how to analyse them in practice. Methodological problems abound such as how to relate cause and effect or the selection of criteria for evaluation. The levels of quality and documentation in most prototypes are questionable or unknown, and therefore unreliable for ongoing evaluation. Setting standards and data gathering guidelines, as well as the development of adequate process models that outline the dimensions for evaluation may be a starting point for researchers and practitioners.

The third attribute is commitment to an evaluation program that stipulates specific acceptance criteria and the timing for walkthroughs. The program should have two objectives: (a) to provide a formal mechanism by which end users can maintain their influence on the process and on operational aspects of the system; (b) to ensure that experimental activities are sustained until no longer needed. Because of the changing nature of most problems, integration of these sub-tasks is a concern which calls for organic organizational approaches instead of a strict separation of tasks characteristic of more mechanistic organizational forms (Nosek, 1984).

The fourth attribute concerns project breakdown. Each deliverable in prototyping culminates a process that needs to be managed almost independently of what comes next. Deliverables must be highly autonomous and the duration of atomic tasks should not exceed a very limited amount of time. Accordingly, the system architecture should be conceived in small and independent modules to accommodate quick and minor modifications.

Deferral of assignments is the fifth attribute. Test scenarios cannot be planned for the entire prototyping project. Therefore, project management should delay allocations and assignments and design decisions until satisfaction with early deliverables allows the group to define the next set of work products. This attribute explicitly conforms with the organization's own efforts to suspend closure. Land (1982) points to the tradeoffs of this approach. He argues that it enables developers to tailor the system to the requirements as they are at the time of implementation. One consequence of this is increased flexibility to mold the intended behaviour of the system as development progresses. On the other hand, it makes project control more difficult.

## Future developments in prototyping

While the interest in experimental methods continues, it is almost certain that further technical developments will increase the support of computer-based systems development automation, including computerized project management. Five potential areas of further development can be highlighted:

## Requirements elicitation tools and techniques

Introspective techniques are needed to assist end-users to articulate by themselves their information requirements. The objectives of any elicitation tool are: (a) to assist an individual to provide a personal account of his or her requirements in a systematic fashion, and (b) to allow the user to try out alternative designs on the basis of initial conceptualizations of requirements.

Techniques that draw from Personal Construct Psychology (Kelly, 1955) have been used to elicit information requirements from individual decision makers (Gutierrez and Tseng, 1988). A key to this idea is the inclusion of the end-user as the operator of an elicitation tool. Interaction with the program is a process of consolidation and learning and the output is a conceptual formulation of some aspects of the system requirements in first form.

Valusek (1988) has investigated Concept Mapping as a technique to provide specifications for builder tools such as E-R diagrams and semantic nets. Concept mapping is a technique that provides a vehicle for communication of concepts and understanding and it is useful for identifying the boundaries of the problem space.

The integration of elicitation tools in software engineering environments is needed and can provide a basis for participative systems design.

## Problem formulation and assessment support

Information requirements analysis is normally associated with problem formulation and documentation of the intended behaviour of a system. But in certain conditions, such as the ones found in E-type or U-type problems, requirements cannot be formulated – they must be discovered. Objectives and constraints must be spelled out and uncertainties aired before formal specifications can be determined.

This issue is concerned with the necessary support that must be set up early in the project, and with formal direction and control mechanisms that take into account description and diagnosis of wider organizational needs. This support implies the use of techniques and tools to assist in the organization, documentation and direction of meetings by groups. Techniques based on game playing can prove useful. The function of a pre-requirements stage is to explicitly identify a role for the prototype, the important players, and the direction of the exercise. Some techniques concerned with problem formulation and assessment are Future Analysis (Land, 1982) an assessment of the changes which could occur over the expected life of a system; Objectives Analysis (Mittermeir et al., 1987) a procedure to assist end-users to articulate their objectives at an abstract level; Flexibility Analysis (Fitzgerald, 1990) an examination of potential changes to the organization, the business or the environment in which the system will operate over various time horizons; and QUEST (Quick Environmental Scanning Technique) (Nanus, 1982) a procedure for developing a shared understanding of high priority issues.

Current research into the use of technology for group decision making is particularly relevant (Phillips, 1989). A fundamental idea behind this approach, is the emphasis on cooperative work where the participants who are senior functional managers and representatives from various interest groups, formulate and analyse alternatives and elaborate on available information. The technology involves the use of computers with some kind of audiovisual, communications or collaboration tools. Normally there is a group facilitator who advises on the use of the technology and coordinates the flow of the proceedings.

## Prototyping for narrow domains

Very High Level Languages are designed to provide a degree of abstraction which conforms more closely with the level of conceptualization and formulation of their users. These languages normally sacrifice flexibility and generality for simplicity and ease of use. Some apply to certain application areas. For instance SBAL (Small Business Accounting Language) (Podger, 1979) provides a view of accounting that can be shared by specialists and users in order to explore a variety of solutions and to determine where to build flexibility into the system. Other languages such as MODEL II (Prywes et al., 1979) focus on automatic programming for a well-defined class of problems.

Other very high level languages such as Prolog, can be used as formal specification languages when based on a specific domain of analysis. Prolog is useful in this context for its underlying semantics which provide a very high degree of abstraction. Progress in knowledge engineering techniques will most probably result in further developments for domain specific applications and expert systems prototyping.

## Developments in CASE technology

A promising area is developments in Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) tools. CASE technologies have demonstrated their potential for conceptual modelling and as vehicles of communication. Most recent developments have seen advances in their procedural components, which have resulted in numerous tools for prototyping applications. Most CASE tools today offer prototyping capabilities that include screen and report layout painters; screen navigation; menu building utilities; and code generation to exercise input data forms.

For CASE tools to make a significant impact in prototyping for the analysis of ill-defined problems, they must accommodate facilities to support end-user interaction, similar to the elicitation techniques referred to above. An important element is the inclusion in these tools of tutoring systems or intelligent assistants similar to Bachman's Hypertext reference and self-instruction utilities (Gane, 1991). Their role would cover several aspects confronting the inexperienced computer user, namely, how to make efficient use of the system, and how to deal with the problem at hand with minimum intervention by the specialist.

## The prototyping resource centre

The last area is in providing adequate support for prototyping by the organization, beyond the one that flexible project management can provide. This type of support involves changes in organizational design, changes in management style, professional development, and personnel actions such as reward structures and staffing. For example, Boar (1984) describes the characteristics of a prototyping centre, based on his experience at AT&T. In his approach, the prototyping centre represents a new formal data processing service alongside the production, information, and applications development centres. Similarly, Nosek (1984) describes a case where the organization created a resource centre where prototyping users could obtain advice and information on adequate tools. In this example Nosek notes that the organization undertook considerable change in the areas of information technology acquisition, designing and programming practices, and the organizational design.

## Conclusions

Current prototyping practice, has taken a predominantly product driven perspective. However, effective use of prototyping techniques demands that the organization not only equips itself with adequate technology, but most importantly that it responds to procedural and organizational issues as well. This paper has attempted to highlight the importance of a few key process concepts: the organizational commitment to and practice of self-evaluation; the need for flexible project management practices; and the recognition that the nature of problems can help to determine the type of effective experimental strategies as well as their timing.

The paper advocates the adoption of a concurrent problem solving approach which seems to fit with fundamental prototyping goals: the practice of critical thinking; the exercise of suspension of closure; and flexible and iterative development practices.

As we leave behind the era of clearly defined system requirements to solve a new class of problems which are essentially more complex, dynamic, and loosely defined, we clearly see the need for experimental development strategies. We indicate five areas where evidence of further developments will impact the power and capabilities of prototyping approaches. These five areas are: developments in tools to assist end-users to elicit their requirements; support in pre-requirements assessments to conduct future, objectives or flexibility analyses; prototyping tools to support analysis of specific categories of problems or domains of analysis; developments in CASE technology; and organizational initiatives to formalize prototyping services.

## Appendix

## Prototyping and other experimental techniques

Game playing (Quade, 1985) A form of simulation modelling in which users and developers play major actions or decision processes involved in the system being analysed. The objective is to gain insights from the simulation of the behaviour of major elements in the model. The players are often assisted with computer simulations that are used to represent different decision scenarios or design alternatives.

Exploratory prototyping (Floyd, 1984) A strategy to resolve communication problems between developers and user groups. It has a predominantly process perspective because it de-emphasizes the use of the technology as a driving criteria. The only use of automated tools in exploratory prototyping is in demonstrations. The objective is to use the prototype as a catalyst to elicit ideas and to promote cooperation between the involved parties.

System simulation (Bratley et al., 1987) Development of a system sketch with an automated design tool. The simulation does not represent an operational prototype because it cannot be exercised. Applications of systems simulation are also in demonstration because they allow users to see how the system will look. This approach facilitates the design of human-interaction characteristics of the proposed system.

Scenario-based design (Hopper and Hsia, 1982) An operational form of system simulation. A scenario can be run to exercise systems events so that the user can experience the operation. Scenarios are excellent tools to model input-process-output screens and associated dialogues.

Experimental prototyping (Floyd, 1984) A strategy to exercise an operational working model which is designed to test hypotheses about the system being analysed. Developers take shortcuts in order to produce a working prototype as soon as possible. Efficiency and completeness are sacrificed for testability and modifiability.

Automated software synthesis (Bersoff and Davis, 1991) Automation of software development. Software tools such as the ones used today in many CASE environments, are applied to develop at levels higher than applications programming. The same tools are then used to generate lower-level instructions.

Production prototyping (Janson and Smith, 1985) A full-scale representation of the basic design idea developed on a stable base and employing sound techniques and materials. Development proceeds in evolutionary fashion.

Reusable software (Bersoff and Davis, 1991) Common patterns of tested programming that can be rapidly catalogued, saved and retrieved from a common pool of generic code; and rapidly incorporated into a newly developed application.

Pilot systems (Gosden, 1979) Access-constrained versions of a final system, with limitations on the population they serve, and the load that they handle. They are normally adopted as pioneer projects.
