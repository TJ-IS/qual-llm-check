# Making database marketing work

R. SHAW

Managing Director, Shaw Consulting, 46 Cranbrook Road, London W4 2LH, UK

This article examines the use of marketing databases and the issues encountered in making then work effectively. The first part of the paper examines the history of database marketing and the development of two different approaches. In the first approach, the database is used tactically as a high quality mailing list. In the second approach, the database is used strategically, to re-engineer the sales and marketing process. The second part of the paper looks at the practical issues in implementing the strategic approach. Risk management rules are proposed to deal with these implementation problems, and sources of advice and assistance described.

## Introduction

Database Marketing today is doomed. How many businesses have thrown thousands of pounds, even millions, at the marketing database? How many now are rushing to the bank to cash in the returns on their investment? How many are questioning the wisdom of it?

Database marketing need not be doomed. However, the blind, unquestioning adherence to a ‘database’ strategy needs to be shaken up and questioned. A fundamental rethink is badly needed before database marketing can be made to work effectively and reach its full potential.

## The vision - computer integrated marketing

In database marketing, the state of affairs is very confusing. Few people can agree on a definition of database marketing. In 1987, I defined database marketing as follows:

"Database marketing is an interactive approach to marketing, which uses individually addressable media and channels (such as mail, telephone, and the sales force):

\- To extend help to a company's target audience

• To stimulate their demand

\- To stay close to them by recording and keeping an electronic database memory of customers, prospects and all communications and commercial contacts, help improve future contacts and to ensure more realistic planning of all marketing."

(Shaw and Stone, 1988)

Lisa Petrison, Professor of Marketing at Northwestern University, cites this in her 'History of Database Marketing' to be the first definition of the term. The concept I envisaged was of an integrated, closed-loop marketing process where information about the ‘customer’ was the main driving force. It was a break with earlier approaches to marketing, where ‘product, promotion, price and place’ were the driving forces.

The origins of this approach go back over a century. Some say it began in 1872 with the Mongomery Ward catalogue, since Ward carefully monitored each of his customers' orders and sent personalized sales letters based on his knowledge of individual customers. Others believe that database marketing began in the 1930s, when Aldens began to rank the value of their customers on the recency, frequency and monetary amount (RFM) of their purchases, or in the 1960s when powerful mainframes allowed list brokers to sell contact names and addresses for junk mailing purposes. Some see it as a 1980s phenomenon, resulting from the fusion of 'integrated marketing' and 'integrated computer systems'.

## The two database marketings

This integrated, closed-loop vision has been lost in the headlong rush to make a ‘fast dollar’ on databases. Most direct marketing agencies say they practice database marketing. List brokers have become trendy and they now have ‘databases’ for rent. The service bureaus are now ‘database’ management companies. Analysts offer ‘database modelling’. From breakfast cereal to cigarettes, from mortgages to pensions, from cars to computers, vast storehouses of marketing data are being filled up. For ten years this has been happening. Now in the 1990s, business executives are beginning to question these massive investments.

Is database marketing an illusion? Or is it the marketing of the future? Here's where it gets confusing. It's both.

There are two database marketings. The first Database Marketing (call it DBM1), is the lists plus data definition which the traditional advertisers have embraced. It does little to question the logical sounding, conventional wisdom of marketing:

(1) the right kind of awareness leads to positive attitudes;

(2) attitudes leads to behaviour;

(3) people do what they say they do.

However, there is a problem. None of these things are true. There is no convincing evidence that attitude leads to behaviour, or that recall leads to purchase. Look at IBM—they have 98% awareness and their sales are plunging.

The second approach to Database Marketing (DBM2) has rather different underlying tenets:

(1) past buyer behaviour is the best predictor of future buyer behaviour;

(2) one purchase is simply an event. To understand the true worth of your marketing programme you have to calculate buyer lifetime value;

(3) buyers are more important than non-buyers;

(4) certain buyers are more important than other buyers;

(5) buyers are likely to share certain characteristics;

(6) prospective buyers are likely to look like current buyers.

This second approach, DBM2, emerges from the behaviour predictors which the more sophisticated direct marketers have used for decades. The Recency/Frequency/Monetary value (R/F/M) formula is the most widely used. As a simple targeting philosophy, it has two major drawbacks: it cannot be used for non-customers and it advocates the targeting of top-ranking customers (i.e. those customers who buy everything offered).

DBM2 goes beyond the simple direct mail targeting approach and uses the R/F/M formula to identify those areas where marketing processes are weak and which need re-engineering. DBM2 is a radical philosophy. Its key concept, the 'Contact Strategy', is illustrated in Figure 1.

The idea here is that high-value customers should be treated differently – frequent, high-quality contacts and communications. Conversely, low-value customers only merit low-value contacts and communications. The basic idea is not altogether new. National account management, frequent flyer programmes, etc. have emerged in the past as examples of this approach. The important difference is that this approach unifies such tactical efforts, and provides the measurement framework that allows new processes to be designed with great precision.

In the past, approaches such as national account management ran into difficulties when the details of which accounts to allocate to which account manager had to be resolved. It was rare for there to be the facts and figures on which to base a precise decision, and consequently many marketing processes suffered from inefficiencies caused by lack of measurement.

DBM2 is an approach which can resolve these inefficiencies. It leads to the re-engineering of the marketing process. It is an approach which is immensely threatening to traditional marketers, who as a result have to change the ways they have been doing things for the last 50 years. It heralds in new techniques, such as telemarketing, which can improve the efficiency of traditional marketing processes. These re-engineering problems merit some careful attention.

## The last bastion of computing

In the rush to computerize in the 1970s and 1980s, somehow marketing got left behind. Rather late in the day, the computer department turned its attention to marketing. Systems analysts made brave forays into marketing departments asking, as analysts traditionally do ask: 'What function do you perform? . . . What are the marketing processes in your department?' After being greeted with blank looks they finally discovered a winning question: 'What data do you need?' This question unleashed a torrent of answers. So, the marketing database was born.

Unfortunately, other areas of the business than marketing have realized some time ago that accumulating a database, by itself, has no intrinsic benefit. Competitive advantage, customer service and productivity – all three visions of computerization – have created both successes and failures. But they are increasingly united by a common theme. To reap the benefits of IT, companies must not simply automate the status quo but instead must do new things in new ways. This means questioning the day-to-day traditions of decades of business. Take Ford as an example.

What seemed at first a mundane efficiency campaign became a challenge to Ford's long established ways of doing business. Ford employed about 500 people to order components, receive the parts and pay suppliers. Mazda, a Japanese car-maker did the same job with less than 100. An elaborate computer system was installed. This cut Ford's order-shufflers only to about 400. So Ford's people took a harder look at Mazda. They discovered fewer computers.

Mazda's secret was that it did not wait for invoices from its suppliers. When goods arrive at the loading dock, a warehouseman waves a bar-code reader over each box. That single action enters the parts into inventory, updates production schedules and sends electronic payments to the supplier. This seemed like magic to Ford's managers. They had viewed invoices as essential, like double-entry book-keeping. Once shown the trick, the possibilities for boosting efficiency by eliminating the complications of matching parts and invoices seems obvious.

## Emergencies create database marketers

Even the naive observer realizes that most business won't change unless something truly life-threatening occurs. Once the house is on fire, they'll leap (yes – even if they make American autos), but not before.

That's the reason why the most active database marketers to-date have been in industries with marketing emergencies: computers, financial services, airlines, tobacco manufacturers.

When you are leaping out of a burning building, you should always remember to look before you leap. Choosing to leap is hard; leaping to safety is ten times harder . . . especially when computers are involved.

## Computers introduce new ways of getting things wrong

Although computers have been associated with business change for decades, it is not a role for which they are completely suited. IT is expensive and complicated. Banks, for example, first organized their computer systems by account number because that is how manual ledgers are recorded. Now however, they want to reorganize their systems to work by customer names to provide more sophisticated services. One bank, which prefers to remain nameless, calculates that reorganizing all of its systems will take about five years and cost nearly £1 billion.

The Sloan School of Management at MIT, considers there are five stages to the exploitation of information technology. Each creates different opportunities.

## British Telecom covers up

My own definition of Database Marketing was born in 1986, when I was developing a Direct Marketing System for British Telecom.

The Direct Marketing manager wanted a database containing details of 20 million consumer and business customers to support his mailshots. Over 1 million enquiries per annum would be stored on the system. Sales leads would be identified and distributed to the field sales force. Feedback of information on what happened to the leads would be recorded. Overall, a closed-loop integrated marketing system (i.e., DBM2) was envisaged.

I was asked to plan, design, and build the system between August 1985 and February 1986. The project team worked round the clock, over the holidays, and delivered the system on-time and on-budget. Then the problems started.

The first sign that something was wrong was that we got no feedback from the field. Then we discovered that the sales leads were going straight in the dustbin. So we looked at the sales force. For small businesses, they were visiting customers on-average once every five years. They could not cope with sales leads. Something fundamental was wrong!

Eventually there was a cover-up. The users complained about the Direct Marketing System, and we were forced to 'lose' it. So it came to be renamed – the 'Database Marketing System'. In fact this was how the term 'Database Marketing' came to be coined. The proposition was that Database Marketing would solve the problems which were inherent in the earlier 'Direct Marketing' system.

Looking back at the problems today, with the help of the Sloan work, it is easy to see how things went wrong.

In 1985 BT had no concept of an integrated marketing process. Marketing was split in two. There was Headquarters marketing, who ran centralized advertising, sales promotion and centralized enquiries, and District marketing who ran local advertising, sales promotion and enquiries. Two computer systems were consequently built: CSS and DMS. CSS essentially automated the status quo at District level and DMS redesigned business processes at Headquarters.

The more that DMS measured the marketing processes, including some District processes, the more it became apparent that BT had to do more than automate the status quo.

Slowly but surely, management came to accept that business process redesign was needed. Telemarketing was introduced, at first centrally, then at District level. At first it supplemented the traditional sales team but, where it proved to be the more cost-effective contact strategy, it took over their jobs.

Today BT has fully accepted the need to redevelop its business processes. Project Sovereign undertook to streamline the business processes. Project BOAT went further and, through workflow computer technology, is redesigning the entire business network. BOAT encompasses customer databases, such as the original DMS, but now goes much further in automating routine tasks which make use of the database. The flexibility of the BOAT technology will enable BT to continue to modify and evolve its marketing processes, in a way that was impossible under the old CSS regime.

## What else goes wrong with database marketing?

It would be wrong to assume that the main problem in database marketing is the process and organization, and that technology is not a problem.

Most companies attempting to use database marketing for the first time have serious problems with the technology. Problems are especially acute for companies which try to build the systems themselves without outside help.

Why are there so many computer disasters? Suppliers often seen totally unreasonable. They are a common cause of unreasonable behaviour. Yet what is reasonable to expect? Who can help determine what is reasonable?

The list of ‘goofs’ is legion, with embarrassing contributions from hardware and software suppliers, advertising agencies, data processing bureaux and list brokers. Even if it seems that nothing that could possibly go wrong, some suppliers seem to go out of their way to invent a catastrophe of their own. Blame is often placed on the ‘unscrupulous salesman’ or the ‘incompetent analyst’. The result is often punishment of the innocent and promotion of the uninvolved.

## Who is to blame?

There was a prestige car manufacturer which employed a bureau to run its loyalty programme. They were disturbed to find that a successful second sale to a customer deleted details of the first sale, making it impossible to track the effectiveness of the programme. Worse still, they were all being sent letters as first-time purchasers. When the bureau laser-printed a six-figure volume of letters back to front, and automatically enclosed them in window envelopes, and mailed them, that was the last straw. They decided to move in-house.

General management were blinded by science when they first attempted to computerize. They did not understand ‘computerese’. They did not understand what the tools for containing and processing their information could do or how they did it. At first they chose a packaged software product for simplicity.

The system went down – they were paralysed. They had to buy in expensive maintenance. They then discovered that their packaged software program could not do what they wanyed, without expensive modification to meet the real needs of the business.

Finally they chose to custom build a new system. A feasibility study was started. They thought that their staff, coordinating with the DP staff, together with several contract programmers brought in by their advertising agency, would have sufficient understanding of their business and its information needs to construct the perfect system.

A recession set in. They had to change some aspects of the business to respond to the marketplace. This affected the viability of the custom system, which was being constructed on the premise of the pre-existing conditions. The system had to be altered in mid-construction.

The custom development became extremely expensive. The budget was overshot and the system was not even operational. Does this sound familiar?

Then a fault was discovered in a software program. It was discovered that every time a letter is printed, the records were corrupted. This did not appear until six months after the system began operation. The contract programmers' warranty only lasted three months from acceptance after testing. Economic loss was specifically excluded from their contract, as was the cost of manual entry to correct errors – not to mention the loss of goodwill with customers.

This example raises three important questions. Do you need help to avoid the risks? How do you assess the risks? Who can help you avoid them?

## Do you need help?

Sales and Marketing Directors and managers are often reluctant to seek assistance, especially in the computer field. This is because of three factors.

The first is credibility. A common fear is that seeking assistance might show up an inability to resolve the problem yourself. This fear should be weighed up against the risks of failure. Success requires paying attention to numerous technical details, and planning actions which have not previously been undertaken or are to be done in ways substantially different than traditional methods. Perhaps a little outside expertise is valuable?

Second there is the fear that the cost will be prohibitive. If, however, the work is divided into small discrete tasks, and advice is only sought where necessary, then you should be able to guarantee value for money.

Third, there is lack of knowledge of who can do what and who cannot do what. Broadly speaking, you require someone to help you arrive at the best systems solution to suit your needs and budgets. They should be able to define needs in English, not jargon, and without insulting your intelligence. Like a doctor, their job is not to ask the patient ‘what do you think is the matter with you?’, but to enquire about symptoms, make various checks, diagnose any problems, prescribe a solution, and stay with the patient until they are happy.

How do you assess the risks? This is one of the most important issues for sales and marketing managers to address, yet all too often they tend to ignore the risks. They often are in such a hurry to acquire systems, that they do so blindly and end up with costly equipment that actually hinders rather than helps.

Bureaux concentrate on building and maintaining databases. They typically offer low-cost solutions, and their quality is usually somewhat ‘cheap and cheerful’.

Usually they offer a formula solution, which they modify a little to meet individual client's requirements. They have usually done this hundreds of times. If what you require is their formula, then they are a cheap way of getting what you want.

Do not, however, expect them to help you much with Strategy and Planning, Cost-Benefit Analysis, Project Management. Where Functional and Technical Design are concerned, you can expect to get a formula. Bureaux rarely have any applications to offer, beyond simple direct mail. However, they can be a useful way of piloting a system, before committing major investment funds to its development.

## Packages pile up

There has been an explosion in the number and the quality of software packages and services available to support marketing and selling. Over 300 'off-the-shelf' marketing software packages are now available in the UK.

There are many variations between packages, both in the range of business needs addressed and in the method by which individual needs are satisfied. Package suppliers try to make their solution meet your needs by various means: letting users alter the packages to fit their individual needs by using 'set-up' utilities (normally the extent of such alterations is somewhat limited); offering a more expensive package 'tailoring' service; allowing clients' technical staff to alter the package.

Most packages aim to be flexible and easy to use by non-technical users. Often one package will not provide all the flexibility needed, and so a solution involving two or more packages (e.g., a marketing databases plus a spreadsheet) is the right answer. Sometimes information from other departments has to be merged with marketing data, and some packages offer the capability to merge such data directly, without needed to retype it. Sometimes, multiples locations have to be supported, and some packages offer answers to this requirement.

Packages should not be selected simply on the basis of suppliers' demonstrations. A systematic selection process is needed, to avoid the pitfalls. ## Dealers, agents and value-added resellers

Where you want a complete solution, involving several packages and also hardware, there are hundreds of small dealers, agents and 'value added resellers'. These companies mix-and-match software and hardware, and put together a system to meet users' requirements.

Like bureaux, do not expect them to help you much with Strategy and Planning, Cost-Benefit Analysis, User Requirements, and Functional Design. However, they will help with Technical Design, Installation and also Training and Maintenance. Do be careful, however, to ensure that you sign a clear Maintenance Contract, that offers what you want.

## Advertising agencies

Most advertising agencies will offer to help develop systems. My experience is that in 99% of such cases they subcontract the work. If they subcontract to a contract programmer (it happens frighteningly often) the consequences can be disastrous. Programmers are not normally skilled in Strategy or Planning, Change Impact Assessment, Cost-Benefit Analysis, User Requirements, Functional Design, Technical Design, Package Selection, Training, Maintenance or Project Management. Therefore, my general advice is do not use them.

## IT consultants

Today there are many large, high quality IT specialists operating world-wide. Their advice is sought by more than five out of ten British companies. Major firms include Andersen Consulting, CAP Gemini Sogeti, Hoskyns, and Logica. All claim to provide better value for money in developing systems than in-house people.

How do they do this? Basically through concentrating on what they are good at: i.e., by training their people in the latest and best techniques, and by developing tools of their trade (Andersen Consulting had an annual internal expenditure programme of US \$250 million in 1989).

How can they be used? They can often provide assistance with Strategy or Planning, Change Impact Assessment, Cost-Benefit Analysis, User Requirements, Functional Design, Technical Design, Package Selection, Training, Maintenance or Project Management. When systems are complex, they can provide particularly good support. They can provide some of the best project managers available and, sometimes, good analysts and programmers.

The only words of warning are that in practice they lack the marketing and sales skills; they often do a good deal of on-the-job learning (at the client's expense). Some may have a vested interest in recommending the biggest, most expensive, custom-built system they can persuade clients to accept, avoiding packaged solutions, and recommending that consulting staff continue throughout the project.

## Conclusion

Finally, once you've made the decision about Database Marketing, act on it. Avoid at all costs indecisive dabbling. Remember what happened to the Swiss watch-making industry. They spent years complacently looking at the digital watch from Japan, until it suddenly swept across the world watch markets. Today they have reaped the consequences of indecision and inaction and the 100 000 watch makers are now reduced to 10 000.
