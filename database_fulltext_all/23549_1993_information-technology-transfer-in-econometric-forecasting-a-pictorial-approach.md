# Information technology transfer in econometric forecasting: a pictorial approach

P. PETE CHONG, YE-SHO CHEN and JAMES M. PRUETT
Department of Quantitative Business Analysis, College of Business, Louisiana State University,
Baton Rouge, LA 70803, USA

Successful information technology transfer requires effective communication and clear, concise information exchange. This paper, using the Louisiana econometric model as a case study, proposes a pictorial approach to present and manage complex factors essential to information technology transfer. The approach utilizes multi-layer entity-relationship diagrams to provide a meaningful framework for the entire forecasting process, provide clarity to ensure better model maintenance when changes in social/economic structures require reformulations, and provide a procedural and data dictionary for clear documentation. The pictorial approach is both intuitive and readable, capable of serving as a task management tool, a model implementation aid, and a system maintenance resource.

## Introduction

Large businesses today are becoming information-based organizations. The development of information technology (IT) not only changes the way business is conducted but also changes the structure of the entire organization (Drucker, 1988). Technology transfer occurs when there is intercompany cooperation, a joint venture, an acquisition, or the purchase of technology (Moenaert et al., 1990). Thus, technology transfer requires effective communication and clear exchange of information among parties to ensure its success. Madu (1990) classified three types of problems associated with technology transfers in general when communication breaks down:

(1) Structural failures, e.g. cultural and political differences;

(2) Technological failures;

(3) Behavioural failures, such as workers' resistance to change.

IT transfer faces similar problems. Kwon and Zmud (1987) classified voluminous research on IT implementation into factors research (individual, organizational and technological variables), process research (social change activities), and political research (stakeholders as special interest group). However, due to the lack of a directing and organizing framework, IT implementation has been slow (Cooper and Zmud, 1990). Cooper and Zmud further listed four classes of infusion levels according to the required structural change within an organization.

Based on Lewin's (1952) change model, Zmud and Apple (1989) proposed a stage model of IT implementation activities consisting of five stages: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, and routinization. The presentation of each stage consists of a process and a product. For example, the process in the initiation stage concerns the scanning of organizational problems and opportunities, while the product is a task/technology match (Cooper and Zmud, 1990). Thus, both the output and the transformation function are identified in the model. Similarly, according to Galbraith (1990), many high-technology manufacturing operations can be described as a sequence (process) of modular core technologies (product) called technological bundles which in turn affect the flexibility to adjust quickly to changing demand and resource requirements.

A significant problem in the current process–product approach to IT transfer is the lack of powerful tools for communication between the IT developer and the IT user. As such, many IT transfers fail to achieve their original goals. A recent example is the IT transfer in econometric forecasting. In order to project the future performance of the economy more accurately, economists often develop econometric models based on historical data to depict the interactions among many economic indicators. On the national level, both the Wharton Econometric Forecasting Association (WEFA) and Data Resources Inc. (DRI) have developed highly respected models.

Prompted by the success of these econometric models, the US Department of Commerce, Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), in an effort to cut its expenses, decided to subscribe to the DRI model and eliminate its own national econometric model in 1990 (Young, 1990). Thus, econometric models developed in the private sector are now being used to shape the policy making of the federal government. Unfortunately, such practice of skimping on modelling and statistics in general eventually cost the government dearly. Not only did Young (1990) warn about incompatibilities among some of the time-series data published by the BEA, a recent report (Betts, 1991) by a National Research Council panel concluded that because the reliability of some federal data models was not adequately validated, major errors were produced. The report further blamed problems with the model on faulty data and inappropriate assumptions.

In this paper, we propose a pictorial approach to IT transfer in econometric forecasting. Specifically, we use the Louisiana Econometric Model (LEM) developed by faculty members at Louisiana State University and Southeastern Louisiana University as a case study to illustrate the application of this powerful communication tool between IT developers and users of IT products. As a prelude to examining the paper, Section 2 shows the current approach to econometric forecasting. The pictorial approach as a promising solution to the problems of the current approach is presented in Section 3. The advantages of using the pictorial approach are discussed in Section 4. Finally, in Section 5 we present the conclusion and possible future research opportunities.

## The makeup of an econometric forecasting model

## Requirements analysis

In addition to the previously mentioned national models, state governments have also developed econometric models to monitor their own economy. The work may be undertaken either by the executive branch of the legislative office, or by commissioning major universities in the state. It is not unusual for major banks in a state, in conjunction with other firms in the business community, to provide grants to state universities for the development of econometric models to produce annual or quarterly economic projections for the state, counties and Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSAs) (AUBER, 1988). These econometric models contain many variables and equations. For instance, the econometric model for Washoe County, Nevada alone consists of 32 exogenous variables, 64 endogenous variables, and 65 equations (Ghiglieri, 1990). On an even larger scale, the econometric model developed by the Division of Economic and Business Research at the University of Arizona contains well over 800 equations (Silvers, 1990).

Since the economic forecasts generated by these models are of intense interest to the diverse audiences in business communities, government agencies, and the general public, it is important that the communication methods related to the model are effective and efficient. The data used in econometric models, such as employment data, personal income and GNP figures must be collected from various sources. It must be consistent and comparable both cross-sectionally and longitudinally within the model. Moreover, some of the variables — such as oil prices, foreign exchange rates, and the level of federal spending — are exogenous to the model (or best guesses) and must be estimated before entering the model. Due to the complexity of these models, a method of managing and presenting these variables and their attributes is needed to ensure a smooth execution of the forecasting process. Over time, changes in social/economic structures require that model formulations be modified accordingly (Cargill and Raffiee, 1990); therefore, it is important that the method of management and presentation allows easy model maintenance. Furthermore, it is necessary for the methodology to be comprehensive as well as comprehensible so that economists can exchange ideas, critique the model properly, and recognize the underlying elements of the forecasted data so that only the valid forecasted results are included in their research.

## The present approach

The management of an econometric model includes two components: resource management and model elaboration. The resource management component concentrates on how to document, analyse and manage the time-series database. The model elaboration component establishes the mathematical relationships for the variables in equation form. In fact, an econometric model can be defined as ‘a consistent set of econometric equations and construction identities aiming at the modelization of an economic behavior’ (Collet-Petitjean et al., 1987).

The construction of a model consists of four steps: design, specification, adjustment, and application. Econometric models estimate values of dependent variables according to known data. In the case of the LEM, the model estimates future employment and personal income growth according to WEFA's projection of national and international factors.

Because of the scope of econometric models, problems arise in their presentation. The most widely used presentation approaches for such models have been narrative in nature. The models are usually presented by describing the most important components and variables, and the deterministic relationships among them. In the case of the LEM, for instance, the emphasis has traditionally been placed on oil prices and their effect on Louisiana's economy, although the collapse of oil prices in 1982 and 1986 has reduced the degree of importance of oil and gas industries on Louisiana's economy.

Due to the weakening of the US dollar in the currency market, Louisiana's chemical industry experienced some revival in the latter part of the 1980s. In addition, port activities in the New Orleans area increased during that period. Because of these changes in important relationships, the effect of foreign exchange rates is also included in the LEM (Scott et al., 1989). The lengthy description of the model is often accompanied by tables and graphs to enhance understanding.

Another approach used to describe econometric models is through mathematics. For example, the Washoe County Model (Ghiglieri, 1990) is augmented by 65 equations which detail the mathematical relations among variables, in addition to the usual narrative. These equations are intended to compensate for the lack of specificity in the narrative and represent an efficient communication method with experts; however, the formula approach is formidable to untrained audiences who, unfortunately, represent a major portion of the readership. Because of the voluminous, cryptic, and complex mathematics, this group is often unable to understand either the model or its underlying assumptions. This can, of course, lead to the misuse of the forecasting models.

Some modellers, e.g. Nevada Regional Model and Chapman Model, take still another approach to the presentation problem: they use diagrams to explain the relationships between different variables in their models (Schwer and Carroll, 1990; Chapman College, 1989). However, their graphic presentations usually are limited to an overview of the model and do not provide a clear description of the sequence of the forecasting process, i.e. some variables must be estimated before others. In addition, the details related to variable attributes are often left unstated in the diagrams.

## The pictorial approach to econometric forecasting

## The entity-relationship approach to database design

The concept of the pictorial approach evolves from Peter Chen's entity-relationship (ER) approach (1976)—a well-accepted methodology for database design (March, 1987). Chen's ER approach to database design contains two major steps:

(1) Systematic determination of the entities and relationships in the project and the construction of an enterprise ER diagram (ERD) that is intuitively understandable to management;

(2) The revision of this conceptual ERD to a form that can be conveniently converted to relational tables for database implementation by programmers (Chen, 1989).

In this approach, an entity is a functionally grouped part of an organization, while a relationship is the interaction among these parts. The relationship, on the other hand, has some cardinal values. For instance, a worker may participate in several projects, and a project may involve several workers; thus the relationship between the entities WORKER and PROJECT is many-to-many. The essence of the ER approach is to modulate the project functionally into separate tasks and then assign proper tools to serve their diverse needs. For instance, the designing of an enterprise ERD at the conceptual level does not require managers to learn computer programming, and programmers would not have to know the organizational structure of a company in order to convert the ERD to codes at the physical level (Chong et al., 1991).

## Evolvement of the pictorial approach from the ER approach

Similar to the ER approach to database design, the pictorial approach combines the use of diagrammatic and descriptive components. The diagrams are used to provide an overview of the model at the conceptual level, whereas the descriptions are used to show detailed properties of each variable and procedure along with their attributes at the physical level. Using a road map as an analogy, the diagrams are used as a visual aid to show the relative layout (relationship) of various geographical areas (entities), while the descriptions serve as a data dictionary and provide details about each geographic area (such as the name of the city, population figures, sizes, and the general climate) and travelling information, e.g. New Orleans is located 70 miles southeast of Baton Rouge.

More importantly, the corresponding relationship in the pictorial approach not only indicates the variables involved but also contains the ordinal value which carries the information of the sequential order of data transformation procedures. Thus, the two major components in an information system, i.e. the information and the procedure used to transform it, are both represented in the pictorial approach. In other words, in the mapping function of $f(x)$ , both $f(\cdot)$ and x are documented. A comparison of the ER and pictorial approaches is summarized in Table 1.

## Diagrammatic presentation of the LEM

The LEM is a predictive model built upon numerous iterative regression analyses of historical annual averages of US, Louisiana, and MSA economic data. The economic forecast is presented to the general public each year at the Annual Business Expo in Baton Rouge, the capital of Louisiana. The model utilizes WEFA forecasted national and international economic indicators (such as US sectorial non-agricultural employment, Gross National Product, interest rate, and foreign exchange rate) to predict Louisiana's annual employment and personal income levels. Because of the unpredictability of crude oil prices, they are estimated in ranges by model researchers to provide best-case and worst-case scenarios. The forecasted state employment and personal income figures are then used as input to a local employment model to forecast the employment and personal income levels of each of the eight MSAs in Louisiana.

## Descriptive presentation of the LEM

Once the ERD is constructed, the next step is to identify appropriate attributes and their values for each of the entities and relationships. Attribute-value tables are then constructed for entities (variables) and their relationships (mathematical formulae composed of variables). Table 2, the relationship table, lists the formulae represented by procedures 1 through 7 in Figure 2 and procedures a and b in Figure 3.

Once the LEM contains the actual coefficients for all the relationships (Table 2), hypothetical Assistant A can simply enter the projected value of US manufacturing employment and the foreign exchange rate obtained from WEFA to estimate Louisiana's manufacturing employment level. Table 3 provides a full description of each entity, lists the sources for each variable, and includes remarks which further clarify the assumptions and realities of the model. Thus, the task for Assistant A is clearly laid out; he can follow Table 3 to find the proper data from the right source and enter the information into the formulae listed in Table 2. On the other hand, Table 3 allows researchers to examine the assumptions made in the model and to make adjustments when unexpected events, such as the sharp oil price increase brought about by the Iraqi invasion of Kuwait, do occur. In short, the relationship (procedure) table follows the sequence of model solving specified in the ERD. The mathematical relationships, which have been estimated and tested by economists through econometric analysis, are then shown in equation form and listed in the order they are processed. All essential characteristics of variables and formulae are documented in this dictionary.

## The advantages of the pictorial approach

## Readability: a communication tool

The pictorial approach serves as a communication tool that allows the transfer of technical knowledge to the general public. The multi-level diagrams are intuitive and easy to follow, the relational table provides the details needed for closer model inspection, and the entity table lists sources and model assumptions. Since the procedural sequences and variable properties are clearly identified and documented, model understanding is greatly enhanced.

## A task management tool

The multi-layer ERDs provide the model manager with a complete view of the model, a global view at the upper level and more detailed views at the lower levels. The manager can therefore efficiently delegate assignments according to their functional relationship to individual researchers with minimal processing anomalies (Blanning, 1989). In other words, the manager can separate groups of entities according to their position geographically or relationally on the ERD and assign them to different task forces. The data needed for each task force then is limited to that functional group of entities only, thus eliminating the need to provide all of the data to all task forces.

## Identification of variable weight

Audiences can detect the importance of an economic indicator simply by checking the number of relationships that a particular variable has; that is, by counting the number of lines coming out of an entity. For instance, in Figure 2 it is clear that there are more relationships involving oil prices than any other indicators, thus signifying the heavy dependence of Louisiana's economy on the price of crude oil. ## A system maintenance guide

When model reassessment is needed, the ERD also helps modellers to see clearly all the relationships that require maintenance. For instance, the importance of oil and gas to Louisiana's economy has been declining since 1982 (Brar et al., 1989). As a result, Louisiana's economy is tied to the national economy more closely than it was before (Brar and Chong, 1989). The modeller can then go back to the ERD and trace all relationships involving GNP or oil prices and modify the corresponding mathematical formulae where needed. Furthermore, if particular groups of formulae no longer produce satisfactory results, checking the ERD for all inter-related variables may lead to the identification of a cause that can be traced and remedied. For instance, if the late 1980s' rapid increase in the business service sector's employment is indeed caused by the reclassification of previous manufacturing employment (such as from manufacturer's outsourcing), perhaps the portion of the model involving service employment should be reworked. The extent of the 'damage' then can be evaluated by examining the ERD.

## Systematic documentation

Documentation is a very important part of software engineering; it is equally so in model engineering and management. Documentation can be used both as a user's manual and as a guide for troubleshooting. The importance of documentation in the area of economic analysis was recently underscored by the BEA director's calling the improvement of research methodology documentation in his department a major achievement in recent year (Young, 1990). Since variable attributes such as data sources and model assumptions, and the mathematical formulae of the model are clearly shown in the pictorial approach, models can be tested, replicated, and critiqued without ambiguity.

## Conclusion and future research

'Econometric models' is a term often shrouded with mystery and treated with distanced fear. It need not be so. As Gray and Nunamaker (1989) stated, 'The best GDSS (group decision support system) software helps rather than frustrates individual users'. This should be true also in the area of econometric model presentation, management and documentation. The pictorial approach described in this paper considers the specific need of each level of model management and provides proper tools to each of them. It utilizes ERDs as intuitive maps for model presentation, ordinal-valued relationships as guides of model management, and a detailed data dictionary as the central base of reference and documentation. In fact, one fear of an understandable model presentation is loss of respect for a model. That is, researchers know that models that are not easily understood have a certain mystery, while a more understandable presentation may somehow trivialize the painstaking process of model development. It is also important to point out that the model presentation and management is developed top-down. However, the development of the model itself uses a de facto middleout approach, since individual mathematical formulae that link variables in various relationships are usually developed in random sequence.

The application of the pictorial approach can be extended to other models used in other disciplines as well. For instance, the pictorial approach is an excellent tool for modelling case studies, especially in the field of finance where the complexity of the case and the numerous distracting variables seem always to present a major obstacle to understanding. By showing graphically the variable relationships and the sequence required to (with formulae presented in the relationship table) produce the end result, the entire process becomes intuitively clear (Figure 4).

Similarly, the technique can be applied to teaching various grammatical rules, such as how to hyphenate a word, by presenting students with a graph with the flow of reasoning (instead of data) process. Most importantly, the pictorial approach provides a systematic way to document a model's methodology so that other researchers can replicate the process or further their research without misunderstanding. Several software packages for model management, such as iTHINK, have the capability of zooming in for detailed data or formula description and setup, and zooming out for global presentation and model solving.

As mentioned earlier in the introduction section, Cooper and Zmud (1990) suggested that in the initiation stage of the IT transfer process, the product is a task/technology match. For solving similar problems in matching entrepreneurs and venture capitalists, Chong et al. (1992a) proposed a solution based on the ER approach to identify entities (product) and relationships (process) among selection variables.

Taking the principle of the ER approach one step further in DSS modelling and integration, Chong et al. (1992b) used the pictorial approach to design a DSS/Expert system for labour market analysis. The logic of this extension can be illustrated via the analogy of the application of mathematical operators (+, -, etc). The functions of these operators are either similar or identical in arithmetic, algebra, and calculus; the difference is that the operatees are constants in arithmetic, e.g. $2 + 3$ , variables in algebra, e.g. $2X + 3Y$ , and functions in calculus, e.g. $f(X) + g(X)$ . From the ER approach perspective, the operators represent relationships while the operatees represent entities — be they constants, variables or functions. Therefore, with some modifications the same scheme can be used to represent different levels of operations management from strategic planning to operational planning.

The major problem with the pictorial approach is the process of drawing a complete diagram when there are 800 equations! Either the paper has to be very large or the resolution has to be very good. Fortunately, the natural solution is to follow the concept used in developing data flow diagrams and increase the number of layers of ERDs. Unfortunately, questions still remain. Should the limit be 50 equations per diagram or 200? On this matter the pictorial approach may benefit from psychological experiments that focus on the complexity of each level of ERD. For instance, short-term memory (STM) studies have determined that the typical maximum retention rate for humans is seven or eight digits; however, people can still store longer phone numbers in STM through a process called ‘chunking’, i.e. grouping area code to be one item, local code to be one item, etc. Thus, instead of remembering 1-800-555-1212 as a long string of unrelated digits, a person can store the information as ‘1800’, ‘555’, and ‘1212’ in the STM without exceeding the limit (Anderson, 1980). Cognitive experiments may be devised to determine the optimal size of each layer so as not to overburden the reader or increase unnecessarily the complexity of the model.
