# Stages of growth in end-user computing: applications in the health sector of developing countries in Asia-Pacific

ROHAN JAYASURIYA

Department of Public Health and Nutrition, University of Wollongong, Northfields Avenue, Wollongong, NSW 2522, Australia

Information technology is being adopted in the health sector of developing countries at a rapid rate to enhance management of health services. Lessons from the experiences of the business sector of developed countries are useful if adapted to the situation. End-User Computing (EUC) has been recognized to have potentially the greatest impact on productivity and on the efficient use of resources in developed countries. The experience of developing countries in introducing IT is reviewed. Based on the stage hypothesis model of growth in computing, a framework to analyse and predict EUC growth in the health sector of developing countries is described. Using the structure, people and technology parameters of the framework, benchmarks applicable to the health sector in developing countries for each stage are applied to case studies from the Asia-Pacific region. The utility of the framework in identifying key issues that need to be addressed in planning information systems in developing countries is presented.

## Introduction

Information technology has over the last two decades permeated into the health sector in developing countries at a rapid rate. In the early years, the health sector of many of these countries was introduced to the technology through donor assistance. The dramatic decrease in costs and the ‘micro-computer’ revolution has made this technology within the reach of even the poorest country in the 1990s.

Many of the ‘middle income’ countries introduced computerization to the health sector in the early 1970s and have gone through the same learning process as the developed countries. These countries are now grappling with the problems of integrating and coordinating the developments that are now being introduced with newer technology such as networking, CASE tools etc. The poorer countries face different challenges, having the technology introduced by donors and expatriates and having insufficient local expertise and resources to maintain the systems.

While recognizing the contribution IT has had on the advances towards clinical decision making (i.e. CAT scan, MRI etc.) this paper focuses on the major productivity gains that the health sector in developing countries could make in using IT to improve managerial decision making which is crucial in countries with limited resources. End-user computing (EUC), which is based on micro-computers, allows users to gain independence from systems dependent on computer professionals and has been defined to include information centres, personal computing and part of 'office automation' (Lockett,

1988). EUC, which enables users to develop their own applications, has been said to have potentially the greatest impact of any development of computing (Couger, 1986). It is recognized that most of the gains in productivity and efficient use of resources in IS developments would be made in the end-user environment as witnessed by its impact in developed countries (Rockart and Flannery, 1983; Gerrity and Rockart, 1986).

Qualitative methods including the use of case studies have often been argued to be more appropriate to understand important research problems of information management (Craig Smith, 1990). There is also evidence that purely quantitative methods of research lose important ‘qualitative’ aspects of decision making (Caplan and Duchon, 1988). This paper therefore utilizes evidence gathered through a survey and case studies (World Health Organization, 1990) and through consulting experience of the author.

The Western Pacific Region of the World Health Organization (WHO) comprises a very diverse group of countries which includes developing countries of East-Asia and the pacific region. In size it varies from the country with the largest population in the world to tiny islands with less than 50 000 inhabitants. Many of these countries are seeking guidelines and strategies to develop effective IS for the health sector (WHO, 1990). Based on evidence drawn from the literature of experiences in developed countries a framework for analysing the growth of EUC in the health sector of developing countries has been proposed (Jayasuriya, 1992). Case studies from the Western Pacific Region are used to illustrate its utility in identifying issues in IS development in the health sector and critical issues that need to be addressed for continued development of EUC are discussed.

## Information technology in developing countries

Over the last two decades IT has been introduced to developing countries initially for processing large volumes of data such as financial statements, census data, compiling statistics (routine statistical processing) or large scale clerical work (Kaul, 1987). Bhatnagar (1990) identified the low usage of computers in developing countries to be due to factors such as low computer literacy of management and technical levels, lack of competitiveness, poor communication infrastructure and the high tax on importation of hardware and software relative to labour costs. Other disadvantages that these countries face in addition to English not being the main language are the constraints in relation to an acute shortage of computer professionals due to scarcity of training and the brain-drain of computer professionals to developed countries (Gupta, 1988).

The developing countries that ventured into computerization in the 1970s (based on mainframe machines) have faced many problems that would not now be faced in using the portable, robust microcomputer based systems that would be better suited for countries without a well developed IT infrastructure (Walsham, 1989). On the other hand, others have highlighted the problems in implementation due to information analysts and computer specialists who find it difficult to understand the social context and the organizational culture within which the technology is implemented (Sanwal, 1989). Schware and Trembour (1985) describe a case where the need for adaptation of a standard training plan was not foreseen which resulted in delay in the implementation and leaving behind inadequately trained personnel at the end of the project. In introducing a computerized IS in Haiti, socio-organizational issues such as frequent changes of staff, fear of losing a job and laziness to learn new tasks led to resistance of staff to change to a computerized system (Auxila and Rohde, 1988). The introduction of a computerized personnel management system in Uganda faced problems due to insufficient analysis, poor training and political pressure to introduce a system before the organization was ready (Nabbarro et al., 1988). In India, efforts to integrate IS among organizations revealed differences in the formats and codes in the existing databases (Sanwal, 1989). In fact, the introduction of a computerized IS in Egypt had to be delayed as it was identified that the data systems were incomplete and inaccurate (Schware and Trembour, 1985). Though developing countries can borrow rather than invent the technology (e.g. hardware), some aspects must be adapted to suit the environment, especially the approach to design, selection of priority applications and training methods (Bhatnagar, 1990). The importance of factors related to the specific organization was revealed in a case study where the immediate organizational issues of job security, managerial power and work efficiency in two subsidiaries of a multinational corporation located in different countries were more influential in determining the differences seen in implementation of a computer system than the differences in national culture and politics (Robey et al., 1990).

One important distinction in the development of IT in developing countries in contrast to developed countries, is the critical role played by governments as against the major role of the private sector in developed countries. In the case of the smaller and poorer developing countries the role of the government is even more pervasive as they are the biggest employers and appropriate investments could only be made by them (Bhatnagar, 1990). More interesting is the fact that many of these systems are now end user driven and seem to have an immediate impact on the productivity in terms of local or decentralized development (Kaul et al., 1989). New local applications at district level have aided project management not only by faster processing of data but by improving the quality of project monitoring and optimizing the use of resources locally. By placing microcomputers at the point where data is generated as well as where it can be utilized it is possible to develop local applications with higher impact (Kaul et al., 1989).

The trend towards the use of microcomputers for a multiplicity of purposes in developing countries is likely to continue and governments need to formulate policies and programmes to support the introduction and use of appropriate applications. These applications, in the context of large labour surpluses are likely to be more oriented to improving higher level activities in planning and management rather than labour saving applications.

As in the case of developed countries, the health sector of developing countries has lagged behind in the introduction of IT for management. Though the application of IT for clinical decision making is received well, especially by clinicians who have returned from training in developed countries, health professionals are generally wary of new technology that has not been part of their basic training. Most managers of health services and hospitals in developing countries are medical graduates, whereas in developed countries many are non-medical personnel trained in management. Very few medical administrators in developing countries have had formal training in the use of computer technology for their work in contrast to managers in business. Another important difference is the complexity and multitudes of 'products and services' that are provided by the health system. In developed countries in recent times computerized recording systems are in use that enable management IS to be developed that can cope with the complex classifications of these products and services. However very few developing countries have yet introduced these systems, partly due to the poor primary record keeping and storage capacity at institutional and field service levels. Early developments in computerization in the health sector had been of large mainframe computers mainly in the Central Health administrative offices for analysis and these are now being replaced with powerful microcomputers (Mandil et al., 1988). The availability of microcomputers has led to many decentralized applications being developed in developing countries for Primary Health Care programs (Wilson et al., 1988). However there is very little guidance for managers and decision makers in developing countries to identify the state of development in computer systems and to prepare plans for continued growth of the systems.

## Models of growth for end-user computing

Modelling the stages of growth of computer usage in business organizations has enabled a better understanding of IS. Nolan (1973) proposed his first model when he identified four stages: initiation, contagion, control and integration. Later he used this as a prescriptive tool for managers to deal with computer growth (Gibson and Nolan, 1974). Subsequent change in technology and understanding of information as a resource influenced his 1979 version when he added two other stages: data administration and maturity to expand the model to six stages (Nolan, 1979).

While Nolan's models have influenced much of the thinking in modelling IS development, researchers have since questioned the empirical validity and plausibility of the model (Benbasat et al., 1984; King and Kraemer, 1984). Critics have questioned the empirical foundations of the model, such as the use of change in budget as a single surrogate measure of different variables. A similar stage model for IS in human services was proposed by Schoech and Schkade (1980) where they identified six stages: initiation, extension, modification, integration, data administration and maturity. They identified the extension stage as an expansion of automation into other operational functions with emphasis on user-oriented applications. This in fact coincides with the end-user focus of application generation in an organization. The next paper that has significant influence on this review is the proposal by Huff et al. (1988) to provide an understanding of the growth of EUC and a framework for its management. The authors based their proposal on the supposition that the same set of general conditions that prevailed in the 1970s regarding computing growth prevails today in regard to growth of EUC. However they consider the model to be descriptive and therefore serves as a useful model to observe and analyse EUC development.

Huff et al. (1988) state that maturity conceptually refers to application maturity (i.e. the extent of development of applied end user skills) and proposed that operationally it can be measured in terms of interconnectedness of the application with other components of its surrounding user computing environment. Based on this measure of application maturity they identified five stages: isolation, stand alone, manual integration, automatic integration and distributed integration. The final stage of the model (in the context of the business environment in developed countries) is reached when EUC in an organization uses applications on data distributed throughout the organization after a rational examination of the fit between the business strategic plan and IS strategy.

## Framework for end-user computing

The differences in culture in public organizations and the extent of centralization of management exercised in government organizations of developing countries has influenced the development of this framework for EUC. The basic premise that guide the growth stages is based on Huff et al.'s (1988) level of 'interconnectedness' of information in the functions of the organization as a whole. It was agreed that this model should be dynamic to incorporate future changes in technology and management practice and the stage of 'maturity' was defined to be reached when there is a close match between EUC strategy and organizational strategy and information is utilized as any other resource in achieving organizational objectives (Jayasuriya, 1992).

An ‘initiation’ stage as identified by Schoech and Schkade (1980) is not particularly relevant to developing countries entering computerization at a stage where user-oriented tools for application development are available. They would also leap over the ‘isolation’ stage used by Huff et al. (1988) and enter the ‘standalone’ stage. The term ‘isolation’ however is retained for the first stage to emphasize that these applications are introduced in different parts of the organization, usually by professional enthusiasts (e.g. epidemiologists, accountants etc.) working in isolation without any coordination and control by the organization (Jayasuriya, 1992).

The next stage of development occurs when applications need to be shared by other units, unlike in a private enterprise where purchase decisions can be made by divisions, formal requests need to be made to higher echelons. Public organizations (especially in developing countries) first respond to these requests by forming a committee process for decision making especially when ‘new technology’ is involved. This stage has been called appropriately ‘organizational integration’ to emphasize the arguments made by Walsham et al. (1990) to look at social and organizational context of IT development. The ‘manual integration’ stage of Huff et al.’s model occurs partly during this stage as there is integration between units for data and application transfer (e.g. basic hospital statistics are provided for planning and finance) but as distinct from the next stage the use of technology is minimal as only manual- or diskette-based interchange of data occurs.

The next stage is called ‘technological integration’ and is identified from the aforementioned stage to be an important advance in the context of EUC development in developing countries. Two technologies, database technology and networking, need to be established for organizations to reach this stage of development (Jayasuriya, 1992).

The last stage is called ‘strategic integration’ to encompass the close integration of the data resource to strategic management in the organization. Many developing countries are still being exposed to concepts of strategic management and it is postulated that EUC developments in this area (i.e. involving expert systems, executive information systems (EIS) and group decision support systems (GDSS)) will occur only after top management in the organizations have embarked on this process. ## Organizational structure

The parameter of organizational structure plays an important role in the development of EUC. Historically, countries (and MOHs) that ventured into computerization of the health sector earlier are seen to have more formal structures for IS in place. The other important variable to affect the IS structure is the centralization/decentralization strategy of the organization. Table 2 shows that in the early stages of development, though some structures are in place (as in the case of PNG), other aspects such as policies and standards are not set as the organization is still in the early stages of development. Singapore and Malaysia entered into computerization in the 1970s which would explain the advancements in organization for IT. The other factors that influence the formalization of organizational structures for IS are the size of the organization and the magnitude of investment in terms of money and workforce. Obviously, controls and organizational structures need to be placed in larger organizations and where the cost of investments are higher. Singapore spends about US\$ 2.5 million per annum while PNGs estimates are in the region of US\$ 200 000. Though the expenditure of Singapore is mainly on mainframe and minicomputers (that are mostly used for clinical information systems), it forces the establishment of organizational structures and controls that would also encompass microcomputers. Another factor that would influence setting of policy and standards in the health sector are the existence of national computer policies and standards (as in the case of Singapore).

An important factor that determines the growth of EUC is the political ‘power’ of the interest groups. When the EUC applications are introduced to an organization in politically important areas the resources are made available for further growth. This is illustrated by the case of PNG where priority was given by the country (and therefore donor assistance) to the implementation of decentralized management at provincial level. Therefore the Management Support for Provinces (MSP) unit was able to command resources and priority over the other units to further develop the EUC applications initiated by the unit. In the case of the other two countries the hospital information systems are politically more powerful and the growth of EUC is given relatively less importance. A proxy measure of the power relationship relates to the level in the hierarchy of the ministry of the initiator of EUC. In the early stages, each EUC user depends on their professional ‘power’. A person is identified to be the chief of EUC only later in the process. With the stages of growth the position of the chief of IT also rises in the hierarchy and the ‘power’ of the head of EUC is derived from his or her position.

## Technology

As presented earlier, the stages of EUC development are influenced to a great extent by the technology. Technology such as database and networking, that allows integration of the systems, plays an important role in EUC growth. The advantage ‘newcomers’ have is that they do not go through a long learning period in incorporating new technology into the organization. The dramatic reductions in price of technology have also removed some of the barriers for the poorer countries and opened alternate strategies for EUC development. Table 3 illustrates the cases of the three countries on this parameter in the framework.

The case studies highlight the technological advances that need to be adopted in the growth of EUC. The country's infrastructure in telecommunications play an important role in this. In Malaysia, although the infrastructure is available the health sector has not yet made the investments to fully utilize the technology while in the case of PNG the strategy to decentralize has precipitated the need to use appropriate technology in developing IS. The case of Singapore illustrates the appropriate harnessing of the advanced technology available in the country for the health sector.

## Workforce (people)

It is acknowledged that EUC was a response to the problems organizations had with the traditional IS development that were dependent on the computer professionals and the inherent backlog of application development. It is also known that major productivity gains are made where the tasks of the end user could be enhanced by computerization. Table 4 shows the analysis of the people parameter under the framework for the three country cases.

The factors that underlie the people parameters for EUC are the level of computer literacy in the population and, specifically, the computer awareness of management in the health sector. Singapore has a very high skill level in computer technology and a large computer literate workforce to draw on. In the case of PNG the lack of computer personnel and the dependence on expatriate expertise is a major handicap to EUC growth. On the other hand in the case of Malaysia, expertise is available although other constraints (such as funding) have delayed the growth of EUC.

In assessing the people parameter for EUC, growth consideration needs to be given to socio-organizational factors that impinge on the behaviour and attitudes of people towards IT. Computer anxiety has been identified as an important factor that determines acceptance of IT in an organization (Torkzadeh and Angulo, 1992). Sociocultural factors also play a role. In industrialized societies people accept change brought about through new technology faster. Singapore is a case in point, as in the last decade the economy has transformed towards that of an industrialized country. In Malaysia, on the other hand, change is not well tolerated by those who have come through a typical bureaucratic administration and paper based IS. In this context, PNG is a unique case where there is evidence of rapid diffusion of technology within the last decade with health personnel in the provinces adapting to computer technology without any resistance. These variations make it difficult to provide a valid benchmark for socio-cultural factors that could be generalized to all countries. A fair indication of the cultural acceptance of IT is inbuilt in the bench-mark 'level of users of IT' that relates to the people parameter of the framework.

## Conclusions

Gupta (1988) identified the main difficulties developing countries face in using IT effectively to be due to

(1) weakness in policy and planning for IT (2) short supply and lack of experience of computer personnel

(3) difficulties in identifying appropriate applications (4) lack of infrastructure (reliable electric power and telecommunication facilities).

These same factors can be identified to be retarding the growth of EUC and the framework could be used to identify issues that are not allowing the organization to progress to the next stage.

The progress that Singapore has made in EUC could be attributed to the equally strong developments in the organizational structure, people and the technology parameters. The coordination of computer policies and standards by a strong national body provides the impetus for the health sector to develop its policies and standards.

The lack of qualified personnel was identified as the main shortcoming for IS developments in most of the countries of the Western Pacific Region of the WHO (1990). A major issue developing countries face is their inability to attract and retain experienced computer personnel in the government sector. Singapore has overcome this problem by employing them through the National Computer Board with a different pay structure from other government departments (Kaul, 1987). Countries like PNG that have very few skilled staff need to train end users and develop strategies that are less dependant on the need for expatriates. It is also observed that countries need a critical mass of these professionals that could provide the backstopping for maintenance and developments in EUC to take place. If overt dependence is placed on 'grey analysts' (i.e. someone who has little or no formal information systems training but develops systems for others) there is a potential danger of inadequate validation and testing of the system and maintenance becoming a major long-term liability if the person leaves the organization (Lockett, 1988). The cases of PNG and Malaysia illustrate that developing countries have been able to identify appropriate priorities for IS development. In both cases the IS developments have been in keeping with national health strategy of decentralization and priority given to the delivery of primary health care. However, the relevant issue is whether sufficient resources are available for adequate development of the IS to support the strategy. Though hard data are not available it is generally found that investments and recurrent funds for IS are generally inadequate. In the case of PNG any new developments are dependant on donor assistance. A similar situation exists in the Philippines too, where although the Health Management Information System for Primary Health Care has been identified as the priority area for EUC development, the project is dependent on external aid (WHO, 1990).

The crucial role IT plays in the growth of EUC is illustrated in the case of PNG and Malaysia. Both need to incorporate networking technology and electronic data transfer (through modems) to progress to the next stage. In the case of PNG the telecommunication infrastructure is not yet developed to enable this development though LAN technology is feasible within the MOH.

PNG illustrates a case of rapid development of EUC within a short period despite constraints in personnel and technology. The framework allows the prediction of limitations in future growth due to constraints in technology infrastructure and IT personnel. It is also important to link the IT strategy to the management strategy of the organization. This is illustrated by the case of PNG where the reason underpinning the acceptance of EUC by managers in the provinces was the link made to introduce IS as an important component of management development (Newbrander and Thomason, 1988).

The speed of growth in EUC is also modified based on socio-organizational factors that are specific to the organization (Robey et al., 1990). While national, cultural and social influences play a part, the factors that immediately impact on the organizations (in this case MOHs) are specific issues such as political power of interest groups, job security and computer anxiety. The example of PNG illustrates that in comparison to Malaysia all three factors were favourable, and therefore there was less resistance for the introduction of EUC at the provincial level. The complex nature of interactions among socio-organizational factors make it difficult to set generalizable benchmarks. Further indepth research into factors that facilitate and retard IT acceptance in the health sector of developing countries is indicated.

Growth models for computing have been of use to managers and decision makers to appreciate progress made, assess current status of the environment and to obtain insight into further developments (Huff et al., 1988). The above illustrates the utility of the proposed framework in identifying key issues that MOHs need to address in planning for development of EUC within a National Health Information System. The framework also provides useful parameters (structure, people and technology) on which strengths and weakness of the organization can be assessed as a first step in formulating strategic plans for future developments in EUC and health IS in general. Huff et al. (1988) state that an optimal EUC strategy does not exist and that it should evolve and change in relation to the growth stages. The framework provides a tool to assess whether different components of EUC development are proceeding as required for each stage which would enable managers to take decisions for rapid development or controlled development of each component. This framework is useful to compare EUC development among countries or regions within a country and is also useful for program evaluation.

## Biographical notes

Rohan Jayasuriya graduated from the Medical Faculty of the University of Colombo, Sri Lanka in 1974. Having completed postgraduate studies in Community Medicine (MD) in Colombo and Public Health (MPH) at Johns Hopkins, USA, he worked in the Health Planning Division of the Ministry of Health from 1980–1988.

He was the national project coordinator for the National Health Information Systems Project since its inception in 1982 until 1988. From 1988–1989, he was at the University of Papua New Guinea and is currently at the University of Wollongong where he teaches health information systems and health planning.

He has worked for the WHO as a short-term consultant and temporary adviser in health information for the South-East Asia and Western Pacific Regions. His current research interests are in the impact of information technology in health services and primary health care information systems.
