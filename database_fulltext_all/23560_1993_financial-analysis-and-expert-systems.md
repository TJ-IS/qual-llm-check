# Financial analysis and expert systems

A. BIRTS and L. McAULAY

School of Management, University of Bath, Claverton Down, Bath BA2 7AY, UK

M. KING

Loughborough University Business School, University of Loughborough, Loughborough, Leicestershire LE11 3TU, UK

Financial analysis expert systems have been developed but there is little evidence to suggest that such expert systems are as widely used in the financial analyst community as other information technology products. The research findings presented here suggest that one explanation for this non-use of expert systems may be that users do not have a need to use the existing technology. We arrived at this conclusion by researching the rich picture of the task and the needs of a small number of financial analysts. In so doing, we were able to develop insights into the topic area which to our knowledge are new. Our analysis may confirm a need to re-orientate expert systems development. Thus, the selection and use of research methods which are geared to shedding light on users' needs may be valuable to practitioners, in developing product development strategies, and to researchers, in casting new light on their topic areas.

## Introduction

This paper is concerned with the ways in which financial analysts use knowledge in their everyday working lives. The investigation, which was carried out in the Summer of 1992, suggests reasons for the relative non-adoption of expert systems by the financial sector in the UK. In particular, it highlights the need to put user's perceptions of their task and of IT at the heart of expert systems development.

Interest in expert systems for financial analysis has a body of literature extending back more than ten years (e.g. Bouwman, 1983; Elliott and Kielich, 1985; Mui and McCarthy, 1987; Sena and Smith, 1987; Blocher and Scalf, 1988; Holsapple et al., 1988; Kerschberg and Dickinson, 1988; Klein, 1988; Narasimhamurthi and Dechen, 1988; Lee et al., 1989; Blocher, 1990; Hartvigsen, 1990). There are examples of commercially available systems (e.g. The Athena Group, 1987; Hartvigsen, 1992) for which some success has been claimed. However, the evidence of adoption of expert systems by finance and accounting practitioners in general is disappointing (Edwards and Connell, 1989; Wilson and Sangster, 1992).

The potential contribution of expert systems to financial analysis has mainly been related to the interpretation of ratio analysis. Ratio analysis (Lev, 1974; Foster, 1978; McMullen, 1979; Bernstein, 1989) is the calculation of measures of performance based on figures provided by financial reports, predominantly the profit and loss and balance sheet statements. Given a set of calculations of ratios based on figures presented within financial statements, an expert system makes an appraisal of the financial health of the company.

However, the notion that the central expertise of Financial Analysts comprises an interpretation of a series of ratios may be naive. Hartvigsen (1992, p. 20) points out that the evaluation of financial statements involves two approaches:

(1) ratio analysis;

(2) the evaluation of marketing issues and organizational management capability by means of qualitative approaches.

Hartvigsen (1992) suggests that expert systems should have a role to play in the second qualitative area. There is therefore some confusion over the potential role of expert systems; is it to perform an evaluation given a series of quantitatively based ratios, is it to provide an assessment of the qualitative aspects of the analysis or is it to carry out a combination of these two aspects of the problem?

The main body of this paper is devoted to reporting an investigation designed to discover the tasks in financial analysis for which expert analysts would welcome expert system support. The research methodology is described and then the research findings are reported at length because of the unexpected results. The implications for expert systems development are considered in the final discussion and conclusions section.

## Research methodology

The research methodology initially followed the work of

Bouwman (Bouwman, 1978, 1983, 1984, 1985; Bouwman et al., 1987). Data was captured by tape recording financial analyst experts as they thought through a set of data for a company with which they were largely unfamiliar. The experts were asked to express their opinion on whether they would take an interest in the company. This would imply that they might at some time in the future advise clients to buy shares in the particular company. Six experts were chosen as research subjects in consultation with a further expert who remained independent of the substantive task and was purely an adviser.

The choice of the number of subjects was influenced by the nature of the research and its resource implications. In similar research, Johnson et al. (1989) reported on the expertise of auditors once the tape transcripts for two subjects had been analysed, Bouwman (1984) used eight subjects, of whom five were students and three were qualified accountants, and Bouwman et al. (1987) used 12 subjects, all of whom were considered to be experts in their field. The danger that the reliability of the present findings might be limited by using only six subjects was realised at the beginning of the research and provision for extending the number of subjects beyond six was created. However, we found that six subjects provided adequate data in the sense that each additional subject beyond the third subject added less and less to our joint understandings of the phenomena under investigation and after six sessions conclusions had emerged with sufficient clarity for us to be assured of their validity, within the limitations of the research method. The deterrent to using larger numbers of subjects is the time necessary to make sense of the rich picture which emerges from the data when they are analysed in detail. Easterby-Smith et al. (1991, pp. 92–3) suggest this as one of the drawbacks of this kind of research. Other drawbacks can occur when unconscious, semi-conscious or non-verbal forms of reasoning are significant and when the relationship between the subject and the interviewer might affect the quality of the data (Easterby-Smith et al., 1991, pp. 92–3). The choice of individual subjects reflected a desire to produce results which could carry conviction and so it was felt to be desirable to use experts who were respected in their own community. All six subjects were respected by our adviser and were employed by respected organizations.

A second independent adviser suggested a particular company which should have been unfamiliar to the subjects and for which financial data could be assembled and presented in an undisguised form. The actual data we provided comprised the financial statements for the company for the five most recent years, data published by Extel (1992), press clippings from McCarthy Information Ltd and the latest set of interim results as reported in the Financial Times. Our two independent advisers confirmed that this data set closely matched the data set provided by Bouwman to his experts in the USA. Bouwman et al. (1987) indicate that this data set appeared to be adequate for the needs of their subjects.

Bouwman et al.'s (1987) analysis of subjects' recordings included breaking the transcripts down into lines comprising single ideas (protocols), the coding of protocols to describe the types of decision making behaviour and information being used and the definition of episodes comprising consecutive protocols with unifying objectives. Bouwman (1978) also included an analysis of the chains of reasoning which could be followed through the transcripts. Bouwman's method of analysis of transcripts is an adaptation of the research method forged by Newell and Simon (1972) for which the difficulties are by now fairly well understood (see Libby, 1981, for instance). Major difficulties are the time required for a complete analysis and the subjectivity of the analysis.

Given that Bouwman's research is relatively mature, the first phase of our analysis was to check that our results were consistent with his findings. The second phase was to investigate ways in which we could build on his work. Our investigation suggested the potential benefits of grounded research (Glaser and Strauss, 1967). Pidgeon et al. (1991) suggest that grounded theory provides an appropriate means by which a knowledge engineer can obtain a faithful representation of qualitative data derived from human experts. Jones (1987, p. 25) remarks that grounded theory is important because ‘rather than forcing data within logico-deductively derived assumptions … grounded theory … “fits” and “works” because it is derived from the concepts and categories used by social actors themselves to interpret and organize their worlds’. The technique seems, therefore, to be consistent with investigating users’ perceptions of their tasks.

## Research findings

This section presents an analysis of the findings to arise from our research and shows the extent to which they extend Bouwman's theories. However, it is first helpful to provide a brief sketch of the company considered by the subjects. The company has a turnover of £90 million and is valued at £60 million based on the market value of its shares. A relatively high proportion of its shares are family owned. It is small by the criteria used by our subjects and was chosen because the subjects are familiar with the major companies in the industry. In broad terms, the company operates in the food manufacturing industry. It experienced a poor profit record in the late 1980s, following the acquisition of another company, but the most recent trends suggest a good profit result for 1992. The company is subsequently referred to as Company X to maintain confidentiality.

## Overall task

A process summary of the way in which the analysts handled the task is provided in Figure 1. The first stage is to analyse the financial reports. This involves looking at specific trends in figures such as sales, profit and external debt and includes the calculation of ratios. The analysts appear to be looking for exceptions to their expectations and seem to form an early picture of the company based on models of typical companies. The next stage is to address qualitative issues so as to enlarge the conceptual picture of the business formed from the financial analysis. This leads to a list of questions which the analysts would normally resolve by talking to the company. A picture is produced of the competence of the management by assessing the quality of the responses to such questions. Factual evidence is also gained which increases the analyst's knowledge of the company. Given a substantial knowledge of the company, which seems to be summarised as a 'story', the next stage is to produce forecasts in terms of profit and loss, cash flow, possibly balance sheet and the P/E ratio, a particular measure used by the City. Comparing the P/E ratio with forecasts for similar companies determines whether the company is over- or under-valued and justifications for the under- or over-valuation are sought in terms of the company's 'story'.

This seems to be an iterative process in which financial analysts amass a very detailed picture:

S4: ‘we tend to go round and round the system and looking deeper and deeper and deeper and deeper until you sort of finally give up on it ... I think the turn-off point is when it’s time to move on ... or whether it’s time you published something.’

Not all stages need to be completed sequentially and different stages are given more emphasis than others by individual analysts. So, for instance, forecasting might be carried out before contacting the company and developing the ‘story’ might contain more in the way of qualitative issues, such as the potential success of products, than financial analysis.

The overall task that we encountered seemed to be significantly wider in scope than our reading of the literature on financial analysis indicates. The next sections explore this process in more detail and show analysts' use and attitudes towards the adoption of information technology in their working lives.

## Acquisition of data by analysts

Analysis of the transcripts revealed four issues associated with the acquisition of data: the multiple sources of data used by financial analysts, directed search, the need for financial analysts to own information, and the building of a conceptual model of the organization under investigation.

## Multiple sources of data

Contrary to Bouwman's findings, we discovered that financial analysts use a wide range of sources of data. They were all familiar with the type of data we provided but used additional sources. Among the additional formal sources referred to were the computerized on-line database 'Textline', competing financial analysts' reports and forecasts, shareholders' circulars and trade journals. It was also noticeable that informal sources were influential. All of our subjects referred to Company X's advertising campaign on the television.

One of the important aspects of the analysts' expertise appears to be that they know the data sources well; they know what to expect when they consult a particular source and they know the limitations and advantages of each

## source:

S4: ‘we checked through what they would do on a marketing front and we backed that up by the trade press and the supplements and the Grocer and similar magazines … where Company X will tell you what their strategies are.’

S1: ‘most of that information (e.g. market, products and operating details) you are not going to get from published companies stuff.’

S3: 'right, I would probably then turn to something like the Extel cards and the McCarthy cards because the Report and Accounts don't really give me any idea of what brands the company has got.'

One of the most important sources of data is the company itself:

S1: 'but of course, a lot of this in the real world you would actually build on by talking to the company.' S4: 'we would also get on the phone to Mr X, who's about to retire, to see if he would tell us anything ... just to check our forecast ...'

The interaction with the company appears to be a major aspect of the analysts work:

S6: 'that is my added value, that I know them, that I can actually go to this company ... I think I almost serve as one of those little angels that pop up on your shoulder and say, "That's not what you said a year ago. You told me this was going to be a super investment". If one was to try and justify my job ... I think we provide a control mechanism on management's ego and management's complacency and management ignorance as well.'

So, the company as an information source is a two-way process; analysts learn new information, confirm their own analysis and feedback their existing knowledge bases for the potential benefit of the company and investors.

## Directed search

All subjects tended to begin with directed search and the object of their search was often the profit and loss account. For instance:

S1: 'I think first of all, we are looking at turnover [total sales value] and trading profit lines.'

Our findings accord with Bouwman et al. (1987) concerning directed and non-directed search and the major significance of the profit and loss account. In common with Bouwman, our subjects used non-directed search to become familiar with the information provided, included ensuring that they had not ‘missed anything’.

## Ownership of information

Ownership of information appears to be a significant issue from our analysis but does not seem to be singled out in Bouwman's research findings. Data was often restated on the subjects' terms rather in the form originally presented. For instance, S5 prepares a table of numbers, representing the financial performance of each company, at an early stage in the analysis process. Similarly:

S6: 'I guess what I will probably do is to create my own five year sales and operating profit record ... and then just look at the trend.'

S1: 'and then I would take some notes, as I went through, of key events.'

S2: 'this is usually something I do ... which is a list of the corporate activity; what the company has done.'

The issue of ownership is particularly important for the adoption of IT by analysts. S6 provided a coherent explanation of the underlying issue, which is that the analyst needs to 'get his/her hands dirty by handling the data' in order to relate to them. The use of IT does not necessarily help in this process:

S6: 'I tend to do it initially with a paper and pen ... then when I've got them right, I'll put them on a PC. I find it easier to work with paper and pen though. PCs are fine for storing them; you loose paper very quickly. In many ways, I am simply replaying what is here ... in a framework that I understand and is clear to me.'

S6: 'I think some of those big (computerized) models ... you are just relying on these little things (PCs) to click out at the bottom and think ... well ... that's pretty interesting.'

The point is that ‘that’s pretty interesting’ is not sufficient; the analyst needs to become personally involved with the data. Hence the drawing up of tables according to personal styles and the drawing out of key pieces of information.

This does not mean that IT has no role to play in facilitating the ownership process. S2 used a spreadsheet at an early stage:

S2: 'and then I run through and do a full breakdown with profits and loss, balance sheet and then a cash flow (forecast) and I make all these things fit into a spreadsheet that I'd created'.

S2 was enthusiastic about the importance of her spreadsheet. The ownership aspects of this is that the spreadsheet was developed by S2 herself and so in owning the spreadsheet she also came to own the data. A complicating factor in this is that the use of the spreadsheet blurs the edges between acquisition and information processing. Many of the statements made by S2 regarding her spreadsheet have direct relevance both for acquisition and information processing.

## Construction of a conceptual model

Bouwman has established the concept of the template of the typical company as an explanation of expert behaviour. Data for the company under investigation creates a picture which is related to existing pictures of typical companies as a basis for evaluation. Our research extends this theory because we found that the analysts in our study held rich and varied models to explain and provide evaluative material for a wide range of issues. The following quotations provide a flavour.

The first example draws upon models of costs of national advertising campaigns and models of relationships between revenue and adveritizing spend.

S1: ‘they are not a bad brand … they have obviously got a big problem about marketing that now. I mean, I am familiar with brand X as that’s on television … [from the revenue] that’s not a lot of money on which to fund a national advertising campaign. So there is a marketing issue straight away.’

Upon completion of a part of the analysis, S5 concluded that the relationships explored made sense in terms of her conceptual and predetermined financial model:

S5: 'just sort of everything there seems to fit together.'

S1 uses quantitative models in everyday work and applies sensitivity analysis as a technique to explore the effects of alternative scenarios:

S1: 'I've been doing some modelling with [a company in the tobacco industry] ... and I've actually got a rough profits model. And then once you've done that you've got an idea of what the sensitivities are.'

Modelling can become complex. For instance, S6 illustrates how models of management behaviour and examples drawn from personal experience of industry, which are themselves filtered from complex financial relationships, may combine to provide a verdict about the nature of some business investment decisions:

S6: 'some businesses are committed to spending their money badly. If you are in milling now, you have no choice to invest in the efficiency of your mills. That will not give you very good returns ... it wouldn't give you as good a return as would the bank ... but you have ... actually no choice, unless you are going to exit that industry.'

S6 provides an explanation for the fact that much of that which analysts do in the earliest stage of their analysis is to build a conceptual model of the company under investigation:

S6: 'well yes, and it gives me a feel for ... it sounds crazy ... but I actually get a feel, almost a conceptual feel for the business ... how and when ... almost like a three dimensional picture on how it's progressed along various lines.'

The word ‘feel’ was also repeatedly used by S1, for instance:

S1: 'and try and get a feel for how they're improving efficiencies in the plants they're continuing with.'

S1: 'to get a feel about whether they know what they are doing ... whether they have got a good Financial Director.'

This appears to be partly an ownership issue:

S1: 'it's really trying to explain to your own satisfaction why [the company is] doing what it's doing.'

and partly because understanding the business helps the analysts to take their financial analysis further:

S1: 'it's really with the intention of being able to rationalize the movements in the revenue and profit lines.'

In constructing the conceptual model of the company under investigation, there is evidence that the analysts are building on existing pictures as a learning process:

S6: 'well, I am starting from the basis that ... I know company X are a commodity X company ... I know what products they produce ... and I know a reasonable amount about the competitive environment and the operational dynamics of the commodity X industry.'

S3: 'their big brand would appear to be brand X. I know that actually ... I have seen the advertisements on television. I already know that brand X outsells [established world-wide brand name] in Scotland.'

S3: 'but again, it all ... but it all concurs with what I know about the industry.'

The purpose of modelling is to understand the business in both financial and management terms:

S4: 'looking ... trying to find out ... what is driving the financial [factors] ... also looking at what the management game is and what they've been trying to do with the business and whether the strategy is going to stretch ... or going to stack up in the company's competitive environment.'

## Information processing

Three subsections are necessary to explain the data we collected: structured processes, filtering and the formulation of evaluations. Bouwman does not appear to make any significant statements directly relevant to the material presented here.

## Structured processes

A range of structured processes were adopted by the subjects:

(1) the calculation of ratios;

(2) comparison of ratios with peer-group;

(3) the preparation of forecasts;

(4) the construction of spreadsheet models.

## Calculation of ratios

All six subjects used ratios to a greater or lesser extent, but none used ratios to the extent implied either by the financial analysis literature or the expert systems in financial analysis literature. A small number of critical ratios, rather than a complete and systematic decomposition of the company's performance in ratio terms appeared to be the norm. The key ratios appear to be profit margins (concerned with product profitability in percentage terms), the gearing ratio (concerned with the extent of external debt) and the P/E ratio (price per share divided by profit per share).

Apart from the direct use of the calculated ratio in evaluation, a second motive for carrying out ratio analysis links to the acquisition phase, most explicitly the drawing up of a conceptual picture;

S6: 'I am not a huge believer in ratio analysis. I will look at things, try and conceptualize a picture of the company's finances and how it is moving, and then, perhaps, if I see an anomaly I will use ratio analysis to try and find out why. But this is a means to an end, not an end in itself.'

In general, it seems that the analysts were using ratio analysis to find anomalies and inconsistencies. For most, this seemed to be a means of establishing questions that would be posed of the company:

S6: 'and what this would all be gearing up to would be if I felt this would be worth taking to the next stage then I would set up a meeting with the company at which I would have a list of questions which your analysis would be geared to creating ... and, over time, as you come across things, your automatic check list of questions increases.'

A final motive for ratio calculation is in the preparation of forecasts, as explained below.

## Comparison of ratios with peer-group

The ratios alone carry little information and it is necessary to make a comparative study with similar companies. Since we became aware of significant differences in the detailed calculations carried out by the different analysts, the comparison only makes sense if both ratios for the company under investigation and for the reference group are common to the analyst and the analyst's company.

## The preparation of forecasts.

A primary motivation for preparing ratios appeared to be the use of such calculated ratios in the preparation of forecasts. For instance, all subjects calculated the tax rate (tax as shown on the profit and loss account as a percentage of profit before tax). This ratio is not found in standard text books and there is no established means by which it could be used to interpret the performance of the company. Subjects used the ratio in their calculations of forecast profit after tax.

All subjects prepare forecasts as a matter of routine in a structured fashion. The forecasts give rise to a forecast P/E ratio which can be used to formulate an evaluation for the company.

## The construction of spreadsheet models.

There was evidence that spreadsheets were used for both ratio calculations and the preparation of forecasts. S2 was the most enthusiastic user of spreadsheets and provided a substantial amount of research data on the motivations for doing so. It appears that the spreadsheet acts as an aide memoir at the acquisition stage and provides a degree of structure in the way in which information is processed:

S2: 'when 1990's [Financial Reports] come through, then I would go through 1989's figures again, checking they haven't changed. Often they have, and there would be new statements and you would want to see why. And normally, it's a completely reasonable reason. Sometimes it isn't. And in those cases you would want to dig a bit deeper. But the point about this is that [the spreadsheet model has] got a greater capacity than my brain has ... it draws things to my attention which I certainly couldn't possibly do sitting there with tons [of figures] ... it's very systematic.'

It is important to note that even for S2, IT is only a part of the total process and is used as a tool for those aspects of the overall task where the computer can add value to the human information processes. S2 places her spreadsheet in context in the following way:

S2: 'so, if you are looking at the full picture of the company, what you are really saying is, "We have had a conversation with a company, we like the sound of the strategy, we think that the strategy is feasible, both in terms of what management is capable of doing and also in financial terms; what resources it has available". And the thing that I found was quite helpful about the spreadsheet is that it is completely systematic and would pick up any holes ... in the company's armour.

If the company cannot tell me where they are going and why and how they are going to do it in financial terms, then we don't invest in them.'

## Filtering

Before an evaluation was formed, the subjects carried out a filtering exercise which reduced the mass of data provided to a very small number of issues. For example, following the financial analysis phase, S3 took a single thought forward to the next phase. This single thought was that the company did not appear to be attractive because it failed to expand in the period 1987 to 1990. All other aspects of the financial analysis were expected and were not carried forward.

S3: 'so it's a mixed performance ... trends one might expect ... it doesn't necessarily look interesting from an investment point of view just from looking at these figures because ... a small commodity X manufacturer should evidence a massive growth in the market between 1987 and 1990.'

Contrasting two subjects' responses to a single issue provides an interesting insight into filtering:

S3: 'talk [in a report produced by the Chairman of Company X] about the Monopoly and Mergers Commission Report, which came out in the middle of last year ... which was really nothing much. A lot of money spent on a fairly useless report.'

S6: 'he makes reference to the [Monopolies and Mergers Commission Report] ... Some of the smaller drinks companies were the catalyst for that report. They were complaining that they were being squeezed by the big boys ... unfairly squeezed. Yet these guys [i.e., Company X Directors] were suggesting that they saw no justification for this report in the first place and are not concerned whether its recommendations are imposed or not ... I'd say, if they were not the ones that were bleating, then that would seem to be a positive thing.'

Both subjects have filtered a major Monopolies and Mergers Commission report into apparently simple impressions; S3 filtered the report into the impression that the report was worthless, S6 filtered the report into an impression concerning motivations for the report. S3 takes Company X's statement and effectively dismisses it because she could see no significance in the report in the first place. S6 creates a plus point for the company because he was unhappy about the motivation for the report and was pleased that Company X disassociated itself from the report process.

## Formulation of evaluations

Figure 2 presents the evaluations drawn by all six subjects in our research. S6 explains the process as one which involves three elements: fundamentals, technicalities and sentiment. The term ‘fundamentals’ relates to the financial and managerial analysis which is carried out by analysts and which is described in the preceding sections. S1 and S5 couch their evaluations in terms of fundamentals. Technical considerations include whether the shares are worth trading in and the priorities that analysts set themselves; a ‘time is money’ technicality. This was the factor which caused S2 to reject company X because it was too small. S3 and S6 provide evaluations based on a combination of fundamentals and technicalities. ‘Sentiment’ includes relationships with the company. S4 provides an evaluation based on a combination of fundamentals, technicalities and sentiment.

Given the volume of data that subjects considered during the sessions, the brevity of the evaluations is an aspect which deserves attention. Even in the most complex evaluation, that provided by S4, there are only a very small number of issues which influence the final conclusion. This suggests the critical importance of the filtering process. It also shows the importance of modelling. In a sense, the subjects already knew company X before they started to look at the data provided because they had a model of a company in company X's industry, together with a model of the small, family owned business. This is graphically illustrated by S1's evaluation that the company is 'dull', implying that small, family owned companies have no story to tell which will interest investors.

It is difficult to find any evidence of complex inferencing in the work of our sample of analysts. Instead, it appears that the inferencing is relatively simple. This is evident from the reasoning chains which lead to the evaluations. One of S5's reasoning chains is provided in Figure 3 to illustrate this.

The most time consuming part of this reasoning chain is that associated with calculating the forecast profit figure based on projections and ratios. The use of spreadsheets in this modelling operation appears to be a common response to the relative inconvenience of manipulating numbers and financial relationships to achieve a forecast. The calculated forecast is then compared with the share price, taking data from the database of share prices published by the Financial Times. From the figure of forecast profit per share and the present price per share, a figure of P/E is calculated. Inferencing involves comparing the forecast P/E with calculations made by the analyst for similar companies, showing that company X's P/E is high, and given this high P/E the company is considered to be expensive.

## Discussion and conclusions

Filtering and modelling were significant information processing activities. Inferencing played a lesser role and did not appear to present complexities or difficulties for the analysts. The processes that financial analysts engage in are complex in symbolic terms but there is no evidence that analysts experience any difficulties in this regard, applying filtering and modelling strategies to resolve the difficulty of coping with very large volumes of data. They do not appear to engage in the activity for which expert systems could add value; extensive inferencing.

Some parts of the analytical process are not simple in human terms. Computers, and particularly spreadsheets, are used in these tasks. Spreadsheets are used for storing data in a systematic fashion, calculating figures which are not easily manipulated through mental arithmetic and modelling financial relationships.

In other words, spreadsheets are used by analysts because spreadsheets do well that which the human expert believes he or she does relatively badly. Expert systems do well that which the human information processor believes he or she does well (the manipulation of symbols) and also do that which the human information processor does not appear to do at all (extensive inferencing). The latter point is important because it is in the complex drawing out of conclusions as an inferencing process that an expert system could compete for effectiveness with the financial analyst.

Where does this leave the literature on expert systems and financial analysis? A number of points need to be made. Firstly, our picture of financial analysis differs from that painted in the existing literature. The existing literature appears to suggest financial analysis as a rich inferencing process based on ratio analysis, possibly coupled with qualitative analysis which again leads to rich inferencing. Our research suggests weak inferencing but rich strategies of data acquisition and information processing. To that extent, our research extends the existing literature.

Secondly, it might be argued that expert systems might still be valuable in undertaking part of the work of the financial analyst; the interpretation of ratios. It could be argued that the expert system might structure the work and throw insights into the data which go beyond the cognitive abilities of the human information processor. Such normative expert systems might improve the work of practitioners without being descriptively valid of the work that such practitioners do at present. We would accept that this could be so; that our research might be limited by being descriptive of the work of a small number of analysts and that a normative approach to financial analysis might provide tools which outperform our analysts.

However, if this turned out to be so, we would still need to make the following points based on the evidence

provided by our subjects:

(1) Normative expert systems would need to be developed in-house because there was substantial evidence from our transcripts that financial analysts see themselves as competing with other financial analysts as information processors, both in terms of speed of data acquisition and ability to take the data through to a conclusion which will allow them to make statements that are new to the market place;

(2) Normative expert systems would need to compete with existing technologies such as spreadsheets and would need to be at least as acceptable to users. This suggests that expert systems of the future need to be at least as convenient to use as Excel and the most recent releases of Lotus, including the versions of Lotus that run under a Windows environment;

(3) Normative expert systems would still need to allow financial analysts to own the data since ownership is an essential part of data acquisition.

(4) Normative expert systems would be required to produce lists of questions to allow the dialogue between analysts and companies to continue.

The first and second points provide a justification for Barthet and Hanachi (1991) and Berry and Hart (1990, 1991) bringing the issues of the man-machine interface and standards to the centre stage of expert systems development. This would seem to re-orientate the emphasis of development for expert systems tools such as shells.

The third point implies that expert systems can only play a supporting role for the financial analyst. The reason for this stems from the fact that the result of analysis comprises a recommendation to either buy or sell shares. This recommendation will be on the basis of P/E but will also rely on the interpretation of the P/E in terms of the company 'story'. The 'story' must attract investors and/or provide a compelling explanation of the recommendation. The use of the term 'story' and the importance of being able to 'tell a good story' about a particular company are therefore important to analysts in producing commercially valuable information. Present expert systems do not appear to be geared to developing such stories and it is difficult to see how present technology would do so, at least in the immediate future.

The need to develop a ‘story’ additionally necessitates that analysts develop a feeling of ownership, which seems to be an important aspect of their work. If expert systems are to be adopted by financial analysts then design must ensure that users continue to be allowed to develop this feeling of ownership. Developments in the use of IT in Decision Conferencing might provide ways in which IT in general can support decision making and simultaneously foster ownership. Perhaps the use of expert systems in Decision Conferencing could tap this potential.

The fourth point suggests a new goal for expert systems development. Present systems appear to aim for an interpretation of financial health. Our subjects used ratio analysis to provide a basis for the questioning of companies and for input to a forecasting system. A reorientation in systems development objectives would seem to be appropriate for those who seek to develop expert systems for financial analysts in the future.

Finally, our analysis of the work of financial analysts appears to extend Bouwman's work and suggests that financial analysis is considerably more rich in terms of strategies for the acquisition and processing of data than was previously believed. In this way, researching a topic through the motivation of expert systems seems to lead to new insights into the topic area. Similarly, knowledge of research methods may be valuable to practitioners. One reason for the ability of this paper to look at financial analysis in the way that it does might be that the research method, combining protocol analysis with grounded research, is itself new. Perhaps a grounding in research methods which are geared to addressing the issue of user needs will enrich the process of the systems life cycle (e.g. Senn, 1990) for the benefit of practitioners.
