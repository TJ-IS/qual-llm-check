# Information systems strategies and the management of organizational change – a strategic alignment model

JANICE M. BURN

Department of Computing, Hong Kong Polytechnic, Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong

This article presents findings of an investigation into the relationship between organizational configurations and alternative patterns of information systems (IS) development. The study focuses on the IS strategic process. A theoretical framework is developed to examine the relationships between organizational and IS strategy formulation processes. The framework was evaluated in 56 organizations. Results of the analysis support the theory that different stages of growth in the use and development of IS require different approaches to strategy, and that different approaches to strategy are favoured by different organizational configurations. Evaluation of the relationship between organizational change and IS developments reveals a pattern of strategic alignment reflecting the interdependencies between the organizational configuration and the stage of IS growth. It would appear that transitions through this alignment model are characterized by periods of dynamic change which can be predicted at certain stages of growth. The model may prove useful both to assist in the management of organizational change and to identify appropriate IS strategy formulation approaches.

## Introduction

The effective use of information systems technology (IST) as an element of competitive strategy has long been of critical concern to IS managers and general managers alike. While numerous examples of successful implementations are reported in the literature (McFarlan, 1984; Rockart and Scott-Morton, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Henderson, 1990) there are many examples where investments in IST resulted in no measurable impacts (Clemons and Weber, 1990; Curley and Henderson, 1989). Associated with this concern, and the apparent inability of many organizations to integrate the use and management of the IST infrastructure into the mainstream of the firm, senior IST managers have consistently ranked the problems of improving the strategic planning process for IS as one of the key issues for researchers to address (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Niederman et al., 1991). Problems relate to the whole process of planning including the alignment of IS strategy formulation with organizational strategies. This latter aspect of the problem is the main focus of this paper and specifically, the selection of strategies for IS planning appropriate to the stage of IS growth and organizational development.

This paper proceeds as follows. First the relevant literature is reviewed and the linkage between IS and organizational strategy explored. Second, the framework for the organizational cultural audit is developed and its application in 56 companies evaluated. Finally, a model for strategic alignment is proposed providing a basis for further longitudinal research.

## The literature

## The evolution of strategy formulation theory

The first stage of the research study was concerned with the development of the strategic alignment framework. This framework derives primarily from a comparative review of strategy formulation theory from both organizational and information systems theory viewpoints. Figure 1 shows a partial chronology of strategy formulation theories and also depicts the interplay with organizational theories from the typologies and power/behaviour literature.

## Organizational strategy formulation

As can be seen from Figure 1, the process of formal strategic formulation has itself been the focus for many changing views over the last 30 years and can be expressed as a phased theory of evolution. Phase 1 can be identified with a recognition of the need for long term planning (Andrews, 1980; Ansoff, 1965) and a functionally based plan covering several years into the future. The focus was highly prescriptive and top down. By the early 1970s in phase 2, the interest had shifted to a focus on business planning (Lorange and Vancil, 1977) with organizations requiring several hierarchically related and mutually supporting strategies. Put simply, this is the formulation of a corporate plan with top down guidance and bottom up plans at divisional and functional levels.

Phase 3 is characterized by the portfolio approach (Henderson, 1978) where the essence of the approach is to view the organization as a series of separate strategic business units having largely independent products, markets and missions. These units are viewed in terms of their cash needs and growth potential, in order to identify an appropriate balance between the growth of the corporation and its relative position vis-a-vis the competition.

Figure 1 Strategic formulation - a partial chronology

These first three phases can all be viewed as variations on the classical rational approach to strategy formulation, and each can be criticized for its over-emphasis on analytical strategy to the neglect of qualitative organizational realities.

Phase 4 is typified by Porter's work (1980, 1985) and is also prescriptive. The emphasis here is not so much on the tailoring of individual strategy models but rather on the selection of one model from several generic classifications. Initially, this approach focuses on the competitive position of the firm in the context of its industry infrastructure. Later it was extended to suggest that all competitive strategies can be reduced to three basic alternatives: cost leadership, differentiation and focus.

In order to select an appropriate strategy, value-chain analysis is used to identify the steps by which an organization adds value to its products or services.

In parallel with this steady progression of ideas related to formal strategic processes, there were also developments from the Behavioural School described by Quinn (1978) as the power-behaviour approach to strategy. Here the focus is on the social context rather than the analytic process. These views (Cyert and March, 1963; Simon, 1965; Pettigrew, 1973) support a coalition view of goal formation and an organizational environment where social/political behaviour is closely representative of organizational decision making.

A steady convergence of ideas forms the basis for phase 5 – incrementalism, and the gradual emergence of phase 6 which places strategy formulation in the context of a broader holistic analysis (Snow and Miles, 1983; Quinn, 1985; Pettigrew, 1985; Mintzberg, 1987, 1989, 1991). These ideas are emphasized in Mintzberg's writings on strategy (1991), with strategy being a plan, ploy, pattern, position and perspective which capture both the component parts of strategy as well as the context. As the organizational context changes in a deliberate or emergent fashion, so the approach to strategy changes. These transitions will normally follow a pattern suggesting several configurations of organizations with distinguishing characteristics.

The early phases are characterized by functional approaches with the stage of growth models (Anthony, 1964; Nolan, 1979) followed by business systems planning. The emphasis on strategic business units in phase 3 is supported by the use of critical success factors (Rockart, 1979) and portfolio analysis (McFarlan and McKenney, 1982). The Porter (1980, 1985) influence was carried through into IS strategy formulation in phase 4 with the competitive five forces model and the value chain and other derivations such as the customer resource lifecycle (Ives and Learmonth, 1984). In more recent years (phase 5) the emphasis has been on a contingency approach (Nolan, 1984; Bhabuta, 1988; Hirschheim et al., 1988; Parker et al., 1988; Earl, 1989) suggesting that different stages of growth in the use and development of IS require different approaches to strategy. This staged growth approach to planning is supported by Sullivan (1988) who suggested that planning may be viewed as a rational response to the evolving IS environment. At the same time, however, this viewpoint offers a plethora of IS strategy frameworks from which to choose and, often, inadequate guidance on a selection method.

Just as early approaches to organizational strategy were limited by their prescriptive views, so IS strategy has focused on specific methodologies which are process-oriented rather than contextual. This has undoubtedly contributed to some of the many failures in the adoption of IS planning approaches (Galliers, 1991) and, indeed, to related IS failures themselves. As organizational strategy enters into an era where the focus is contextual, it would seem likely that IS strategies will also refocus. Refocussing requires a framework which also addresses the integration of the business and IST domains and their cross domain alignments (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1992).

## The linkage between IS and organizational strategy

Several IS researchers suggest a contingency approach to

IS, whereby a framework can be developed to assist organizations to select the most appropriate strategic planning style dependent upon the growth stage of IS within the organization. This framework frequently assumes the form of an evolutionary growth model which can be useful in managing change related to the increasing pervasiveness of IST dependency within the organization. Such stage models, however, can by myopic in their neglect of organizational changes which can impact on the role of IS and which may require redirection or refocussing of the IS strategy.

Whilst there is widespread agreement that organizational and IS strategies should be linked or interdependent (McLean and Soden, 1977; Martino, 1983; Hartog and Herbert, 1986; Galliers, 1987; Lederer and Mendelow, 1987; Niederman et al., 1991) or even integrated (Galliers, 1991), little evidence exists to show the nature of such interdependency and the ways by which linkages can be achieved in practice (Henderson and Sifonis, 1986, 1988; Nath, 1989; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1992). The key issue which this study addresses, therefore, is whether such interdependencies can be shown to exist and, if so, how organizations can achieve more effective alignments.

Henderson and Venkatraman (1992) propose a framework which can help to define the nature of such an alignment. They suggest that strategic management of IST requires appropriate choices to position the firm with respect to a dynamic and uncertain IT marketplace, as well as effective decisions that define the implementation strategy for building and operating the IS infrastructure. In essence, they argue that the challenge of managing the IS function is similar to that of strategic management of the enterprise, and as such, effective management of IST requires alignment of a complex set of choices reflecting both strategic and functional perspectives. This echoes an earlier argument by Pyburn (1983).

The Henderson and Venkatraman framework suggests that the strategy - IST relationship should be conceptualized in terms of two fundamental dimensions and their alignment:

(1) Strategic integration involving the alignment of external and internal domains

(2) Functional integration involving the integration of the business and the IST domains and hence cross domain alignment of the relationships along the two diagonals of the matrix.

In the majority of IS strategy related research the IS strategy follows from business strategy as a functional integration and it is this cross domain alignment which forms the classic linkage (Pyburn, 1983; King, 1984; Galliers, 1987). In other words, the process is framed by the assumptions and decisions of an a priori strategic business plan. The external–internal alignment model, widely employed in strategic theory (Thompson, 1967; Andrews, 1980; Snow and Miles, 1983) recognizes the need for external alignment of IST strategy and for cross alignment with business strategy. In this research model, the organization can choose the initiating domain based on the driving influence at the time. Thus IST can lead or follow business strategy and the organizational and IST infrastructure, to react to or initiate strategic change. It also indicates the need to focus on different planning orientations depending on the focus towards external impact or internal alignment.

In order to provide a framework within which these issues can be explored, two audit checks were developed independently; one reviews the alignment of organizational strategy and structure and the other reviews the alignment of IS strategy and structure. The two phases of audit check, when applied together, are referred to as the organizational cultural audit (OCA) framework. Each of the two phases is linked to a particular style of planning and these planning styles are then compared for alignment purposes. As an empirical check the suggested planning styles were compared with actual planning styles in use within the organization and also against the IS infrastructure model. From these comparisons a suggested model of strategic alignment was developed which identifies the need for focus on a particular domain for cross alignment and the appropriate planning orientation. In addition, there is reason to believe that the stage transitions which organizations experience as they become progressively more dependent on the use of IS follow a pattern of dynamic change.

## The organizational cultural audit (OCA) framework

The organizational alignment phase is a three stage process examining the organization in context, its strategic approach and the functional implementation of strategy. The following sections discuss its major underpinnings.

## Organizational theories selected

The significant influence of Mintzberg (1973, 1976, 1978, 1979, 1983, 1985, 1987, 1989, 1991) on strategy formulation theory is apparent from Figure 1. His work thus provides the major underpinning for the organizational models, hereby facilitating analysis of the organizational context through his model of organizational configurations (1989), and also an analysis of approaches to strategic planning (1973).

Despite his near dominance of organizational strategy and structure research, little empirical work has taken place relating Mintzberg's theory to IS research. Of the few examples that do exist, Earl (1990, 1993) has reported a study of strategic IS planning approaches in UK companies that suggests a close alignment with Mintzberg's view of strategy. Broadbent and Weill (1993) use the realized strategy framework of Mintzberg and Waters (1985) as the conceptual basis for their examination of the extent to which business strategies were enabled, supported and stimulated by information strategies. Their analysis and suggested alignment model, identified while this study was in progress, is consistent both with Earl (1989) and Henderson and Venkatraman (1992). Leifer (1988) also used Mintzberg's work to propose a staged model of IT decentralization.

Given the emphasis within this study on external-internal alignment, the theories of Miles and Snow (1978) based on such an alignment model were also selected to provide a richer view. In their study of organizations they identified four adjustment patterns which classify organization types each with its own strategy and a particular configuration of technology, structure and process consistent with its strategy. These adjustment patterns have been tested with respect to the extent of centralization of IS services within an organization (Tavakolian, 1989), strongly supporting a correlation. The patterns also form the basis of an extended strategic alignment model in Henderson and Venkatraman's research (1989).

## Information systems theories selected

Despite criticisms which have been levelled at his work (see Benbasat et al., 1983; Drury, 1983; King and Kraemer, 1984), Nolan's model has formed the basis of substantial further research. Many of the more recent 'contingency' models are extensions of the staged growth model and attempt to provide a more detailed contextual view. Earl (1989) and Galliers (1991) in particular have reviewed these models and both expanded them and developed frameworks within which they can be applied. The stage growth model (using the more commonly applied four stage version) was therefore used to identify the IS context within which a specific planning style would normally be applied. Earl's (1989) sectoral dependency model allows for a very similar analysis to the Miles and Snow (1978) model and so can identify the overall strategic approach and contingent variables. Finally, the portfolio analysis model, based on Earl's interpretation of the McFarlan and McKenney (1982) model, was used to analyse the specific planning modes adopted for IS developments. In this way, a three stage model similar to that developed for organizational alignment can be applied to the IS environment.

## Evaluating the framework

## Applying the framework

The framework was used to develop an audit portfolio – a complete set of guidelines which directs the manager through the model and six separate stages of evaluation. At each of the three stages which comprise the organizational alignment phase, the manager performs a 'health check' on strategic variables. From this analysis he then selects a model which most closely describes the organization. This could be a single model such as machine bureaucracy or a hybrid such as machine/professional bureaucracy. The former favours control from the top with hierarchical planning, whereas the latter will represent a conflict with both top down pull and bottom up push. Depending on the primary focus, one of these will normally be favoured over the other. In this way, the manager can see where there are conflicts within a stage, and between the three stages.

A similar analysis is performed in the IS alignment phase and then the two alignment models selected are compared for consistency in strategic approach. This result is then compared with the actual IS strategic formulation approaches used in the organization since, as Pettigrew (1973) and Quinn (1985) both note, this may diverge greatly in practice from the planned model. The generic model used to identify actual strategy relates directly to Earl's (1989) multiple methodology of planning styles, allowing a selection of bottom-up; top-down; top-down/bottom-up; bottom-up/top-down; multiple (including inside-out) and unfocussed. Ultimately the analyst is required to identify where fits or misfits occur and why and what to do about this. Questions which must be answered are:

Does organizational strategy lead the IT strategy or does IT strategy lead the organization?

Are either of the models in transition and if so in what direction?

If a change occurs in one what is the likely impact on the other?

The SISP chart (Table 2) provides a summary of the findings and the basis for the above analysis. The form is kept deliberately simple and it is recommended that any supporting analysis does not exceed two pages in order to focus the attention on the critical issues.

The OCA framework was designed to fulfil two main objectives. Firstly, the framework has research value. It can be used to test whether any patterns exist in the development of IS planning styles and if these can be related to organizational planning styles. Secondly, and perhaps more importantly, the framework has practical value. It was designed as an audit tool to help managers involved in strategic planning for IST to better understand the role of IST in their organization and how IST can be used, to support strategy. The framework itself comprises a set of eight forms which are supported by user documentation, step by step instructions and a reference reading list. This is intended to help the manager develop an on-going portfolio of his organization such that changes in structure, strategy or style will all be reflected in the audit as the organization and its use of IST change over time. It should be remembered that, though the OCA yields only a snapshot of the system at a point in time, many snapshots taken over time will build up a detailed history of the organization. The SISP chart may indicate a healthy organization but may also flag possible causes for concern in the future. More importantly, it can also show the indicators to be observed and remedies to be applied.

## The strategic alignment model

Figure 3 shows the strategic alignment model and the pattern of staged growth in IST usage within the four different configuration models for which results were obtained. The strategic support level is identified as that level within which the majority of organizations of each type fall, or the perceived minimum level to which they aspire, and is assumed to be the minimum level of IS stage growth which an organization would need to maintain to provide full support and alignment with organizational strategy. Hence, in a professional bureaucracy, stage 2 level of IS growth providing full operational control and some management control would be an effective support for an organization which operates within an environment of limited planning and control, and extensive horizontal decentralization. Reaching this stage would provide a natural alignment between IS and organizational strategy. This does not mean, however, that this stage is an easy one to reach and in order to arrive at this level, the organization must be prepared to adapt the approach to IS strategy in accordance with the IS stage model and to cycle into and out of each style. The professional organization would have to allow a bottom up style of approach to prevail in stage 1 developments and to pull this together into a bottom up/top down approach for transition into stage 2. This would normally lead to a situation where the IS strategic approach would lag behind the organizational strategic approach.

Should the organization wish to progress beyond the minimum level of IS support then it must be prepared to adapt its overall approach to strategy in line with the IS growth stage desired. This implies that the organization views IS as a strategic necessity and so must accept the dominant influence of IS on the strategy planning process. A professional bureaucracy requiring maximum IS support would therefore be advised to consider the adoption of a multiple approach to strategy and to establish the organizational typology to support this before attempting to progress beyond stage 2 of IS growth. This implies a ‘quantum’ change affecting both IS and organizational strategy. Should an attempt be made to progress incrementally, by way of top down management then the organization may find itself becoming firmly entrenched in a machine bureaucracy approach with repercussions on the organizational culture and the IS stage development which will inhibit further progress. At this stage, therefore, the IS strategic approach would normally lead the organizational strategic approach.

This lead situation is rarely found in practice since the effect of it is to pull the organization to change overall strategic approaches in order to facilitate IS developments. This reflects the support role of IS which is assumed by many organizations. Those where IS is perceived as the delivery vehicle such as banks or with increasing dependence on IS for services such as quantity surveyors or government departments dependent on IS for mass statistical processing or complex calculations are examples in the case analyses where the lead situation is accepted.

Further case analyses show that there are many situations where both organizational and IS strategy are out of synch with the strategic typology which the organizational culture prefers. This is possible at any stage other than the strategic alignment level and may be a conscious decision to drive the organization through a particular transition stage or the result of poor strategic management. This model, does, however, show the choices faced concerning the selection of a strategic mode if they wish to rectify the situation.

## Conclusions

This study supports the existence of a relationship between organizational and IS strategies and suggests the pattern of strategic approach which would be needed for an organization to progress through the stages of IS growth. These findings, however, are the result of preliminary investigative research from which it is hoped tentative conclusions can be drawn for later longitudinal studies. Change occurs over long periods of time (several organizations in this study quoted stage transition periods of five–seven years) and external influences also activate change.

The important feature of the model is that it can identify for management where major transition points occur and also the change factors which need to be managed if staged IS growth is to be accomplished effectively within the existing configuration model. The model also supports the need for organizations to recognize a lead-lag model for IS and organizational strategic interdependency. As different transition points are reached it may be necessary to effect change in the organization, the IS or both. These alternatives are identified within the models of change and so can form part of the long term planning for development and growth.

As it stands, the OCA is an effective data collection instrument with an in-built analysis model based on the six underlying theories. This enables the analyst to build a consistent model of both IS and organizational typologies and effect a comparison. From this, conclusions can be drawn concerning the strategic alignment and also the need for change. In all of the cases studied, the case analysts were able to suggest a strategy which would be consistent with the organization achieving alignment.

The OCA can, however, act as a much more powerful tool when used in conjunction with the interdependency models. These models allow the analyst to predict future patterns of change and to identify the critical transition stages which may impact on the culture of the organization and its strategic typologies. This approach can be viewed as part of an ongoing planning process through which the organization can position itself according to its strategic needs at any one time. Not only does this improve the practice of strategy formulation, it also improves the process. By bringing together the analysis of corporate strategy with IS strategy, the enterprise is forced to accept a broader vision of the role of IS and also to initiate dialogues which can support this. Ruohonen (1991) suggests that organizational, cultural and political factors affect and even destroy IS planning activities. This research focused on developing strategy for IS within this organizational context and hence a way to achieve mutual understanding of planning objectives in the organizational and IS environment. Further research is needed and data has now been collected from another 60 organizational analysts. This gives the basis for longitudinal studies as well as further validation of the suggested model. Preliminary analysis supports the alignment model proposed and also suggests particular patterns of change which will be adopted by different organizational configurations to progress through stages of IS growth. This will form the basis of a second paper which will focus on change management in the alignment of IS strategy within a dynamic organizational context.

## Biographical notes

Janice Burn obtained her Masters Degree at Warwick University, UK and her PhD in Information Systems at Hong Kong University. Prior to joining Hong Kong Polytechnic as a Principal Lecturer in 1985 she held similar posts in the UK and Canada. For the last five years she has been course leader for the Masters Degree in IS.

Her research interests are in the area of information systems strategies, globalization of IT, IS research frameworks and information resource management. Her particular focus over the last five years has been directed towards Asia and cross cultural studies. She has published over 30 papers and co-authored three books.
