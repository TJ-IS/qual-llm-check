# The economic significance of infrastructural IT systems

PETER MONK

University of Strathclyde, Glasgow, UK

This paper develops a new framework for analysing how and why IT is being applied to the infrastructure of the ‘information economy’. The interaction of micro and macro aspects of IT provides an economic rationale for the IT community to go beyond questions of firms’ operations and strategies and to look towards national and international economic change. The micro-economic roles of transactions, communication and information infrastructure are analysed. These are then considered in terms of IT-related policy, from the local to the international level, illustrated by property rights legislation and changes in the European economic environment. It is concluded that the significance of IT lies primarily in its role as the very means by which markets and other economic mechanisms operate.

## Introduction and methodology

Contemporary management practice is challenged by two sets of issues that arise from the continuing development of information technology (IT). The first set relates to organizational choice and behaviour, familiar topics within the IT community. It includes choices about the development, adoption or use of IT components and systems in product and process design; in operations management; as aids for decision making; and as factors influencing organizations' strategic behaviour. These micro-level issues have been extensively debated in the systems, economics and management literatures and they may be characterized as being within the scope of potential managerial control.

The second set of issues is currently less familiar to managers and IT practitioners; here, macro-level issues relate to the transition of advanced economies from an industrial mode of operation to one based on information (Machlup, 1962; OECD, 1986; Hepworth, 1989; Goddard, 1990; Antonelli, 1992); Perelman (1991) provides a more socio-historical view. The problems raised by this transition to an ‘information economy’ extend beyond managerial control — some are currently topics of concern to national and international policy makers — yet they have important practical implications for the management of organizations.

While arguments about the changing nature of technological development merit some interest in themselves, their significance for theorists, practitioners and managers only becomes apparent when the application of technology is considered. Much of the literature on the economic and social effects of IT has emphasized the generality of application of that technology. Not only are IT components and systems used in a wide variety of products and processes, they also act as a new and powerful medium by which information may be treated as a commodity (ITAP, 1983). Although the scale and sophistication of these applications are not to be underestimated, they do tend to disguise more fundamental aspects of contemporary technological change.

A point often ignored in economic theory, perhaps because it seems too obvious for comment, is the necessity of some means by which economic mechanisms as well as activities can operate in the material world. Market transactions, wage and profit distribution, saving, investment and other micro-economic phenomena all require some technological means in order to occur. Moreover, the operation of these mechanisms can only occur through acts of communication between geographically located economic agents; acts of communication which are necessarily organized events (Hepworth, 1989). The technology in question thus covers both physical means and abstract organizational technique, whether these are as simple as the quill pen and double-entry book-keeping or as sophisticated as broadband telecommunications systems and automated stock dealing. The multiple roles of information in economic mechanisms and activities require some such technological means within any form of economy. In the contemporary European information economy, those means are increasingly provided by infrastructural IT systems.

The purpose of this paper is to establish an analytical framework for investigating two related aspects of the present transition to an information economy. The first is how and why IT is applied to economic ‘infrastructure’—the underlying technical foundations of the economy. The second is the relevance of macro-level IT and information policy to the decisions made by managers and others at the micro-level of economic activity. The concern here is with the very mechanisms that define how an economy operates at the local, national and international levels; for example, necessary conditions for production, the operation of markets, mechanisms for consumption, saving, investment and so on. All forms of economic infrastructure, whether transport, energy supplies or telecommunications, have a strategic significance for organizations that both influences their behaviour within markets and goes beyond immediate market considerations. Particularly in the current European context, the infrastructural systems of national economies have implications for organizations operating in the international arena.

This paper focuses on the rationale for, and implications of, the construction of infrastructural IT systems such as large-scale computer networks and telecommunications facilities, in both the public and private sectors. These technical platforms to support new information flows represent strategic information systems in the broadest sense. This analysis suggests that such information systems are not merely vehicles for generating profits for their corporate owners; their primary significance lies in their role in the operation of the economy as a whole.

Consideration of both micro and macro issues raises a significant methodological problem; namely, that a distinct and separate analytical approach is conventionally seen as being required for each set of issues. Macro issues demand a different 'level of analysis' from micro issues and, consequently, sound analytical practice seeks to avoid confusion between aggregated and non-aggregated phenomena (Blaug, 1980). However, in the study of IT as much as in economics, micro-level decisions and actions may be seen as part of the underlying rationale for macro-level policy development. Equally, macro-level policies such as legal regulation affect the environment within which micro-decisions are enacted. In this way, the two levels are mutually influential; the major contention of this paper is that both directions of influence have direct practical implications for the IT-related decisions taken by managers and practitioners.

The analysis below progresses from specific, micro-level foundations to more general macro-level policy considerations. The nature of transactions and communication in the economy are discussed, followed by a consideration of the economic role of infrastructural IT systems. These issues hold implications both for public policy making and for the formulation of corporate policy within private sector organizations. Property rights legislation and practice, and the changing economic environment of Europe are examined briefly in the light of this discussion. Strategic implications of these issues for management are considered; suggestions are then offered for a new research agenda on how managers and IT practitioners might meet the challenge of operating in a new economic environment — an environment that the IT community is helping to create.

## Transactions and communication in the economy

The economic significance of all types of IT systems depends in part on the reasons why economic agents — individuals and organizations — value facilities for processing and communicating information. In the case of infrastructural systems, the relevant question is why might economic agents value facilities for communicating information across the breadth of the local, national and international economies. Foundations for an answer may be discovered by examining two (of the many) roles played by information in economic activity.

Firstly, information is implicit in all exchange transactions between economic agents, whether as individuals or as private or public sector organizations. Irrespective of the degree of competition in any market, the very nature of sale and purchase, barter or other trading activities requires information to be communicated effectively between the parties to trade (Coase, 1937; 1960; Scitovsky, 1954; Dahlman, 1979; Stiglitz, 1985). If that information transfer (communication) is inhibited for any reason, the respective parties will remain uncertain, if not totally ignorant, of the other's trading position. Effective communication of both quantitative and qualitative information about goods and services is a strictly necessary condition for their exchange as commodities; effective communication is a strictly necessary condition for the sustained operation of all types of markets over time.

Secondly, information is implicit in all production and management activities within firms and other organizations (Williamson, 1975). Economic production may be considered as the process by which resources are transformed either into commodities, for sale in markets, or into new resources for future internal use by the producer (Monk, 1989). In this view, the purpose of production is to add value to the original set of resources employed, by means of processes that are structured and subject to control. Technology may be considered as providing the structural (design) information required for these processes of resource transformation. While technological information of this sort is strictly necessary for production to occur, otherwise the form of the production process would be undefined, it is not sufficient in itself to provide all the information required for production in practice. Operational information is also needed for command and coordination, for the management of production activities. Without this operational information, production facilities would remain idle, offering potential but no action. The key feature of both technological and operational information is that in each case it must be appropriately and dynamically distributed amongst the agents engaged in production activities. This need for information distribution over time is met through communication between the members of producer organizations.

The two roles of information in the economy outlined above are implemented through two types of transactions: exchange transactions between economic agents and managerial transactions between members of producer organizations. Not only do both types of transaction necessarily involve communication but also all such transactions must be predicated on some notion of value, in order to be definable as non-random events enacted by economic agents. Such valuation need not be related to prices in markets; in the case of managerial transactions the relevant value concepts would derive from the context of management as a production activity. Put simply, people working in producer organizations must be assumed to be able to make judgements about the value or significance of the information needed for their work. This assumption is necessary if those people are to be taken to process and communicate information between themselves in an ordered and broadly rational manner.

While the problem of how to value information is considered in more detail later, it is worth noting here that this problem is both difficult to resolve and of immediate practical relevance to the construction of IT systems. For example: Ahituv and Neumann (1987), Feldman and March (1987), Monk (1992) and others have addressed the problem in relation to the identification of requirements for new systems; Strassman (1985), Ward (1990) and others have addressed it in relation to appraisal of IT investments; Hepworth (1989) has addressed it in relation to organizations' rationale for demanding IT and telecommunications facilities. In the present context, it is assumed that economic agents value the information they process or communicate, and that such valuations may be explicit or implicit, formal or informal, quantitative or qualitative. This means that in the absence of a market valuation ('exchange value'), agents are able to ascribe a 'use value' to a given set of information or given act of communication. This assumption is necessary to underpin, firstly, the notion that IT systems development is based on plausibly reasoned investment intentions and, secondly, the idea that both exchange transactions and managerial transactions are events undertaken by purposeful economic agents.

Transactions necessarily involve two-way communications, in order that both parties may be certain that transmitted information has been received. However, there are some instances of economic communication that do not involve exchange or managerial transactions. Prominent amongst these is search activity, whereby agents receive information from others about market opportunities such as goods and services for sale, or employment vacancies. The economic theory of search was first developed by Stigler (1961); a more recent contribution on search in labour markets is McKenna (1985). An important aspect of search activity is that it can, but need not, result in either exchange or managerial transactions taking place. An agent's search for information within a market, or within a production information environment, initially involves one-way communication. Only if some conventionally accountable economic event is to occur will this one-way traffic then lead to two-way transactional communication.

In searching for an explanation of the economic significance of information, and hence information systems and their supporting technologies, it is necessary to look beyond transactions and focus instead on the nature of communication (Antonelli, 1992). Both common experience and the laws of physics deny the possibility of instantaneous communication. All acts of communication are costly, if only in terms of time to individuals; typically, such costs will also include financial expenditure and the use of other resources. Acts of communication are thus necessarily economic events; not only do they require the use of resources they also impose opportunity costs on participating agents. Opportunity cost in this context refers to the value, in use or exchange, perceived by agents of activities foregone in order to undertake a particular act of communication. For this reason, as well as that of the content of the information transmitted, both one and two-way communication acts are significant in the operation of the economy. This significance is obtained irrespective of whether such transfers of information between economic agents are ever consummated in the traditional manner of exchange transactions.

## Information infrastructure

The argument so far may be summarized quite simply: the operation of a modern economy is necessarily predicated on costly acts of communication, i.e. one and two-way flows of information, which can only occur given suitable technological means, specifically a communications infrastructure. Like all other information systems, the communications infrastructure of an economy is potentially subject to technological change, specifically in regard to the use of IT to support the system's operations. The continuing development of IT offers new technological possibilities for effectively and efficiently supporting economic acts of communication. The fundamental economic requirement for communications infrastructure thus provides strong incentives for both industrialists and policy makers to promote the development of IT as a network technology. In economic policy terms, the most significant developments in IT are its current and potential applications to the means of exchange and managerial transactions between individuals and between corporate economic agents. The underlying purpose of infrastructural IT systems, i.e. public and private computer networks and telecommunication systems, is to increase the efficiency, effectiveness and functionality of transactional communication mechanisms in the economy.

Clearly, particular organizations are likely to justify their own investments in, say, intra-corporate computer networks in less grandiose terms. Private sector firms have in the past had good reason to be more attuned to cost and profit concepts than to notions of the efficiency of economic mechanisms. Yet these narrowly commercial and broader economic concepts are now intimately related, even within the arena of interest of private firms. The various individual efforts of firms in implementing new information systems contribute to changes in the operation of the economy as a whole, as well as its performance. Quite directly, new networked IT systems change the technological means by which information is transferred between economic agents. Those systems provide new means of enabling the information transactions that are a strictly necessary condition for the economy to operate at all.

Viewed in this light, the significance of firms' micro-economic decisions to adopt IT innovations can be seen in broader, policy-relevant terms. The diffusion and use of IT innovations results in changes in the operation of the economy, as well as in local changes to specific activities and patterns of organization within firms and industries. For this reason, effective investigation of the economic significance of IT requires analysis that goes beyond parochial concerns with the automation and extension of firms' means of production. Certainly these industrial and commercial issues are relevant but they are, so to speak, the trees not the forest. At a time of widespread international economic change, it is prudent also to consider their broader macro-economic context.

In short, the development of an economy's information infrastructure is not just a matter of firms improving access to corporate information, gaining a ‘competitive edge’, shortening response times in the face of volatile markets, and so on. These Victorian revivalist notions of how to succeed in business may still be pertinent to the micro-decisions of small-scale IT investors, yet they are outdated as a basis for understanding the role of IT in the information economy. Infrastructural IT systems form the technological basis of the fundamental operation of advanced economies in the closing years of the twentieth century. Information infrastructure is thus a matter of strategic importance for organizations in all sectors of the economy.

The significance of infrastructural systems development for the IT sector is more than just a gigantic sales opportunity. It means defining and producing structural conditions for all forms of economic activity which, in turn, influence demands and possibilities for further technological and economic development including, of course, continuing demands for more IT. Put less formally, the IT industries have in effect got their hand on the tiller of the economy but appear not to realise it yet, or perhaps they do and that is why they are keeping quiet about it.

## Policy relevance

The analytical framework outlined above is founded on the relation between two propositions. Firstly, the micro-level decisions and actions of IT users, investors and other economic agents take place within a political and economic environment that is strongly influenced by macro-level policy making. Secondly, part of the rationale for macro IT policy formulation, notably on innovation, information and communication issues, is derived from actual or potential implications of the micro-level IT activities of economic agents. Rather than continuing to separate two related levels of analysis, this framework allows interactions between the micro and macro aspects of IT to be investigated. The purpose of this section is to indicate the potential complexity of those interactions and their strategic importance for both public and private policy formulation.

The key issue here concerns the variety of parties interested in infrastructural IT policy. In the private corporate sector, different types and sizes of firms across the full spectrum of industries may be expected to have different vested interests in the promotion of improved systems for economic communication. Equally, they may be expected to hold different views about the usefulness (and value) of innovations in IT infrastructure, such as new telecommunications services (recent empirical work on this issue is reported by Capello and Williams (1992)). In the public sector, a similar diversity of interest may be expected amongst policy makers with responsibilities for development in local, regional, national, intra-European Community and international economies (Estabrooks and Lamarche, 1987).

Considered together, private and public sector interests in the use of infrastructural IT systems to support and develop economic activity present a substantial matrix of policy positions. In each case, macro policy formulation will be related to particular micro-level activities. For example, local and regional policy makers may seek to enhance small firms' access to ISDN services by trying to influence the rate of implementation of digital telecommunications exchanges. By contrast, national policy makers may be more concerned to influence the activities of large firms in adopting critical-mass IT innovations such as electronic funds transfer (EFT), electronic data interchange (EDI) and satellite-based communications (e.g. VSAT systems). Equally, small firms may have to adjust their investment behaviour according to the availability of, for them, useful telecommunications services, whereas large firms may be in a strong position to influence the direction of national and intra-community IT policy. As suggested earlier, the macro and micro levels of IT-related decision making are mutually influential; the nature of that influence, across the matrix of policy positions, at present remains open to empirical investigation. However, the scale of the issues to be addressed by policy makers and by managers can be indicated by illustration.

Two contemporary economic policy issues are briefly considered below as challenges to management thinking in relation to the use of IT as an infrastructural technology. These are firstly, property rights legislation and practice, and secondly the European economic environment. Each issue presents theoretical difficulties; neither is a straightforward problem amenable to a ‘correct’ solution. Each one has immediate and practical implications for the management of organizations that depend increasingly on IT-based communication, both for their internal operations and for their engagement with other economic agents. These two issues have been chosen as initial illustrations of a growing coincidence of interest between policy makers and managers in the effective operation of the European information economy.

## Property rights

The first challenge to management thinking comes from the problem of property rights in information. Only some types of information in the economy are currently protected by copyright and patent legislation. Other types, notably various forms of knowledge, cannot be protected in the same way. This means that many of the resources and commodities in an information economy, including much of the available technology, are not covered by property rights legislation. How, then, is it possible to manage the production and distribution of information such that appropriate individuals or organizations gain and retain value from those activities?

The answer to this question is far from clear as it involves, at the least, related problems of property rights, legal liabilities and economic incentives (Monk, 1989, pp. 176–9). It is evident from the extent of production and distribution activities that some ad hoc solutions have been found by managers in the information sector, notably in the software industry (Perelman, 1991, Chapter 5). Yet the absence of comprehensive property rights in information militates against traditional economic incentives for its production. Information producers face the same problem as creators of unprotected technological inventions; they have little incentive to incur the R & D costs of production if they are then unable to appropriate most if not all of the commercial gains to be made from sales of the new product (see, for example, Dasgupta and Stoneman (1987) and Stoneman (1987)).

However, stringent legal protection of the property rights of information producers would tend to restrict the overall distribution of information in the economy. Given that information production is a costly process undertaken for the purpose of gaining value, any inhibition of information distribution would imply restriction of the availability of a valuable resource to firms and individuals in the economy. Different potential users of that information might well face incentives to avoid legal restrictions by becoming producers themselves and replicating the effort of invention. From a macro-economic perspective, widespread duplication of information production would be inefficient and wasteful; it would be in effect a mirror-image of the inefficiency that would be created by inadequate legal protection of information property.

Property rights in information form an essential part of the legal environment for transactions and communication in an information economy. Information transactions are fundamental to the operation of such an economy but their legal status is being undermined by technological change, particularly in IT. Current efforts by the European Community to draft legal protection of copyrights in IT software illustrate the problem. According to CEC (1991), the Commission's aim is to outlaw software piracy, which may be seen as a form of theft, yet to allow ‘reverse engineering’, an activity which is seen by the Commission to be necessary to promote technical innovation. They explicitly seek to allow independent debugging of products and inter-working between IT systems.

From a technical perspective, there is a very fine line between piracy and reverse engineering, one which relies to a perilous extent on the intentions and interpretations of the software copier. Originality in information production is notoriously difficult to define, especially in the creation of software products such as database, spreadsheet or operating systems that perform generic functions. While the CEC might be applauded for its bravery in attempting to resolve this sensitive issue (that is, a potential conflict of interest between macro-level information policy and micro-level IT activities) doubts must be reserved about the practicality and robustness of the currently proposed solution.

## The European information economy

The second illustration of how IT infrastructure might challenge management thinking concerns the changing economic environment in Europe, consideration of which must address two related questions. The first is that of the future of the Single European Market, due to be implemented by the end of 1992. The second is the shape of ‘Europe’ itself, which has become open to debate (yet again) in the light of political upheavals since 1989 along the eastern border of the European Community (EC). In each case, the role of infrastructural IT systems in economic activities and mechanisms is very likely to influence the practical consequences of international political negotiations. The key aspect of IT here is telecommunications.

The degree of success or failure of the Single European Market will be determined in large part by the ability of economic agents, whether individuals or organizations, to communicate with each other. Aside from obstacles created by differences of language between nations, economic communication will require technological systems that are compatible, effective and efficient. This is the reason that underlies the European Commission's enthusiasm for telecommunications policy, its support for an integrated market for telecommunications equipment and services and its concern for the role of communications infrastructure in regional economic development (CEC, 1988; 1991).

Similar considerations apply to the changing relationships between the EC and its eastern neighbours. The telecommunications infrastructure of the EC is much more highly developed and sophisticated than the systems in Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Bulgaria and Romania or, further afield, those of the Baltic republics, the Russian Federation and the other ex-soviet states (CEC, 1991; 1992). This difference in technology represents a significant obstacle to the development of trade between organizations in the information economy of the EC and those in the industrial economies to the east. (A number of aspects of infrastructural IT are currently subjects of programmes of action by the EC, including the PHARE programme of aid for the restructuring of the economies of eastern and central Europe (CEC, 1991).)

An analogous situation exists within part of the EC itself. Organizations in the newly reunified German economy face severe communication problems due to the disparity between the telecommunications infrastructures of the ex-FRG and ex-GDR regions of the country. However, these difficulties have been recognized by policy makers and efforts have been made to effect rapid improvements to available infrastructure, including through the use of satellite-based systems (CEC, 1990, pp. 72–5).

Managers preparing to exploit new opportunities for trade across the EC and beyond thus face a challenge to traditional thinking. Instead of merely being concerned with problems of production and commerce, they must now consider the means by which managerial and exchange transactions can occur. Put bluntly, the critical question is to what extent the technological means exist for the operation of basic economic mechanisms, such as the establishment of contracts and payment of debts for the supply of goods and services. While much else about the 'new Europe' is uncertain, managers may be sure that its transactional infrastructure, and hence its economic environment, will be more complex and more variable in quality than in the past. Given this context of uncertainty about fundamental economic means, processes and mechanisms, traditional management assumptions about how market-based economies operate must be open to question.

Infrastructural IT systems are not so much means to competitive advantage for organizations within markets as, increasingly, the very means by which markets and other economic mechanisms operate. Gaining advantage from an understanding of the economic role of infrastructural technology means looking beyond the bounds of immediate corporate activities. The changing environment of policy and practice in the information economy sets the context of contemporary management decisions. It forms the background against which the viability of strategies must be evaluated; it is this changing background that directly affects both the performance of organizations in the economy and the possibilities for their longer term development.

## Conclusions

The analytical framework sketched in this paper inevitably asks more questions than it answers; investigation of the economic significance of infrastructural IT systems requires a new research agenda if it is to benefit managers and practitioners. Two major research questions remain unanswered. The first is that of how the many and varied interests of micro-economic agents (especially firms) interact with those of macro policy makers. For example, which types of firms are subject to which levels of policy? To what extent do policy makers take an active interest in firms' formulation of corporate IT strategy? What are the potential implications for private sector firms of the success or failure of public IT and information policies, notably at the European Community level?

The second major research question is simply: how is it possible to value information? The question may be simple but the answer is certainly not. At present, economic theory can offer little help in this regard other than to indicate the inapplicability of conventional concepts of value. Indeed, the valuation of information is arguably one of the most difficult and intractable problems faced by economists, whether as theorists or as industrial practitioners. As Machlup (1962) and others have pointed out, there can be no unitary measure of output from information production that is statistically independent of the inputs employed. Unlike the outputs from physical production activities, which are definable and countable, e.g. so many personal computers, so many tons of steel, information production necessarily results in output that is heterogeneous. That is, all units of output are different by definition; ‘more information’ means a greater variety of different items of information, not more copies of the same item.

This apparently innocuous observation has a profound implication for the conduct and management of organizations. There is no method yet available to measure productivity in the information sector. Specifically, there is no method for measuring productivity and, hence, efficiency in any of the organizations in the dominant sector of the economy. How, then, might it be possible to manage the production of information on a rational basis? The term ‘use value’ has been used in this paper to indicate some as yet undefined concept of value that can be applied to resources that are not subject to exchange as commodities in markets, such as firms’ in-house databases. This has assumed that some concept of value of employed resources is strictly necessary for any form of managerial decision making. That assumption must, of course, be open to question. What is clear, however, is that conventional accounting concepts of value such as costs and prices, assets and liabilities cannot be developed easily by managers in an economy where so much information is produced and used yet so little of which is treated as a commodity.

Managerial assessment of infrastructural IT systems and their implications thus requires a recognition of the need for a change of analytical level. Rather than continually focussing on intra-organizational problems of IT implementation and use, managerial analysis must recognize the national and international dimensions of IT as an infrastructural technology, specifically in terms of national and international economic policy and practice. These are difficult issues, certainly, yet the IT community's historical experience of dealing with its industry's unprecedented growth over three decades provides room for optimism. Analytical perspectives on IT have changed in the past in response to rapid technological development. It is the contention of this paper that they will need to do so again.

## Biographical note

Dr Peter Monk is a Lecturer in Management Information Systems in the Department of Management Science at the University of Strathclyde. His experience includes both technical and economic consultancy in the IT industry, as well as research on the economics of IT and innovation at the Universities of Bradford, Newcastle and York. He is joint author of Technology and Competition in the International Telecommunications Industry and author of Technological Change in the Information Economy (both published in 1989 by Pinter Publishers). His research interests currently include requirements analysis and the economics of information and large scale communication systems.
