# Teleworking: an information technology tool for integrated broadband communication development in rural areas of Europe

T. TSILIGIRIDES

Informatics Laboratory, Agricultural University of Athens, 75 Iera Odos, 11855 Athens, Greece

Information technology has an increasing impact in the development of rural areas but the development and implementation of telematic services in these areas present special problems such as the appropriate mix of communication technologies, the commercial prospects of recovering the cost of investment, the social problems etc., which currently limit their effective use. With the recent introduction of narrowband ISDN (Integrated Services Data Networks) in Europe it is now possible to offer various services on a single subscriber line. In addition, with the development of both high reliable fibre-optic transmission and very fast switching systems, the introduction of broadband ISDN can also be considered. Unfortunately, the less favoured regions of EC (European Community) lack infrastructure. The development in these areas will be a gradual process. Large investments will be required, and the systems installed will have a lifetime of some decades. Therefore the right decisions on the chosen technologies and on the appropriate system configurations have to be taken to ensure success. In this work, the teleworking prospects as an IT tool for rural development are studied in detail. The motivation, the technology and the prosperity of teleworking in the less favoured regions of the EC are analysed and discussed. The analysis considers the existing telecommunication infrastructure in each one of the six peripheral member states in relation with the development and implementation of some important telematic systems in these areas. The main features of the proposed technologies as well as the establishment of the possible cost effective strategies for IBC (Integrated Broadband Communication) implementation in rural areas is also provided. It appears that teleworking is a key element for rural development and can be implemented through the realization of the IBCNs (IBC Networks). By taking lessons from some of the most important experimental initiatives which have already been introduced in some countries, useful recommendations in the policy to be adopted by the EC are drawn and presented.

## Introduction

Information technology is having an increasing impact on every day lives, even on those living in rural areas. It can be defined as the acquisition, storage, processing, transformation and dissemination of information (audio, image, data). Three major sub-fields can be identified; microelectronics, computers and telecommunications. During the last years, genetic engineering, a new sub-field is likely to add significantly to future advances.

Over the past 20 years the IT environment world-wide entered a period of rapid change brought about by the convergence of several factors, most of them related. These included the rapid evolution in information and communication technologies, the appearance of new teleservices and bureau services, the emergence of new market trends and the rise of a political view that forced a critical review of monopoly, state enterprise and regulation. The most dramatic changes in recent times fall in the decrease in the cost of storing information electronically. As a result, computers have already progressed from large central sites processing large volumes of data to more local installations where smaller amounts of data can be easily stored and retrieved. This information can then be manipulated and transformed to the style of output required by the user.

The rapid evolution of information and communication technologies in the 1970s and 1980s had a major impact on work patterns, but within traditional organizational structures. The transition to the information based activities which will dominate the economies of the developed countries in the 21st century will have much more influence on where and how people work. The introduction of multimedia technology will enable a new wider range of work to be decentralized through the use of telecommunications. The next generation of office equipment will enable the companies to gain competitive advantage by cutting overheads and concentrating on their activities, by right sizing and out-sourcing for specialized services and by decentralizing to avoid the cost of urban congestion. These trends can only be realized through the substantial growth of telework.

Teleworking has been defined as a way of working in which the work is carried out at a distance from the employer or contractor for whom it is intended (Brain and Page, 1991). A special kind of teleworking is the telecommuting in which the motivation is the reduction of the daily commute trips. According to Niles (1990) the telecommuting has been defined as sending the work to worker instead the worker to work; the partial or total substitution by telecommunication technology with or without the aid of computers for the commute to and from work. In the following both terms will be used synonymously.

Teleworking has been made possible by recent advances in both information and communication technologies allowing users to have access to information from outside a traditional office environment. Original ideas of teleworking were based upon the substitution of homework for work in the office. This reflected the new possibilities brought about by the integration of communications with computers in the early 1970s. In this context, the idea of a teleworker emerged as a worker who could utilize a home terminal to interact with both computer and other workers using electronic mail. This approach when combined with the use of the telephone became the core technological model for teleworking. Many jobs ranging from order entry to telesales can be carried out using this approach.

During the 1980s there were a number of technical developments which changed this model. A typical corporate IT environment consisted of a distributed network based around a set of local area networks interconnected by digital lines and multiplexing equipment. A central main frame was usually replaced by personal computers. In large corporations, digital telephony from local private automatic branch exchanges were mixed in with digital traffic from local area networks sent over broadband (more than 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ ) lines.

Today, there is a great divergence between the home communications environment, still dependent on modems and analogue lines and the corporate network which can utilize new developments such as high bandwidth, local area networks, digital trunks and sophisticated multiplexing facilities. Large corporations have already begun to utilize video communications by connecting corporate conferencing sites into their private digital networks. This situation allows the homeworker to carry out a range of tasks using the combination of the local processing power of the personal computers and the dial-up facilities of the corporate network. The integration of telephone and fax with personal computer allows many jobs, using non-textual forms of information to be teleworked. Today, more than ten million people in Europe could be considered teleworkers.

In addition to the rapid growth in all aspects of computers and communications, a further level of convergence will take place in the next decade. This convergence is about the integration of computers, communication and television creating a new multimedia environment which will have extremely profound implications in all aspects of life, creating a completely different approach to the way teleworking should be viewed.

The paper is organized as follows. In the following section the relation between the Integrated Broadband Communication (IBC) and rural development is studied in detail. The typology of European Community (EC) regions with respect to IBC as well as the existing situation in telecommunications in each one the less developed member states of the Community (Greece, Ireland, Italy, Portugal, Scotland and Spain) is described with aim to examine the effects in rural development policies. The introduction of some important telematic systems using IBCNs (IBC Networks) in these areas is also examined in this section. In the third section, the introduction of teleworking and its effects in rural development are discussed in detail. Teleworking typology helps in recognizing the telecommunication needs. Advances in multimedia technology and in networking infrastructure such as in radio, in mobile and in satellite systems are the main contribution towards the development of teleworking as an IT tool for rural development. The teleworking policy to be adopted by the EC is examined in the fourth section, where the main initiatives are reviewed and the main lessons drawn are provided in the form of recommendations. Finally, in the last section, the conclusions are drawn.

## IBC and rural development in the EC

## Motivation

The dominant trend since the beginning of the industrial revolution has been from rural to urban growth. People and particularly the younger job seeking population moved to the urbanizing areas where better paid and more varied jobs were available. As a result, rural areas in the EC have experienced a steady decline in job opportunities for the younger population, as well as in quality of education, health-care and commercial services. Today, the common problems in rural areas of EC are the declining agricultural revenues, the rising unemployment, the loss of young people to better paid jobs in towns and cities and the relative decline in the availability of public services (health and education) compared with those offered in the urban areas. In addition, the enlargement of the community in the 1980s aggravated the difficulties with the common agricultural policy. Over 30% of farmers are now under-employed and half are aged over 55.

The 1980s have produced further growth in new industries dependent on information services and communications. According to Johnston (1992), over 50% of the employment now involves use of information management and telematic systems. Eighty per cent of new jobs are created by small business and 70% are in the service and information sectors. It has been predicted that by the year 2000, the great majority of jobs will be in the service sector and that a large portion of people working in this sector will be able to work from their homes.

New employment creation in peripheral and rural areas together with policies of support for training better healthcare, and better management of local resources are emerging as the dominant objectives in the regional and rural development. In this respect teleworking is a very important aspect in rural development. As Johnston (1993) reported, about five million new jobs will need to be created in rural areas of EC over the next decade to maintain the current levels of population and prosperity. While this is a major challenge, particularly in those countries with substantial agricultural employment, it is only about 5% of the new jobs that on current trends will be created in the more dynamic urban centres over this period. If the development of new organizations structures for telework can decentralize about 10% of new employment, not only would many of the problems of regional and rural economic development be eased, but significant progress could be made in the decongestion of major cities.

The determination of which activities will benefit from advanced communications services will depend on a number of factors. These include the number of discrete steps in the production process and the degree of information content in them, the extent of gains to be made from centralization and coordination of production, marketing and distribution process, the number of transactions between firms, customers and plants in a sector, and finally the type and amount of non-price competition, especially that based on rapid market response. Both the public (in the sense of universal service) and private sectors are likely to benefit most from broadband services. Activities in the public sector include health care, education, agriculture (cooperatives), community services (transportation, police etc.) and banking. Activities in the private sector include industry, shipping, education tourism, entertainment, press, retailing, banking, fishing, residential and building. More details are given by Tsiligirides (1993).

## Typology of EC regions with respect to IBC

In assuming that based on IBC technology we can develop applications of sufficient importance to merit consideration (a major and still unsubstantiated assumption), regions have to be formalized into a classification system according to which they can be evaluated in terms of a set of characteristics. This approach reduces the complexity of the issues to three types of regions.

The first type comprises regions that can reasonably expect to see the introduction of IBC on a commercial basis, with a return to investors achieved within an acceptable short period based solely on income from tariffs. Such regions are characterized by high density populations, high gross domestic products, well-paid skilled workers, good existing infrastructures and strong sectors in using advanced communications. They require little special policy consideration in order to create an appropriate supportive environment. Such regions have been the main subject of EC policy and programme initiatives. Only some large urban areas of the less developed countries of Greece, Ireland, Italy, Portugal, Scotland and Spain might fall into this category.

The second type of regions cannot expect the introduction IBC to be a viable proposition, if this will be viewed in a narrow commercial basis. This type might include declining industrial regions, or fast-growing less developed regions which might be rural, with reasonable general infrastructures and growth prospects in sectors important to advanced communications. In such cases the IBC could play a central role in a regional development strategy provided that the areas have been targeted by the national government for special attention. The return on investment would be calculated in this context. Clearly, the volume of investment required is likely to be available only at EC level. Although the current EC policy on telecommunications may present some obstacles to this approach, some recommendations will be provided.

The majority of the less favoured regions fall into the third type. The characteristics of this type of regions include low population density, difficult terrain, low gross domestic product and incomes, poor general infrastructures, demographic problems and poor educational facilities. Such regions would receive a better rate of return on alternative investment strategies such as road, rail and air infrastructures, training and educational facilities etc. that would bring them into the second type, or into different development trajectories.

From the above it appears that the second type of regions represent the best opportunity for significant broadband networks development of EC provided that IBC applications of sufficient importance, like teleworking, will arise.

## Existing telecommunications infrastructures in the less developed EC member states

Value added services have been a major opportunity for rural development, but the peripheral regions of EC lack infrastructure on telecommunications. The 1980s saw the emergence for the first time of a coherent EC policy on telecommunications. It occurs in the form of several separate but related objectives drawn up in the Green Paper on the development of a common market for the telecommunications services and equipment. These promote the creation of an advanced European industry and service providers (CEC, 1987).

While the introduction of competition into the telecommunication market of each member state of EC began before the publication of the Green Paper in 1987 (CEC, 1987) the process of adjusting the national legislation in accordance with the market reform has only recently entered into a new phase. Following the adoption of the community directives, member states have substantially modified their national legislation, or they are in a revision stage to make them more compatible with the community law. Common fundamental principles in each of these laws are the separation of the regulatory functions from the network operation, the liberalization of terminal equipment, the liberalization of services and the maintenance of special rights over the network infrastructure and the telephone services. However, the methods used to apply these principles vary.

A memorandum of understanding has been signed by most CEPT (European Posts and Telecommunications Conference) member countries which aims to implement a European Broadband Interconnection Trial, known as EBIT, which will provide a testbed for broadband in Europe, providing switched 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ using either satellite or a combination of satellite and terrestrial technology. It may allow less favoured regions access to an international broadband switched network, as well as access to islands of broadband developed in the core countries. Support of this scheme should be given by the less favoured regions as a means to participate in a European-wide broadband pilot scheme. The ISDN (Integrated Services Data Network) and the public land mobile network GSM will probably not cover all European countries with all the assigned services. The solution proposed is to install at an early stage an overall long distance digital broadband transmission network covering the whole region of Europe in a grid structure. Thus, all regional and existing networks can be interconnected on a Europe-wide level. The alternative is that two-speed Europe could emerge, with extremely good communication facilities between the core regions, and less favoured regions lagging behind.

In the following, an overview of the current status in telecommunication infrastructure, as well as in the changes that have taken place and those currently underway in the less advanced member states will be provided. To facilitate the comparison a summary is made (see Table 1). The aim is to explore the main difficulties in the introduction of IBC services, such as the teleworking, and to propose the most appropriate solutions to implement these services in rural areas of EC.

## Greece

Greece has recently passed a new legislation on telecommunications which confirms the existing monopoly in the PSTN (Public Switched Telephone Network) operation and considers liberal policies on the value added network services. The government (1990–93) intends to privatize 49% of OTE, the Greek PTT (Post Telephone and Telegraph Authority) but it is very unlikely that this level of privatization will be finally achieved. The terminal equipment area is under competition but the market is generally weak. The major part of telephone exchanges currently in use is still analogue consisting mainly of microwave radio and coaxial cable systems. Some pulse code modulation systems have been used in the network as 'pair-gain' systems.

Digitization of the PSTN has already started. By the end of 1992 almost 8% of the local exchanges, 50% of the trunk exchanges, 55% of the transmission systems and 100% of the telex system were digital. Some of the most important Greek islands and cities are now connected with fibre optic link. The HELLASPAC, the X.25 PPSDN (Public Packet Switched Digital Network), giving data transfer rate 9.6 kbits s $^{-1}$ and in some cases 19.2 kbits s $^{-1}$ , will be expanded in direct relation to demand. Interconnections with the telex and the videotex system HELLASTEL have already been established. Greece has signed a memorandum of understanding with the CEPT (European Posts and Telecommunications Conference) for providing ISDN services by the end of 1993. For this purpose the HELLASCOM network providing point-to-point access of 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ unstructured channel, or of 30 × 64 kbits s $^{-1}$ structured channels, or multiple of 64 kbits s $^{-1}$ channels have already been established. This pre-ISDN phase will continue during 1993 covering most of the major urban areas of Greece. Interconnections with other European ISDN networks are not planned until 1994, when all European PTTs have to conform with the common ISDN standards, like EURO-ISDN.

Greece is the only member state that does not yet maintain a mobile telephony system although it has already implemented a radio-paging network. Recently a decision to liberalize mobile terrestrial services has been taken. Under this scheme GSM will be installed and operate in the Athens area by the end of 1993 and later in other areas of Greece. Although the new legislation is flexible, no licences for satellites in the domestic network have been awarded yet. In addition, some restrictions have been forwarded recently in the distribution of television and radio.

## Ireland

Ireland passed legislation confirming a licence on the Telecom Eirann, the national PTT, to operate a monopoly in telephony and telephony-based services. Telecom Eirann will also become a carrier of television and radio through its network. The new legislation reinforces its monopoly but also provides procedures for licencing value added services and third party resale of lease lines.

The legislation also makes provision of regulatory and operational functions.

Ireland is relatively advanced in the digitization of its network, owing to the major investment programme completed recently. About 50% of customers are connected to digital exchanges and the main transmission routes in the country are now digital. Transmission is provided on digital microwave radio systems, digital coaxial cable systems and new fibre optic links. This level of digitization is exceptional given that the country became fully automatic only in 1987.

Over the next planning period it is proposed to fully digitize the transmission network and to increase the percentage of digital switching to 60%. There will be no changes in the telex network as there is currently no demand. The PPSDN is expected to grow using the national transmission network. Mobile telephony is scheduled to be extended in phases to cover most of the country. There are no plans to use satellite connections in the national network. Up to now two independent trials have been carried out to introduce ISDN services using different equipment. Full ISDN services will be provided in 1993 in accordance with the memorandum of understanding already signed with CEPT.

The situation in Northern Ireland differs. Transmission network is currently a mixture of analogue and digital technology. The switching network is analogue, but by the beginning of 1993 it will be digital. The PPSDN is integrated into the British Telecom network giving a sufficient configuration for the period. Telex connection is provided via a concentrator in the British Telecom network. Mobile telephony is not expected to extend beyond the main population concentrations and there are no plans to use satellite links in the public telecommunications network. Lastly, ISDN services will be available but the demand in rural areas will dictate the penetration of the services.

## Italy

The general framework for telecommunications legislation is provided by the Telecommunications Code which was updated in 1990 providing technical definitions for network architecture and function and classifies services. A number of state-controlled companies exist which maintain the exclusive rights for the installation and exploitation of the public telecommunications network and services. Since there is a desire to integrate these separate companies and split the operational and regulatory functions a revised ten year plan is expected to clarify their roles and their responsibilities. Both the issues of public licences and global legislative framework refer to global transparency or non-discriminatory requirements have already been discussed.

An investment programme is being finalized with aims to extend the infrastructure, to accelerate the development and to realize a set of pilot projects introducing telematic services, particularly in south Italy which suffers from lack of infrastructure. No commercial ISDN services are currently provided while there is no restriction in the distribution of television and radio.

## Portugal

In Portugal, telecommunications were totally controlled by the state. According to the basic law on telecommunications activated in 1989 the participation of foreign companies in the capital of public or complementary telecommunications carriers is limited to 25%. Three state-controlled operators share the total provision of the network infrastructure and basic services which include fixed telephony, telex and data transmission services. Considerations have been given to the privatization of some of the newer, non-basic services classified as complementary and of added value. The first are those associated with networks based on leased lines but with their own switching or processing unit. These would include mobile GSM, radio paging and videotex services. Value added services refer to telefax, voice mail and audiotex services. Access and usage conditions were established in 1990. Thus, value added services are subject to simple authorization procedure while complementary services using fixed networks must meet certain technical and financial obligations to receive licence.

The Portuguese transmission network is being digitized very quickly, mainly using a locally manufactured primary pulse code modulation system. The Portuguese Atlantic islands are connected to the mainland by submarine cable. Satellite connections are used also for communications to and from islands. Digital systems make up about 25% of the regional transmission network. The switching network is mainly analogue but digitization has already started. The approach to digitizing the network varied for different networks. In cities there will be an overlay digital network for customers requiring enhanced services. Medium sized towns and city suburbs will continue to use electromechanical units until digital equipment becomes cost-attractive.

Transmission will show a greater penetration of digital technology as it will be used primarily in the trunk network. The telex network will expand to meet demand without major configuration changes. The packet switching network will expand to meet the demand. The mobile telephony network will have three switching centres and sufficient base stations to cover the major urban areas. ISDN services will be available on a commercial basis during 1993. A pilot phase is interconnecting a number of islands via CCITT#7 signalling system providing experimental services to selected users.

## Scotland

Scotland is controlled by the UK telecommunications legislation activated in 1984, under which licences came up for review in 1989 without major changes. There have been a number of small electronic exchanges in service for several years. These are currently being enhanced to perform in a manner similar to digital units and this, combined with replacements that have occurred recently in the switching network, results in the region having slightly less than one third of its subscribers connected to digital units. The transmission network is a mixture of analogue and digital with approximately 60% of the capacity links being digital. The junction network employs primary pulse code modulation systems for about 35% of its requirements with very limited use of optical fibres. The remainder of the junction network comprises low frequency analogue connections.

The long term plan, particularly for the north area of Scotland, is to have the trunk and junction network upgraded in line with the switching network, to achieve a 98% digital network by 1995. Telex switches will be replaced by appropriate concentrators. The packet switching network and the mobile telephony will be expanded as necessary to meet demand. There are no plans to use satellites for public telecommunications purposes within the region. Permission to carry television and radio are open to interested parties. Lastly ISDN services are already available on a trial basis.

## Spain

The basic legal framework for telecommunications in Spain was provided by the Telecommunications Law activated by the end of 1987. This law distinguishes between basic and value added services and provides the separation of the regulatory and operational functions of the telecommunications monopoly. Telephonica, a 47% stated-owned company is responsible for the provision of telegraph, telex and basic telephone services. Foreign participation in Telephonica is limited to a maximum of 25% of total investment. No general regulation for value added services has been issued yet. Therefore, concession is granted if the service cannot be provided by the public network. The Spanish network is developing in a similar fashion to other less favoured regions in EC, in that approximately 6% of lines and 18% of links are digital.

The administration planned to have 40% of all lines and 70% of all transmission links digital by the end of 1992. In addition the packet switching network will be developed by adding an additional layer to its hierarchy, providing digital subscriber access at 64 kbits s $^{-1}$ . The mobile telephony network will cover 23% of the country which equates to accessing 70% of vehicles in use in the country. ISDN was being introduced in a pilot project in 1988 leading to commercial trials in 1990. By 1993 the provincial capitals and major population centres will be included in the ISDN network. Plans do exist to connect satellites with the existing PPSDN.

## Telematic systems in rural areas of the EC

The development and implementation of telematic systems in rural areas present special problems such as the appropriate mix of communication technologies, the commercial prospects of recovering the cost of investment and the social problems which currently limit the effective use of these new technologies. The peripheral regions probably do not have the appropriate infrastructure in telecommunications. Their introduction in these areas will be a gradual process. The investments required will be large, and the systems installed will have a lifetime of some decades. It is therefore essential to ensure that the right decisions will be taken on the technologies and on the system configurations.

Value added services have been seen as a major opportunity for rural development. It is an area where a competitive market has been developed in EC member states. Based on recent information given by Borthwick (1992), the consumption of value added services by the EC business community in 1990 was estimated around 3 billion ECUs plus another 1 billion ECUs for residential use. The revenue from these markets in 1992 approached 5 and 1.5 billion ECUs respectively. The largest single value added service market was the use of on-line database which was estimated to be between 1.5 and 2 billion ECUs in 1990. Other important markets were the reservation systems and funds transfer estimated between 300–500 million ECUs each.

Isolated users and distant communities have to be connected to the new services and networks. Depending on how and where users are located as well as which services are implemented, different architectural cost effective solutions have to be used, taking into account the specific user needs and the environmental characteristics. Cable connections are very costly and unsuitable in the difficult rural terrain. Thus, radio, mobile and satellite VSAT (Very Small Aperture Terminal) technologies are very likely to be used. Moreover, due to the potential economy in network infrastructures the integration of radio, mobile and VSAT services is envisaged as an appropriate technology to cope with the problems inherent in rural areas. This will give an increasing number of users access to a large number of ISDN and IBC services regardless of whether they are fixed or mobile.

The IBC should allow the coexistence on one network of all the services, including the transmission of moving images. It should provide the infrastructure and service functions for any existing combination of services from narrowband to broadband with a view to their functional integration. Advances in switching and transmission techniques, as well as in the intelligent networks will allow these services to be combined easily and mixed. In the long term, the communication needs of business and individual users will converge. Therefore it will be essential to adopt an IBC strategy which will combine these needs to achieve economies of scale. The strategy should also take into account the high-capacity needs in information transport of some large electronic users.

The reduction in the cost of transmission is certainly a desirable objective, but it would appear to be even more important to integrate services functionally in such a way as to be able to offer new, value added services in those forms best suited to the users. One of the most attractive characteristics of IBC environment is that it will give service providers and users the ability to integrate different services in a flexible or even ad hoc manner. For example, this would allow the user to add a data service to the existing voice transmission service, to temporarily increase the communications capability in order to set up a video-conference, or to integrate computing, telecommunications and audio-visual services.

Europe has to develop an advanced system on telecommunications which will be able to create new opportunities for employment and for the promotion of international competition. A more likely scenario is the gradual, but ultimately limited, spread of large private broadband networks among branches of giant corporations, associations of producers and suppliers, and major metropolitan areas. There is no clear evidence to suggest that applications exist of sufficient commercial or social importance to justify the high level of investment required. In addition, it is not yet known whether the IBCNs will be available to smaller firms and domestic users on demand as in the case of public networks. Given the pressure of competing investment demands, it appears that to install an IBCN in all areas would be a waste of scarce resources. But it also implies that the dominance of major urban areas in the developed countries is likely to be reinforced by broadband.

## Teleworking and rural development

## Motivation

Experimentation with new telematic systems shows that teleworking has emerged as a key aspect in order to identify those areas of work to which IT can bring immediate and worthwhile benefits in rural areas. It offers the prospect of a great diversity of employment and helps to alleviate some of the structural and social problems in rural areas. It can help to bring a better demographic balance back into rural communities, but only if the efforts of business to promote teleworking are matched by the efforts made by the public authorities to ensure that education and health services in rural areas are as good as those offered in major cities. This trend gives peripheral and rural areas an opportunity to compete on an equal basis for the new employment opportunities.

The motivations for telework are numerous and affect the employers, the employees and the society. A summary of the advantages and disadvantages is provided in Table 2. Thus, firms or organizations from both private and public sectors are attracted to teleworking primarily because of the reduction in the costs. This can be achieved by arranging for employees to work part-time in lower cost areas, reducing demands on and costs of main offices. Cost savings can only be affected if they are not swamped by additional telecommunications costs and inordinated demands. Additional reasons are the access to new pools of skilled labour and a more flexible and stable workforce. Improvements in quality of life for employees can be a powerful reason for both employers and employees to introduce teleworking. Critical factors on the impact of workers' motivation, productivity, recruitment and retention are the additional flexibility in allocation of time, the reduction of community stress in combination with the increased community satisfaction, more quality time at home and the lower housing and commuting costs.

From the society point of view, teleworking is useful for environmental and economic development reasons. Furthermore, teleworking can play a significant role in job creation, particularly in areas of high unemployment, provided that a training link will be established. It also offers a way to balance the provision of economic opportunities between urban and rural areas, despite the declining opportunities in traditional rural occupations.

## Teleworking typology

There are many different forms of teleworking and many different bases from which it is implemented. The main current forms are part-time teleworking from home, and full-time or part-time teleworking from a centre. While teleworking has sometimes been identified or compared with home-working, the comparison is misleading and has tended to marginalize the potential of teleworking applications. Full-time teleworking from home will be the exception rather than the rule. The increasing tendency is for teleworkers either to be based at centres close to their homes on a full- or part-time basis, or to maintain their main base at their employers' offices while working on a split-site basis from home for one or two days per week.

## A typology of the teleworking can be:

The home teleworking, in which jobs are performed at home using basic telecommunication equipment such as personal computers, fax machines etc. The communication is based on analogue lines and modems.

The mobile teleworking, in which jobs are performed usually in non-fixed places, using mobile terminals, small computers, fax, audio, video, videophones and other types of equipment.

The teleworking centre, in which jobs are performed at the offices enabling people to work closer to their homes through the provision of sophisticated information technologies, allowing the decentralization of units and tasks.

The teleworking park, which is a combination of several teleworking centres to form a science, technology, business or industrial park, and finally:

The teleworking network, which is the organization of several units that are interconnected with other units of other organizations through the telematic networks. Teleworking networks are intensively used for advanced information technologies such as electronic mail, video-conferencing and electronic data systems, thus reducing the travelling in relation to the high level of interaction among the units.

## Delivery of teleworking services

From the teleworking prospective, technology can be seen as an attempt to reduce techno-economic constraints to proposed teleworking structures. Technical objectives for teleworking can all be reduced to the concept of location independence in such a way that any managerial structures can be implemented readily. Thus, a particular technology is relevant to teleworking progress in so far as it contributes to the goal of location independence.

The principal reason for providing the less favoured regions with broadband is that industries and multinational companies require teleworking services with other sites in other countries and also with their head offices. For Greece, Ireland, Portugal and Spain all these communications would be international. For Northern Ireland, North Scotland and Southern Italy, some of their telecommunications would be to core regions within the country. It is therefore necessary that these regions have international broadband gateways.

## Multimedia advances

In a multi-media environment a number of different media such as text, graphics, video, sound etc. are integrated together in an information system where they can be stored, manipulated and viewed as a single entity in support of applications. Combining media enables communication to be much more effective, particularly for teleworking.

Imaging transmission facilities would have distinct advantages for teleworking. A large class of jobs like printing and publishing, medical, agriculture, computer aid design, computer aid manufacturing, video production etc. are using the imaging technology. However, the conventional analogue lines are usually not suitable for teleworking, particularly in those cases where images play a large part in the work. Basic rate ISDN when combined with compression is suitable for work with still images and low-end videoconferencing. To support a more general multimedia environment including high quality video, at least 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ is required. When HDTV (High Definition TeleVision) becomes widespread then even higher rates will be required.

Currently the CCITT (International Consultative Committee for Telephones and Telegraphs) has evolved the H.261 standard for teleconferencing, whilst the ISO (International Standards Organization) has evolved the JPEG standard for still images and MPEG standard for video. The trend is towards common standards for the new range of high resolution imaging and television systems. According to Bedford (1991), there is an increasing realization that HDTV has important applications in a number of industrial areas. Thus, advances are expected in the convergence of workstation and HDTV technology as well as in the definition of a flexible set of standards for the interchange of both moving and still images with different resolutions and technical parameters from different application areas. Clearly, broadband video and multimedia services will have a significant impact in the scope and modalities of teleworking.

One of the most radical developments that has emerged during the last few years is the virtual presence, namely the ability, to act as if you were in a different environment. Current systems use a number of devices to provide an interface between the user and reality. Since virtual world can be presented by digital data it is possible for distant users to share one by utilizing telecommunications links. There are many important application areas of virtual presence in teleworking development. The first is the virtual meetings, which might be viewed as an extension of multimedia groupware concept. In this case personal interaction in the workplace is more realistic than using teleconferencing or videophony systems. The second application is the telepresence which offers the possibility of linking remote teleworkers with robots, mechanical equipment or instrumentation.

## Switching technology

In Europe, digital transmission is currently based on G.702 standard of CCITT, with a basic transmission rate $2 \, Mbits s^{-1}$ divided into $32 \times 64 \, kbits s^{-1}$ channels. In the current system each analogue voice channel is digitized at the exchange and multiplexed into a $2 \, Mbits s^{-1}$ frame for transmission. Digital transmission is normally used between exchanges but for large users it is possible to lease point-to-point connections based on either $64 \, kbits s^{-1}$ or $2 \, Mbits s^{-1}$ . Private corporate networks can be created using special equipment for multiplexing voice data and video into leased lines. The existence of these corporate networks with their range of local area networks based services is the major reason for the current divergence between facilities available to home and centre teleworking.

In many countries the advances in intergrated circuit technology have resulted in the public organizations in telecommunications gradually changing the mode of operation of the conventional switched telephone network to an all digital mode of working by introducing ISDN. Such networks can be used for both voice and data, the two services operating concurrently if this is desired. Because initially ISDN has to be deployed using existing network facilities, initial emphasis is placed on the narrowband ISDN, which operates between 64 kbits s $^{-1}$ to 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ . The broadband ISDN, extending the narrowband ISDN to several hundred Mbits s $^{-1}$ , depends on the large-scale use of wideband transmission media such as the optical fibre, and on the use of very high speed switching elements.

The bandwidth requirement of moving video is quite substantial. It depends on the picture quality. For a TV-quality transmission, a data rate greater than 100 Mbits s $^{-1}$ is usually required which can be reduced using data compression algorithms. Thus with 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ the loss of quality may be acceptable for many applications. This can be compared with 8 kbits $s^{-1}$ or less for digital voice transmission. Hence the basic rate ISDN bandwidth (64 kbits $s^{-1}$ ) is not adequate for transmission of digitized moving video, unless the data stream is highly compressed. Computer inter-working can also place severe demands on bandwidth, for example in the transmission of large graphic files.

The introduction of ISDN can be seen as a way of extending network digitization directly to the subscriber providing direct access to a range of facilities. However, ISDN is being accepted partially in different EC member states and is not universally available. The prime rate ISDN (2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ trunk standard) will be provided in teleworking centres, while the basic rate ISDN (2 B + D) offers digital services on standard analogue lines. It provides a new range of possibilities to home teleworking such as more rapid transmission of large data or graphics files, combined data and voice calls, videophone services at low frame rates and resolution etc.

Broadband ISDN will be the major development in communications during the next years. It will support a range of multimedia services rather than being primarily oriented towards telephony. Standards have not been defined yet by CCITT, but the key characteristics will be the much higher bandwidth based on 155 and 622 Mbits s $^{-1}$ , the new access and switching methods based on asynchronous transfer mode and the interconnection with local and metropolitan area networks offering a whole range of telecommuting services not currently available. Broadband ISDN requires complete rewiring with fibre-optic cable, or equivalent high bandwidth medium. Initial implementation of broadband ISDN will be based on the 'broadband islands' centred on existing teleworking and business centres. This approach will involve much lower risk to telecommunications operators. The development will be according to demand patterns and the cost will be more economic than the 'fibre-to-home' approach, which strongly depends on the distribution services such as the television.

## Radio systems

Traditionally, radio systems have been used to provide rural solutions. They also provide a temporary solution in cases where cable schemes are not to be implemented for some time period. In terms of frequency, radio systems are classified as HF, VHF, UHF, SHF line of sight and scatter, each one with some specific set of design rules. Single channel point to point VHF/UHF and multi-access systems are usually used for distribution applications. In this mode, each customer has sole access to the channel that he is allocated. In contrast, tropospheric scatter systems, large capacity multiplexed SHF and medium capacity multiplexed VHF/UHF systems are used for transfer and trunking applications. In this shared channel mode, a given number of radio channels allotted to an area is shared among a large number of customers. Since the radio link is not able to pass DC signal, conversion equipment is allowed to be used for customer signalling.

Two basic cell patterns can be adopted in case an area is to be covered by a number of radio base stations. For efficient use, frequency allocation arrangement into cells of a typical radius of about 30 km is required. Low capacity radio relay systems with capacity from 12 to 60 channels are useful for trunking purposes using frequencies from 360 to 470 KHz and from 790 to 960 KHz. High capacity radio relay systems can be used in the rural networks by providing all or part of their capacity. With the appropriate equipment some of 12, 24 or 120 channels of the total SHF link may be available to link some specific or local centres of the national network. Tropospheric scatter radio links can be used in rural networks providing 12 and 120 channels which are suitable in cases of difficult terrain such as connection of a long distant island to the mainland or to another island. Lastly, broadband radio schemes may involve community based broadband services using radio rather than installation of optic fibre. This may involve frequency allocation problems and the use of more expensive radio equipment than is already used to serve such rural customers. However, radio may still be indicated for dispersed customers in case the cost of optic fibre is high.

The radio topologies to be used would be similar for broadband and narrowband but some differences may arise due to the different propagation at SHF and UHF. New developments in wavelength systems operating at GHz have to be monitored. A major advantage of these systems is the possibility of using small size cheap antennas. In contrast, in some rural areas the problems associated with the propagation in adverse weather conditions could be a disadvantage.

## Mobile systems

A mobile radio system enables communication by radio from land stations to mobile stations and vice versa. It is an extension of the PSTN to moving vehicles and is usually explored in areas where the fixed telephone networks are highly developed. However, mobile radio service is a communication tool best suited for rural areas where telephone networks have not been extensively developed. The radio configuration zone can either be a mountain top, large output transmission system, or a multiple zone system. The first system allows coverage of a wide area from a single station and is convenient in rural applications. However, it could make frequency coordination with high density cellular systems difficult. The second system can be extended relatively easily according to the size or form of the service area. Spectrum efficiency is high but the circuit configuration becomes more complex.

At present, mobile telephony is only available on certain limited regions of the community and is based on specifications that are not universally compatible. With the introduction of a second generation digital cellular mobile telephony system, and with the GSM system allocating the frequency bands 905–915 MHz and 950–960 MHz to the new services, a single community system can be reached. GSM system has been operating from 1991. During the first years of operation it will leave many areas uncovered in the less favoured regions, primarily for economic reasons. Thus satellite systems have to be used as complementary to the GSM system.

One problem associated with the mobile usage in rural situations is billing. The normal policy for billing mobile customers is to charge a high trunk rate for the call, on the basis that the customer could be located anywhere in a given country. This high rate may not be acceptable for rural customers given that the normal usage will be from some fixed location. The necessary equipment and tariff modifications may be difficult to perform unless the system is designed initially with the possibility of rural customers in mind. Ideally a service available on the IBCN should also be available to mobile users. However, spectrum and economic constraints limit the practical possibilities. In the RACE definition phase it was concluded that it would be feasible to provide full speech service plus limited amounts of data services up to 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ on a unified basis. The dominant terminal would be a pocket terminal which implies mass usage. In order to produce a feasible specification for a portable terminal, some criteria must be satisfied such as the performance, cost, size, weight, power consumption etc.

## Satellite systems

The interconnection of isolated communities usually poses difficult problems because the terrestrial networks cannot be implemented. An alternative solution is to use satellites. Satellite technology has been unexplored in Europe, even though it is particularly suited to providing connectivity and support networks in regions of low population density. To a large extent this is due to PTTs' or ex-PTTs' policies in their respective countries. Within Europe, usage is strongly constrained by rules and regulations, both at a national and international level. In a number of European countries this situation is now changing as privatized PTTs or new operators in telecommunications are being licensed to provide satellite services. Standardization is helping, with ETSI (European Telecommunications Standards Institute) specifications being used as the basis for earth station authorization by all countries in EC.

Virtually all existing applications of satellite communications are corporate networks in which a satellite network is used to replace an existing terrestrial X25 network, a network using modems over an analogue leased lines or the PSTN. Data rates are rarely higher than 19.2 kbits s $^{-1}$ . In many cases connections are only one way. Both basic and primary rate ISDN are within the scope of the existing equipment. The second generation of equipment, capable of providing bandwidth on demand at rates of up to 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ , is currently under development. Existing satellites are capable of supporting broadband ISDN at 155 Mbits s $^{-1}$ with relatively minor developments in the existing equipment. At the moment none of this potential is being exploited.

Satellites are usually designed to carry different types of signals such as analogue television, digital telephony, data etc, depending on the requirements. 72 MHz repeaters are used by PTTs within Europe to carry digital telephony on time division multiple access carriers at 120 Mbits s $^{-1}$ . Transmission costs are independent of distance and the only charge associated with a satellite network is the lease cost for the repeaters. Contrary to leased lines, an inherent transmission medium, satellite capacity can be allocated independently in each direction. Thus, by taking advantage of non-symmetric applications, cost savings can be achieved. Satellite solutions are rapid to implement. This is in contrast to terrestrial networks where new links have to be physically installed. The inherent capability of a satellite to broadcast over geographically dispersed areas makes broadcast networks ideally suited for information providers. Thus low cost receive-only terminals can receive news, weather services and financial information on stock and commodities.

Three satellite solutions are currently available. These are the interactive VSATs network, the meshed VSATs network and lastly the satellite mobile. These networks are broadly similar in capability to existing and future terrestrial technologies. A VSAT is a small earth station with antenna diameter typically less than 2.4 m consisting of a compact outdoor unit to receive and transmit signals and an intelligent indoor unit housing the modem, the satellite interface and terrestrial interface unit. A network VSATs provides one-way data communications video broadcast, and/or voice communication. The most common interactive VSATs network configuration is the star network based on time division multiplexing. User data rates are generally only 64 kbits s $^{-1}$ maximum, but a small number of channels may be paralleled to implement 2 B + D ISDN. This type of configuration can provide a very low cost, one-way broadband network ideally suited to access multimedia databases. In rural areas such a network may be the basis for the development of an agricultural, veterinary, medical or tourism telecommuting information system.

The meshed VSATs network is a variation of the interactive VSATs scheme which is much more suitable for rural areas. It has a distributed topology, much higher performance, dynamic allocation capacity and on demand duplex data rates at up to 2 Mbits s $^{-1}$ . As a result, in the meshed VSATs network, earth stations tend to cost two or three times those of a traditional VSATs network. Although meshed VSATs network has limited use in Europe, it is in fact the most prominent candidate scenario for future teleworking applications. Only France and Scandinavia have operated this type of networks for some years and therefore have some experience.

A great range of services has been added recently from low speed messaging operating at a few hundred bits $s^{-1}$ to high speed data supporting 64 kbits $s^{-1}$ connections. All the services offered by satellite mobile connect directly into the public network. On-going research extends the capabilities of all these networks to higher data rates including basic rate ISDN. A major disadvantage of satellite mobile services is that both terminal and satellite transmission costs are high. In contrast, their main advantage is the possibility to establish a temporary site in the most isolated areas which can have an immediate connection to the public network. The use of the land mobile or semi-permanent terminals depends on the local regulations. In some countries their use has been permitted almost without restriction; in others it has not been allowed yet. With the introduction of satellite services the situation is now becoming more relaxed. Details are provided in Table 1.

In Europe, the introduction of the Eutelsat system as the first European satellite system in 1983 provided a major expansion in satellite communication activities. The Eutelsat system offers digital telephony and television transmission between European countries as well as TV distribution to cable networks and individual domestic receivers. Furthermore, from 1984, Eutelsat initiated the satellite multiservice system offering a range of digital carriers to the European business community via small (3.5–5.5 m diameter) gateway earth stations.

At the international level, the Intelsat organization introduced a Vista service in 1983 to cater for the rural and the remote communications needs via the Intelsat network. The service provides the basic requirements for rural telecommunications such as the facility for one or more voice channels plus low speed data applications. The emphasis is on low cost earth stations with shared access to a dedicated lease space segment capacity. Typically, a number of small earth stations of size D1 (4.5–5.5 m diameter) are connected in a star configuration to a large central earth station of size D2 (11–13 m diameter). These services are offered in global, hemispheric and zone beam transponders of Intelsat satellites.

Clearly, there is potential competition between cable and direct broadband satellite technologies. Today, in addition to fibre-optic or radio links, most of the less favoured regions of the EC have plans to establish links for international digital telephony using satellites. Since 1990, there are at least five very high powered satellite systems over Europe. These are the ESA (European Space

Agency) launched in July 1989, the OLYMPUS-1 satellite with five years life to evaluate experimentally and promote new applications and services, the TELECOM-1 in France promoting business communications, the DFS/Copernicus in Germany, the ITALSAT in Italy and the private ASTRA in Luxemburg.

## Teleworking policy

## Initiatives

Teleworking initiatives have been established in many states. The USA has shown a great interest in teleworking from state to federal level and plays an important role, particularly in energy conservation strategies. Some of the most important initiatives are (1) the California telecommuting projects (Niles, 1990), describing a number of initiatives primarily motivated by the public sector and concerning the transport congestion and energy; (2) the Hawaii telework demonstration centre (SMS Research, 1991), describing a third-party satellite office set up in a residential district of Hawaii, which facilitates teleworking by a group of employees and aims in demonstrating innovative ways to reduce traffic congestion; (3) the global agricultural technology sales and services in Washington state (Niles and Don Dillman, 1990), providing research-based information intensive services, telemarketing, travel and transport arrangements, documents processing to the farmers and businessmen in the area; (4) the Jamaica Digiport providing an advanced telecommunications system to the Jamaican workers to compete effectively with the mainland US workers in tasks like telemarketing and data entry, and (5) the state of Arizona telecommuting program aiming to reduce traffic congestion and air pollution.

The general consensus in the USA is that in addition to part-time, split-site, telecommuting from home, centre-based teleworking will emerge as a predominant form. Generally, proprietary satellite offices are preferred to share accommodation managed by third parties. It is estimated that 5.5 million people are currently telecommuting in the USA. This number is expected to double by 1995. Many small companies and nearly 500 major USA corporations have formally established participation in telecommuting programmes with their employees.

Singapore is one of the best examples of countries introducing IT. SMEs (Small Medium Enterprises) are adopting modern management practices and new technologies very successfully. The various initiatives have also resulted in widespread adoption of IT among SMEs. As appears from the findings of a recent survey on IT usage in Singapore (NCB, 1992), in 1983 only 13% of companies with more than ten employees made use of computers. This figure has increased gradually to reach 84% in 1992. Among computerized companies there is now one personal computer to every 3.8 employees, compared with 1 to 5 in 1989. According to Yap (1993) a survey has shown that 75% of women working with computers supported the idea of teleworking and 83% preferred to telework for three days from their homes.

In Europe a range of initiatives indicate promising prospects in teleworking. Companies have been decentralizing operations and locating specialized departments in remote areas with low overheads and costs for some time. Interesting applications can be found in those countries providing a suitable network infrastructure. Thus, the Dutch Ministry of Transport having completed a set of trials formalizes the status and workable methods for government employees to telecommute one to three days per week from home. In the UK, British Telecom and Mercury Communications are setting a number of trials across the country promoting teleworking. Since 1992 British Telecom has introduced the on-line information directory for its subscribers using home working personnel and basic equipment such as personal computers and telephone lines. In addition, British Telecom also examines the introduction of videoconferencing to reduce the lack of personal contact between the teleworkers. Also Rank Xerox in London (UK) has introduced teleworking due to high fixed cost. It has been calculated that due to overheads the company had to pay three times more for the workers living in London than in other places in UK.

A new management scheme has already been introduced by which specialized personnel work in different and more flexible ways, making use of computers and e-mail. This had the double effect of substantially decreasing the cost for the company and workers getting a better salary. A trial involving provision of directory enquiring services is being developed in Scotland, while in Wales, progress toward teleworking has been succeeded by securing data-entry contract work from major organizations such as the department of Health and Social Services. In France, many teleworking applications have already been developed including full time secure employment for local people at their rural centres. A long time ago, Scandinavian countries introduced the telecottage idea which is a community-based computing centre combining access to shared facilities in rural areas.

Teleworking has emerged as a major theme in the ORA (Opportunities for Information Technology and Telecommunications in Rural Areas) research and development EC programme, including the use of new telematic services in rural areas. Three new collaborative research projects of teleworking were underway by January 1992. They are currently funded under the RACE II/EC programme and they are addressing the technical (APTITUDE), the commercial (MITRE) and the social (PATRA) problems of telework in the European environment.

## Recommendations

The development of teleworking particularly in the less favoured regions of Europe is dependent on complex factors including social, managerial and technological issues. Recommendations can be proposed in several aspects, the most important of which are in the approach followed, the applications to be developed, the technology advances and infrastructure policy, and lastly in funding. To facilitate the analysis a summary table has been made (see Table 3).

The primary approach on the development of teleworking in rural areas should concentrate on the development of business. Effective teleworking business in rural areas requires the development of novel applications with a close integration approach which is not concentrated in one market sector, and addresses financial viability, training, technology and user interface considerations. Predicting the development of such applications in rural areas is much more difficult since it involves differences in the ways of working; existing workers traditionally made little use of advanced technology. It also requires the creation of a completely new range of jobs. The primary motivation is the public sector since it generates employment and economic growth in the less favoured regions. There are two potential sources of teleworking labour in rural areas, namely the existing labour force and workers who move either as part of a company initiative, or as individuals. It is unlikely that organizations will distribute their existing permanent staff to rural areas. Perhaps the most obvious approach is to provide means by which lower cost rural labour could be subcontracted to core region organizations. Thus, in the short term, an approach based on the provision of low cost labour could be effective. In the long term, an approach based on higher skills and value added marketing services seems more likely to be successful.

Teleworking cannot be seen as an isolated application area but it must be integrated into the total context of rural development opportunities. It may be regarded as a process of decentralization in the information economy on every level from local to global. Research into innovative local applications, by means of pilot projects, should be of high priority. High value added applications which exploit local assets and skills with the potential for ultimate commercialization should be especially supported. Projects should make provision for training, marketing and publicity as part of their activities to facilitate and encourage the participation of new organizations in the business teleworking.

Expenditure on infrastructure should concentrate on providing broadband access points at a community level. Centre-based operations should be of prime focus for teleworking in rural areas. Teleworking centres (usually satellite) are more effective than home working for many reasons such as share of equipment, provision of sophisticated and cheap telecommunication infrastructure, possibility to negotiate new tariff structures based on leasing or other more flexible type concepts, integration with training, security, social isolation, etc. The size of such centres could vary according to the local needs and opportunities. In order to ensure viability it is likely that such centres would be multi-purpose and include uses other than teleworking, such as public health administration business, training etc, so as to share facilities and reduce application costs. Local infrastructure should be developed when more information about the viability of the applications is available, possibly in conjunction with specific home-based applications such as cable television.

## Conclusions

In this work a general overview of teleworking as it applies in Europe has been provided. Our attention has been given to the development of IBC in the less favoured regions of the EC in which telework has profound social and economic consequences. It appears that teleworking plays an important role for IBC development in rural areas. These areas present a tremendous new opportunity for European business to benefit from the advantages of telework. In addition, telework presents an opportunity for rural areas to gain the diversity of employment on which their future is strongly dependent. New employment creation in rural areas together with policies of support for training, better health care, better management of local resources and increase in the prosperity and the quality of life are likely to emerge in the long-term as the dominant objective, which can be successfully faced by teleworking development. In addition, experimentation with new telematic systems for telework and work management systems provides useful lessons for the policy to be adopted.
