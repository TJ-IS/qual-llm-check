# User satisfaction and service quality of the IS department: closing the gaps

RICHARD T WATSON

University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia, USA

LEYLAND F. PITT and CHRIS J. CUNNINGHAM
Henley Management College, Henley on Thames, UK

DEON NEL

Aston University, Birmingham, UK

Measurement and management of user satisfaction with information systems (IS) have been issues of concern for practitioners and academics in the field for some time. This paper introduces the 'Gaps' approach to service quality, a topic widely discussed in the marketing literature, and proposes that a similar approach is applicable in the IS domain. The user gap is explained and its measurement suggested, and the other gaps within the IS arena are described and their causes and cures addressed.

## Introduction

Nobel laureate and economist Solow comments, 'you see computers everywhere but in the productivity statistics' (Loveman, 1991). His observation resonates with many managing directors, who are concerned about inadequate measures for assessing the value of their large investments in information technology (IT). Information systems (IS) managers who ignore this concern with measuring the worth of IS are likely to find it increasingly difficult to justify capital investment in IT. The problem exists because IS has limited instruments for measuring its success and contribution to organizational productivity.

Deming (1981) argues that output quality, service level, initiative, and cooperation are appropriate criteria for evaluating white-collar productivity, claiming that when quality is stressed, efficiency generally increases. These are different criteria than the economists' ratio of inputs to outputs, which was the standard for the receding industrial era. IS managers need to incorporate Deming's ideas in their assessment of IS's effectiveness.

Although a number of researchers have struggled with measuring the effectiveness of the IS department (see Bjørn-Andersen and Davis, 1986), the problem of assessing effectiveness has still not been resolved. User satisfaction is still the most widely used surrogate measure of IS effectiveness (Melone, 1990). Rockart (1982), however, has proposed that the quality of the IS department's service, as perceived by its clients, is a key indicator of IS effectiveness. Moreover, Conrath and Mignen (1990) have found that the principle reason IS departments measure user satisfaction is to improve the quality of their service. Rushinek and Rushinek (1986) assert that user satisfaction studies have ignored the impact of service quality on user satisfaction. If we combine the thoughts of Deming and these IS researchers, we see there is a case for suggesting it is more appropriate to measure service quality than user satisfaction because this is what IS really wants to measure.

In this paper, we argue for adopting service quality as a measure of IS effectiveness. Service quality is of major concern in marketing, and this is the appropriate reference discipline. In the area of services marketing, a model has been developed for describing and understanding the service quality process (Parasuraman et al., 1985). An instrument, SERVQUAL (Parasuraman et al., 1988), has also been constructed that assesses the quality of service from the customer's (or user's) perspective. The use of SERVQUAL in the measurement of service quality as a surrogate for user satisfaction of an IS department is described by Pitt et al., (1992). In this paper, we posit that the 'Gaps' model of service quality is a useful process for understanding the effectiveness of IS. As this paper proposes that service quality is an alternative to user satisfaction, the current measure of IS effectiveness, it is important to consider the role of the IS department.

## Is the IS department a service provider?

In reality, the fundamental role of the IS department, within most organizations, is that of the classical services marketer. The services it provides are no different from any other kinds of services, such as financial services, professional services or health care – they possess the well-documented characteristics of services as opposed to physical goods (Bateson 1977; Berry 1980; Lovelock 1981; Rathmell 1966, 1974; Shostack 1977). The department provides, to other divisions, branches, functions or departments within the organization, a service that is essentially intangible, meaning that unlike physical products, its quality cannot be touched or seen, but only experienced.

Intangibility may be the cause of difficulty that users have in understanding the service offerings of IS departments. While the services of the IS department may have a few tangible attributes, such as equipment and user manuals, typically called search qualities that can be viewed prior to use, the resulting user-relationship problems of intangibility are significant. Furthermore, the services provided by the IS department are inseparable. This is related to intangibility, and means that the services of the IS department are created and used simultaneously, which is typical of the simultaneous production and consumption which characterizes most services. Frequently the user's presence and participation in the production of the service is necessary. As there is high user involvement in the production and consumption of most services, the standardization and control thereof are difficult to maintain. The implications for service quality and the measurement and management thereof are thus obvious.

The services of the IS department are heterogeneous. There are several aspects to their heterogeneity. First; a variety of services are offered by IS (e.g. help desk, network connection, software selection). Second; the same person may be required to perform a range of services (e.g. write a program, answer technical questions, document a system). Third; many, if not all of the services of the IS department services are performed by people, and people do not always perform consistently. There may be variation from one service to another within the same department, variation in the service a single individual provides from day to day, and variation from user to user. Thus standardization and quality of service are extremely difficult to control. However, this also leads to opportunities for the customization of services in order to meet users' specific needs. Because of these factors, the IS department is often faced with the dilemma of providing efficient standardized service at some acceptable level of quality, while simultaneously attempting to treat each user as a unique individual.

The management of user satisfaction issues has been a concern for IS managers for some time. It is our contention that IS managers can gain from adopting a services marketing approach to the management of their relationship. In this paper we introduce the Gaps model of service quality (Parasuraman et al. 1985) to the IS community, and attempt to make it directly relevant to the management of user relationships.

## The Gaps model of service quality

The Gaps model of service quality has its origins in the marketing department of Texas A&M University throughout the 1980s (Parasuraman et al., 1985, 1988; Zeithaml 1981; Zeithaml et al., 1985, 1988). Following intensive exploratory work involving service customers and the managers of service organizations, Parasuraman et al. (1985) proposed a model of service quality that can be summarized as follows:

Service quality as perceived by a user depends on the size and direction of the gap between expected service and perceived service, which in turn depends on the nature of the gaps on the service provider's side associated with the design, marketing and delivery of the services.

The Gaps model has received much attention in the services marketing literature of late. It has also been expanded on, and is the focus of a book by the same authors (Zeithaml et al., 1990). Our adaptation of the model, with relevance to the IS department and its users, is illustrated in Figure 1. The model illustrates the service quality relationship between the IS customer and the IS department. From the user's point of view, service quality is the difference between the perceptions of what is expected and what is received from the IS department. If expectation equals perception, the user is technically satisfied. When perception exceeds expectation, the user is more than satisfied and can even be excited and delighted. When expectation exceeds perception, however, the user is dissatisfied and there is a service quality problem.

The focal point of the Gaps model is users' expectations of IS. These expectations are formed by five forces: word of mouth communication; personal needs; past experiences; communications by vendors (Pitt et al., 1992); and, communication by the IS department. Conversations around the water cooler, before meetings, over lunch and so forth are important moulders of users' expectations. Other users relate their war stories and their successes. Users have needs that they expect the IS department to meet. For example, the diffusion of personal computers resulted in many users demanding access to corporate data stored on a mainframe for use in spreadsheet sets on personal computers. Past experiences condition expectations. Previous service experiences sets expectations for future encounters. One unusual feature of the IS domain is the role vendors can play in establishing expectations. Vendors often influence users' expectations through advertisements, presentations or sales calls. Vendors, in trying to sell their products, often raise expectations by parading the positive features of their wares and downplaying issues such as systems conversion or compatibility. In essence, vendors want to raise users' expectations so that they will become dissatisfied with the existing system and buy new products. IS has no control over vendors' communications, and must recognize that they are an ever present force sculpturing expectations. This is not necessarily bad. Vendors' communications can be a positive force for change when they make users aware of what they should expect from IS. Communications from the IS department are another factor in determining expectations. In particular, IS can be a very powerful shaper of expectations during systems development. Users are reliant on IS to convert their needs into a system. In the process, IS creates an expectation as to what the finished system will do and how it will appear.

The gap between expectations and perceptions – gap 5 in the model – is caused by four other gaps, which are internal to the IS department. Gap 1 is a gap between IS managers' perceptions and the user's actual expectations – quite simply IS managers do not always understand what users' want. Gap 2 is a gap between IS managers' perceptions of user expectations and their ability to translate these into IS service quality standards. In other words, IS managers might know what users expect, but are unable to set quality standards. The distance between the IS service quality standards that are set, and the IS service quality that is actually delivered by the department is gap 3. It is a result of the IS department not keeping its promises. Gap 3 can be avoided if IS managers are realistic and conservative in setting standards. Finally, gap 4 is a gap between IS service delivery and external communications by the IS department – the service that is actually delivered might not live up to promises made by the IS department.

## The gaps, their causes and cures in an IS environment

In this section we address the gaps as they pertain to an IS environment within an organization, identify some possible causes and suggest ways in which the IS department may address them.

## Gap 5. The user's gap

Gap 5, the user's gap, is the difference between what the user expects of the IS department serving him or her, and what is perceived to be delivered by that department. No matter how rude, ungrateful, unsophisticated or perhaps even stupid users may seem to be to the IS professional, the latter must realise that users do not directly cause their own IS service quality problems. Indeed, the user is always right. These problems are occasioned by one or more of the other gaps in the model. It is up to the IS manager to ascertain the nature of the user's gap 5, and then to address the other four gaps that are causing it.

Gap 5 can be measured for an IS department, and this is undoubtedly where the IS professional should start. While specific measurement of gap 5 is beyond the scope of this paper, it is worthwhile noting that the developers of the model have devised an instrument, SERVQUAL, that measures the gap. Briefly, it requires a polling of user expectations of a service provider, followed by a polling of their perceptions of the actual service being delivered by that provider. The difference between expectations and perceptions is thus an excellent indication of the extent of gap 5. However, it is not inconceivable that other measures of IS user satisfaction as suggested in the IS literature, or as currently used by IS departments, would be a suitable indication of gap 5 as well. Once gap 5 has been measured, the IS manager can direct attention to it effectively by addressing the other four gaps in the model.

## Gap 1. Not understanding what users expect

Understanding what users expect is a critical prerequisite for delivering quality service. Being able to deliver what users will perceive as excellent service requires IS managers to know what users expect. Being wrong about this at worst means that the IS department may lose out to external service providers who understand what users expect. Indeed, the dreaded talk today in many organizations, including IS departments, is ‘outsourcing’ – acquiring services externally such as training, catering and IS that would traditionally have been sourced from within. At best it might mean expending substantial sums of money, time and effort on things that probably do not make that much impression on users. What causes gap 1 to exist? Following the analysis of Zeithaml et al. (1990) we identify three major causes of this gap in the IS environment.

## A lack of user needs orientation

A lack of user needs orientation has to do with the extent to which IS managers make conscious attempts to understand user expectations through formal research efforts. Does the IS department research its users regularly and is this research sufficiently focused on service quality expectations, rather than on technical issues? More importantly, do senior IS executives make an effort to understand the survey findings?

Applying Anthony's pyramid (1965) we can identify three levels at which IS managers need to examine users' needs (see Figure 2). At the operational level, IS management needs to discover what facilities and resources are required to meet users' needs on a daily basis. Issues to be addressed in this area include response time, uptime, and troubleshooting support. At the tactical level, users' application needs must be addressed. Too frequently IS has relied on the cumbersome systems development life cycle (SDLC) for determining users' needs. However, as Davis (1982) points out, this method is appropriate only when there is a high degree of certainty about users' needs. A general consideration of determining users' needs and some alternative methods are presented by Wetherbe (1991). The highest level for assessing needs is strategic, where the IS department must consider the overall objectives of the organization and create an infrastructure to serve these goals. The IS department needs to translate the organization's strategic plan into an IS plan to serve organizational objectives.

## Ineffective communication

IS managers must communicate at the three levels indicated by Figure 2. They must seek, stimulate and facilitate the flow of information from IS employees at lower levels. Where this is ineffective, gap 1 is inevitable. IS personnel who are in daily contact with users on site learn a great deal about what users expect, what frustrates them and what delights them. Where IS managers do not encourage suggestions from front-line IS personnel (or for that matter, do not seek or encourage communication with them) they are losing out on a very valuable source of information regarding the expectations of their users.

IS managers must encourage communication across the hierarchy. The IS managers must talk with other functional managers to determine their objectives and identify systems development opportunities to support their goals. IS project managers must also maintain constant contact with their sponsoring partner and work diligently at building the rapport that is often a prerequisite for truly understanding their needs.

Prior research suggests that managers who do not communicate with the CEO have great difficulty establishing an appropriate IS plan (Watson, 1990). Thus, communication is necessary with the executive team that sets the organization's direction. Ideally, the IS manager should be part of this group; the dominant coalition (March, 1962), so that IS both shapes and understands the strategy and builds an infrastructure to support the strategy.

## Excessive levels within the IS department

A third cause of gap 1 may be an excessive number of levels between the top-most and bottom-most levels in the hierarchy of the IS department. Too many levels within the organizational hierarchy separate top IS managers from those who have contact with users on a daily basis. If this is reflective of the picture within large organizations, it is possible that user-concern messages from bottom to top within the IS department are communicated upward, only to meet a middle manager who strangles them.

If these three factors are the causes of gap 1, what are possible cures? We suggest a number of approaches. Strategic use should be made of user complaints and complaint information. In most IS departments complaints may still be regarded either as a nuisance at worst, or something to be sorted out and blissfully forgotten at best. We believe that more effective IS departments are starting to gather meaningful data about user complaints, analysing them, and using them effectively.

Astute IS departments would do well to study what is happening in other similar situations. Thus, they may learn much from IS departments in other companies. They may find it useful to study other organizations who provide services of a similar nature such as consulting firms, information providers and marketers of high technology products, where users are faced with high risk and high uncertainty. IS departments will also find much value to be gained from studying key users – major users within the organization who are large enough to warrant in-depth individual scrutiny.

IS departments will also benefit immensely from the creation of user panels – typically consisting of 10–15 users randomly selected. At least one of these panels needs to be representative of managers from different functions so that IS and these managers can understand how needs cross functional barriers. These panels should meet four or five times per year to talk about the products or services the IS department provides. It should be noted that these are not panels conducted on behalf of the IS department by a consulting firm – that, is ‘copping out’. They are panels owned, facilitated and run by managers in the IS department itself.

## Gap 2. Setting the wrong IS service standards

Understanding what users expect from IS service quality is not enough to ensure it. This intelligence must be converted into meaningful service quality standards for the IS department. There are four chief causes of gap 2 or why, despite the fact that they may know what users expect, IS managers do not set acceptable, appropriate service quality standards.

## Lack of commitment to IS service by senior IS executives

Despite the lip service paid to it, in many IS departments there is little or no commitment by management to service quality for users. Short-term objectives such as cost reduction, capacity utilization and efficiency are far easier to assess and therefore seem to merit focus. Frequently, IS service quality is defined from the IS department's point of view, and not the user's. Middle management and contact employees cannot be expected to commit to quality if senior levels in the IS department do not.

## A perception of infeasibility

In many IS departments, there is a perception of infeasibility – ‘It would cost too much’, ‘It can’t be done’, ‘Users expect too much’, ‘Users are unreasonable’, ‘Users do not understand’. Frequently from a conventional point of view this may be true. However, it is our contention that astute IS managers will find unconventional ways of giving users exactly what they want. A brief case illustrates this point.

A group of 50 executives on a one-week marketing program at a large business school are playing a management simulation game. The executives are divided into ten teams of five members each, and are required to make decisions, twice daily, for five days. The school's IS department has thoughtfully provided each team with a PC for decision analysis, and writing of decisions to a disk for subsequent reading by the game's administrator on the computer on which the game is run. At the end of round one, the game administrator gives each group a 50 page printout of their results. The ‘unreasonable’ users immediately point out that there are five group members and that each would like a printout. In an attempt to avoid copying $4 \times 50 \times 10$ pages twice daily, the IS department installs four page carbon copy paper onto the dot matrix printer for the administrator. The ‘ungrateful’ users immediately point out that there are five of them in the group, not four, and that they still demand five copies. The IS department, by now all muttering under their breath about the arrogance and rudeness of marketing executives, begin to negotiate the services of a temporary assistant with a part-time employment agency, who will make 4000 copies each day for the next four days.

Ingenuity to the rescue. If the school will buy ten inexpensive dot matrix printers, and attach them to the

PCs in the group rooms, says a programmer, she will write a file enabling the results to be rewritten to the group diskette, which can then be copied to the line printer. The cost of this purchase will be twice that of employing a temporary assistant and photocopying, but importantly, the school will have invested in assets that can be used productively by other groups in the future. The executives can then also print off as many copies of the report as they like, albeit on relatively slow, noisy low resolution dot matrix printers. The executives are happy with the solution because they have control over it. Suddenly, it no longer seems necessary to have a copy of the results each, as they gather round their new printers to see the consequences of their decisions begin to appear before their eyes...

## Inadequate task standardization

Inadequate task standardization as a cause of gap 2 has to do with the extent to which hard and soft technology are used to standardize service tasks. Here the IS department must ask the questions: Are we successfully using automation to achieve consistency in serving users? Are programs in place to improve operating procedures to that consistent service is provided to them? For example, the IS department could look at using an expert system for answering common operating systems questions. An expert system provides consistency and continual availability.

## An absence of goal setting

If service quality goals are based on IS department standards rather than on customer standards and expectations, then there really is an absence of goal setting. Frequently, IS departments will set goals in terms of capacity utilization and similar measures instead of in terms of user satisfaction and service quality.

## The above four issues can be viewed as causes of gap 2. What are some cures?

Firstly, management in IS departments must commit to quality. This requires leadership from the very top. Without this commitment and an acceptance of the fact that it is not always easy, quality service and certainly user satisfaction are pipe dreams. If top management within the IS department is not committed, it is unlikely that other personnel within the department will be. An aside to this is that if middle management is to be committed, service quality must not be seen as a ‘flavour-of-the-month’ idea, but rather as a long-term philosophy by which the IS department will be managed, through thick and thin.

Secondly, feasibility can be perceived only if the organization is amenable to change - innovative and willing to be different. The mentality then almost becomes one of 'Anything the user wants is feasible until proven not to be. And then only after rigorous testing' Or: "The answer is Yes – now, what was the question?"

Thirdly, tasks can be standardized by using hard technology or machines in place of human effort or contact, or by improving work methods (soft technology), or by a combination of the two. Auto-tellers and engine diagnosis machines are excellent examples of standardized hard technology; standardized gardening services, self-service buffets or 'What fun, you cook your own meal, restaurants are examples of soft technology in other areas of services marketing. There are undoubtedly many areas in which the IS department can search for additional advantages.

Finally, gap 2 can be closed by realistic, determined setting of service-quality goals based on user requirements. These goals must be specific, accepted and measurable. They must be challenging but also realistic. Gap 2 will not be closed overnight – however, if attention is given to these causes and these cures, management will be a long way towards specifying the correct service quality standards.

## Gap 3. The service performance gap

Gap 3 in the model – the gap between service standards and service delivery – is probably the most serious from the IS manager's point of view. It suggests that while service quality standards may have been set according to knowledge of user expectations, the IS department is not delivering according to these standards. Yet, it is also the gap which is easiest to identify, rectify and manage (Zeithaml et al., 1990). The causes and cures of gap 3 are discussed in more detail below.

## Role ambiguity

Role ambiguity in the IS environment exists whenever employees do not possess the information or training necessary to perform their jobs adequately. They will be uncertain about what managers and supervisors require of them, and what they should do to satisfy these expectations. What must the IS department do? Provide role clarity. This must be done by communication. Employees must have accurate information about their roles in the IS department, with specific frequent communication about what they are expected to do. There should of course be feedback; they must be told constantly about how well they are performing according to standards and expectations. Thirdly, role ambiguity must be eliminated by instilling confidence and competence, and this can be achieved only by constant attention to training, from both technical (or knowledge) and inter-personal (skills) perspectives.

## Role conflict

Role conflict occurs when employees within the IS department perceive that they cannot satisfy all the demands of the individuals they must serve. A simple example of this is the conflict between the instruction of a manager to service as many users as possible, and the user's desire for individual attention. This will create stress if the reward system within the IS department is geared to quantity rather than quality. To overcome this the IS department should strive to eliminate the distress of role conflict by attempting to define service roles and standards in terms of user expectations.

## Poor employee-technology-job fit

A shortage of IS skills can result in a poor fit between employee and job, and between technology and job. As one IS manager puts it 'At the salaries we pay, I'm surprised anyone with skills in computers and software wants to do the job, let alone the incompetents we have doing it'. These two causes can be cured by giving adequate attention to the type of people IS departments put into jobs, and by making sure that they have the appropriate technology needed to ensure the user's expectations are met. Of course, while there remains a shortage of skilled IS personnel, many organizations will have difficulty in translating this solution into action.

## Lack of perceived control

Lack of perceived control describes the extent to which employees within the IS department believe the department can actually control the standards that have been set. Perceived control involves the ability to make responses that influence threatening situations and the ability to choose outcomes or goals. The IS department employee who has no choice but to refuse a user a justified request when the supervisor is on lunch perceives then to have little control over the situation. What management within the IS department may need to do is empower employees – give them far more capacity to make more meaningful decisions if they affect users.

## Lack of teamwork

Teamwork – or lack thereof – is a problem facing many IS departments. From our perspective, one of the most important aspects of teamwork is the extent to which employees view other employees as users. Another is the extent to which employees feel personally involved and committed to the IS department. Teams need to be built, they generally don’t just happen. Teamwork is at the heart of all service quality initiatives – if employees can’t work together, then service cannot ‘come together’ for the user.

IS departments have used teams for many years. Systems development has nearly always been a team effort. But, the team concept has frequently been poorly communicated up the pyramid to senior management and down the pyramid to operational personnel. Senior management must comprehend that IS is part of the team that manages the organization. In the information age, IS is a critical player in most corporations' future; it must be part of the executive team. IS operations personnel and their clients must redesign themselves into a cohesive team that operates with a common purpose.

A final strategy for closing gap 3 is the management of users. This is imperative when users play an active role in the quality of the service delivery that they receive. Accountants are reliant on clients keeping records for tax purposes, and airlines are more efficient when passengers arrive promptly for check-ins. Smart service providers will be effective at actually managing users in the role they have to play in service delivery. The implications of this for the IS department are obvious. Successful IS departments are those that enable users to understand that a great deal of the quality of what they receive is dependent on their own participation and cooperation. This does not have to be done in a dictatorial, 'we know what's best for you' fashion – what is needed is a subtle communication of the fact that the service quality received will be a function of user participation. This is not a new message in IS. Systems development has continually stressed user involvement, but the message needs to be reiterated and given a service orientation. Operations personnel also need to understand that service also requires user involvement.

## Gap 4. Service delivery and communication by the IS department

The fourth gap in the service quality model is that between what the IS department promises about service and what it actually delivers. It is important to understand that at this point, if the logic of the model has been followed, the IS department is delivering the quality of service users require. Much of the communication by IS departments creates expectations in the minds of users. When what is delivered does not match these expectations the user is often dissatisfied to an extent that the IS department would have been far better off had it said nothing. The major cases of gap 4, and possible cures, are discussed below. There are two major causes of gap 4, with specific reference to the IS department.

## Inadequate horizontal communication

Inadequate horizontal communication is characterized by members of the IS department, at the same hierarchical level, making promises on behalf of one another, or simply communicating badly with one another. Users are informed that a particular IS department member will address their problem, or that another section within the department will deliver the service. When the user seeks this, the ‘other party’ may have been badly informed, or not have been informed at all about the user’s problems or needs.

## A propensity to overpromise

Frequently representatives of the IS department will make promises to users which will raise expectations that can never be met. At other times, non-personal communications such as newsletters, bulletin boards, or information sheets by the IS department will raise expectations to unrealistic levels. It is important to note that the user will ordinarily not have had these expectations – they have been created by careless communication or, sometimes, a general lack of communication.

The cures for gap 4 are simple yet very hard to follow, because it is frequently exciting to promise the unattainable. The IS department needs to work very hard at improving horizontal communication within, and to ensure that individuals and divisions do not make promises, unthinkingly or on behalf of others, that cannot be kept.

## Conclusion

There has been a consistent pattern of grossly misunderstanding the potential of IT. For example, at the dawn of computing the total market for computers was forecasted to be 50 machines (Watson and Pitt, 1993), and in the early 1980s, IBM estimated the personal computer market amounted to 5000 machines (Lewis, 1992). Organizations have consistently discovered new opportunities for IT, which has moved from being a tool for automating clerical processes to a device for gaining competitive advantage, sharing expertise, supporting decision making, creating electronic markets and so forth. It is now time for IS management to evolve the concept of its role in the organization.

Traditionally IS has viewed itself as a systems developer. This is most evident in the central focus of most university IS programs that are built around systems analysis and design, database and telecommunications. However, systems development is only one of the three key roles of IS. IS is also an infrastructure developer and a service provider. More recently, there has been a surge of interest in IS strategic planning and the role of IS management as an infrastructure provider. To some extent this is a reaction to the proliferation of personal computers and IS managers' response to meeting users' needs for access to data.

Little attention has been paid to the service role of IS. This paper has introduced the service concept and provided a framework, the Gaps model, for IS managers to diagnose service issues. Our intention is that this paper will stimulate IS managers to visualize IS as a classic service provider and take the necessary action to supply consistent, high quality service to their clients.
