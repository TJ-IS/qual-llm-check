# Information systems methodologies in use

CHRISTOPHER WESTRUP

Liverpool Business School, Liverpool John Moores University, 98 Mount Pleasant, Liverpool L3 5UZ, UK

Research into information systems development (ISD) methodologies is generally restricted to the creation or elaboration of methodologies or the provision of CASE support. This paper proposes that a fruitful area of research, which will provide insights into the effectiveness of ISD methodologies, must centre on the observation of the practice of systems developers, users and managers, and the study of methodologies in use. Such a programme requires certain techniques, ideally longitudinal ethnographic case studies. Preliminary results from a couple of case studies indicate that ISD methodologies are not used as prescribed but this does not necessarily prevent the successful completion of development projects.

## Introduction

About 15 years ago the field of information systems development (ISD) witnessed a rapid growth in the number and sophistication of IS methodologies (Olle et al., 1982; 1983). Prior to that date ISD occurred as an ad hoc collection of techniques but since then the emphasis has been on the systematic development of IS though clearly not all systems development follows this pattern (Mackay and Lane, 1989). One important aspect in the deployment of ISD methodologies is an awareness of the importance of obtaining user requirements near the outset of systems development as it is well known that the cost of fixing requirement errors found in a prospective system increases dramatically as the project continues (Boehm, 1981). Many see that the interaction of designers and users is a key consideration for the successful development of IS so that, amongst other reasons, user requirements may be established with confidence (Hedberg, 1980; Kling, 1980; Mumford et al., 1983; Ehn, 1988). Some of these ideals have been translated into widely used techniques such as prototyping or have remained confined to specialized methodologies such as ETHICS or approaches such as the Cooperative Approach (Mumford et al., 1983; Greenbaum and Kyng, 1991). Friedman and Cornford (1989) suggest that ISD has moved from a phase that is primarily influenced by software constraints in which methodologies were a prime solution to a phase in which user relations have assumed key importance. However, the picture in any one site of ISD is more complex and it frequently embodies the tensions between prescribed ISD methodologies, their use by developers, and user relations. Or, put another way, each situation exhibits characteristics of methodologies in practice and it is an exploration of these characteristics that forms the body of this paper.

The paper begins by looking at methodologies and the search to improve them for the development of IS. Methodologies act to prescribe developers' and users' behaviour while aiming to make the intermediate results of this process more visible to aid managerial control by, for example, the use of phases and milestones. Several approaches have been taken to improve methodologies notably by the provision of CASE tool support, by moves towards complete automation of the development process and by the incremental elaboration of existing methodologies. All of these strategies neglect the issue of how methodologies are used in practice and the role of users in this process. The argument moves to an exploration of this issue using a case study approach spread over the period of the development. It is based on a detailed description of the interaction between developers and others. This approach avoids some of the difficulties of earlier studies though it raises some of its own and is unusual in its focus on methodology use in ISD.

Some of the issues raised by two case studies, drawn from the public and private sectors, of ISD methodologies in use are discussed in the next section. These show that methodologies are used in a complex manner in each site of their application. The ISD methodology can be seen as supporting the systems developer by providing a framework of action but it is not followed in detail by developers even though systems may be implemented successfully.

## ISD methodologies: from prescription to description

Arguably, in the absence of widely recognized and enforced certification of ISD practitioners, ISD methodologies perform a very important function. In a weak sense, it is clear that systems development is bound to be improved through the systematic application of techniques that remind developers of aspects of improvement which should be considered. This is a simple codification of experience. However methodologies have developed considerably further than this. Two main trends can be seen. First, a systematic development of techniques such as entity relationship diagrams, data flow diagrams and the like has occurred, and second, this is enforced by a rigorous prescription of the stages that must be followed for the successful completion of a project. This gives developers support as to what to do at various points in the process of development. Moreover it provides managerial oversight of this process by presenting a series of milestones to be completed. In this strong sense, methodologies are expected to ensure the successful implementation of systems development projects. This trend is exemplified by the prescription of external bodies for either a specific methodology such as Structured Systems Analysis and Design Methodology (SSADM) by the UK Civil Service or a specific process of software development such as certification of an organization's software development standards in the US by the Software Engineering Institute (Cutts, 1987; Humphrey et al., 1991). Thus, the use of a methodology provides an insurance for the successful completion of a project despite the absence of certified IS professionals.

Indeed, many have suggested that it is necessary to go further again, from support of ISD by methodology to automation of the same process using sophisticated methodologies and CASE tools (Couger et al., 1982). However some indications show that existing methodologies do not prescribe procedures in full and automation requires the invocation of less explicit meta-rules which are assumed by developers in their actions but which require detailed description for automation to occur successfully (Ryan, 1988).

A second strand of development which runs in parallel with that of automation but is less ambitious, seeks to improve existing methodologies slowly through the provision of CASE tool support and the assimilation of features from either other methodologies or though an improvement in the theoretic descriptive power of ISD techniques (Olle et al., 1982, 1983, 1988). Arguably, even radical critiques of widely used ISD methodologies (Hirschheim and Klein, 1922a, b) seek to create or improve existing methodologies through the incorporation of theoretic ideas from differing paradigms. However these trends all share a similar assumption which expects that if an adequate methodology is prescribed and sufficient controls are provided within that methodology then the use of that methodology can be determined. This parallels a general preoccupation within ISD regarding the design of systems rather than their implementation and subsequent maintenance; i.e., expecting that systematic designs of solutions for problems will result in solutions occurring. Such a focus is not restricted to ISD but is shared by much of management theory and teaching (Mitroff and Churchman, 1992). Neither of these presumptions should be taken at face value and it is likely that much of future understanding of ISD will derive from the observation of the processes of ISD which can inform ISD theory rather than the other way around. For example, studies which observe users constructing their own requirements can given an insight into theoretic calls for increased user participation and proposals to improve developer and user communication (Hirschheim and Klein, 1992b).

## A methodology of ISD methodology use

It is unfair to state that ISD theory is devoid of interest in the organizational application of IS; an area which is covered extensively in the MIS literature for instance. The argument relates to the ways in which the use of ISD methodologies are investigated and described in the literature. Let us take the example of user involvement to illustrate this point. An early criticism of much of ISD theory was that it was based on poor research designs which prevented useful, i.e. replicable results for being garnered. Ives and Olsen (1984, p. 601) reviewed the literature on user involvement and systems success and concluded as follows:

'The practitioner may have found this discussion [in this paper] disheartening. Not only has empirical research been unable to foresee when and what types of user involvement are appropriate, it has not convincingly demonstrated that user involvement contributes to system success.'

Subsequent work has focused on providing increasingly sophisticated instruments to represent the process of ISD and the role of user involvement within it. For example, Bailey and Pearson (1983) produced a detailed questionnaire which seeks to measure user satisfaction. This questionnaire has been used in a variety of later studies (Baroudi et al., 1986; Baronas and Louis, 1988; Tait and Vessey, 1988). However some authors have expressed dissatisfaction with their methodology because questionnaires only measured post hoc responses to the situation. Tait and Vessey (1988; p. 105) sum this up clearly by suggesting that 'longitudinal studies should be conducted to determine user attitudes at the time of development.' [emphasis added]. Perhaps Newman and Noble (1990) have come closest to fulfilling this aspiration though in a different way than was expected by Tait and Vessey. In an interesting study, Newman and Noble describe a case of ISD in a university admissions office and relate it to various theories of organization. They arrive at a contingent description of ISD in which certain models of organization accord with certain phases of system development. However, their account is based mainly on two sets of interviews one year apart and an analysis of documentation. Thus they characterize their study as exploratory conducted over multiple (two) time periods using Franz and Robey's (1987) classification of research methods. By moving away from questionnaires and the problems of structuring responses of participants Newman and Noble arrive at a more nuanced account of ISD but, nonetheless, they remain reliant on retrospective descriptions by actors of the process of ISD.

Some authors have suggested a detailed observational process of ISD based on longitudinal studies of systems development (Pettigrew, 1985; Vitalari, 1985) while others have proposed that a detailed ethnographic technique may allow the more accurate determination of user requirements as well as providing grounds for IS research (Randell et al., 1991). Few have followed these ideas in detail (Murray and Willmott, 1991) and it is difficult to find anyone who has examined the use of ISD methodologies from either or both of these two perspectives. Indeed, some would argue that detailed case studies are of limited application and cannot be congruent with the canons of scientific method. But such criticisms can be countered in three ways. First, it can be argued (Lee, 1989) that case study research can be made to fit scientific method through an attempt to develop and critique hypotheses of ISD. Second, case study research can be endorsed for certain situations; for instance, Benbasat et al. (1987) argue that case studies are useful in situations where'... research and theory are at their early, formative stages and "sticky, practice-based problems where the experiences of the actors are important and the context of action is critical".' (Benbasat et al., 1987, p. 369). A third, more challenging, perspective questions the value of applying uncritically scientific methods in a field which is the domain of a social science (Klein and Lyytinen, 1985) and proposes that more nuanced techniques are needed to establish useful knowledge of ISD. This paper concurs with this latter position.

To summarize, existing studies of ISD tend to concentrate either on improving techniques or methodologies based on fairly abstract criteria or, when they do represent the practices of ISD, restrict themselves to inadequate techniques such as questionnaires (Cicourel, 1964) or retrospective accounts of ISD processes through periodic interview. Instead, it is suggested that to establish understanding of the use of methodologies and the role of users in this process requires an ethnographic and longitudinal study into the processes of ISD. Such studies share the problems of being time-consuming, of requiring considerable access and of being, at best, representative of one site. But, by the same token, they may illustrate responses to the use of methodologies which are more pervasive.

## Methodologies in use

Having discussed the need for investigating methodologies in use the way has been paved to describe some outcomes of preliminary research. This section begins by discussing how methodologies are acquired by developers and then describes some of the ways in which methodologies are used in practice and the involvement of users in the process of ISD. An important outcome is that ISD methodologies are reinterpreted in each situation of their use and this may assist the successful implementation of information systems.

## Acquiring a methodology

One obvious feature of methodologies is that many of them are not in the public domain and are proprietary products which are guarded often with considerable care. Others such as SSADM are well referenced and described in detail (Cutts, 1987). Thus, one way of obtaining knowledge of a methodology is through reading its documentation. It is a common assumption that the written documentation is the methodology and this viewpoint manifests itself in various ways. For example, if a problem arises in a stage of ISD a common response is to go to the manual and see what is prescribed at this point or it can be seen in the secrecy that surrounds proprietary products. The idea accords with notions that a methodology in use is the simple application of the techniques in the manual. But, this approach is leavened by other sources. For the prospective user of a methodology, a period of tuition is frequently given that is based on documentary material but is developed by the teacher showing the key issues in the methodology and giving access to some of the experiences in using the methodology. This period varies considerably from methodology to methodology but is common to most. A third way in which a methodology is acquired is through working with others who are familiar with it and learning through doing and asking others what should be done at various points. Again this form of learning is very common. But, this move from manuals to formally taught situations – to experience by doing or asking others – changes the nature of the methodology from a procedure that is laid down in a manual to a set of working practices which are based on that manual. To see the difference, imagine trying to construct the working practices of a group of systems developers from the methodology documentation or, conversely, imagine trying to document fully and unambiguously the behaviours of an experienced team of systems developers. In other words, it is not simply that designers ignore or deliberately avoid doing certain actions prescribed in a methodology rather than the mere doing of these actions in themselves leads designers away from written procedures and they cease to do things purely 'by the book'.

Frequently, this has the effect of contextualizing the methodology to the circumstances as they are perceived by developers and it enables the methodology to operate more effectively. Of course, this process may be controlled to some extent. Management control is exerted by demanding specified outcomes at various stages of the ISD project to ensure that the processes that led to this outcome have been completed satisfactorily. Much hangs on the word satisfactorily; if this is determined primarily by the presence of completed products at this point then no problem exists so long as the products are of the required standard but it can be a major leap of faith to presume that because these products are presented, the previous stages have been completed in the ways prescribed by the methodology. An increasingly common aid to standardizing the process of ISD is the use of CASE tools. Clearly active CASE tools force compliance with the embedded methodology within the tool when they are used but even passive CASE tools tend to encourage this process if for no other reason than the visibility of the intermediate work is enhanced by known access by managers to the appropriate computer databases. In part this aspect of CASE tools has been influential in their take-up in different organizations. LeQuesne (1988) has argued that the successful implementation of CASE tools depends to a large extent on whether the standardization required by the tools accords with current organizational procedures and environment. When this is not the situation then CASE tools often are not used.

In short, although ways may be attempted to control the deployment of ISD methodologies through management control, the use of CASE tools or extensive training, the proposition remains that a methodology does not remain a fixed set of rules in use but is always interpreted in each setting in which it is used. The expectation is that studies of methodologies in use will observe this and see how they are made to work, or otherwise, in their sites of application. It is to some examples of methodologies in action that we now turn.

## Using a methodology

This section draws on a couple of examples of ISD methodologies in use to show the variation in the use of methodologies. Two studies are used from different organizational settings: the first is derived from an ongoing longitudinal and ethnographic study of the development and implementation of a hospital nursing IS while the second is taken from interviews with key participants in the introduction of a financial accounting system for a defence contractor organized by management consultants. It was a condition of access at both sites that confidentially would be observed. The first part of this section gives a resume of the two sites followed by an overview of some of the characteristics of methodologies in use that were drawn from these cases. The remainder of this section illustrates these points in more detail.

The development of the hospital nursing IS is part of an extensive national programme in the NHS to computerize nursing and clinical systems. The aim is to let nurses and clinicians make better judgements based on computerized IS and so improve patient care (DHSS, 1986). This case is taking place in a district Health Authority with two 800-bed hospitals at the edge of a large conurbation in northern England. The Resource Management (RM) initiative commenced in the autumn of 1990 with the appointment of an RM project manager at each hospital. A detailed project plan was constructed early on with the initial completion of a nursing IS and a case-mix database of clinical information expected three years after commencement. The overall project was divided into seven sub-projects; each of which has a steering committee and a person who is 'tasked' to manage that project. The nursing project steering committee has a membership drawn from the district and the two hospitals. It includes the director of nursing services at both sites, a couple of specialist senior nurses, the district director of information and the project nurses from each hospital. Nurses and nurse managers are represented by the director of nursing services. A project nurse was appointed a year after the RM project began at each hospital with a three year contract and a remit to coordinate the purchase and implementation of a computerized nursing IS for the hospital site. Each nurse reports to the nursing project steering committee and to the RM project manager. There are some 23 prospective nursing IS on the market and each one has to be customized for any particular site (Greenhalgh, 1991).

In the first six months, by Spring 1991, the management structure of one of the hospitals was completely revised to a model with ten clinical directorates or sub-units each of which has a manager, a business manager and a nurse manager. Within a month of taking up post, the project nurse ran a two day seminar for nurse managers on resource management and nurses' information requirements. This was closely followed by the setting up of a nursing IS working party by the project nurse. Each nursing speciality has one member on the party who is a senior nurse with managerial responsibility for a ward. The aim of this working group was to assist in deciding on the nursing information requirements for the new system and to participate in its selection and implementation. The working party met every fortnight over six months prior to the production of an operational requirement document that defines the nurses' information requirements and is used as a basis for software suppliers to bid for the introduction of a nursing IS at the hospital. The operational requirement (OR)

document, defining nursing information needs and many other details was sent out to potential suppliers who signalled an interest in bidding for the contract of developing and installing a nursing IS. About a dozen replies to the OR were received and these were quickly whittled down to three ‘serious’ replies based on an accurate compliance with the specification (Interview notes, 1992). From this group of three, two were chosen by an in-house developed scoring system to go forward to the next stage; that of negotiating and tendering for the actual contract to develop and install the nursing MIS. A detailed timetable was developed by the project nurse that complies with the rigid timetables laid down by the Department of Health and follows the procedures codified into a methodology for the introduction of a nursing IS developed by management consultants specifically for the NHS (Resource Management Directorate, 1990). It showed that the system would begin to be installed before Christmas 1992. This has not happened because the district decided in late summer 1992 to go for a workload methodology developed by the local Regional Authority called Group-Action $^{1}$ . By summer 1993, one supplier has been chosen and implementation is expected in September 1993.

In the second case the defence company is medium-sized with some 450 employees that are spread on four sites but it was only the head office with nearly 300 employees that was involved in the development of the financial system – the other three sites continue to use PC based systems. This company is relatively new – it was formed nearly three years ago from a demerger of a major defence contractor that had encountered some serious financial problems. It is a specialist company that provides sophisticated radar systems to both the Ministry of Defence and other countries. The employees are highly skilled and they work on projects of varying size and duration to fulfil specific orders or develop new products. The desire to install a new financial system was not new and a three month project to procure a system in 1990 produced no more than a report as it was felt unwise to install a financial system in isolation from other areas of activity. However, it was the demerger that provided an impetus for change because the existing system operated on a remote IBM mainframe provided by the head office of the previous group. The costs charged to the new company for this service were high and the flexibility of the system was very limited. Pressure for change from this source coincided with the arrival of a new financial controller who had experience of procuring new financial systems and it was he who persuaded the board to ask management consultants to bid for the procurement of a new financial system.

Two of the big six accountancy/management consultancy companies bid for the contract. The loser was somewhat tarnished by a poor record as auditors to the previous group but one of the reasons why the other company won was its detailed project planning that indicated 'a clear plan of action and approach' (Interview notes, 1992). The presentation to the board that clinched the contract used the management consultancy's in-house methodology Acme $^{2}$ to structure the project. The project began the following month and took 15 months to complete. The new financial system has been in operation for over a year. The installed system uses a client server architecture with package software that is based on a widely used relational database. Some bespoke work was carried out to tailor the package. The system can be judged a success based on the criteria of system use and user satisfaction (Malone, 1990).

The project covered five phases of the Acme methodology – each phase is subdivided into modules giving 24 modules in all and, in turn, each module is composed of between six and ten tasks that should be allocated to developers and/or users. The five major phases cover a strategic review, a delineation of business opportunities, an assessment of the current IT environment, the development of a new IT architecture and finally a detailed implementation phase. Research focused on the first four stages of this methodology.

Some general points emerged from these two cases which can be summarized as follows. First, in neither case was the prescribed methodology used as it was described in the manuals. Second, this did not act to prevent the completion of the project and could be seen to enable the success of the project. Third, a methodology can act as an insurance for developers who can always argue in future that they followed the methodology despite subsequent development failure but, at the same time, an adherence to a methodology was in terms of completing deliverables rather than an exhaustive compliance with the tasks to be performed in each stage. Fourth, methodologies act to constrain the involvement of users within the development process even if the methodology is designed to enhance this facet of development or, in other words, methodologies are not in themselves a guarantee of perceived successful user involvement.

To look at these issues in more detail, it perhaps is not surprising that a methodology of ISD was followed to the letter but the extent of deviations from the methodology were dramatic. In both cases the importance of a methodology was stressed for the systematic ordering of work to be done. As the consultant in charge of the defence project put it, the aim of the methodology is that 'any consultant can come into a project in Mauritius and know that they are on page 47 [of the manual of the in-house methodology'] (interview notes). However, within this project the methodology was rarely evident; some phases were dealt with in detail, others were skipped and some techniques were used that were not found in the methodology. This was more than just a contingent picking and choosing of parts of the methodology dependent on the circumstances of this particular project but appeared to have little in common with the mandatory in-house methodology. This resulted in some curious paradoxes. The management consultant used the language of the methodology in conversation with others, for example in phone conversations. He would refer to a certain phase in a project but at the same time it appears that what was done, in that phase, did not accord with what the manuals prescribed. This firm of management consultants used different people at different times in the lifetime of the project. At the beginning of the project a partner by himself from the consultants interviewed the directors of the defence company but then took no further active role; the management consultant in charge of the project then did subsequent interviewing of all the key personnel and remained with the project throughout. Other more junior staff did various tasks from time to time. It would be expected that a systematic methodology would be very beneficial in coordinating this work but it was not used as such in any detailed way. A further paradox was that the ISD methodology prescribes the use of Entity-Relationship (E-R) diagrams as a means of representing the existing and desired data structures in an organization. In this project this technique was not used and a much more rough and ready diagram was in circulation, which the consultant explained was one the client management could understand. On detailed questioning, it appeared that neither the management consultant nor many of his colleagues used nor could use E-R diagrams. In the hospital case, the project nurse followed the methodology more closely but she did leave out some stages that were recommended e.g. step 3 which defined the current situation. However, she did not inform anyone that this step was ignored nor was this evident in the completed deliverable; this was an operational requirement for prospective tenderers of the nursing IS.

Turning to the second point, in both cases the introduction of the new system has been successful to date. Success in the hospital case is difficult to assess because the system is not fully implemented but the planned milestones have been completed more or less on time and there are few visible sources of discontent with the process so far. In the defence case, the financial system has been successfully implemented in that the system is now running and was completed on time and within budget. Thus it appears that non-compliance with the prescribed ISD methodology has not affected the success of the development project. Indeed, it can be argued that it is conducive to success by enabling a more contingent and flexible process to occur in a specific site.

Third, compliance with an ISD methodology may act as an insurance for developers. This was most apparent in the hospital case in which the project nurse has only had experience of one previous and smaller project and where throughout the country experience in this area is limited. She was clear that being seen to follow a methodology was very important in this situation because, as nursing IS are relatively rare in this country, the outcome of this process is largely unknown despite her public pronouncements within the organization. If, by some unforeseen circumstance, the project was adjudged a failure, she would always be able to show that she was following the prescribed methodology. However, at the same time, this does not prevent her from deviating from this methodology from time to time though these deviations are not mentioned to others. An example was seen in a working party of senior nurses (Nursing Information System Working Group) brought together, following the methodology, to assist in the specification of the new nursing system. One meeting was to be devoted to ranking the preferred functionality of a new system by the working party as recommended by the methodology. At this meeting, the nurses were unable to decide what was desirable so the project nurse stepped in and decided what was most important: 'workload [measurement] is the key one [requirement]' (Project meeting notes). So, the methodology was followed but not in the way that would be expected.

In the defence case, compliance with the methodology was limited as has been mentioned above but, in this instance, the management consultant was experienced in this type of project and implied (in retrospect) that he was confident from the outset that the project could be completed successfully.

Finally, the question of user involvement in ISD and its relation to ISD methodologies needs to be addressed. Some methodologies (Lundberg, 1982; Mumford, 1983) emphasize the learning process that is involved in the development of an IS but many others do not and tend to relate problems in implementation to user resistance (Hirschheim and Newman, 1988). Within the defence case, users were widely interviewed at an early stage in the process to discover the functions they performed and the ways in which those functions were carried out. These interviews, while not directly asking prospective users to determine their requirements, did have the effect of mobilizing considerable support and interest in the project. However, once this stage was complete, these users were not involved again for three months when they commented on prospective financial systems. After a supplier had been chosen by the board based on the recommendation of the management consultants, users only became involved again when the system was implemented. In other words, the project alternated short phases of intensive user involvement with long periods of inaction. The defence company's project officer viewed users as being ‘a problem’ at the implementation stage because the initial interest had been lost. In this case though the consultant's methodology was not used in depth, a detailed project plan was in operation. User involvement then had to fit into this project plan as one of key attributes of success was seen as being able to complete the project on time. Methodologies tend to be designed to schedule the activities that should occur in IS development but, in this case and probably in others (Hirschheim and Newman, 1988), the shoehorning of user involvement to certain deadlines may have the effect of replacing longer term effectiveness, in terms of a well informed and supportive user base, by shorter term efficiency, through the timely completion of the system development.

The hospital case adhered more closely to a methodology which also supported user involvement to a greater extent with questionnaires, a representative working party, workshops and demonstrations of prospective systems. In this example, even though user involvement was encouraged in the methodology, the use of the methodology limited the degree of that involvement. For example, to return to the issue of constructing the IS requirements, the working party was tasked in the methodology to complete a detailed checklist of prospective requirements for the new system. In this, it appears that the methodology supports user involvement but, in use, the experience was that nurses did not understand all the technical terms that were contained in the questionnaire that had to be competed in a set time. Thus, much of that time was devoted by the project nurse in explaining technical issues such as the difference between batch and on-line processing. As a result the time constraints did not appear to allow those nurses to contribute effectively to the production of those requirements. This difficulty was compounded by the rigorous, technical and specific way in which these requirements had to be phrased for inclusion in an operational requirement for prospective tenderers. In brief, though many methodologies ignore user involvement, those that do address this issue, when they are used, run the risk of circumscribing the usefulness of user involvement through limiting the time available for users to go through a necessary process of learning.

## Conclusion

This paper began by suggesting that rather than just focusing on the theoretical elaboration of ISD methodologies or the provision of CASE tool support, a fruitful area of research centres on observing the practice of systems developers and others, and the processes of ISD methodologies in use. It was argued that longitudinal ethnographic studies will prove most useful rather than a reliance on questionnaires or retrospective interviews. Some issues that are raised by studying methodologies in use were pursued in the context of two case studies, one of which has used a longitudinal ethnographic technique. In these instances, it was found that the methodologies were not used as prescribed but this appears to have little effect on the success of these projects. Methodologies seem to grant developers legitimacy in following the activities they undertake even if methodologies are not followed in detail. However, a tension between ISD methodologies and user involvement in development was encountered even in situations in which the methodology specifically prescribed steps for the participation of users. The primary difficulty appears to be the desire to keep to project time scales which render user participation to be more symbolic than effective. These examples are both drawn from a specific area of IS development, the acquisition and customization of mini based software packages which is becoming an increasingly important way of developing IS systems. However, a similar approach could be applied to the work of software developers (Low and Woolgar, 1991) and extensive interviewing of software developers in the US has shown a gap between the theory of IS development and the practice of IS developers (Curtis et al., 1988).

It is likely that many examples are to be found where a methodology has been followed strictly and a successful implementation has followed. A strict adherence to a methodology is enforced by active CASE tools that are beginning to be used more widely. However, even with a strict adherence to methodological rules we can expect considerable room for interpretation ranging from the scoping of the project through to decisions on levelling in data flow diagrams. More generally, the investigation of ISD methodologies in use is likely to show that specific methodologies are applied in a variety of ways at different sites. Such findings would not invalidate the use of ISD methodologies but it does suggest that more research is needed to explore how methodologies are applied and that effort in this direction may be rewarding in observing ways in which methodologies are made to work in different settings despite developers not adhering to prescribed techniques.
