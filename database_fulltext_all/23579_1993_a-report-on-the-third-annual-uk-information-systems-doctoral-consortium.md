CONFERENCE REPORT

# A report on the Third Annual UK Information Systems Doctoral Consortium

BRENT WORK

Department of Mathematical and Computing Sciences, University of Surrey, Guildford, UK

The University of Warwick hosted the Third Annual UK Information Systems Doctoral Consortium from September 6–8, 1993 $^{1}$ . The ESRC and Glaxo helped to fund it. The Consortium brought together 41 doctoral candidates from 25 British Universities and nine experienced PhD supervisors from the information systems community $^{2}$ . It gave doctoral students an opportunity to present their research to a larger group of peers than is usually available within their own institutions. It also provoked debates concerning research methods and the state of information systems research in Britain. As a result of these discussions, the nine Consortium faculty selected five students and two alternates to represent the UK at an International Doctoral Consortium held annually at the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS), the premier forum for information systems research.

Professor Sir Brian Follett, the Vice Chancellor of Warwick University, began with a welcoming address. The first half of the programme divided delegates into eight groups, each consisting of one faculty member and several doctoral candidates. Each group addressed a broad theme:

\- Software quality improvement

• The software development process

\- Aligning business and information strategy

\- Users of information systems

\- Applications of information systems

\- Evaluating information systems

\- Object-orientation

\- The impact of information technology on organizations and markets

Within these groups, doctoral candidates presented and discussed their research aims, methods, and (in some cases) their tentative findings. Faculty members encouraged them to consider their intended contribution to information systems practice and research. Within these sessions the exchanges were stimulating. The controversy which often raged over specific research aims or methods led to deeper considerations of the nature of PhD degrees and of the information systems discipline itself. This part of the programme concluded with reports from each group to the Consortium as a whole concerning the issues which the group had discussed.

The second half focused on disciplinary questions. Delegates formed their own teams in order to consider one of three issues:

\- Raising the profile of the information systems discipline within the UK

\- Improving understanding between doctoral students and supervisors

\- Reviewing articles for learned journals

The teams then presented their conclusions to the Consortium. The presentation sparked a lively discussion. The exchange of views over supervisors' responsibilities for their doctoral students was particularly vibrant. Apparently, the doctrine that lecturers should be consumer-oriented is heartily accepted by the PhD students whom they supervise. On the other hand, the debate which arose concerning the apparent tension between relevance and rigour in information systems research suggested that doctoral candidates have a less generous attitude towards the prospective consumers of their own research.

The consortium left me with five lasting impressions. First I was reminded, yet again, of the multidisciplinary nature of information systems. Presentations ranged from the mathematical to the anthropological with a variety of social science and engineering subjects considered in-between. It is this very diversity which makes information systems research so exciting for me.

Secondly, while this diversity is intriguing, it has always masked the real size of the information systems community. Because its members might be found in business, computer science, mathematics, sociology, economics, or even language departments, it has been difficult to know just how large the UK community is. However, it seems that the Consortium may act as a focal point for information systems researchers. This year's event not only had the largest participation, but also involved the greatest number of institutions yet. Clearly, a sense of community is growing.

Thirdly, it appears that there are only a few universities in the UK which have a sufficient number of information systems doctoral candidates to sustain the spirited debates which are common at the Consortium. This causes students to feel a sense of isolation both personal and professional. The Consortium acts as an antidote and this alone justifies its existence.

Fourthly, the growing maturity of the discipline impressed me. Peter Keen first suggested in 1980 that in the information systems community there is little cumulative tradition of research $^{3}$ . Most information systems researchers have, historically, created their own frameworks and models to explain the phenomena which they studied rather than built on the work of others. In recent years, however, there has been a growing tendency to adopt theories from reference disciplines such as economics, sociology, philosophy, and linguistics. This tendency was evident throughout the Consortium. For example, the group which I attended considered structuration theory, transaction cost economics, resource dependence theory, phenomenology, hermeneutics, agency theory, soft systems theory, and grounded theory within the context of computer assisted software engineering (CASE), executive information systems (EIS), electronic data interchange (EDI), and office automation.

Fifthly, during the three years which I have attended the Consortium, the quality of the research has improved. The theoretical underpinnings have become more sophisticated. Doctoral candidates are more aware of methodological issues, although ideology is perhaps still the most frequent reason for selecting a research method. This year's students stated the aims of their research more clearly and succinctly. I would like to think that the two previous doctoral consortia played some role in this improvement.

My own reaction to the Consortium was enthusiastic. I found the proceedings far more stimulating and original than those of many large, international conferences in which the sessions focus on narrow topics and consist of 'refereed' papers authored by the former students of a single professor. Certainly, those delegates with whom I spoke all felt similarly. I would certainly recommend the Consortium to any doctoral candidate who is doing research in information systems.

After three years the Consortium is now established. Cranfield Institute of Technology will host the Fourth

Annual Consortium with support from the Open University. The Universities of Bath, Strathclyde, and Southampton have agreed to sponsor the following three consortia. The organizing committee of next year's event has proposed that the Consortium be extended in order to accommodate invited papers from leading researchers in the field which attempt to provoke debate on major issues in information systems research. The committee will also consider a request from this year's delegates to include tutorials on specific research methods.

Credit for the success of the Third Consortium must go to the working party which organized it: Bob Galliers (Warwick Business School), Tom Gough (University of Leeds), and Leslie Willcocks (Templeton College, Oxford). Of course, the real heroine was Bernadette Baker (Warwick Business School) who had to carry out the instructions of the working party. Also, I feel that I must mention Frank Land (Visiting Professor at LSE) who first conceived the idea and Anne Leeming (City University Business School) and Bruce Weber (now at the Stern School of Business of New York University) for making what was only an idea reality. Finally, I want to express gratitude to Glaxo which has provided financial support to all three consortia and to the ESRC which gave a generous grant for this year's event.
