# Reaping the benefits of a responsive IT department

NICK RUSSELL and SIOBHAN MUSKETT

Price Waterhouse, London EC2Y 9PB, UK

In many companies, the IT department fails to enjoy the recognition and cooperation its members feel it deserves. The authors contend that the root of this problem is organizational, citing evidence from a large scale international survey of IT professionals. They describe a number of specific organizational problems that contribute to the lack of understanding and impede the creation of a flexible and responsive IT department. They then outline a number of potential remedies for those organizational problems and discuss practical ways in which necessary changes might be made, based on the experience of companies which have already addressed the problem.

## Introduction

What curse is it that plagues IT departments and ensures that, despite high levels of expertise and professionalism, many extra hours of 'slog' to get systems working properly and the advent of systems with undreamed-of processing and automating powers, there is a growing disenchantment within many businesses towards their IT departments? For frustration and tension there certainly has been in recent years.

The discontentment started because promised systems were so often late, over budget and, when they arrived, did not offer the ease of the use, simplification of process or staff savings that had been expected. As more major systems were implemented new problems emerged, notably that working with them required changes in organization and practice for which the business was poorly prepared. In addition, senior executives felt out of control because of their dependence on systems they did not really understand.

More recently, concern has focused on the highly centralist tendencies of many IT departments, just as other functions are decentralising their activities as much as possible. Resignedly, business managers are concluding that IT and the rest of the business just don't understand each other.

In gathering data for its annual publication, the Information Technology Review, Price Waterhouse conducts regular large-scale surveys of the opinions of an international panel of 5000 IT professionals around the world. Additionally, it carries out surveys of specific groups, such as the programme of interviews of 102 board-level executives of 100 major companies worldwide, undertaken in 1991. The results of this research indicate that the root of many IT department's difficulties is organizational. While IT departments often have special problems all their own, it is the attempt to increase flexibility and responsiveness across the whole business that sheds light on why IT and the rest of the business have suffered from a ‘culture gap’ that has prevented them understanding each other.

The good news from the most dynamic organizations is that they recognize that both sides in this ‘falling-out’ are to blame (not just the IT experts), and that there are practical steps that can be taken to remedy the situation. The bad news, which is no surprise, is that it doesn’t happen overnight.

A number of key objectives have been identified which, when achieved, seem to be the most effective in creating a responsive and flexible organization – the kind that IT departments aspire to be. These are:

(1) universal commitment to serving the customer's needs, for both external and internal customers;

(2) clear communication of the company's business goals, giving staff a clear understanding of the context and final objective of their particular activity;

(3) breakdown of vertical department hierarchies in favour of cross-functional working, each function contributing their expertise to developing effective business solutions;

(4) removal of excess management layers, stimulating devolution of decision making and cutting out bureaucratic procedures;

(5) empowered and empowering managers who seek to motivate and support their operating staff rather than control them, and who have confidence in their own project and staff management skills;

(6) effective teamworking, particularly from multifunctional project teams, to develop innovative and widely accepted solutions to business problems.

There is a danger that these objectives will be dismissed on the grounds that they are unattainable in ‘real’ organizations, although their desirability is universally accepted. Yet companies do derive sustainable advantage over their competitors by fulfilling these goals, through a single-minded commitment which starts at the top and permeates through the whole organization. This article discusses why each of them is important for improving the business effectiveness of the IT department and how they might be achieved.

## Focus on customer service

In his recent book ‘Managing IT at board level’, Kit Grindley (1991) of the London School of Economics and Price Waterhouse, concludes that the major problem that has led to business’ disenchantment with IT has been the separation of power from responsibility. The IT department held the power to create and control the IT systems, but no responsibility for the business results they generated. The system users/customers, on the other hand, had full responsibility for the operating performance enabled by their systems, but little power to run or amend those systems. Looked at this way, it is hardly surprising that relationships have been soured.

Focus on customer service principally concerns acceptance of responsibility for delivery of the solution/product/service that the customer wants. In organizations committed to customer service, the most important employees are those who directly manage the relationships with the company's customers; the support departments are responsible for delivering the services the front line staff require to sustain those relationships.

Customer-driven organizations effectively turn their organization pyramids upside down, as shown in Figure 1. At the top of the structure are the front line staff who deal directly and regularly with customers. Underneath them are the support functions who provide the systems and services they need to deliver results to the customer. For these functions, the customer service personnel are their customers.

This philosophy upturns the organization because it transforms the role of managers, whose responsibility becomes to enable and support their operational staff rather than to control them. The top management have to identify the resource and other needs of their customers, the company staff, and their duty is to coordinate the delivery of those resources. This is true within each department as much as across the company as a whole.

Recognizing the primacy of the customer marks no small change for many IT departments. Contrast these two quotations from IT managers in the PW survey:

"Technical people are prima donnas. They don't see the business comes first".

"We saw our main competitor connect their customers directly with their database. Lights flashed on for us. Once the computer is connected to the market, it must influence the market. We had to follow suit."

The inverted pyramid highlights the fact that IT only confers competitive advantage when it allows the company's customers to be better served than the competition's. That then needs to be the focus of the IT department's efforts and the acid test against which investments are judged.

A large chemical manufacturing company illustrates the impact of a customer service orientation in IT and how to set about achieving it. The IT department had identified a number of key problems; they were not seen as delivering value for money or a high level of service, the service level agreements with other corporate departments were onerous and morale among their own front line operations staff was poor. A number of previous investigations had only offered vague descriptions of the areas of customer dissatisfaction and the IT operations had reached crisis mode, so a project team was charged with finding a concrete and specific answer to the problems.

It was recognized at the outset that full commitment from both the customers and the IT senior managers was needed, leading to joint workshops to establish a vision and objectives for the future. Best customer and IT service practice were described to stimulate discussion and suggestions for improvement were grouped under the headings of Culture, Organization, People and Systems (COPS).

The project team put flesh on this framework by visiting other companies to gain more ideas and then held Customer Service workshops with various types of users to clarify the different needs they had. At the same time, they identified a number of ‘Quick Win’ service improvements on which to take immediate action. The team recognized that as well as the ‘tide of change’ to a customer service focus, there needed to be a number of individual ‘waves of action’ to demonstrate the project’s energy. The immediate changes showed both the department and the customers that something was happening and built up support and momentum.

To complete the process, the team involved line managers in analysing the workshop findings and developing a longer term plan. The outcome was much greater understanding between IT and the other functions and a department that was focused on customer service and the requirements of the business.

## Universal awareness of business goals

Historically, IT departments have been less concerned with the overall goals of the business than with delivering or maintaining specific systems to perform particular tasks. They have not been expected to lift their eyes from their screens to put their work in context or develop a broader vision of the company's multifarious interlocking activities.

Ironically, the success of IT has played a large part in convincing top managements that their businesses benefit when their workforce have a fuller awareness of their mission and aims. IT has highlighted, through the integration of previously separate systems and processes, the interdependence of a business's various activities. IT systems, by taking over many of the routine and repetitive tasks, have turned many staff into ‘knowledge workers’ who make judgements in ambiguous situations and who can suggest positive improvements to processes from their detailed knowledge.

The survey of IT managers previously mentioned indicated that in many cases, neither the IT staff nor their colleagues elsewhere in the business are in the best position to operate as effective knowledge workers because they do not clearly understand each other's contribution or goals. Grindley (1991) describes the problem as the 'Culture Gap'. The two charts show respectively the operational symptoms of the gap and the effects that the gap has on the business. Of course the problems listed reflect the priorities of the IT side but this scarcely lessens their importance (Figure 2).

The implications of these charts are clear: in many companies there is a failure of communication, both information and context, between staff in different functions. IT staff appear particularly isolated. This impedes the development of flexibility and responsiveness in the organization because staff are not clearly aware of the firm's overall objectives and vision.

For IT departments therefore, the challenge is to convey a better sense of what IT can offer, while developing a more commercial understanding of other functions. This will reduce the culture gap and ensure that the systems designed more truly reflect business requirements and expectations. The IT director is critical to this objective. He/she sits on the main board and justifies that position by his/her contribution to business issues and by understanding fully their implications for the IT systems.

His/her other main responsibility, along with the other directors, is to ensure effective communication of corporate goals and plans to the operating staff. This should be seen as part of the empowering managerial role required in a customer driven organization. A regular programme of briefing meetings with immediate subordinates to allow two way feedback should be set up at each level of the hierarchy. This will allow information to cascade down and up the company and provide staff with the additional knowledge they require.

## Breaking down department hierarchies

Large organizations have a tendency to develop functional departments containing many specialists and experts, which become inward looking and isolated from other functions. This is an easy trap to fall into; line management is all performed within the department and promotion prospects are usually confined there. In addition, the departmental team share a common knowledge base often with its own language and worldview – and recognize their primary loyalty to those closest colleagues with whom they work. This is particularly true of IT departments, whose functions and skills are very poorly understood by outsiders.

The new priority given to process redesign and the need to provide quick response to customers mean that the vertically oriented introspective department is a serious handicap to performance. Rather, departmental staff need to build strong cooperative working relationships across all the other functions. For IT staff, charged with developing systems to join together the inputs and outputs of those functions, such cooperation should significantly improve the fitness of the end result.

IT professionals' historical commitment to central mainframe systems and an autonomous corporate department to support them have been the cause of much hostility from the rest of the business as decentralisation has become more common. Departments which are replacing their vertical hierarchies with a more outward looking structure resent the continuing centralisation of IT, particularly as desktop and laptop computing have become so much more powerful.

This is a real challenge to IT, a test of their commitment to integrate with the main business and to give priority to business goals. There are undoubtedly circumstances where a large central mainframe offers the most economic and functionally effective solution to the company's data processing needs, whose benefits can be readily demonstrated. Nevertheless, IT department heads must be willing to rigorously scrutinise their organization and operations and ensure that they do not advocate a large centralised outfit for reasons of empire building, increased management posts or defending an elite, isolationist IT culture.

An ‘ivory tower’ IT department may seem to offer defence against change and the influence of the uninformed, but this is illusory. In the longer term, IT will only justify its own influence and continuing investment by delivering benefits to its users. Its chances of doing so will be determined largely by its willingness to get involved with business problems and share ownership of them with its customers. This is likely to require a structure in which the members of the group who manage relationships with the users are given the greatest influence over departmental operations and style. Such a structure will be horizontally oriented and outward looking, dramatically different from existing vertical hierarchies.

Figure 3 below, taken from ‘Thriving on Chaos’ by Tom Peters (1989), depicts his vision of the flexible, porous, adaptive organization of the future. No rigid, regular boundaries here, or strongly delineated management hierarchies within functions. Peters (1989) advocates it because it works, obtaining higher motivation and output from employees as well as increased flexibility and lower costs. Initiative, inventiveness and cooperation are empowered to the greater benefit of the organization.

One survey respondent described the underlying issue thus

"Human resources and IT are the lead functions today. They are leading the information revolution, which redefines growth, not as a process which moves from the employment of a hundred to the employment of two hundred, but as a process enabling a hundred, struggling with a business bottleneck, to function more effectively as twenty."

## Removing excess management layers

Surplus managers are most often found in the insular hierarchical departments just described. They reflect the view that all promotion should take place within one area of specialism and that seniority is related to superior expertise rather than man management skills. The effect of excess layers is to blur responsibility and accountability throughout the management hierarchy, increasing the number of meetings, slowing decisions and encouraging the avoidance of risks.

Removing management layers has been found to clarify the responsibilities of those remaining and to improve their effectiveness. The managers are busier so place more emphasis on their priorities and less time worrying who is checking up on them. Meetings and bureaucracy reduce and decisions are taken lower down the organization. As a result the business becomes more responsive and delivers faster results reflecting commercial needs.

IT has had a poor reputation for project management for a number of years; projects are invariably late and over budget. Some of this is due to the open-ended brief for the systems work undertaken. No-one has done it before, so it is hard to estimate what resources will be needed to deliver. Even so a large part of the blame lies with unclear management roles and a shortage of project management and motivational skills. Cutting out layers would provide IT departments with an ideal opportunity to foster new behaviour patterns from their managers, including much greater business awareness and acceptance of responsibility, along with the people management skills previously neglected.

A public sector organization, seeking to change its style and reduce its costs, recently conducted such an exercise in its IT function. It had a number of one-on-two and one-on-three reporting lines and hence a large number of management tiers. It first identified all the distinct operating units at the bottom of the department, performing discrete tasks. It then proposed four managers to oversee the supervisors of those 23 units with management ratios between one-on-five and one-on-eight, grouped by relatedness of tasks. One second tier manager was proposed to oversee the four managers. When the structure was implemented, two management tiers were abolished, resulting in much clearer accountability and effective devolution of decision making.

## Fostering empowering managers

'Empowering' may be a fashionable jargon word, but it originally gained currency because it clearly described what it meant. The main thrust of the range of changes being wrought in dynamic organizations to make them more responsive is to involve and motivate the workforce by entrusting power to them. Whereas more conventional organizations use a variety of controls and checks to ensure that staff don't cheat, make mistakes or take risks, empowerment allows staff greater leeway to do those things by lightening the controls. The underlying company value is a belief that staff who clearly understand the company's goals and objectives and how their own work contributes to them will repay trust with improved performance – harder, better, more intelligent effort. In place of control the empowering manager provides support and assistance, coordinating resources and assisting communication between suppliers and users.

IT staff have long suffered from their image as a group of ‘alternative’ workers who are satisfying personal rather than company goals and who lack commitment to business results. The picture of the long-haired sandal wearing, unconventional IT worker has been slow to fade. Consequently insufficient effort has been made either to explain the business context of their work, or to capitalize on their vision of how systems might link operations together. It is intrinsic that empowering managers would seek to remedy both those failings – having in turn been given the freedom to do so.

The concept raises again the issues of linkage between power and responsibility and highlights why trust is an important ingredient. If greater influence is entrusted to the IT professionals with regard to business – rather than just systems – issues, matching responsibility for and commitment to business results will also be expected. IT staff will need to demonstrate their active concern to ensure that their systems fulfil commercial expectations as well as providing bug-free operation. Empowerment offers a similar challenge to IT staff as to engineers and the other specialists whose expertise is beyond the understanding of the business managers and administrators. Technical solutions must not be ends in themselves but rather means by which the company adds value and competitive advantage.

Achieving the trust and empowerment that a responsive IT department will need requires regular communication across as well as up and down the company and an emphasis on support and cooperation between functions to deliver results to the customer. There must also be a commitment to reduce bureaucracy and get away from elaborate control procedures.

Having established an appropriate environment, direct management training is still needed to help managers focus on the specific changes demanded in their operating style. IT managers, like other functional specialists, will have been recruited principally for their technical knowledge, not their man management skills. The prescribed changes will require dramatic shifts in approach, together with a variety of new skills; intelligence and general education are unlikely to be adequate to equip managers for their tasks. Coaching in the desired behaviours increases both competence and confidence, leading to a faster and better adjustment.

## Effective teamworking

It should be clear from the other organizational changes described that the emphasis of flexible organizations is on cross-functional cooperation, on concern with results not procedures and on striving towards shared company goals rather than addressing departmental and political agendas. Multi-functional task and project teams are often found to be the best vehicles for achieving these ends.

IT departments are very effective at putting together teams of IT professionals and now need to broaden those teams to embrace colleagues from other disciplines, particularly the systems users, in order to increase the utility of their designs. Training in such teamworking will be required and personal commitment to achievement of shared goals will also be critical. Fortunately, smooth running teams are amongst the most satisfying and motivating structures within which to work, so that, once underway, they can be expected to generate their own commitment and dedication.

A trading company operating in 17 European countries offers a good example of the benefits of the multifunctional team for tackling IT projects. Their task was to identify improvements to the common business processes undertaken in all the countries in which they traded. They were also asked to suggest how IT could be used to improve the processes and results.

The coordinator set up a team that was both multifunctional and multi-national with representatives from all 17 countries. Among them were specialists in production, marketing, sales and finance, a couple of IT project managers and a few hybrid business/IT managers.

They set about their task by agreeing their common business objectives and then sharing information from their differing national and functional perspectives about their existing local processes. From this base all worked together to quickly identify how they could harmonise their processes internationally, assisted by a single IT system for each process.

The team was spectacularly successful in the speed and quality of the solutions it came up with. The coordinator commented that the IT opportunities were particularly well addressed, delivering IT system proposals that were operationally highly effective in speeding up the processes and in exploiting the best features found in different countries. They were also well received as there was a strong sense of joint ownership on the team and an acknowledgement that the IT solution had a strong practical business orientation.

Diversity of background experience, joint commitment to the team tasks and exploitation of the different team roles available to its members were felt to be the key features that allowed the team to flourish and perform, transforming a free flow of ideas into a series of practical actions.

## Generating IT responsiveness

Organizations which have successfully implemented the transformations described above have found that it not only dramatically increases their flexibility and responsiveness to the point of conferring competitive advantage, but also that it has a powerful liberating and motivating effect on their workforces, yielding greater unity of purpose and superior results.

For IT departments, the change closes the culture gap between them and the rest of the business, increases their influence over business direction, allows fuller realization of the benefits of IT and provides greater satisfaction to the workforce. These benefits are clearly well worth the effort required to achieve them and change becomes an opportunity rather than a threat.

An example of the process and its benefits is provided by a division of an integrated oil company which radically reassessed its organization and operations. It was one of a number of change initiatives underway in the company.

Their IT predicament was similar to that of many companies. Information systems were being used for control rather than support, and they had a history of costly development overruns. They perceived that the IT department's knowledge of the core business was too low, yet the departmental career structure devalued time spent working in other disciplines.

To address these problems, they set up a programme of face-to-face and telephone interviews and group discussions with business managers, IT project managers, IT users and IT staff. Their investigations included a review of each group's attitudes to each other to explore the extent of the cultural divide.

Armed with this and a lot of project related information, they set up a ‘visioning’ workshop involving representatives of all the key groups. This workshop defined a series of high level actions and set up task forces to draft the necessary plans.

When the task force plans were complete, a ‘commitment’ workshop involving the senior business and IT managers was held to review them and ensure commitment to their implementation. Outcomes highlighted by this process included:

(1) designated managers to track the benefits arising from the process;

(2) a radical rethink of the requirements for management information and control;

(3) freedom for users to rely on decentralized systems which met their needs;

(4) a ‘joint process of discovery’ between IT staff and users for systems whose requirements are difficult to specify at the outset;

(5) revised career structures, including longer placements, with accountability, for IT staff;

(6) senior business managers to give top priority to contributing to key IT projects;

(7) a strong cost/benefit analysis argument for a longer term process of change.

The diagnostic process itself was a highly positive experience as it highlighted to all the value of cooperation and clear communication across the business functions. The changes it proposed are now being implemented and the benefits are being felt by all, though it is understood that developing full IT responsiveness will take some time and result from an accumulation of learning and experience.

## Conclusions

In conclusion, it seems that the curse which plagues many IT departments is that they are too unresponsive to the business context of the companies of which they are a part. Their managers are in danger of taking too narrow a view of the role and benefits of IT for the company, which they are unable to sell to their business departments. This creates a culture gap between IT and the rest of the firm, preventing either side making the most of the benefits IT could offer.

Whatever the route taken to achieve a responsive IT department, whether delayering, multi-functional teamworking, empowerment of managers or any other, the key issues remain the same. The IT department is responsible for providing a supporting infrastructure that assists its customers across the company to achieve broader business goals. It needs to build bridges across the company to its customers in the other departments to ensure clear communication and developing understanding on both sides. The topics outlined here describe ways in which these bridges can most effectively be built and sustained. Once established, these bridges allow a shared appreciation of both the organization's operating objectives and of how the IT department can respond flexibly to the information technology demands and opportunities implied.
