# Computer supported collaborative working: lessons from elsewhere

CHRIS CLEGG, PATRICK WATERSON and NEIL CAREY
MRC/ESRC Social and Applied Psychology Unit, Department of Psychology,
University of Sheffield, Sheffield S10 2TN, UK

In this paper we review two application areas in the field of IT concerning advanced manufacturing technology and office automation. We argue that new systems in both these areas have been predominantly technology-led in their development and implementation, excluding adequate attention to their human and organizational aspects. We describe two case studies as illustrations of these points and argue that the reasons for this technology-led approach lie in a complex interacting set of social systems that work effectively to marginalize the human and organizational aspects of the new technologies.

The applicability of these findings to the domain of Computer Supported Collaborative Working (CSCW) is examined. There are two main sets of lessons that can be learned: those that apply generally to the field of IT, and thereby have relevance also to the special case of CSCW; and those that are particular to the conduct and content of research and development into CSCW. These lessons are outlined in the form of sets of suggestions.

## Introduction

This paper is concerned with the human and organizational aspects of new computer-based technologies. Whilst there remain large gaps in our knowledge about these issues, we also understand a great deal about them. Such understandings however are not always translated into practice. This paper is predicated on the assumption that research and development into some relatively early application domains of IT has generalizable relevance to newer, emerging fields. As such we review some of the lessons from earlier studies and consider their potential meaning for the particular domain of Computer Supported Collaborative Working (CSCW).

At the outset we should be clear about some terminology. First, we prefer the term ‘collaborative working’ to that of ‘cooperative working’ because the former seems to us the more generic description. Second, a number of issues under the heading of ‘the human and organizational aspects of IT’ are incorporated. These include: the ergonomics of the technology (Oborne, 1985; Dainoff and Dainoff, 1987; Grandjean, 1987); its impact on user’s health and safety (Pearce, 1984; Karwowski et al., 1988); the quality of the human–computer interface and the user’s interactions with the system (Nickerson, 1986; Shneiderman, 1986); the allocation of tasks between humans and computers (Clegg et al., 1989); the impact of the technology on the user’s job content and job quality (Otway and Peltu, 1983; Cummings and Blumberg, 1987; Wall et al., 1987); the wider impacts on how the work is organized and structured (Child, 1984; Hirschheim,

1985; Hirschhorn, 1986); and issues associated with the management and processes of change (Blackler and Brown, 1986). We should also add that the tone of this paper is polemical – the intention is to promote debate and action.

We begin the paper by reviewing two principal application domains in which we have experience, those concerned with the implementation of advanced manufacturing technologies, and with the development of complex systems of office automation. We argue that each of these application domains has been technology-led and, in large part, has excluded detailed consideration of the psychological and organizational concerns listed above. The notion of technology-led in each of these areas is identified in terms of a number of interconnected attributes using the notion of an ideal type.

Two case studies then illustrate some of the ways in which new technologies are developed and implemented; one from a manufacturing domain, the second concerned with system development. The first case involves the implementation of a Computer Aided Design Computer Aided Manufacturing (CADCAM) system in a UK engineering company. The second focuses on the introduction of Information Engineering (IE) in a large UK service organization. IE is a computer-based methodology for developing complex computer-based systems. These cases are apposite here in that they are both examples of CSCW, and at the same time illustrate some features of technology-led change.

We then consider why the human and organizational aspects of IT are marginalized, arguing that it results from the interaction of a wider set of social systems. Some of the contributory social factors are delineated.

Whether or not these findings have any applicability to the domain of CSCW is examined at the end of the paper. CSCW is subject to the same social conditions and forces as other forms of new technology, and is thereby prone to the same problems. There is some evidence that supports the proposition that CSCW is, in part at least, technology-led. There are potential lessons of two kinds. First, some suggestions for change are made that are applicable to the field of IT generally, and that are also of potential use to the CSCW community. Second, we argue that knowledge of these other areas, coupled with an understanding of progress in this particular field, allows us to make some substantive suggestions for the content and conduct of research and development into CSCW specifically.

## Implementation of advanced manufacturing technology

By Advanced Manufacturing Technology (AMT) we mean any form of computer-based technology that is involved directly or indirectly in the manufacturing process (Sharlt et al., 1986; Wall and Kemp, 1987). The most common forms of AMT include computer numerically controlled machine tools, robots, flexible manufacturing systems, computer integrated manufacturing, computer aided design, CADCAM and computer aided production management. These technologies are in widespread and increasing use (Wall and Kemp, 1987; Edquist and Jacobson, 1988; Van Cott, 1991).

Our core proposition is that many of the new investments in and implementations of AMT have been technology-led. This proposition is not novel. Here however, we try to identify what we mean by this relatively systematically, characterizing ‘technology-led’ in terms of a number of key attributes using the notion of an ideal type. It is easiest to do this in the form of a set of separate propositions recognizing that these issues are highly interconnected.

1. The introduction of AMT follows a pattern of sequential development, with the technology considered in advance of its human and organizational aspects, as illustrated in Figure 1. This can be contrasted with the joint or parallel development advocated by the socio-technical theorists (Cherns, 1976, 1987). Put the other way round, genuine socio-technical development of AMT is rare (Clegg, 1993).

2. In such implementations, technological concerns attract most organizational resources (in terms of time, energy, commitment, money, skills, etc), in comparison with the resources invested in human and organizational concerns. Most of these resources are in the managerial gift, but this statement applies to all resources, including users' time (see point 11 below).

3. High technology solutions are often sought, even when they are not necessarily appropriate. Such choices about the type and level of technology are usually made by technologists (as opposed to users). There are few models or tools available to decision makers that help them make contingent choices about the level and type of technology that fits their business and their needs (for an exception, see Ayres and Miller, 1983). Some high technologies also become fashionable and subject to widespread hype (e.g. flexible manufacturing systems).

4. There is a huge emphasis on new functionality, on the new clever things that the technology can do. New functionality is seen as a key competitive weapon by developers and suppliers. Functionality becomes an attribute of the technology, rather than a characteristic of the task that the user needs to undertake.

5. There is also a heavy marketing emphasis. There is a recognizable supplier community competing to identify and develop new markets. The suppliers compete in terms of design innovations and functionality, rather than in terms of actual performance. By contrast, there is less of an identifiable and powerful lobby of users, i.e. one that can shape the needs and conduct of the market. New products are supply-pushed into the market place rather than demand-pulled.

6. Investments in new technology are sometimes loosely coupled to the business strategy, to meeting the particular needs of the business (Piercy, 1987). There is a lack of strategic planning and thinking about AMT, especially concerning organizational change.

7. Investments in new technology are justified in terms of rapid payback, usually achieved through cost savings. This encourages a view that their principal benefit is in reducing the demand for human skills (either through reduced 'headcount' or 'deskilling').

8. There is a surprising lack of attention to careful and detailed evaluation of new investments in technology. There is a lack of attention to the actual performance of new systems and the extent to which they meet stated objectives. There is usually an absence of evaluation of the impact of such technologies on their users or on a range of wider organizational factors.

9. AMT has been appropriated by the engineering community. Professionally this is their domain of expertise, opening up new career opportunities. Locally, at the level of the individual firm, there is the sense that engineers 'own' the new technologies, especially during development and implementation.

10. Equivalently, there is a lack of ownership and appropriation by end-users (defined broadly to include actual end-users and the line managers responsible for running AMT). Interestingly too, the whole debate about end-user participation has been couched in terms of the 'problem' of getting users to participate with system developers and implementers, who are seen as the legitimate experts in this field. The debate has NOT been couched in terms of the difficulty of getting technical experts to participate with end-users in developing systems that meet their work needs.

11. Where users are actively involved in the process of system development, this is more likely to be in testing the functionality of the technology (e.g. user acceptance testing processes) and in training how to use it. Put another way, user issues are technically defined.

12. Within manufacturing, the human operator is seen as a source of error, of unpredictability. Wherever possible, humans should be designed out of advanced systems. Where they cannot be, they get the 'left-over' tasks that cannot be automated. Choices about the allocation of system functions are techno-centred and based on Tayloristic assumptions about human operators. The limits to automation are set by technical feasibility and cost, not in terms of designing an optimum work system.

13. Organizations introducing AMT tend to satisfy (Simon, 1960). They get by. In Blackler and Brown's (1986) terms, they often work hard at replacing an incremental and piecemeal implementation (Model 0), with a task and technology-centred approach (Model 1) (Symon and Clegg, 1991).

14. The new technologies are seen as a new way of doing the same things, a change in medium. They are not seen as a catalyst for the planned reorganization of working practices and structures. Where there is a recognition of the possibility of organizational change, e.g. in the case of the current interest in business process re-engineering, this is seen as re-engineering the social structure to fit the new technology; i.e. it remains technology-led (rather than socio-technical) in emphasis.

15. Consistent through all the above comments has been the underlying point that most AMT implementations ignore the human and organizational factors (the social factors in socio-technical theory). There is a lack of detailed consideration of the ergonomics of the new system, the health and safety implications, the quality of the human-computer interfaces and interactions, choices about the relative roles of humans and computers in the system, the quality and design of end-users' jobs, the ways in which the organizational systems and structures need to change, and the ways in which change is managed. Thus whilst there is ample evidence that there are social choices to make, few resources get invested in these issues. Put another way, there are few genuinely socio-technical developments in the field of AMT; and where they are attempted, the emphasis is usually on improving the social systems and factors around a given technology. (Of course there are some exceptions to this, e.g. the work by Rosenbrock and others in developing more human-centred technologies for use in European manufacturing; see Rosenbrock, 1989).

16. At the same time, there is evidence that investments in AMT are often relatively disappointing, failing to meet original expectations and aspirations. Even recognizing that formal proposals for capital expenditure are political documents liable to exaggerate expected performance, nevertheless actual outcomes are widely held to be disappointing (Majchrzak, 1988; Kearney, 1990; Symon and Clegg, 1991; Smith, 1993).

We should be clear about these propositions. We are not claiming that all new manufacturing technologies are implemented in this way, nor that any single implementation completely fits this specification. We recognize that these attributes would not necessarily cohere in any single instance. Nor are we claiming that these issues are limited to AMT; we believe that some hold currency in other domains of IT. Furthermore, some are not limited to IT at all, for example regarding the lack of evaluation of change (point 8 above). However, we are claiming that this is a powerful ideal type to which many organizations have implicitly or explicitly subscribed within the domain of AMT.

A further issue concerns the extent to which such an approach is changing, perhaps becoming outdated? Our own experience leads us to believe that manufacturing organizations, in the UK at least, are less technically oriented than they were, say eight years ago. In particular, some of the features described above are certainly less prevalent; for example the need for IT to fit into the business strategy is widely recognized (point 6 above). Furthermore, it is clear that some of the leading international research and development groups in this area are also less technocentric than they were, advocating the search for appropriate technologies rather than the universal pursuit of computer integrated manufacturing (see Bullinger, 1993). But these remain qualifications of the core argument; our interpretation is that the field of AMT remains largely technology-led, implicitly or explicitly subscribing to many of the characteristics above.

## Development of office automation

Here we are concerned with the ways in which large scale office systems are developed, paying particular attention to the ways in which their human and organizational aspects are addressed. In this section we use the same rhetorical device, trying to characterize the world of software development in the form of a set of propositions.

1. Large scale software development projects are extended over time, often lasting several months or even years.

2. They involve many people with different skills and backgrounds, for example including systems analysts, programmers, project managers, users of various kinds and their managers, user project managers, etc. These processes are highly social and collaborative (rather than individual) activities. They are also quite highly differentiated, by which we mean that people in different roles may have quite different perceptions about the processes in which they are involved, and in their outcomes.

3. People working on such projects may do so on different ‘contracts’. For example, some may be seconded to a project on a part-time basis for a specified period, whilst others may be full-time for all or some of the lifetime of the project. Roles of individuals may also change; for example an individual may begin on a project as an analyst, then work on programming, then on system maintenance. Others may remain in more specialist roles, e.g. database design. The process is also modularized, being organized in discrete packages of work.

4. These projects are goal-oriented, usually with specified objectives. They are planful, involving workplans, deliverables and critical paths. Increasingly they involve project management techniques, reporting structures and control procedures.

5. These processes often utilize specific tools and methods, for example to help project management. Increasingly too, methods are being developed and deployed to help with technical aspects of systems development. Such structured methods are becoming increasingly popular, more 'cradle-to-grave' in emphasis, and more technically-based (i.e. computer supported, perhaps in the form of CASE tools). In one interpretation, project managers and technologists are recognizing that these processes are poorly integrated. Unsurprisingly perhaps, they are seeking technical solutions to what may be regarded as an organizational problem of integration.

6. All these structured methods and techniques have a heavy explicit and implicit technical emphasis. Few of them include consideration of relevant human and organizational factors (Hornby et al., 1992). Whilst there are a few emerging human and organizationally focused techniques (e.g. ETHICS – see Mumford, 1986), these are relatively rarely used (Maclaren et al., 1989).

7. At the same time, the users of such methods are pragmatists, using them when it suits them and in ways that suit them, rather than 'by the book'.

8. System developers report that they are hugely constrained, for example by time and money, but also by their role and the accompanying expectations of their clients, bosses and employers. Given their technical background they are also ‘constrained’ by their knowledge, expertise and skills.

9. System development processes are also quite messy and ill-structured. There is an enduring tension between getting the system fixed in its requirements, and ensuring it meets emerging and changing user needs. These processes are emergent and opportunistic. Design, as with decision-making, is not entirely structured and rationalistic.

10. The lack of user ownership mentioned in the context of AMT is also relevant here. There is little evidence that end-users have appropriated the process of software development. Whilst the process of system development is undoubtedly plural and social, the model is one of partial expertise. The dominant expertise is technical; there is less application domain knowledge. Only relatively rarely are end-users heavily involved and influential in the process of software development (in spite of all the rhetoric about user-centred design). In practice when users are involved, they can be seen as a further complication and constraint in a job that is already difficult enough (Heinbokel et al., 1993).

11. People involved in system development do not see the human and organizational issues as their problem. They do not feel responsible for these issues; if anyone's, that is the role of the client or user project manager. System developers are not trained in these issues and claim no particular knowledge or expertise in them. They are not encouraged to, or rewarded for, considering them (Perrow, 1983).

12. Overall, these processes are not the carefully integrated, cascading projects that some would have us believe, with seamless transitions from one defined stage to another. Rather they can best be seen as relatively disjunctive, fragmented, non-integrated processes. This view is represented in Figure 2.

13. In this context it is very difficult to undertake participation, and this can be seen in two complementary ways. Influence on the earlier design of a system by its users is difficult in such circumstances. Similarly, participation on the part of strategists and analysts later in the process, is also hard to organize. They have usually moved on to other work. Such forms of genuine participation (i.e. incorporating information sharing, interaction and influence) seem rare.

14. Given these disjunctions in the process, the fragmentation of expertise and effort, and the overall lack of participation, the opportunities for individual and organizational learning seem strictly limited. Indeed, if one wanted to design a system that would inhibit individual and organizational learning, it could well look something like this.

15. As with AMT, this domain operates in such a way as to exclude active consideration of the range of human and organizational concerns identified earlier. The field of software development, as characterized above, is technology-centred.

Again we should be clear about our propositions. We are not claiming that this characterization of the system development process is true in all cases of office automation, nor that these factors would necessarily all coexist in any single instance. This is offered as an ideal type representing a particular way of working which we believe is common. The core argument remains: the human and organizational aspects of new technologies are under-represented during development and implementation.

## Case studies

Two case studies follow: one concerned with manufacturing industry and one with office systems development. Both cases involve technologies and ways of working that incorporate CSCW; the first, CADCAM, the second, IE.

## Case study 1: implementation of CADCAM

This study involved the introduction of a CADCAM system into a large engineering company in the UK. It is more fully described in Symon and Clegg (1991). In this case an engineering company, of approximately 2000 employees, was implementing a state-of-the-art CADCAM system with a view to achieving a number of objectives; in particular, reducing product lead times, improving design for manufacturability, and improving the relationship between design and manufacture. The company was organized in separate product groups for marketing and design functions but had a single manufacturing facility that served all the product groups.

The decision to invest in CADCAM was taken at Board level but its implementation was managed and led by the corporate engineering function. The senior engineer concerned was determined to undertake a planned and well managed programme of implementation; he and his team worked very hard to avoid the piecemeal (Model 0) approach described by Blackler and Brown (1986), preferring a task and technology centred approach (Model 1).

Symon and Clegg (1991) studied this implementation over a period of 18 months using a variety of methods and techniques. A soft systems analysis (Checkland, 1981) was used to organize data from a series of interviews and led to a first report. Thereafter, participant observation techniques, a tracer study of the progress of products through design and manufacture, further interviews, and a questionnaire survey were used in preparation of a second report to the company.

The conclusion of this study was that the implementation was unduly technology-led, and that the company was utilizing an inappropriate ‘root definition’, namely that it was

'replacing an existing (largely) manual system for design and manufacture with a CADCAM system; this represents a change in medium and this process is 'owned', led and managed by corporate engineering, with help from management services and manufacturing. The emphasis is heavily on technical issues and problems, and the view is that the organizational issues can be addressed later. This process is being undertaken with the minimum of scarce resources' (Symon and Clegg, 1991, p. 280).

## An alternative implementation strategy was proposed;

'The key task is to design an organizational system integrating design and manufacturing, assisted by CADCAM. This process is 'owned' and managed by both design and manufacturing with help from management services, and with a strong emphasis on end-user participation. The objective is to resolve jointly the technical, strategic and organizational issues. The process should be undertaken cost effectively using appropriate resources' (Symon and Clegg, p. 280).

The first set of recommendations to the company was based on this revised approach and included: the commitment of more resources to organizational issues; planning of the opportunities for organizational change; more participation by users (especially in manufacturing); more education of the potential of CADCAM; greater infrastructural support for users; and a focus on evaluation. Some specific alternatives were also identified for ways in which the work could be reorganized to promote greater integration between design and manufacturing.

Some of these issues were addressed by the company (most notably the initiation of a CADCAM user group)

but no-one addressed the strategic issues, especially concerning organization structures and organizational change. The new technology is operational and working efficiently in a local sense, but it is not meeting its original objectives. Symon and Clegg's (1991) interpretation is that the technological focus and the attendant failure to consider the strategic organizational issues arose from four factors: the highly differentiated and political environment; scarce organizational resources; an action-oriented managerial style; and the complexities and uncertainties inherent in the problem.

## Case study 2: implementation of Information Engineering

This study was concerned with the implementation of IE in a large service organization in the UK. IE is a software development methodology which encompasses all the major stages in the traditional software lifecycle (planning, analysis, design, implementation, maintenance, etc). It differs from most other methodologies of this kind in that most of the work involved in its use is carried out using on-line CASE (Computer Aided Software Engineering) tools.

IE is being implemented in the IT development department of a major UK corporation. The department is responsible for developing new IT systems for internal use. All their projects involve many individuals with a diversity of skills and expertise (e.g. programmers, system analysts, testers and project managers), and take between a few months and several years to develop.

Amongst the many advantages over other existing methodologies that are claimed by supporters of IE are: an increase in the productivity of project personnel; increased communication between system developers and end-users; and improved quality in terms of the efficiency and usability of final systems. Interviews carried out with users of IE, as part of an ongoing longitudinal study of the software development process, revealed a number of problematic issues relating to the implementation and continued use of IE.

First, many developers felt that the implementation of IE had been planned inadequately. Rather than being piloted on small scale projects with associated lower risks, IE had been introduced on projects where training in the use of the methodology and its associated CASE tools had not been provided adequately. Effective use of IE requires a thorough knowledge of the 'philosophy' of systems development that it champions, as well as the specifics of its use. Both of these aspects of IE require a lot of time and effort, neither of which are available to developers within the often hectic, and frequently chaotic, environment of the day-to-day workings of projects. In addition, problems associated with the use of CASE tools were not given enough attention by those responsible for the implementation. CASE users generally felt a lack of 'ownership' of the tools, a sentiment which was reiterated by many others when asked about their attitude towards IE in general.

Second, IE is a methodology designed to be used from 'cradle to grave' and not on projects where many of the stages in the software lifecycle had been completed already. This meant that attempts to use IE were hampered by problems relating to incompatibility with past methodologies which had been used to plan and design systems.

Third, and perhaps most importantly, many developers felt that an important aspect of the implementation of IE which had not been given enough thought was the way in which it brought about changes to the roles and working practices of system developers. Many developers felt that the roles of analyst and programmer, for example, were being changed as a result of the introduction of IE. The boundaries between analysis and programming are by no means clear cut or distinct, in practice individuals often may alternate between the two stages, making decisions with regard to coding whilst carrying out analysis work and vice versa. This ‘cross referencing’ strategy is well documented in the literature on psychological studies of programming (e.g. Pennington, 1987; Guindon, 1990). Where analysis and programming are carried out by different individuals communication patterns between project members usually guarantee that a record of decisions made are carried over across the stages. The implementation of IE had led to greater separation and specialism at the levels of analysis and programming, as a result of this change the informal communication patterns and working practices of individuals had been disturbed or become uncoupled. Very often the rationale behind changes made at one level were lost resulting in the needless repetition of this and related work. A direct consequence of these changes being that developers felt that IE had led to confusion with regard to the handover of tasks in these stages as well as how they are carried out. Compounding this problem is the fact that the organization as a whole (i.e. the IT development department) has a different set of procedures, or rubrics, for carrying out analysis and design. Not surprisingly an individual developer feels at a loss when trying to reconcile these three views (existing individual working practices, the methods encouraged by IE and the procedures set up by the organization). Overall, little thought had been given to the potential implications of how work would be reorganized and restructured as a result of introducing IE. This is directly equivalent to the lack of strategic planning evident in the CADCAM case above.

A further interesting irony surrounds this implementation. The IT department when developing new systems for use elsewhere in the company, has well established procedures for analysing user requirements, and for involving users during development (e.g. by appointing a user project manager). But this same logic was not applied to them as users of new technology. Rather than treating developers as direct users of IE and strategically planning for the organizational changes it was likely to bring about within projects and the larger organization, more time and effort was spent on implementing 'state of the art' technology (including IE CASE tools).

We should point out that these two companies are well-established, successful and profitable; they are among the leading companies in their fields. Both had embarked upon major investments in new technology, and both had worked hard to avoid piecemeal and unplanned change. Yet, both programmes of change were largely technology-led. Why should this be so?

## Wider social context

The sections above paint a rather bleak picture of disappointing systems, of a dominant paradigm for new technologies involving very partial, almost exclusively technical, expertise, and of a set of human and organizational concerns, knowledge and expertise effectively marginalized.

If this characterization of these fields is correct, then why should this be the case? If social science expertise is relevant, available and adds value, how is it that our potential users are not beating a path to our door? If socio-technical thinking is so appropriate, why is it not more thoroughly and comprehensively applied?

In this section (from Clegg 1993), we address these issues, but by reformulating the question. Imagine we have been asked to design a social system that marginalizes these human and organizational concerns. What would that social system look like? What would be the requirements of such a system? In this section we specify such a set of requirements, again in the form of a set of statements. Later we examine the implications of this perspective. To reiterate, the goal here is to delineate a set of system requirements, which if met, would effectively marginalize the human and organizational aspects of IT systems. The requirements of such a social system are of several interrelated kinds, as described below.

## A. End-users

1. Users should be excellent copers, even with poorly designed systems.

2. The costs of poor systems design should be borne by users (rather than by their managers or the systems developers).

3. Users should be encouraged that any adverse experiences they have with a new system, will be accommodated in the next version.

4. Users should not be empowered to change practice, nor to 'own' the new systems. Users generally should not be organized as a legitimate 'constituency' (in the way that suppliers are).

5. Few resources should be deployed in bringing together users and developers.

6. Users should be perceived as sources of error and unpredictability that ideally should be designed out (particularly in advanced systems). System failures (especially 'headline' accidents and disasters) should be characterized as 'operator error'.

7. Users should be seen as problems and constraints. They should be seen as somewhat irrational and recalcitrant actors resistant to progress and change.

## B. Suppliers, ‘experts’ and the development process

1. The supply side of the IT industry should be new product and new functionality led, rather than performance and end-user driven.

2. There should be a lot of marketing 'hype' especially at the forefront of the new technologies (e.g. in the field of expert systems).

3. Engineers and new technologists should be allowed/encouraged to appropriate the new technologies.

4. Technological innovation and invention should be highly valued.

5. The major systems analysis and design methods should focus on technical concerns, to the relative (and sometimes absolute) exclusion of psychological and organizational factors.

6. The systems analysis and design methods should focus on making the processes of systems development more effective rather than on making the end systems (i.e. the outcomes) more effective.

7. The criteria for good system design should be technically defined.

8. The development process should involve multiple conflicting goals, within which psychological and organizational objectives should be subservient.

9. The development process should be characterized by its emergent properties, by the involvement of different people over extended periods of time, and by its lack of integration.

10. People involved in these ‘messy’ and differentiated processes should already feel fully extended.

11. Developers of new systems should get no rewards, recognition or support for considering the psychological and organizational aspects of new systems.

## C. Management and organizations

1. Most senior managers should not understand IT nor what it can do for their business.

2. Senior managers, along with accountants in particular, should exert pressure to achieve rapid payback on investments. This encourages the view that new technologies should provide an opportunity to reduce both staffing levels and skill levels.

3. Rigorous evaluation of the actual impact of new investments against their projected performance and objectives should not be encouraged.

4. New technology investments should be under-resourced in terms of time, money, expertise and management commitment.

5. The organizational contexts within which system development takes place should be differentiated and political.

6. Decision-makers in organizations should be constrained ‘satisficers’, struggling to make sense of uncertain and ambiguous information and events.

## D. Research and development

1. Research and its subsequent dissemination should be carried out within traditional academic disciplinary boundaries.

2. More specifically, the psychological and organizational professions should be small, specialized, fragmented, and lack resources and political clout.

3. The psychological and organizational aspects of new technologies should be under-represented during major public research and development programmes.

4. Human and organizational specialists, when they are involved, should be asked to evaluate existing technologies. When they are involved in new system development, this should be relatively late in the design process.

5. Most research and development effort (in this area) should be expended on ‘obvious’ characteristics such as system usability, rather than on more ‘influential’ aspects such as building in organizational requirements, or designing the roles of humans in complex systems.

6. There should be an absence of clear, communicable and enforceable psychological and organizational standards, applicable to the development, implementation, use and evaluation of new technologies.

## E. Education and understanding

1. There should be a strong historical, educational division between ‘the sciences’ and ‘the arts’.

2. More particularly, disciplinary boundaries (e.g. between engineers, line managers, personnel managers, and cognitive and social scientists) should be long-lasting, and relatively impermeable.

3. There should be little cross-disciplinary education and training.

4. The different interest groups should have different worldviews, values, languages, assumptions, expertise and objectives.

5. Education and training at the level of the firm should be seen as costs to be minimized.

6. There should be a lack of knowledge and understanding about the links between technological and social issues, and their joint impact on performance and effectiveness.

7. We should continue to educate and train many more experts in technology than in the psychological and organizational areas.

Several general points should be made here. First there is no claim for originality in this list; most if not all of these factors have been discussed at various conferences and meetings over the last few years. Second, there is no special claim for this particular listing and its organization under the five headings above. Others would doubtless delineate and organize their perceived requirements in other ways. But, the essential argument remains the same. We are arguing that it is possible to locate the reasons for the marginalization of human and organizational issues in a wider set of interacting social systems, and we have tried to identify some of the key social factors above.

So, how well are these requirements currently met? Without working through each of them separately, we claim that many of these conditions are already in place within the UK, and perhaps elsewhere. For example, looking at factors associated with end-users (A above), we believe that users are excellent copers, demonstrating high levels of adaptability (almost to a fault). Furthermore they bear the psychological and operational costs of poorly designed systems, without being powerful enough to initiate or manage changes. Users are not organized or perceived as a powerful interest group, and do not usually have influence over system design. Few resources are invested in bringing together developers and users. So far as the engineering community is concerned, users are often perceived as sources of error that should be designed out, and failures are often characterized as operator 'error'. Users are often seen as a problem and as resistant to change. Similarly we believe that many of the requirements in the other categories are already met.

Such a picture is again rather depressing. If it is true that these conditions are already in place, these constitute a set of social systems that work synergistically and effectively to marginalize the human and organizational aspects of IT. We are not claiming that these processes are deliberately designed as such, nor wilfully aimed at marginalizing our professional concerns. But nevertheless they happen. The logical point that follows is that ameliorative action is hard to plan and undertake. Thus, getting human and organizational issues on the agenda is not solely a matter of involving users in system design, nor of developing more human-centred development methods and tools, nor of funding more inter-disciplinary research, highly desirable though these things are in themselves.

Given the above then, what are the possible lessons for the emerging field of CSCW?

## Possible lessons for CSCW

The core proposition thus far is that new information technologies are often developed and implemented without due regard to their human and organizational aspects, i.e. that these processes are technologically dominated. Furthermore, the reasons for this are embedded in an interacting set of wider social systems. Such a complex, social explanation would help explain why change is so difficult in this area.

If this characterization and the social account of it, is correct, then one could reasonably predict that any other forms of new technology being developed within the same social milieu, would be subject to equivalent social forces, and thereby liable to the same processes and problems. Thus one emerging prediction is that the domain of CSCW would also be technically dominated and pay too little attention to the human and organizational aspects of its development and use. If this were not true then one would either have to argue that the underlying social analysis is misguided, or perhaps outdated, or alternatively that there is something quite different about this new field of enquiry. This last point has particular force. For example, one might argue that the community of researchers and developers who initiated work into CSCW recognized earlier techno-centrism and were keen to avoid it; indeed this perspective provided one of the key motivations for developing work in this area (McCarthy, 1994).

The first question then, is whether or not this field demonstrates any evidence of meeting the ideal types delineated above. Is it technology-led? Whilst we do not feel well placed to offer a definitive answer, we do wish to raise the question and to point out some issues that give us cause for concern.

First, there is some evidence of an emphasis on marketing and on new products; stress is placed on new functionality and the new clever things we can now do. Functionality is exciting and a property of the technology, not a matter of the tasks that people undertake in their work. The supply community (including academics) appears the dominant stakeholder group. In the UK, the current Department of Trade and Industry programme and its pronouncements are reminiscent of the earlier cries that manufacturers should ‘automate or liquidate’, ‘automate or bust’. The fashion that was flexible manufacturing systems is now CSCW.

Second, one could argue that many of the new developments in this field are very partial in their emphasis and reflect the same mindset as earlier generations of technical research and development, especially within the academic community. In the same way that the academic or student working at the word processor became the 'white rat of HCI' (Human Computer Interaction), so academics and researchers in research and development laboratories help define an emerging field by what they choose to study. The paradigmatic cases for CSCW become shared writing and document editing, and technical support for people in meetings. Both, of course, are legitimate areas of work in their own right, but we would argue they are very partial as templates for an emerging area of work. Why choose these? Whatever happened to manufacturing industry?

Third, and closely connected to the above, the conceptual schemas in use within CSCW also reflect the mindset. For example, the most common schema delineating different forms of collaborative/cooperative work relies on two dimensions of time and space (which can either be same or different). This is summarized in Figure 3.

Again this choice reflects an academic bias, a lack of attention to organizational reality, where the division of labour is almost always organized both vertically (between super- and subordinate) and laterally (between different specialisms or roles). Using a manufacturing example, a CADCAM system enables collaboration between people at different levels (e.g. senior designers and designers) and in different roles (e.g. stress engineers, designers, methods engineers, machine operators, etc.). Whilst these ‘collaborations’ are distributed in time and place, they are just as crucially distributed by level, role and expertise. Yet, these issues are under-represented in the current debates about CSCW. The dominant conceptual schema needs changing.

Fourth, when considerations of work organization are explicitly taken into account, they are frequently based upon intuition rather than careful study (Grudin, 1989, 1990). For example, Bannon and Schmidt (1989) describe an early CSCW project management system which attempts to formalize the procedures which people use to guide their work. The system implicitly assumes a 'rational actor' model of work behaviour, one which conflicts with well established theories of decision making and organizational behaviour in general (e.g. March, 1991). Accounts based upon intuition also consistently underplay the role of informal networks of communication and their importance in establishing and maintaining coordination in work settings. (See Heath and Luff, 1991, for a good example of the role of a seemingly irrelevant activity, 'overhearing', in the work of London underground controllers).

Fifth, the problem of ‘user involvement’ is framed in terms of how to get users to contribute to design and development decisions. The ‘problem’ is not phrased in terms of the difficulties of getting experts in CSCW to help users undertake their work activities more effectively. Key choices about where to invest development effort appear technology-led, rather than work activity led. Psychologically, ownership appears to be held by suppliers. New developments appear to be supply-pushed into the market, rather than demand-pulled.

Sixth, the concepts and ideas imported from earlier work on HCI also reflect an inappropriate mindset. Grudin (1990) for example, points out that attitudes and approaches to the design of single user systems have been transported wholesale into CSCW; these often concentrate more upon individual differences rather than concerning themselves with understanding how groups and organizations function and evolve:

'The term computer supported cooperative work, adopted by a number of researchers and developers, explicitly identifies 'work' as the central concern. Even with that emphasis, many members manifest a technology-driven focus' (Grudin, 1990, p. 179).

The term ‘user’ illustrates the point. To call someone a ‘novice’ or ‘naïve’ user ignores the fact that they may be experts at their jobs. Indeed typically the term ‘user’ assumes that everything is in reference to a computer (Grudin, 1993). Such an attitude diverts attention away from the work environment of computer users and from issues relating to its organization and structure, and places more emphasis upon aspects of the technology being used.

Other HCI-derived terms such as 'interface' are also problematic within the context of CSCW. Bowers and Rodden (1993) for example, describe a case study of the implementation of a CSCW network where at least three different senses of the word reflect aspects of the working practices. What they term the 'personal–public' interface, involved demonstrating the consequences of individual actions upon other network users; the 'departmental' interface involved the collective actions of members of the department upon the wider corporate network; finally, the 'organizational' interface represented the set of ground rules which were set in place by the organization, rules which often caused problems of interference with the other levels, or 'interfaces', in the network. This issue is not one of mere semantics, but one of recognizing the diversity and heterogeneity of computer usage which is manifest within organizational settings, usage which can only be appreciated when the full complexity of the problem is recognized:

'Computer systems within work systems are complex artefacts existing within a social setting. Understanding and discovering the different relationships between users, the computer systems and the actual work will be of fundamental importance in developing systems to support this work' (Bowers and Rodden, 1993, p. 261).

If we examine existing programmes of work, there is some evidence in support of the argument that CSCW remains predominantly technology-led in outlook. (See Greif, 1988, for an overview; Grudin, 1989, also reviews a collection of research papers). Nor is this view restricted to critics outside the field. Amongst CSCW practitioners themselves there is a recognition that past and, to a lesser but still significant extent, present day systems reflect a general ‘technology-led’ approach towards the design of CSCW.

On a more positive note however, it is apparent that a number of authors are aware of the problems and lessons from the past, and of the potential contribution of socio-technical theory and wider organizational research. For example, Bannon and Schmidt (1991) propose a wish-list of issues relating to the interactions with and between CSCW systems. Their suggestions include a closer study of the forms of interaction in the labour process itself, as influenced by the resources available, their organizational setting and the different forms of social control in the workplace. In a later paper, Schmidt and Bannon (1992) argue that CSCW practitioners need to gain a better understanding of the tasks which individuals perform, as well as how these tasks are mediated by computer support. Similarly, Olson et al. (1993) argue that the allocation of tasks between individuals, groups and artefacts needs more careful study than it has been given in the past.

Indeed more and more authors stress the need for systems to address organizational as well as technological aspects. This can be seen partly as a response to the problems which the field faced in building early systems which ignored these issues and hence proved problematic in many cases and unusable in others. On the other hand, the design of electronic mail systems is widely seen as an example of a CSCW success story. Grudin (1990) for example, argues that many existing e-mail systems work precisely because they map onto issues of who does the work and who benefits from it, as well as being compatible with existing work practices (but see also Mueller, 1989). Grudin's sentiments echo many of the suggestions we have to make:

'The reception of technology in an organization is determined by the distinctions found in the workplace; to understand this, we must free ourselves from a bias to focus on the distinctions designed into the technology.' (Grudin, 1990, p. 180).

As in most disciplines, it is likely that the gap between theory and practice is large within CSCW; current indications are that this gap is narrowing. We should however, bear in mind the lessons we have learnt from other areas. The title of a recent paper by Norman (1991) would seem to be a good starting point: 'Collaborative computing: collaboration first, computing second'.

We now turn to consideration of potential learnings for the CSCW community. We organize our ideas and suggestions in two sections; those of general applicability to IT including but not limited to CSCW; and those of specific concern to the CSCW domain.

## General suggestions

This section of the paper is concerned with making suggestions that apply to the field of IT generally and also to CSCW. The aim here is to outline a set of specific proposals that address the processes of marginalization described earlier. As such the proposals are pitched at different levels of application and are concerned with different timescales; individually they are seen as necessary but not sufficient to engender the essential changes in the wider domain of IT development and use. They are also intended to be feasible and realistic action levers, though clearly they are not without their difficulties.

1. Users should be the owners of technical changes, and should be made project managers for any technical development or implementation.

This completely recasts the existing ‘problem’ of user participation – the new problem becomes that of getting experts in IT to support users in getting their work done better. Users become more powerful as a constituency, and become ‘owners’ of the work system. The technology is cast in the role of helpful tool.

We do recognize that this is a potential problem for the technical specialists who may feel downgraded. In our experience this is analogous to the implementation of innovations like autonomous work groups and cell manufacturing, where the operators within a work system take control of some issues away from specialist ‘indirect’ functions.

2. We should develop national and international quality standards in each of the separate subject areas embraced by the human and organizational aspects of IT. In the UK these standards should be incorporated within the British Quality Standard BS5750 (see ACOST, 1993). These standards should cover the following areas: ergonomic aspects of the new system; the quality of the human-computer interface and their interaction; the allocation of tasks between humans and computers; the quality of the users' jobs; the ways in which the work is organized and structured; the ways in which change is managed.

3. We should develop and apply methods and tools that help project managers, users and developers incorporate human and organizational issues during system development, implementation and use. These should cover the full range of human and organizational aspects as listed above. These techniques should be generic and simple. Ideally they should be usable alongside existing, more technically-oriented methods. Some of these tools should be aimed at undertaking business process engineering (not re-engineering) in parallel with the development of the new technology.

4. All new technology investments and implementations should be evaluated rigorously, both against their original objectives and against the human and organizational standards described above. Evaluation practices, methods and techniques should be developed and tested; these should also have standards, and fall within the compass of the British Quality Standard BS5750. The explicit goal here is make the scale of the problem visible, thereby creating energy for change.

5. Any new investment in IT should involve the consideration, specification and evaluation of sets of human and organizational goals, choices and outcomes (as in 2, 3 and 4 above). The mindset required is to see work organization as the critical design issue. The technology and its functionality follow from, and support, the design of work.

6. Multi-disciplinary projects should be funded to undertake research and development into the five issues specified above. These projects should involve collaborations between leading edge companies (users and suppliers), academic departments and consultants. We believe consultants are a critical force in this area for supply pushing new knowledge and good practice out into the user community. In the UK, the DTI and the research councils have a critical role to support this work.

7. Training should be provided for user organizations, in each of the first five areas specified above. The goals are to make users more knowledgeable and demanding, to raise their expectations and to provide the means whereby human and organizational knowledge and expertise is demand-pulled into the market, rather than supply-pushed.

8. With a longer term pay-off, we should institute a range of joint technological and social science courses, e.g. at undergraduate, postgraduate and post-experience levels. This is especially relevant given the range of skills which are currently needed to design, implement, manage, use and evaluate IT systems. These interdisciplinary skills are currently in short supply and are increasingly in demand (Olson et al., 1993).

9. The human and organizational disciplines themselves are too differentiated and fragmented. More research and development is needed to develop more integrative approaches, e.g. linking organizational, psychological and ergonomic perspectives.

10. All the above suggestions require an investment of resources. The benefit or added value arises from more effective organizations, and from more appropriate and effective methods of working (including technologies).

## Specific suggestions

In this section we are concerned with specific substantive suggestions for research and development in the domain of CSCW.

1. We propose that research and development into CSCW incorporates a much broader range of work situations and technologies. Studies of a wider range of collaborative work organizations are required, including, for example, manufacturing settings. From a technical perspective, manufacturing technologies (such as CADCAM) and development methods (such as IE) should fall within the compass of CSCW (see also McCarthy, 1994).

2. We suggest that research and development in this domain is more organization-led and end-user focused. Thus the emphasis should be on redesigning work organizations and working methods, and on addressing particular problems faced by, and opportunities identified by people working in organizations. In this perspective the technologies are tools supporting individual and organizational needs. A minimum requirement is for a joint socio-technical approach in this area. A more radical case can also be made for an organization-led approach, in which technology design follows organization design.

3. Certain new forms of work organization and working practices could form the focus for a wide new agenda for CSCW research and development. In the manufacturing sector, just-in-time inventory control, lean manufacturing and cellular manufacturing are topical. In the health and education sectors, there are substantial and far-reaching changes to financial and managerial arrangements. In the field of IT itself there is an interest in finding new ways of developing systems, involving new methods and approaches. Across all sectors there is a concern for total quality management. All of these involve changes in working structures, systems and practices. Many of these innovations necessarily incorporate new forms of collaborative working, and new opportunities for computer support.

4. Given a focus on collaborative working environments, we need to engage in prospective design work incorporating development, implementation, use and evaluation phases, all of which should embrace the full range of human, organizational and technical concerns. Methods and tools, as specified in the previous section, are needed to support these prospective design and evaluation endeavours.

5. Substantively, we propose that there is a need to bring together organizational and cognitive approaches to the study of such complex working systems. We suggest a focus on research and development into what we term 'the division of cognitive labour' (Clegg, 1994; Waterson et al., 1994). Our approach is to try to understand the ways in which, and reasons why, particular cognitive activities are distributed within and between people and their artefacts. This includes an analysis of cognitive activity within the individual (e.g. using the notion of different levels of regulation – see Rasmussen, 1987), between people acting in different roles, both laterally and hierarchically (as in the field of distributed cognition – see Hutchins, 1990), and between people and the artefacts they use (as in the field of HCI). This type of analysis holds promise for improving our understanding of how such complex systems work, for highlighting any mismatches and breakdowns which may occur, for identifying where computer support may be required, and for predicting and evaluating the impact of any changes in this cognitive system (see also Bowers and Rodden, 1993).

## Conclusions

We believe that the field of CSCW should learn from previous endeavours, as well as establish its own unique characteristics and approaches. In spite of the early determination on the part of the CSCW community to provide a different approach, to react against the individualistic and technically dominated approaches in other domains of IT, this has only been partly successful.

The field of CSCW is open to criticism of technocentrism. We are advocating: a focal interest in the issues of work organization and work design, in which technologies are supporting tools; a greater user orientation; a greater focus on human and organizational issues, methods and tools; the use of standards as action levers for change; an investment in training and education; and a greater investment in genuinely socio-technical, and possibly even organization-led approaches.
