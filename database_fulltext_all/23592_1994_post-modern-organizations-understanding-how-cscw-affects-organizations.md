# Post(-)modern organizations: understanding how CSCW affects organizations

FRANK BLACKLER

The Management School, Lancaster University, Lancaster LA1 4YX

The paper reviews the ways organizations are thought to be changing as a result of Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW). While claims which exaggerate the impact of technological changes should not be taken seriously, within the context of current developments in world capitalism CSCW assumes particular importance raising cultural and organizational problems at least as much as economic and technological ones. The flexibility, variety and disorder associated with ‘post-modern’ organizations (i.e. organizations characteristic of the epoch after modernism) necessitate the adoption of ‘postmodern’ approaches to understanding (i.e. approaches to the theory of knowledge developed in linguistics and philosophy) which emphasise the significance of communication, interpretation, improvisation, negotiation and learning processes. The suggestion is that, for the impact of CSCW to be understood, conventional theories of organization should be replaced by theories of collective activity.

## Introduction

Ever since early formulations of sociotechnical systems theory (Trist and Bamforth, 1951) a central theme in the literature on organizations has been how work systems may be developed in which technological concerns are not the sole or principle driving force. Although in the 1970s considerable advances were recorded in the development of approaches that went some way to achieve this aim, particularly in assembly line and flow-process work (see Alioth et al. 1979), during the 1980s it became clear that the new generation of very flexible information and communication technologies were presenting a novel set of challenges. Attempts were made to modify established sociotechnical approaches but certain features of advanced information systems presented problems for traditional techniques (e.g., the way they support nonlinear, unprogrammed decision sequences, see Pava, 1986). Furthermore it become evident that because of the emphasis they place on abstract reasoning skills, work systems based upon computers disrupt the situated action skills that are integral to individual and organizational competency (Zuboff, 1988). Star's (1992) recent observation captures the current view: because information technologies 'go everywhere' and intimately interlace with social processes, they are exposing aspects of social dynamics which, previously, had either been taken for granted or ignored.

Many of the early contributors to the idea of CSCW technologies (see Greif, 1988) certainly believed that they would lend themselves to the development of convivial computer-mediated systems and subsequently the promise of CSCW systems has generally been presented very positively. But as Star's observation implies and as others have explicitly noted (e.g. Galegher et al., 1990) the task of developing user-centred CSCW systems is at least as problematical as it has been with other information systems: the complexities of group and organizational functioning are both difficult to understand and difficult to accommodate in the design process. Some commentators, e.g. Olson et al. (1992), are especially pessimistic about this, arguing that a 'deep understanding' of the phenomenon of group work has yet to be achieved. Knowledge about groups and organizations is always likely to be partial and incomplete, for the further one moves from traditional human factors' concerns with anthropometric or cognitive considerations towards social and cultural matters the less likely it is that insights can be formulated as easily applicable 'laws' of behaviour. Be that as it may, it is undoubtedly true that the difficult behavioural issues raised by information technologies in general and CSCW systems in particular are precipitating an active search for new approaches to analysis.

This paper contributes to the debate, suggesting that recent developments in social theory and organizational analysis complement the trend in IT research towards detailed micro-sociological studies. It is argued that while overenthusiastic presentations of the significance of CSCW systems should be treated with caution, such systems are likely to assume particular significance within the context of contemporary capitalism. Within what some have called the ‘post-modern’ era (i.e. the era after modernism) networking technologies in general are especially important; they are associated with a globalization of the world’s economy and with new patterns of communication within and between organizations. Yet the effects on organizations of these changes are not easy to understand. In this context the suggestion is that ‘postmodern’ theories, (i.e. approaches inspired by a particular approach within philosophy and the humanities) offer a helpful perspective, questioning as they do conventional assumptions about order and predictability. In the emerging socio-technical-economic situation, traditional approaches to organizations, structures and technologies will need to be replaced by theories of activities, dialogue and learning.

## The technological and economic context of CSCW

CSCW products have been defined by Wilson (1991) as including message systems, computer conference systems, procedure processing systems, calendar systems, shared filing systems, co-authoring systems, screen sharing systems, group decision support systems, and advanced meeting and team development tools (involving multimedia systems with voice, video, databases, electronic mail, videoconferencing, group interfaces, etc.). The aims of CSCW, he suggests, are to provide integrated support for face-to-face groups and for groups engaging in activities undertaken at different times and in different places.

Wilson's presentation concludes by reproducing speculations about a ‘collaboratory’, a futuristic mode of organizing scientific work: it is predicted that ‘information communication and processing will allow geographically dispersed communities of researchers to interact, share ideas, data and instruments with much the same ease as those who are colocated. Freed from the constraints of distance, opportunity and choice will determine the composition, size and duration of a research team’ (Lederberg and Uncapher, 1989 quoted in Wilson, 1991, p. 94). Such a style of presentation (which indicates scant regard for the differences between immediate, face-to-face interactions and computer-mediated exchanges) is typical of overviews of CSCW. Such systems are presented as opening an exciting range of opportunities both for organizations on the one hand and for systems’ developers on the other. Even within the academic CSCW literature its key terms such as ‘cooperative work’ (Greif, 1988) and ‘intellectual teamwork’ (Galegher et al., 1990) seem as much calculated to persuade as they are to inform, but within the popular computer literature the rhetoric is quite undisguised. Thus the introduction to a special edition on CSCW in Communications of the ACM in December 1991 claims: ‘collaboration is a sign of maturation … computing is coming of age … the potential for supporting collaboration is limitless … there is excitement and opportunity in seeing what is coming: there is risk in ignoring it’. ‘Teamwork tools lead the way to creative collaboration’ (Corporate Computing, August 1992), ‘groupware will change the way an office communicates' (Digital Review, June 1992) are other typical storylines.

Strong rhetoric, which often hints that the future will be determined by new technological developments, has of course been a consistent feature of the computing literature. Kling has commented on such issues, both in the general context of the whole industry (Kling and Iacono, 1988) and specifically with respect to CSCW (Kling 1991). While there are very good reasons for believing that in the emerging context of contemporary capitalism CSCW technologies will prove to be a highly significant development, it is as well to be alert to the up-tempo image that has developed around them. As McCarthy (1994) suggests in this edition at present there is very little hard evidence about how effective CSCW technologies are likely to be; nor is it certain which people will find it helpful to use them, which organizations will be transformed by CSCW technologies and which will not, and how quickly take-up of the technologies will prove to be. Writing in 1988 I offered a summary of the lessons that had emerged over the previous decade concerning the impact of information technologies generally on work organizations (Blackler, 1988). If the same things are to happen with CSCW technologies as had happened then, the technologies will be adopted at a significantly slower rate than was first anticipated, many of the systems will prove disappointing, choices about their design and introduction will not be as full informed or as fully rational as many commentators imagine, and rather than being applied in an egalitarian fashion there will be a tendency for CSCW technologies to be applied in ways that support the interests of existing dominant elites.

However, such points should not be understood as suggesting that the advent of CSCW technologies will be insignificant. Off-putting though the rhetoric associated with them can sometimes be, when viewed within the context of networking technologies more generally, CSCW systems do seem likely to play an increasingly significant role within contemporary economies.

A range of sociologists and economists are maintaining that developments in contemporary capitalism are under way which are every bit as significant as the move from pre-industrial to industrial society. The emerging social forms have variously been described as 'disorganized capitalism', 'post-industrialism', 'post-Fordism', or 'post-modern' society, i.e. societies in the temporal period after industrialism, Fordism or 'modernism'. As this variety of terms suggests, a range of different interpretations have been offered of contemporary developments, but common themes within them are not difficult to identify. Just as capitalism has, in the past, passed through various stages, the suggestion is that presently it is moving into a further, distinctive phase. Developing their thesis of disorganized capitalism and post-modernism for example, Lash and Urry (1987)

describe the early stages of liberal capitalism (which was based on large empires, small pockets of industry and word-of-mouth communications) with capitalism built on colonial empires (characterized by the development of regional economies and a dependency on the printed word) these compare to contemporary global capitalism, characterized by an international division of labour, a significant role for service industries, cultural fragmentation, a dependency on electronic information and by the relatively free movement of capital across national boundaries. Piore and Sable (1984) similarly point to the disintegration of the conditions necessary for a mass-market production system, as domestic markets in the developed world have been penetrated by externally-produced products, and as the advantages of large dedicated production systems have been undermined. An epoch of order, control and progress is being replaced, such writers suggest, by one of disorder, flexibility and variety.

Others have concentrated on the role the new information technologies are playing in this process. The political economist Castells (1989) offers an overview of the complex interactions between political and economic changes and the impacts of the new information technologies. Post-Keynsian policies of fiscal austerity, a retreat from governmental policies of redistributing wealth, a reduction in public spending and the weakening of trade unions have combined, he suggests, with transformations in the money markets and with the development of flexible methods of production and service provision. Castells refers to this situation as ‘the informational mode of development’, maintaining that the new information and communication technologies have become an essential to the success of contemporary capitalism as labour productivity or energy usage were in previous times.

Scenarios such as this have strongly influenced both the academic and the popular management literatures in recent years. Clegg's (1990) theoretical analysis contrasted the emerging stereotype of the innovative, flexible, multiskilled and niche-marketed organization, with the standard image of bureaucracies. Badaracco's (1991) study of General Motors and IBM explores the significance of the new (global) alliances being pursued by large companies. Nor have theorists interested in CSCW systems failed to recognise the significance of this situation. Malone and Rochart (1991) anticipated the widespread adoption of networking technologies, predicting the emergence of what they call 'coordination-intensive' organizations in the global economy. Reviewing the American experience they suggested that the new communication technologies are supporting new practices and changing corporate structures and management styles; thus, as the cost of information technologies has reduced, matrix structures have become increasingly costeffective within organizations. Between organizations, they are reducing transaction costs and facilitating new forms of contractual relations and alliances.

Not all writers have greeted these developments with enthusiasm. Castells (1989) highlights the social problems that are being created as organizations' traditional dependency on geographical locations is replaced by a dependency only on electronic information flows. Work no longer needs to be produced 'in-house' but may be contracted to outsiders who may be working on the other side of the planet. Reich (1991) has articulated similar concerns, focusing on the social problems associated with the international division of labour within the global economy. But, in general, both management theorists and IT commentators have taken an optimistic position. Thus Kanter (1989) presents a buoyant analysis of the challenges that emerge as the boundaries between one firm and another become blurred by the new interactions and alliances, and Sproull and Kiesler (1991) urge managers to seize the opportunities that they believe electronic networks present to transform work practices. The promise is one or organizations characterized by vigorous internal and external networks, collaborative work relations and significantly reduced hierarchical structures of control. Within, such a scenario CSCW technologies evidently assume considerable importance.

## The intellectual context for understanding CSCW

Just as the new information and communication technologies presented difficulties for established approaches to work design theory, so the advent of communication-intensive organizations presents problems also for organizational theory. The idea that organizations should adopt forms that are suitable for the particular environments in which they find themselves is commonplace within the management literature so the notion that, within the globalizing economy, novel configurations are emerging is not in itself especially surprising. The problem is, however, to develop a vocabulary that will capture the distinctive characteristics of the new organizational forms associated with networking technologies. What, in other words, is an organization which is independent of geographical location, is devoid of conventional heirarchy, and has no obvious boundaries between itself and other organizations?

Developments as unusual as these demand shifts in approaches to analysing groups and organizations; familiar concepts such as structure, planning and control sit uneasily alongside an emphasis on flexibility, variety and disorder. As already noted, a number of commentators are maintaining that epoch-making developments are currently occurring within contemporary capitalism that will lead to sharp distinctions between the past and the future; in a similar manner others are suggesting that new approaches in philosophy and the social sciences are precipitating an intellectual ferment every bit as significant as the time of change depicted in Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose when the culture of one epoch was in flames and nobody could foresee the consequences. Postmodern philosophy is an approach to the theory of knowledge developed within the humanities, linguistics and social theory which self-consciously departs from modernist assumptions (i.e. assumptions prevailing within the period of world history marked by industrialism, bureaucratization, rationalism, individualism and secularism) and proposes new approaches to the nature of truth, language and objectivity.

Postmodernism is not a single creed, indeed there are marked differences between the views of key thinkers associated with them. (Amongst key writers associated with postmodernism are Derrida (1973), who deconstructed latent meanings within texts; Foucault (1979), who emphasized the close relations between knowledge and power and explored how knowledge makes existence thinkable, visible and governable; Lyotard (1984), who articulated his aversion to 'grand theory'; and Jameson (1991), who related cultural fragmentation to economic change and consumer power.) Moreover, the ideas associated with postmodernism are often controversial and have provoked some strong critical reactions (see, e.g., Callinicos, 1989; Hassard and Parker, 1993). Yet despite the way some writers have scrambled to jump on the postmodern bandwagon and others have hastily dismissed the whole project, postmodernist notions have precipitated major debates in many areas of intellectual endeavour.

Within the IT community the relevance of postmodernism was first discussed by Bjorn-Anderson (1988) who examined its significance for approaches to technology evaluation. Here I concentrate on the relevance for IT of postmodernist ideas for organizational analysis. Cooper and Burrell (1988) opened the debate on the significance postmodernism epistemology for organization studies. Gergen's (1992) account extended the approach. Gergen reviews organizational theory as a cultural activity by analysing differences between pre-modernist (romantic), modernist and postmodernist approaches. While romantic perspectives, he suggests, assume that what is significant about individuals is located in their 'deep interiors' (such as the powerful but hidden forces postulated in psychoanalytic theories or theories of psychological needs), modernism was marked by a faith in the importance of reason and observation, championed a search for essentials and progress and emphasized the primacy of logic and planning. The image of organizations as machines epitomizes the modernist orientation, as does Taylor's 'scientific' management, rational economic theory and expectancy theories of motivation and leadership. On the other hand, as Gergen points out, postmodernism questions the feasibility of such approaches, by exploring the implications of the recognition that language does not passively reflect a (knowable) world, rather it represents what is a highly complex situation. Such insights imply that knowledge is not something that is discovered, it is something that is created. Truth is an interpretation, and central issues of concern include the processes through which core assumptions are presented, justified and reified. Within postmodernist approaches, language emerges as the archetypal communal activity, enabling the transmission, negotiation and development of interpretations of the world and events within it. A questioning of one's own suppositions (self-reflexivity) is encouraged by this style of analysis, as is a tolerance of different points of view (or voices).

Kvale (1992) has summarized the significance of postmodernist philosophy for contemporary life:

'To the existentialists the discovery of a world without meaning was the point of departure; today a loss of unitary meaning is merely accepted; that is just the way the world is. Postmodern man has stopped waiting for Godot. The world is not met with despair; rather it is a living with what is, a making the best of it, a relief from the burden of finding yourself as the goal of life; what remains may be happy nihilism. With the death of the Utopias, the local and personal responsibilities for actions here and now becomes crucial' (p. 38).

Thus, while modernism may be characterized by emphasizing reality, reason and detachment, postmodernism emphasizes representation, culture and self-reflection; while modernists regard theories as truth and pursue a search for fundamentals, postmodernists regard truth as a story and champion the exploration of different perspectives; and while modernists emphasize the analytical power of theories, the development of 'knowledge that' and the feasibility of 'progress', postmodernism emphasizes the immediate, 'knowledge how' and the significance of agency. One consequence of a postmodern outlook is the notion that different interpretations of a complex world may coexist. Although never explicitly presented as a postmodern approach, Morgan's popular overview (1986) of organization theory Images of Organization develops this point of view; managers can learn to 'read' an organization in all its complexity, he suggests, by thinking of organizations not only as machines, but also as organisms, brains, cultures, politics etc. The strength of Morgan's 'metaphor approach' is that it shows that while people generally think of their organizations in rational terms as, if not a perfect machine, then as a fair approximation to one, close examination of any organization at all will offer a different perspective. Once one abandons the assumption that social life is rational and goal-directed, organizations can be understood in terms of the accidents of history, or as the outcome of battles for control, demarcation disputes, technological choices, tinkering and muddling through etc. etc. – that is as essentially arbitrary, 'socially constructed' entities.

To the extent that it debunks naïve assumptions of organizational rationality and order, Morgan's approach can, therefore, be dubbed 'postmodern'; yet it stops short of the action orientation that Kvale's analysis associates with this term. An action orientation adds, indeed, a new dimension to the analysis. In everyday practices when members of organizations engage in their roles, they do not think too much about the rationality of their activity nor its arbitrariness. Instead the behavioural routines in which they engage are experienced as natural and unexceptional. The understandings people have of what they do are, inevitably, largely tacit; the explanations they articulate will oversimplify and distort. But it is through their actions that people discover (and rediscover) the significance and meanings of what they do. Roles are enacted through engagement. Central to this process are the core assumptions participants have of appropriate priorities, and the skills they show in improvising around them.

The action-implications of postmodernist philosophies follow, therefore, from the insight that the roles which people enact are socially constructed and are not predetermined by ‘natural laws’. To the extent that, in this sense, the structures of everyday life are arbitrary there is always the possibility that taken-for-granted routines may be revised and reconstructed. This is not how everyday routines are experienced, of course. Because of their pragmatic effectiveness as well as the institutional and linguistic frameworks within which they are embedded, familiar practices acquire an inevitability, a ‘false necessity’. Postmodernist thought points out that the way things happen to be done now is not the only way they might be done, but that institutions and imaginations become entrenched; pragmatism, in other words, becomes a prison. (A theorist who has developed these points at length is Unger, 1987; I outline the implications of his approach for IT design in Blackler, 1992).

Key implications of postmodernism for organizational theory are summarized in Figure 2. While modernism encourages an image of organizations as machines steered by monologue, managed by forward planning, and staffed by like-minded employees, postmodernism suggests that such an outlook is an illusion. Postmodernism suggests that, contrary to first appearances, organizations are in constant flux, as meanings are negotiated and as participants improvize both with and towards their priorities. Whereas the modernist approach is preoccupied with reason and detachment, postmodernism emphasizes the heterogeneity of social life. New priorities for organization theory thus emerge: modernist preoccupations with integration, socialization and unity of outlook are replaced by an interest in the nature of ambiguity, the possibility of dialogue and the development of collective learning. Social order cannot be assumed; the way in which order is created and maintained and the possibilities for its reformation emerge as fundamental issues.

Postmodernism thus provides a way of thinking about organizations which, as they become independent of geographical location, devoid of conventional heirarchy and with no obvious boundaries between themselves and other organizations, are becoming increasingly difficult to understand. Conventional (i.e. modernist) approaches to organizations as machines, it suggests, must be discarded. The assumption that advanced technologies are neutral tools that may be co-opted in the pursuit of established priorities is part of this imagery, as is the notion that organizations may be described adequately as structures (be these heirarchical bureaucracies, decentralized structures or matrix organizations). Familiar props to our understanding are also removed once assumptions about the centrality of rationality, harmony and planning within an organization theory are dropped. Then the nature of incoherency, inconsistency, paradox and dilemma in working relationships becomes easier to see. The emphasis on direction, control and technique that features within modernist orientations is, within postmodernism, replaced by an interest in the processes of knowing and interpreting and in the management of collective uncertainty.

Of course, modernist ideas remain powerful within management (see, e.g. Staw's 1980 analysis of pressures within organizations for rational explanations, and Argyris' 1990 exploration of rationality as a defense against learning) but many familiar ideas within organization theory which challenge simplistic assumptions about the role of rationality resonate with the postmodern agenda (e.g. Cohen, March and Olsen's 1972 garbage can model of decision making, Daft and Weick's 1984 emphasis on interpretation processes, Meyerson and Martin's 1987 analysis of differing approaches to organizational culture, etc. etc.). Complementary ideas have gained ground in social theory also where, in recent decades, there has been an upsurge of interest in the microprocesses of social life. As Knorr-Cetina and Cicourel (1981) put it, as a result of the contributions of symbolic interactionists, ethnomethodologists and others, there has been a move away from normative theories of social order towards theories of cognitive order.

‘Instead of being seen as a monolithic system which regulates individual action order comes to be seen as an upshot of concrete communicative interaction. In a sense, the problem of social order is redefined by turning the traditional approach to social order on its head. Social order is not that which holds society together by somehow controlling individual wills, but that which comes about in the mundane but relentless transactions of these wills’ (p. 7).

Within the IT and CSCW communities, of course, ethnomethodological studies such as Suchman (1987) and Orr (1990) have been profoundly influential. These studies may, perhaps, best be understood as merely the latest in a long line of studies which, but concentrating on what actually happens in organizations rather than what is supposed to happen, are undermining modernist suppositions. The picture which has emerged from such microsociological orientations is of people enacting reality, skillfully improvising, acting collectively, using knowledge that is both provisional and developing, acquiring it by participating in communities of practitioners and developing it through experience and debate.

Researchers associated with microsociological investigations often demonstrate an unmistakable sense of excitement about the significance of their work (see e.g. Star, 1992) although (in the best postmodern tradition) agreement remains to be reached on how the social processes revealed by their studies may best be understood. Within the sociology of science efforts have been made in recent years to explain the way in which scientific knowledge is constructed. Actor-network theory (e.g. Latour, 1987; Law, 1992) has attracted a lot of interest in this field, its distinctive contribution lies in the way it de-emphasizes conventional distinctions between people and technology, suggesting that texts and material things are integral to an understanding of the social, and vice versa. Within the area of organizational analysis, activity theory achieves something similar. Activity theory had its origins in the Russian psychologist Vygotsky's attempts in the 1920s to understand the relationship between mind and society. Contemporary versions take a number of forms (e.g. Engestrom, 1987, 1990; Brown et al., 1989; Lave and Wenger, 1991) but all versions emphasize the significance of the (shared) 'frames' that participants impose on the situations in which they find themselves, i.e. the preconceptions they bring of the nature of their activities. Engestrom's version is reminiscent of actor-network theory in the way it emphasizes the intimate relationships that exist between these frames and the community's resource system (language, concepts, technologies, explicit and implicit rules, roles and patterns of authority, etc.). He emphasizes also the historical origins of these 'activity systems', the incoherencies that can be identified within and between the elements of an activity system, and the ways in which such incoherences come to be obscured or ignored. (For a review of Engestrom's approach see Blackler, 1993; its relevance to IT has been discussed by Bodker, 1991 and Kuutti, 1991).

Less important than the detail of any particular theory of microsocial events, however, is the general thrust of its insights. Key features of ‘collaborative work’ are the incoherencies, inconsistencies, paradoxes and dilemmas that are integral to work systems and the way the ‘relentless transactions’ between them produces a predictable order. Just as Perrow’s (1984) analysis of complex industrial technologies indicated how accidents are a normal feature of life within them, in exactly the same way a postmodern approach to organizations suggests that everyday activities are constantly being interrupted or breaking down. It is thanks to the determination and skill of participants, in both their actions and their language, that particular ‘scripts’ or ‘frames’ are enacted in (i.e.

imposed on) any situation, and a (seemingly) smooth flow of events is sustained. The suggestion is that social order is a product of people's resilience, creativity and versatility. Postmodern approaches to organization theory suggest that the world is as complex as it looks; coherency is not the key characteristic of social life, on the contrary, incoherency and people's abilities to manage it may be regarded as the raw material of organization.

## Conclusion

## Post-modern organizations, postmodern understandings

This paper has reviewed understandings of how CSCW technologies are likely to affect organizations. It was argued that technologies are not deterministic in their effects on organizations, effective organization is not achieved simply by the introduction of new work systems but by what people choose to do, and find that they can do, with them. In the context of changes in the global (post-modern) economy CSCW technologies do, however, promise to be a significant development, facilitating activities that are distributed across space and time and supporting coordination-intensive organizations. However, the changes they may precipitate are not easy to comprehend or manage: CSCW technologies raise cultural and organizational issues as much as they raise economic and technological ones.

The key organizational issue is: under what circumstances can the (global) interactions that the technologies may support become significant occasions for developing meanings, trust, debate, disagreements and learning? This paper endeavours to point the way forward, suggesting that new (postmodern) approaches to understanding are of value, questioning as they do taken-for-granted assumptions about the stability of social events, and encouraging attention to the way interactions are structured. Organizations per se are not the correct unit of analysis, old ways of understanding collective endeavours need to be questioned and perhaps discarded. What appears to be central are the processes through which people collectively construct their activities. What is uncertain is the way high-technology communication systems will influence such matters. The novelty of the emerging socio-technical-economic situation has revealed how little is known about collaborative processes even within familiar modernist organizations, let alone how they may be affected by our high-technology future.

## Biographical notes

Frank Blackler is Professor of Organizational Behaviour at Lancaster University. Previously he lectured at London and Liverpool Universities. His main interests have been in the nature of organizational change, the impact of information technologies, and the nature of knowledge-intensive organizations.
