# The stages model and the phases of the IS field

ANDREW FRIEDMAN

Department of Economics, University of Bristol, 8 Woodland Road, Bristol BS8 1TN, UK

We argue that the organization's internal time-path of computer usage should be located in a context of changes in the IS field as a whole. A model of the history of information systems management within user organizations is presented here, which specifies a series of phases the IS field has gone through. Each phase is defined by a critical problem or set of constraints. The phases model can be used to predict new pressures on IS executives and new directions for focusing research and education resources as well as supplementing Nolan's stages model of computer usage within organizations.

## Introduction

The stages model of computer usage within organizations has been very influential in the business and academic IS communities since it was developed 20 years ago by Richard Nolan. However most empirical tests have not supported hypotheses based on the model (Lucas and Sutton, 1977; Drury and Bates, 1979; Drury, 1980, 1983; Goldstein and McCririk, 1981; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1982; Benbasat et al., 1984; King and Kraemer, 1984; Munro et al., 1987–88). The time has come to consider alternative models and amendments to the stages model. We argue that the organization's internal time-path of computer usage should be located in a context of changes in computer usage in the IS field as a whole. A model of the history of IS management within user organizations over the past 40 years is presented here, which specifies a series of phases of the IS field. Each phase is defined by a critical problem or set of constraints that was considered to have been the main limitation on further spread of computer-based systems.

## Organization stages - Nolan's model

In early versions of the Nolan model, four stages were derived from his observation that the long-term pattern of IS budgets traces an S-shaped curve (Nolan, 1973; Gibson and Nolan, 1974). Three inflection points marking stage boundaries correspond to likely crises in the organization's experience of computers (Figure 1).

Initially a computer is introduced for cost savings in clerical operations. Growth is slow because of the limited range of applications attempted and because of management uncertainty, even hostility, to the computer as a potential change agent. Success with initial applications in Stage 1 leads to strong pressure for expansion. Pressure comes mainly from the supply side as systems analysts become over-optimistic about what can be achieved. Tentative growth of Stage 1 gives way to an explosion of contagious unplanned expansion in Stage 2. A loose working environment develops and costs overrun. Basic maintenance and database building are neglected in favour of more glamorous and technically sophisticated new applications. Eventually the time of reckoning comes. Top managers become aware of runaway IS budgets, and elaborate quality control procedures for all aspects of systems development and operations work are imposed. These measures create a climate where many IS specialists either leave or reduce their effort. Thus too much control results in failure to realize the potential of the computer resource. The maturity stage represents the settling of the computer function into a well-planned and effective resource. Middle managers within the IS function have been trained and habituated to formal control procedures.

Nolan's stages model is not purely descriptive. It is based on a dynamic process embracing all of the stages and dictating their order of appearance. It is management's short-term reactions to a large scale and poorly understood technical event, the introduction of the computer, which drives organizations through the stages. Management of the IS function can be compared with a pendulum, reacting in a short-term way to a technological 'push'. Top managers, swept along by the enthusiasm of their employees and the general IS media, or simply by inadequate attention to what is really going on, are carried on an uncontrolled path of expansion. This is followed by an excessive swing towards cost control and formalization when the enormous costs involved become clear – after the size of IS budgets sets off alarm bells in top manager's attention space. The 'fire' is dealt with by an excess of single-minded energy focused on merely dousing the flames. This fire-fighting model of top management behaviour has been well described elsewhere (Simon, 1957; Cyert and March, 1963). Finally, there is a settling down to some steady-state or equilibrium level of expenditure, formality and control. The stages may be thought of as reflecting general characteristics of emotion elicitation (Millenson, 1967; Gray, 1971, 1982) and influences of emotions on thought, particularly what are known in the psychology of emotions literature as mood congruity effects (Yates, 1962; Bower and Cohen, 1982; Johnson and Tversky, 1983; Den Uyl and Frijda, 1984) (Figure 2).

Nolan extended the model from four to six stages in later work (Nolan, 1977, 1979). The new model was based

In the middle of Stage 3 a basic transition from management of the computer to management of the company's data resources occurs. This makes Stage 4 look like the old Stage 2. It is an expansion stage in which users get interactive terminals and support to use new database technology. Expansion is uncontrolled because cost controls established in Stage 3 were designed for internal management of the computer, rather than for control of its growing uses. End users become the driving force in Stage 4 compared with technically-oriented specialists during Stage 2. In Stage 5, top management counteracts explosive growth in IS budgets with strict user-managed planning and control systems over the new data administration function. These systems are overseen by a senior management steering committee. Stage 6, maturity, is reached when external equilibrium between user demands and their ability to use new data effectively, matches internal equilibrium between the benefits and costs of innovation within IS.

A further change from the 1974 version is the identification of different management-created environments. The basis for this is a distinction between control and slack environments. Financial and performance control systems are present only in the control environment. In the slack environment incentives to use IS in an experimental manner are present. Extra resources beyond what is strictly necessary to get the job done are spent on IS in order to nurture innovation. In Stages 1 to 3 it is management of the computer which is the focus of the balance between control and slack. In Stages 3 to 6 it is management of data resources. In both cases the balance changes from low control and low slack, to high slack and low control when budgets are expanding quickly (Stages 2 and 4), and then to low slack and high control when budget growth is being retarded (Stages 3 and 5). The process ends in Stage 6 when a balance is achieved through high slack and high control.

We will work with a similar distinction between two general types of strategies for managing IS specialists; responsible autonomy and direct control. Responsible autonomy strategies aim to encourage employees to act responsibly or flexibly, in the interests of management, with little detailed instruction or supervision. The key motivating principle is employee loyalty. This is encouraged by various combinations of employment security, social rewards, design of tasks to be intrinsically satisfying, lax supervision and good long-run prospects. Direct control strategies aim to ensure that the work provided by employees may be designed and altered in the same way as other inputs. With these types of strategies managers try to limit the scope for uncontrolled employee actions by close supervision, by dividing work into small tasks and by reducing the conceptual content of the majority of jobs. Managers concentrate on financial means and the threat of job loss to motivate employees. Responsible autonomy and direct control should be considered as two ends of a continuum of management strategies, and two directions along which management strategies may change. They are broad 'types' of strategies encompassing a wide range of specific strategies and practices. These strategies will be used to chart long run changes in management of the IS function during different phases of the IS field $^{1}$ .

## Empirical evaluation of the Nolan model

Hypotheses derived from Nolan's model have not been confirmed empirically. Features which Nolan associated with proximity to the maturity stage have not been found to correlate with age of the IS function or with a collection of other maturity indicators. These include emergence of a formal data administration function (Goldstein and McCririk, 1981), steering committees (Drury, 1980), desire for generalist rather than specialist skills by IS managers and systems analysts (Benbasat et al., 1980), and chargeout systems (Drury and Bates, 1979). Nolan claimed that the stage an organization is experiencing can be identified by scores on type of benchmarks, but Drury (1983) found that such benchmarks did not classify organizations clearly into consistent stages. Some studies provide qualified support for the model. Ein-Dor and Segev (1982) found that IS groups originating in low-level staff departments or in operational departments were eventually moved up organization hierarchies. However half their sample did not move IS groups. Drury (1983) found that all pairs of benchmark variables he tested were positively correlated, indicating more mature organizations show more ‘mature’ levels on each benchmark, even though the benchmarks did not increase at the same rate as each other, or within each of the stages. No study has provided unequivocal support for the model. In spite of these failures in testing, the stages model continues to be widely referred to in the IS literature. Recent Nolanesque models describe the growth of end user computing and have been used to prescribe organization strategies which are sensitive to the stages of end user computing growth (O'Donnell and March, 1987; Alavi et al., 1987–88; Munro et al., 1987–88; Huff et al., 1988). McFarlan et al. (1983) suggested Nolan-like stages for any new IS technology.

Why has the model continued to be so influential? First, it is the only explicit model of the time pattern of IS function development. Second, it is clear and easily leads to testable hypotheses. For this it has appealed to academics. Third, the model has clear prescriptive content. For this it has appealed to managers and to management consultants. Fourth, and perhaps most controversially, we would suggest that the model does summarize certain experiences of many organizations. The model has tested poorly because it is underspecified. Other influences on the time pattern of IS development within user organizations should be considered.

## Other levels of analysis - The IS field

Other levels of analysis can help explain some of the adverse empirical results of Nolan's model. The competitive environment in which the user organization operates is one (McFarlan et al., 1983). Though maturity was a poor predictor of existence of a formal data administration function, Goldstein and McCririk (1981) found that organizations in prosperous industries were significantly more likely to have one. Kraemer et al. (1989) explain the development of IS within organizations by identifying which internal group of managers control IS development and whose interests are best served by IS. Laudon (1989) suggested that a mixture of environmental and internal organization factors be considered when analysing IS in large organizations. For Clement and Choo (1993), various contingent paths for the evolution of IS suggested by Laudon and by Kraemer et al. may provide a better starting point than Nolan's for understanding the growth of end user computing.

Here the wider technical environment is considered as a level of analysis that can influence the development of IS functions within organizations. This environment will be called the IS field (Borum et al., 1992). A technological field may be defined as a social space within which structuring of people and institutions occurs in relation to a bundle of techniques. That is, a bundle of communicatable procedures or knowledge resources. Institutions may be thought of as widely prevalent habits of thought, such as norms of working, or of dress, or justice. But institutions may also be thought of as activities or practices arising from and supporting those habits of thought, such as organizations and jobs, the actual way people dress themselves and the way justice is administered. The IS field may then be defined as the social space structured around the production, use, definition and control of IT. IT is the bundle of techniques relating to computer-based administrative or information-handling systems. People and institutions can belong to more than one field. An IS specialist may also be a mathematician or an accountant. An organization using IS may also be using and developing optical technology or personnel management techniques. The field contains more than IS specialists and organizations producing or using IT. It contains regulatory bodies and regulators such as IS-oriented sections of the Office of Technology Assessment or of the Census of Production. It also contains academics and journalists concerned with IS in addition to education institutions and the IS media.

## Phases of the IS field

The history of the IS field may be divided into a series of phases (see Friedman with Cornford, 1989). Each phase can be thought of as dominated by a particular problem which was thought at that time to be the major constraint on further computerization. Clearly the transition from one phase to another can be identified only roughly. Currently there is rapidly growing concern with systems which interact with the environment of organizations or strategic systems. This seems to be leading to a fourth phase – organization environment constraints – in the 1990s (Figure 3).

## Phase 1: hardware capacity constraints

During the first phase high prices, low capacity and low reliability of hardware dominated the field. Management of computer installations at this time, as defined by a key text on the subject of the late 1950s, was primarily about management of hardware: evaluation of hardware purchases and relations with hardware suppliers (Canning, 1956). Many computer programmers were seconded from hardware manufacturers to user organizations for months or even years. There lines of management authority were ambiguous. User organization staff directly involved in new computer skills were scattered throughout the organization structure (Whisler and Shultz, 1960).

Fast and efficient systems were required. Programmers developed their own tricks and techniques for taking short cuts to improve program performance. Programmer creativity was encouraged. Carl Rush from Standard Oil (New Jersey) emphasized creativity requirements when he worried about hiring the ‘wrong kind of people’, who ‘cannot apply imaginative thinking to the development of data-processing systems' (Rush, 1960, p. 204). IS specialists were managed much like academics or researchers, by a kind of responsible autonomy strategy which may be identified as 'management by neglect'. Buxton (1978) referred to this period as 'cottage industry' programming. Bergland called it the 'golden age' of programming.

## Phase 2: software productivity constraints

Hardware capacity and cost-performance ratios have improved continuously during the history of computing, but by the mid-1960s the lifting of hardware constraints revealed a new constraint on further computerization; the software productivity constraint. A number of influential books and articles appeared in the late 1960s and early 1970s which identified the software productivity gap as the key problem in IS (Brandon, 1967, 1968; Naur and Randell, 1969; Weinberg, 1971; Weinwurm, 1971; Brooks, 1975). Edsger Dijkstra cited a particular date for public emergence of the software productivity phase:

Only a few years ago this was different: to talk about a software crisis was blasphemy. The turning point was the Conference on Software Engineering in Garmisch, October 1968, a conference that created a sensation as there occurred the first open admission of the software crisis (Dijkstra, 1972, p. 120).

Staff costs for IS departments in user organizations were beginning to account for more of the overall computing budget than hardware. Striking graphs showing this change were published by Frank in 1968 and Boehm in 1973. Boehm's graph (Figure 4) was widely cited throughout the 1970s (Frank, 1983). IS management was beginning to be thought of less as a job of hardware evaluation and more one of dealing with growing numbers of highly paid and autonomous IS specialists.

The most visible strategy aimed at solving the software productivity gap was to create finer divisions of labour and to limit working procedures to an easily monitored set of structured procedures. Kraft (1977) predicted that the jobs of computer programmers would quickly become divided and routinized to the point when their work would be barely distinguishable from clerks or assembly line workers. We consider this to be an example of the direct control type of management strategy.

During Phase 2 there were substantial moves in the direction of direct control. Analysts were separated more clearly from programmers. Programs were divided up so that most programmers would concentrate on coding small modules using structured methods. The use of structured methods, for analysis and design as well as programming, was likened to a revolution in the IS field by some. Yourdon's collection of influential articles was titled Writings of the Revolution (1979). Glass labelled structured programming, somewhat satirically, as computing's 'Universal Elixir, circa 1974' (1977, p. 3). However the movement towards direct control was limited (Loseke and Sonquist, 1979; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Friedman and Greenbaum, 1984). Structured methods as were introduced were usually watered-down versions. Often methods introduced were available for IS specialists to use, but not imposed. By the 1980s, at some installations, structured methods were being abandoned (Friedman with Cornford, 1989, pp. 157–8 and 310–11). What limited the movement to direct control?

First, severe labour shortages have existed from the earliest years of the IS field. IS specialists who did not like the restrictions of direct control strategies in one place could easily find somewhere else to work. Brandon (1963) clearly recognized the problem of high labour turnover at the end of Phase 1. The problem continued to attract attention during Phase 2 (Gillis, 1968; Mumford, 1972; Tomeski, 1974) and Phase 3 (CCTA, 1980; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Marion, 1984, Buckroyd and Cornford, 1988). Second, tasks within the IS function were changing due to automation via more sophisticated operating systems. This meant less coding and more design and analysis. Third, severe labour shortages and gradual automation of tasks which were more amenable to direct control stimulated management to develop responsible autonomy strategies. Management by neglect gave IS specialists autonomy with little responsibility. By Phase 2, managers were developing strategies to retain staff and to build up organization loyalty: by developing career structures within IS, but also by providing up-to-date technology, flexible working arrangements, opportunities to increase skills through education and training, in addition to the more obvious inducements through salaries and perks (Hansen and Penny, 1976; Friedman and Cornford, 1981).

## Phase 3: user relations constraints

Since the late 1970s the problem of providing users with systems that they really need or want has been replacing software productivity as the main perceived constraint on the IS field $^{2}$ . ‘The 1980s could go down as the decade of the user’ declared an editorial in the British computing press in 1986 (Computer News, 1986, p. 1). In the 1983 survey of key IS issues, Society of Information Management (SIM) members ranked end user computing second after improved IS planning. Phase 2 types of issues were lower in the rankings. Software development was ranked at 4, productivity improvement and measurement was 5 and human resources ranked 8 (Dickson et al., 1984). When the survey was repeated in 1986 the results were even more skewed towards Phase 3 types of issues. The authors identified the increase in importance of management/enterprise issues as a major trend. Organizational learning, IS’s role and contribution and IS alignment in the organization ranked 3, 4 and 5 respectively, up from 6, 15 and 7 in 1983. End user computing dropped to 6, but this was explained by the authors as follows,

End user computing has spawned a great deal of research and education over the past four years. Information centers have been established by most companies. Users have gained experience and may be doing a better job of planning and avoiding problems (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987, p. 29).

Strategic planning remained issue 1 and competitive advantage entered the rankings at number 2. Software development, measuring effectiveness of IS staff and human resources had dropped to 13, 9 and 12 in the rankings respectively. Elsewhere this change has been described as a shift to 'producing the right system, rather than producing the system right' (Friedman with Cornford, 1989, p. 175). Quality of the system in terms of its value to the users of the system within the organization and to the organization as a whole grew in importance compared with the cost of the system.

Enhanced perception of user relations problems arose in part because of technological advances in hardware capacity and software productivity. Another factor was even more important. Earlier administrative computing systems primarily replaced simple and well-structured manual systems. It was relatively easy to judge the value of those systems because savings in labour costs were relatively easy to identify. With most of these large manual systems already computerized, at least in medium and large sized organizations, subsequent systems have been using existing computerized databases for applications which have no well-structured manual counterpart. Measuring the value of these systems requires substantial effort. Also, higher levels of management have become end users and system clients for these newer systems which are increasingly designed to support management control and strategic management (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Lucas, 1978; Sprague and Carlson, 1982). This tendency for computer applications to progress from lower level, routine, highly structured management activities, to higher level planning and unstructured management activity was predicted in the 1960s (Churchill et al., 1969). Satisfying users has also become more important because the political clout of users has increased. Decision support systems do not have to be used by managers. A computerized payroll or accounts system had to be used once the manual system was replaced. Use of the newer computerized management aids is more discretionary. This has also increased user power.

The spread of PCs to users and increased targeting of user managers directly by computer services industry suppliers has enhanced the ability of users to 'go-it-alone'. This has thereby reduced the monopoly power of central IS functions within user organizations. Emphasis on producing the 'right' system and on user relations during the 1980s is evidenced by rapid growth of information centres (Wetherbe and Leitheiser, 1985; Magal and Carr, 1988) and other support for end user computing (O'Donnell and March, 1987; Munro et al., 1978–8; Huff et al., 1988); by the proliferation of user involvement schemes (Briefs et al., 1983; Ives and Olson, 1984; Friedman with Cornford, 1989); and by the development and diffusion of prototyping or evolutionary systems development techniques (Nauman and Jenkins, 1982; Budde et al., 1984; Doke, 1990). Also a change in emphasis in the systems development lifecycle towards attending to the periods of contact with users is notable; both at the earliest periods with emphasis on preliminary analysis, elicitation techniques and organization feasibility study, and at end periods, with emphasis on demonstration, orientation, training and user evaluation (O'Donnell and March, 1987).

In Phase 3 the proportion of analysis work has increased. More developers have contact with users, particularly with the growth of information centres (Wetherbe and Leitheiser, 1985). Some are called analysts, but the fastest growing group have been programmer/analysts (or analyst/programmers; Buckroyd and Cornford, 1988). These trends can be seen from British surveys which show a falling proportion of applications development staff called programmers and a rise of analyst/programmers $^{3}$ . An in-depth survey of 95 IS managers in the US in 1983 showed a similar shift towards combining analysis and programming functions with 82% using mixed team or matrix organization structures as opposed to the popular programmer pools of the 1970s (Friedman and Greenbaum, 1984). Closer contact with users has also led to greater emphasis on management training and on skills which are industry-specific. Together these trends have led to changes in hiring practices, favouring recruitment of people with more generalist backgrounds. Traditional computer science and engineering qualifications have been giving way to degrees in liberal arts, social science and especially business studies during the 1980s according to the US survey. As one respondent put it, ‘We need Renaissance people who can think and act with an open mind’ (Friedman and Greenbaum, 1984, p. 134).

In 1989 the SIM survey was repeated again. Examining the entire series of surveys the authors concluded that 'business relations issues have dominated the top ten throughout the 1980s' (Niederman et al., 1991, p. 488). However the emphasis on user relations (broadly defined), is expected to wane in the 1990s. Not only did end user computing decline from 6 in 1986 to 18 in rank, but also the ranks of organizational learning, IS role and contribution, and IS organizational alignment declined from 3, 4 and 5 to 5, 11 and 7 respectively. Internal effectiveness issues made a partial comeback, and issues which we would associate with Phase 4 (organization environment constraints) suddenly came into the picture, though only in a limited way. Distributed systems appeared for the first time as an issue (joint 12 in rank) and electronic data interchange rose from no ranking in 1983, to 14 in 1986, to joint 12 in 1989. Finally, the most significant change of the 1989 survey was for information architecture (up from 8 to 1 and not registered at all before the 1986 survey). This is a high-level map of the information requirements of an organization. 'It is used to guide applications development and facilitate the integration and sharing of data' (Niederman et al., 1991, p. 479). Clearly this issue focuses on helping to produce the right systems rather than to produce systems right, a Phase 3 issue; however it may also be interpreted as providing a required platform for dealing effectively with agents in the organization's environment.

## Dynamics

Why has the field shifted from one phase to another? Is there an underlying mechanism for phases of the IS field similar to the general emotional characteristics which generate Nolan's stages? One underlying change mechanism has been structural changes in the 'location' of the IS function within organizations (Figure 5).

Expansion of the computer system core through automation of more technical and more routine activities of the mediating process, and expansion of IS specialist work through computerization of more activities carried out within organizations (and between organizations and their environment), have led to displacement of the IS function away from machine-oriented work and towards support for higher level management functions. In addition a general increase of staff carrying out these tasks either as full time IS specialists or, increasingly, as IS literate mediators between other users and full time IS specialists has occurred (Martin, 1982). The IS function itself may therefore be seen as a shifting entity.

There is a second mechanism behind the phases pattern. All constraints have existed as problems from the outset in the IS field and they all continue to be of concern, even of major concern, at least for some people and institutions in the field. Which of those constraints comes to dominate the field depends on the position and influence of those who will most benefit either by removal of those constraints or by implementation of strategies to remove those constraints. The influence of those associated with particular constraints depends crucially on the role of the IS media and the education, training and consultancy institutions in the field. They amplify certain concerns, stimulating a climate of opinion whereby efforts to ease particular constraints come to be regarded as the most profitable areas for development effort.

We have concentrated on structural factors leading to displacement of the IS function within organizations because the IS field has been highly influenced by people directly experiencing those structural shifts, particularly people working in IS functions in large user organizations and by representatives of companies supplying user organizations. Experiences of both these groups are regularly reported in the IS press. There are others who have experienced somewhat different constraints related to IS systems. During the 1960s many were concerned with the effects of IS systems on direct and indirect users. Novelists and film makers expressed great concern about system reliability and the consequences of malfunctions, especially in military systems (Doctor Strangelove, 1964; Fail Safe, 1964). They were joined by social scientists in their concern for unintended consequences of IS systems use, particularly government systems. They claimed that political power was being transferred into the hands of technocrats and ruling elites and that privacy was being threatened (Ellul, 1964; Boguslaw, 1965; US House of Representatives, 1966; Galbraith, 1967; Westin, 1967; Maynaud, 1968). This body of opinion had little impact on the IS field at the time. In part this was because these groups did not develop detailed suggestions for solutions to these problems. In part it was because the media in which these concerns appeared and the people expressing these concerns were considered outside the IS field. It was a community of insiders, such as those at the Garmisch conference in 1968 (Naur and Randell, 1969), who defined the chief problem in the IS field in terms of a software productivity crisis. They came to define the chief constraint on the IS field during the late 1960s and 1970s. As IS functions come to affect higher level aspects of organizational activities we may expect the issues considered of chief concern within the IS field to become more influenced by bodies of opinion represented in broader media and education sources.

Though we are in Phase 3, Phase 1 and 2 conditions have not completely disappeared. Some of the conditions which generated Phase 1 patterns have re-emerged. Hardware constraints are still a problem for systems programmers and in rapidly growing areas such as microchip programming and the development of packages. Phase 2 conditions continue in certain large installations, especially where systems being developed are large, but use fairly well known technology (Kraft and Dubnoff, 1986). Also in the media, education, research and consultancy sections of the IS field, significant groups and institutions continue to be wedded to the investigation and alleviation of earlier phase constraints. At any point in time, what is perceived as the key set of constraints will stimulate a broad community of people within the field to investigate and try to alleviate the constraint. Careers come to be based on dedication to a particular direction in research, teaching and consultancy. Arguably, it is even more difficult for institutions to change directions. Journals, magazines and learned societies define themselves around specific research issues. Whole departments at education institutions become locked into defining their subject in terms of particular issues. Over time, institutions and people dedicated to past major constraints accumulate in the field, in spite of rapid growth of institutions and communities focusing on the current most fashionable set of constraints. Note the slow change in curricula of computer science departments towards management issues and broader social issues, while favoured qualifications in the field have been rapidly changing towards those from business administration or liberal arts (Friedman and Greenbaum, 1984). However attachment to concern with older constraints does not necessarily mean irrelevance to the field. Breakthroughs in these older areas can have important effects, as is amply demonstrated by recent success of the ISDOS project at developing CASE products. ISDOS, under Daniel Teichroew at the University of Michigan dates from the end of Phase 1.

## Relating organization stages and field phases

Stages influence phases because the complexion of the field will, in part, reflect the constellation of stages which organizations are experiencing at any point in time. This influence will be greater if many large organizations happen to be at the same stage. Concern with software productivity and staff costs during Phase 2 in the IS field may be associated, in part, with the coincidence of Nolan's Stage 3 in many large and influential user organizations in the late 1960s and early 1970s.

Phases influence stages because the IS field contains more than the collection of IS functions in user organizations. It also contains suppliers of equipment and IS expertise, IS academics, trainers of IS specialists and the IS media. The IS media and education institutions represent a mirror of the field to the outside world and back to the field itself, but a mirror that is biased, a mirror that reflects filtered information about the field. The biases are systematic. We have described a primary bias in the IS field as the 'problem of tenses' (Friedman with Cornford, 1989, 33–4). Participants in the field describe what they are doing as though it was what they have done. They describe what is planned as though it is being implemented. The temptation to represent progress in speeded up terms is stimulated by the market value of such information. Organizations want to be identified with the 'leading edge'. This is important for attracting scarce skilled labour. For organizations selling expertise or IS products, it is also clearly valuable for attracting customers. Arguably this speeding up of perceived development at the IS field level was a major contributor to the mechanism behind Nolan's stages. Nolan himself identifies the status value to user managers of working with IS as a contributor to the 'contagious' growth stage. Contagion occurs not only within organizations, but also between them. There is a bandwagon effect. It is at the field level that this factor may be said to operate on top management.

## Influence of the IS field and the phases on evaluation of Nolan's model

## Success

Nolan suggests that the more lax top management is about controlling Stage 2 growth, the more traumatic will be the imposition of formal controls in Stage 3. Conversely, the whole stages pattern is ‘almost entirely avoidable’ (Gibson and Nolan, 1974, p. 86) if appropriate controls (those advised by Nolan) are imposed at the outset of Stage 2. This begs a serious question. Nolan’s first version was widely read in the IS field. If this version declared that the whole stages pattern was almost entirely avoidable, why did new user organizations not learn from Nolan’s model? Why did they not implement appropriate controls at the outset of Stage 2 as Nolan recommended? More plausibly, why did new organizations not learn from the unfortunate experiences of older organizations in the field? Beyond appealing to irrationality or lack of communication, two explanations are offered here.

First, the position of the IS function is always changing (see Figure 5). Latecomers to IT, assuming they purchase new computer systems core, introduce different core from their predecessors. Similarly the types of applications which are well established and supported by IS specialists in the field change over time. This can make the experience of others of questionable value, especially during a phase shift.

Second, latecomers to the IS field have been systematically different from early adopters. Latecomers have been smaller. Cheapening of hardware capacity has led to lower entry costs. The problems smaller organizations encounter when introducing IS are somewhat different from those of large ones. For example, there is a general reluctance of consultants to work with small organizations because the overall investment in IS by these clients is small (Friedman and Monsted, 1992).

## Failure

From the perspective of the IS field as a whole, one particular problem with all of the empirical tests of Nolan's model is clear. They all test using cross section data. IS functions are observed at different stages because they are of different ages at any one point of time, but the observations are affected by events in the IS field as a whole. Many of the items Nolan associated with stage maturity were also fashionable throughout the field. Absence of correlation between organization maturity and desirability of generalist skills (Benbasat et al., 1980) may be interpreted as reflecting enhanced desirability of generalist skills associated with Phase 3 (Friedman and Greenbaum, 1984). Interpreting the desire for generalist skills as tied to field time more than maturity, leads to the expectation that it will be widespread irrespective of organization maturity. If this test had been carried out in the 1950s or even the 1960s, we expect that lower enthusiasm for generalist skills would have been found for the whole sample. From the IS field perspective, experience of user relations problems and susceptibility to current fashion would be expected to influence interest in generalist skills. Mature installations may be less open to current fashion and more tied to traditional skills. Similarly absence of correlation between organization maturity and presence of senior management steering committees, as well as data administration functions, may be interpreted as due to latecomers making greater efforts to overcome Phase 3 constraints. Even the result on chargeout systems may be interpreted in part by the connection of this policy to solving user relations problems (getting users to 'own' systems they demand).

Ein-Dor and Segev's (1982) partial support for Nolan's model can be explained more fully if IS field time is considered. Early in field time new IS departments were placed in operational departments or in low-level staff departments (Churchill et al., 1969). Nolan was correct to note that the IS group was moved as those IS functions matured. Ein-Dor and Segev confirmed this for 23 out of 45 cases. This left almost half of the sample which did not move their IS function. However in most (15) of these cases the IS group was initially located at a high level (either as an autonomous unit or in administrative services). It is likely that these were latecomers. They benefited from the experience of pioneer organizations in the field. Unlike pioneer organizations, latecomers could discover from the experience of others and the IS media that IS would serve a wide range of user departments.

According to several commentators Nolan's concept of maturity is faulty because it implies an eventual static position (Drury, 1983; Benbasat et al., 1984; King and Kraemer, 1984). Nolan's revised version of the model proposes that maturity reached in the old Stage 4 was only temporary. This appears to be inconsistent. However Nolan interpreted maturity as an equilibrium state, not an end state. Even in his earlier 1974 article Nolan suggested that the fourth stage was unlikely to be the last one. Therefore it was not inconsistent for him to imply that maturity ‘matured’ in his later article.

We can time-stamp Nolan's articles themselves as reflecting particular phases of IS field time. The 1974 article reflected observations of organizations experiencing phase 2, software productivity constraints. The 1979 article reflected the coming of a new phase, user relations constraints. The 1990s seem to be a period of transition to a new fourth phase based on the alleviation of environment constraints and an explosion of environment-related systems (now often labelled strategic IS systems). We may expect a new Nolanesque stages model to look something like that represented in Figure 6.

A new Stage 6 would be proposed, perhaps to be called strategic systems, representing contagious growth in IS expenditure on EDI, VANs and systems for which the organization's customers and suppliers are direct end users. Demand for such systems would come from top managers, from customers and from super-organizational bodies like trade associations. The danger of such systems is that by interacting with the organization's environment, they become more open to manipulation by agents who can not be subjected to organizational controls. They become more open to virus makers, agents of espionage, dissatisfied customers or suppliers (note the damage done by the Michelangelo virus on 6 March, 1992). The development of these systems may therefore be constrained by security considerations as well as the difficulty of generating reliable information about the organization's environment. Controls may have to come at super-organization levels, perhaps through universal acceptance of security standards. This is likely to be slow, as experience with getting hardware manufacturers to adopt OSI standards shows.

## Conclusion

A phases approach at the level of the IS field as a whole has been proposed here. It has been argued that an appreciation of the phases of the IS field can help to explain why tests of Nolan's model have not been successful. Analysis at the level of the IS field suggests that the paths by which perception of new constraints on the field are generated and the paths by which such concerns lead to efforts both to alleviate these constraints directly and to adapt to avoid the consequences of these constraints, needs investigation. In order to test for organization stages, the influence of IS field phases as well as different competitive conditions facing particular organizations must be separated out. This could be done in two steps. First organizations to be investigated should be grouped by competitive environment influences, perhaps as suggested by McFarlan et al. (1983), and maturity benchmarks should be grouped by phases. Second correlations between age and maturity benchmarks should be tested for each group separately and then pooled. The phases model can also be used to predict new pressures on the roles of IS managers and executives, and new directions into which research and education resources of the IS field should be focused. It can help to explain how changes in key issues, such as those surveyed by the SIM, are generated, and help us to predict which issues will become important in the future, an important topic which is just beginning to be addressed (Watson, 1990).
