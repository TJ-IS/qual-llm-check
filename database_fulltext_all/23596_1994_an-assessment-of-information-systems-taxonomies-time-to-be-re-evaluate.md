# An assessment of information systems taxonomies: time to be re-evaluate?

E. REED DOKE and TONYA BARRIER

Computer Information Systems, College of Business Administration, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, MO 65804-0095, USA

Dramatic advances in technology, evolving organizational structures and increasing user influence are impacting the types of information systems (IS) being developed. These are not recognized adequately by existing taxonomies which tend to be incomplete, unclear, simplistic, unwieldy, inflexible or some combination of these. Fourteen models from the literature are described and discussed in light of today's myriad systems. A user-oriented two-dimensional taxonomy utilizing user type (institution or individual) and type of system support provided to the user (data, communication, information, or decision) is developed.

## Introduction

The rapid changes in information system (IS) technology are presenting firms with significant challenges and dramatic opportunities. Revolutionary advances in hardware capability coupled with nearly free-fall in prices has shifted numerous applications across the threshold from infeasible to feasible. Concurrently, important advances in software development methodologies and tools have encouraged the construction of many previously infeasible systems. In addition, the changing structure of organizations, specifically the trend toward flatter structures and the accompanying shifts in information requirements, plus the move to increased user involvement has significantly altered the types of IS being implemented.

This explosive progress, while undoubtedly improving efficiency in many firms, forces us to rethink the classification of IS. The traditional transaction processing, management information, decision support systems (TPS-MIS-DSS) categories are clearly inadequate for many of the systems being developed today. Further, this somewhat antiquated perspective is an obstacle to research that requires a clear and representative view.

The terms taxonomy and framework are sometimes used interchangeably in the literature. However, a clear distinction between the two is identified: taxonomy is generally used to describe a classification scheme for 'things' such as IS. Although framework is sometimes used as a synonym for taxonomy, it is more often used to describe models that organize and group 'concepts and relationships'. Since the focus here is the classification of IS, taxonomy will be used to describe these models.

The purpose here is to review the various IS classification efforts in the literature, and to propose a taxonomy that updates the traditional views. Important objectives of this effort are to recognize technology's impact on the types of IS being developed and to provide researchers and practitioners with a more complete, adaptable and realistic description of current IS activity.

## A description of previous taxonomies

During the relatively brief life of computer-based IS, numerous taxonomies have been proposed. In this section 14 of the more significant models reported in the literature are described.

## Leavitt and Whisler (1958)

Perhaps the earliest and certainly one of the most prophetic taxonomies emerged in late 1958 (Leavitt and Whisler, 1958). Although the primary thrust of this work was an exploration of the future impact of IT on managers and organizations, three classes of IS were described which today would be defined as transaction processing systems (TPS), decision support systems (DSS), and expert systems (EXS). TPS processed large volumes of routine information, DSS enabled managers to collect and analyse large quantities of information over a broad range of decisions and EXS were visualized as automating the more structured decisions encountered by managers. Although this effort was remarkably accurate in some aspects, it failed to recognize the future role of IT in smaller organizations.

## Head (1967)

Head (1967) recognized that prior to 1967, the primary focus of automation was processing accounting transactions and printing scheduled reports based on data from these transactions. This research recognized, using Anthony's (1965) management activity framework (operational control, management control and strategic planning) as a backdrop, that the immense volume of data captured by these systems could be employed by management for control and planning purposes.

In addition, Head proposed that the data be refined, defined and organized as a database to facilitate information access. This work represents a preliminary view of the popular TPS-MIS-DSS taxonomy that evolved later.

## Churchill, Kempster and Uretsky (1969)

Churchill et al. (1969) investigated the progressive development of computer applications by studying the relationships between computer-based IS within organizations. The research identified four stages in the evolution of computer applications in an organization. These four stages were then extended into four types of IS. Types 1 and 2 focused on the automation of clerical tasks while types 3 and 4 were related to management activities.

Specifically, type 1 systems resulted from the automation of clerical tasks which enabled faster and less expensive processing of routine business transactions. Individual stand-alone systems were typically developed for the various functional areas in an organization. The type 2 systems provided a logical extension of type 1 by integrating data across functional applications.

In contrast, type 3 systems added decision making algorithms to existing information processing applications. Finally, type 4 systems provided simulation capability plus responses to spontaneous unique inquiries. The authors suggested that these systems normally, but not necessarily, follow a logical chronological development sequence whereby type 1 systems are initially developed, followed by type 2, then 3 and 4. Subsequent applications have increasing levels of sophistication and build on the basic transaction processing applications.

The type 1 and 2 systems identified here suggest the TPS and MIS categories of subsequent taxonomies. However, it is not clear whether types 3 and 4 are DSS or EXS because each appears to have characteristics of both categories.

## Gorry and Scott Morton (1971)

Gorry and Scott Morton (1971) produced a widely-cited IS taxonomy that synthesized and extended the earlier work of Anthony (1965) and Simon (1965) by creating the two-dimensional model shown in Table 1.

Simon's (1965) work identified two distinct categories of decision problems: programmed and nonprogrammed. Programmed decisions are routine with well-defined procedures for decision making. Nonprogrammed decisions are nonroutine and unique for which little experience and documentation exist. Gorry and Scott Morton (1971) adopted the terms structured and unstructured for programmed and nonprogrammed respectively.

As previously mentioned, Anthony (1965) established three categories of management activity: operational control, management control and strategic planning. Operational control focuses on monitoring operational tasks, management control attempts to ensure the effective and efficient utilization of resources (particularly human resources) and strategic planning relates to the identification and selection of organizational objectives and the allocation of resources to attain them.

The six cells of the Gorry–Scott Morton model represent six categories of IS. For example, a typical TPS would be placed in cell 1 while systems supporting unstructured planning activity would be in cell 6. Cells 1, 2 and 3 are termed structured decision systems (SDS) and cells 4, 5 and 6 are identified as DSS.

In addition to relating problem type to management activity, a major contribution of this taxonomy was to recognize the multi-dimensionality of the IS classification problem.

## Dickson (1977)

As with previous research, Dickson's (1977) work produced an IS taxonomy as a byproduct of his primary purpose which was to describe a perceived emerging discipline: management information – decision systems (MIDS). He visualized MIDS as the integration of numerous disciplines ranging from operations research to economics, whose purpose was to facilitate the utilization of information as a resource.

Based in part on the trichotomy established by the earlier work of Leavitt and Whisler (1958), Dickson constructed a five-category taxonomy which he described as a hierarchy with five levels: clerical systems, IS, decision systems, interactive systems and programmed systems.

Dickson's level 1 clerical system is the familiar transaction processing system category. Level 2 IS are described as a system that supports management decision making by providing information to managers. The information orientation of level 2 contrasts with the data processing focus of level 1.

Level 3 decision systems are distinguished by their emphasis on the actual decision-making process instead of simply supplying information (level 2) to the process. The level 4 interactive systems are the level 3 decision systems with the addition of interactive capability – something which is considered essential today. Finally, level 5 programmed systems actually make decisions for the decision maker, however in 1977 these systems were primarily conceptual.

Dickson described these five levels as a hierarchy because subsequent levels are an extension of previous levels in their capability and sophistication. Table 2 summarizes these categories and relates them to current systems types.

## Alter (1977)

The purpose of Alter's (1977) work was to develop a taxonomy of DSS, rather than a taxonomy of IS, however his research established two very broad categories of IS: electronic data processing systems (EDP) and DSS.

This research describes EDP systems as those involved in processing business transactions, storing and retrieving data and reporting information. In contrast, DSS are designed to assist decision makers to select and implement alternatives. Clearly both of these groupings are quite broad. The EDP category, e.g., includes both TPS and MIS. DSS, using Alter's definition, encompasses both DSS and EXS, and to an extent, MIS.

The DSS taxonomy resulting from this research was determined by the extent to which the DSS output determined the decision. Based on an examination of 56 systems in organizations, seven categories of DSS were established:

## Sprague (1980)

Building on the work of Alter and others, Sprague (1980) presented a convincing argument that DSS are a distinct class of IS – not just an extension of MIS – that use data and models to help solve unstructured problems at all levels in an organization. This description was unique because it requires models and data, deals with unstructured problems and supports decision making throughout an organization. Previous DSS definitions lacked this precision.

In making his case, Sprague advocates the EDP–MIS–DSS trichotomy previously proposed by Head (1967) and subsequently supported by others. EDP systems (TPS) focus on processing data at the operational level, are concerned with efficiency and provide limited reporting for management. MIS, on the other hand, are information oriented and generate reports for management and support inquiry. Unlike TPS and MIS, DSS are decision oriented and provide support to decision making throughout an organization.

## Raho and Belohlav (1982)

Supporting the EDP–MIS–DSS taxonomy and building on the work of Sprague and others, Raho and Belohlav (1982) refined the characteristics that distinguish these three categories of systems. Their description of the six characteristics shown in Table 3 serves to sharpen the distinctions.

## Martin (1982)

Although the TPS-MIS-DSS taxonomy was quite popular, some attempted to place IS into only two categories. Martin (1982), e.g. identified systems as prespecified or non-prespecified. Prespecified systems are those whose requirements can be clearly articulated prior to development; non-prespecified are those whose requirements are either unknown or unclear and often benefit from heuristic development strategies.

Incidentally, these two categories are quite similar to the structured decision systems and decision support systems advocated earlier by Gorry and Scott Morton (1971).

## Scott Morton (1985)

Concentrating on a subset of IS termed management support systems, Scott Morton (1985) proposed a three-part taxonomy based on the type of support the system provides the user: data support, decision support and executive support.

Management support systems were defined quite broadly as the use of IT to support management. Within this broad category, data support systems are those that maintain and retrieve information as a byproduct of the basic transaction processing activity, while the decision support description parallels that of Sprague (1980): an automated model-oriented system that supports a recurring specific decision or class of decisions.

The executive support category however, is much more broadly defined. These systems have a data retrieval focus and deal with a manager's information requirements.

Scott Morton's efforts appear to be an attempt to clarify and perhaps expand the management support system concept. A major contribution of this work is to suggest that systems be classified according to the type of support they provide. Unfortunately, the taxonomy proposed received little subsequent support or acceptance.

## Senn (1989)

Senn (1989) proposed the classification of IS based upon the user type: end user or institution. He identified two types of IS: end-user applications and institutional applications. End-user systems are those used for specialized purposes by a limited number of individuals and may be developed solely by the end-user or with the assistance of IS professionals. In contrast, institutional applications are widely used and likely have a broad impact on the organization. Data integrity and security are paramount, and processing efficiency is important. Generally these systems are developed by IS professionals, often in concert with user representatives.

## Silver and Silver (1989)

The concept of open (stochastic) systems and closed (deterministic) systems has been suggested by Silver and Silver (1989). The open systems have outputs that cannot be predicted with certainty. Airline reservation systems are examples of open systems. Closed systems, on the other hand, have outputs that can be predicted with more confidence. Bill of materials and inventory applications are examples of closed systems because these applications can accurately predict the parts required to produce specific items. Different development methodologies are proposed for each system type considering their distinct differences.

## Grimshaw (1992)

More recently, a three dimensional taxonomy utilizing task, technology and time was proposed by Grimshaw (1992). In promoting this taxonomy, the author argues that previous classification efforts focused on subsets of systems and failed to recognize the influence of technology and time. This new model encompasses a broader range of IS and is an important improvement.

Building on the work of Anthony (1965), Gorry–Scott Morton (1971) and Nolan (1984), Grimshaw proposes that IS be classified according to the system task (structured or unstructured), the technology employed (the specific class of hardware and software, and time (actually this is the stage of growth of the organization).

## Ein-Dor and Segev (1993)

Utilizing an extremely broad definition of an IS ('any computerized system with a user or operation interface')

Ein-Dor and Segev (1993, p. 167) identified the chronological development of 17 types of systems ranging from early computing systems to present day CAD/CAM applications by classifying these systems numerically using system attributes and functions. Quantitative differences between the various types were computed and analysed.

While not constructing a practical taxonomy of IS, this work lends support to the argument that different classes of IS do exist. In addition, the research suggests that the development of new systems follows the development of technology.

## A discussion of previous taxonomies

We believe that the taxonomies described in the previous section are inadequate to describe all of today's types of IS. Although each model has undoubtedly contributed to our understanding, each suffers from being either incomplete, unclear, simplistic, unwieldy or inflexible. Table 4 summarizes the taxonomies and the relevant literature.

Head (1967), Sprague (1980) and Raho–Belohlav (1982) all advocate the TPS–MIS–DSS trichotomy. We believe this taxonomy, although very clear and easy to understand, is incomplete in today's IS environment. The existence of electronic data interchange systems, EXS, strategic IS, and geographical IS, to name a few, present the TPS–MIS–DSS taxonomy with a difficult challenge: where do these systems fit? Similarly, the early work of Leavitt and Whisler (1958) (TPS–DSS–EXS) is also incomplete.

The taxonomies proposed by Alter (1977), Martin (1982), Senn (1989) and Silver and Silver (1989), while making important contributions, all contain only two very broad categories. These two-dimensional models are too simple to appropriately classify the myriad systems that exist today. For instance, an IS professional would have difficulty classifying data communication systems/telecommunication systems in any of these two-dimensional taxonomies.

The early classification discussed by Churchill et al. (1969) is guilty of being both unclear and incomplete in light of current IS. The model is unclear because the type 3 and 4 categories represent features which apply to both DSS and EXS. The model is incomplete because like the TPS–MIS–DSS taxonomy, it simply does not accommodate many of the systems being developed today.

Both the Gorry–Scott Morton matrix and the Dickson hierarchy that were developed in the 1970s were quite workable during that time. Today however, they are incomplete because they focus upon a limited set of concepts related to the management of information within the organization and the retrieval of information outside of the organization. A geographical information system (GIS) which manipulates data based upon spacial location (not models) could not be included in these taxonomies.

Scott Morton's data support–decision support–executive support model is both incomplete and unclear. It is unclear because the executive support category is not precisely defined. In fact this category can be used to represent both the traditional MIS and EXS. It is incomplete because some systems cannot be classified using this taxonomy. For instance, the global communication and EDI have no place in the taxonomy.

Interestingly, Grimshaw's (1992) work criticizes previous taxonomies because they assume static conditions and fail to accommodate change. We believe this is precisely the main weakness with his model: the technology and time dimensions are too rigid to allow the model to adapt to a changing technological and/or organizational environments. To illustrate, the proposed technology dimension consists of a specific list of technologies such as type of computer or communications medium. The suggested time dimension loosely follows the stages of computer technology maturity suggested by Nolan (1984) which can be equated to organizational structure. As new technologies are developed and new organizational paradigms evolve, these dimensions would require updating. However, the utility of a taxonomy depends upon its flexibility and adaptability. One that requires continual maintenance as the environment changes fails to provide the firm foundation required to classify IS over time.

Finally, the taxonomy developed by Ein-Dor and Segev (1993) consists of 17 categories based on an extremely broad definition of IS. This research was oriented toward analysing differences between types of systems, rather than producing a workable taxonomy, therefore the 17 category model is too unwieldy for practical use.

## The proposed taxonomy

The previous discussion suggests that previous taxonomies are too limited in scope to represent the vast array of IS that exist today. Numerous factors such as technological advances in hardware and software, evolving organizational structures, and increasing user participation have promoted the development of many new categories of IS such as data communication systems, GIS and spatial DSS. A representative sample of the 'new' systems along with some of the classic systems are listed and briefly described with literature references in Table 5.

We believe the existing taxonomies have become unworkable because the many current systems simply do not fit into previous taxonomies. For example, is an EXS a subset of DSS or, as some suggest, a completely new class of IS? Where does a GIS fit in the previous taxonomies? All of the previous taxonomies have difficulty recognizing many of the PC-based end-user applications in existence today simply because these types of systems did not exist when the taxonomies were developed.

In addition, the primary focus of previous taxonomies has been related to specific concepts related to their individual time frame. However, the work of Scott Morton (1985) and Senn (1989) recognized the importance of the user. Their efforts suggest two important dimensions that a workable framework must recognize: the system user and the system support provided to that user.

## The system user dimension

The system user dimension is an important dimension that helps a researcher or an organization classify the person(s) (and/or entity) who receives the benefit (the output) from the system. The system user is classified according to two categories: individual or institution. From an organizational point of view, the individual classification describes a low-impact system that serves a subset of the organization (or entity). The institution classification describes a high-impact system that serves the entire organization (or entity of interest). In other words, the system serves either individuals within the institution or an entire institution. This concept is an expansion of the institutional and end-user applications advocated by Senn (1989).

The individual system user system may be end-user developed, and is intended to meet the information needs of an individual or a small group. Desktop organizers are examples of individual system user systems. The institutional system, on the other hand, is most often developed by IS professionals and satisfies the information requirements of a much broader segment of the organization, generally serving multiple individuals and multiple departments. Transaction processing systems are examples of institution system user systems.

## The support dimension

The second dimension being proposed, adapted from the Scott Morton model (1985), is the type of support the system provides to the user: data support, communication support, information support and decision support. Data support systems are basically the traditional high volume transaction processing systems.

Communications support systems have become very popular as advances in hardware and software technology have enabled the transmission of data and images through local, national and global networks. In today's world of global competition, these communication systems are critical to the success of many organizations (Stallings, 1988). These systems range from personal communications such as Internet and Compuserve to institutional data interchange applications (Stair, 1992). Further carriers such as AT&T, MCI and Sprint have formed alliances and partnerships (e.g. Infonet, Sprintnet) to build worldwide networks and offer one-stop shopping for all an organization's communication needs (Strauss, 1993).

Here, the decision support category is not the generally accepted view of DSS. Instead it includes, in addition to the previously defined DSS, intelligent decision support and EXS, and is not limited to model-based applications. In general, it includes all automated support for a decision or a defined class of decisions.

Information support systems are developed to support a broader range of activities than do decision support applications. Systems of this type have been previously classified as executive support systems. Further, the emphasis is on both internal and external information storage, retrieval and reporting. Individual information support can be as simple as presentation software, or as elaborate as a strategic information system (SIS).

## The taxonomy illustrated

The taxonomy being proposed here combines the user type (individual or institution) and the support provided to that user (data, communication, information or decision) to create a two-dimensional model consisting of the eight cells shown in Table 6.

Several systems are included in Table 6 to illustrate their classification using the taxonomy. For example, DSS, expert ECS and spacial decision support systems (SDSS) have been classified as individual systems supporting decisions. GIS, MIS and SIS are shown as institutional systems providing information support. Similarly, all of the systems identified in Table 5 plus some representative applications are classified in Table 6 to demonstrate the flexibility of the taxonomy.

It is important to recognize, however, that the eight categories are not necessarily mutually exclusive. For example E-mail could be either an individual or institutional user type. An individual user type would exist if the system was used to meet individual needs; however, if the system was employed in a broad coordinated organization-wide manner, then it would be classified as an institution user type.

## Summary

Clearly, the dramatic advances in technology, the continuing evolution of organizational structures and the changing user role is having an impact on the types of IS being developed today. The emergence of EXS, GIS, SIS and electronic data interchange are indicative of these forces. Equally clear is the difficulty existing taxonomies have in adequately recognizing and classifying these newer system types.

The proposed taxonomy overcomes many of these difficulties by recognizing two important, and somewhat static dimensions of IS: the system user type (individual or institution) and the support provided to the user (data, communication, information or decision). The model is adaptable to changes in organizations, is adaptable to changes in individual's roles within the organization and is relatively insulated from changes in technology. Therefore, it provides both practitioners and researchers a more stable platform for classifying IS.

This flexibility and adaptability is, also unfortunately, a shortcoming: the model does not classify all IS uniquely. As previously mentioned, some systems may be included in multiple cells because they have the potential to have multiple roles within the organization. Further, since the taxonomy relies on subjective aspects that may be unique to individual environments, researchers using this model may establish different classifications for the same type of system. However, this model does represent a new set of static dimensions, and provides a fresh view of IS classification efforts.

## Biographical notes

E. Reed Doke is a Professor of Computer Information Systems at Southwest Missouri State University in Springfield, Missouri. Prior to joing the university in 1976, he was MIS Manager for a large financial institution. He received his PhD in management from the University of Arkansas and continues to serve industry as a consultant in the MIS area. He is a member of Decision Sciences Institute and has published numerous articles and papers related to software development which continues to be his primary research focus. He serves on the editorial board of the Journal of Computer Information Systems.

Tonya B. Barrier received her PhD in computer information systems from The University of Texas at Arlington, and is currently an Assistant Professor in the Computer Information Systems Department at Southwest Missouri State University. Dr Barrier serves on the editorial review board of the Journal of End User Computing. Her research interests include decision processes, measures of effectiveness for intelligent systems, and instruments for computer anxiety and statistical anxiety.
