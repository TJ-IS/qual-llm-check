# The adoption of IT in management accounting: the expert systems experience

ALAN SANGSTER

Department of Accountancy, University of Aberdeen, Dunbar Street, Aberdeen, AB9 2TY, UK

This article considers why, despite forecasts to the contrary and in spite of being apparently well-suited to the technology, management accounting-based expert system developments appear to be virtually non-existent. The manner in which organizational change develops and the theoretical justification for the suitability of the management accounting domain for expert systems developments are reviewed, and a number of hypotheses are considered before describing and reporting the results of a survey into the attitudes and environment of UK practising management accountants. The findings suggest that management accountants may lack both awareness of the term and understanding of the nature of expert systems and that they generally do not believe that software can be trusted to make their job easier and improve the consistency of their decision making. It is concluded that a major educational initiative may be required if there is to be any likelihood of a significant change from the current position.

## Introduction

Changes in the way tasks are performed and the nature of the tasks themselves arise from changes in their environment. From a systems viewpoint, tasks are not ‘closed’ systems and they therefore cannot be isolated from their environment. Each is subject to changes in its immediate environment and also to changes in the wider environment in which their immediate environment is situated. Thus, changes in the way that tasks are performed will result from events arising within the organization in which they are performed, and from events arising in the organization’s external environment.

Generally, people and organizations are resistant to change: 'old habits die hard'. After an event occurs from which change may arise (e.g. the development of some new technology), it is often very difficult to foresee what changes may result. It may be ignored – 'I am happy with how I do it now'; it may be absorbed while leaving the ideologies of the individual or the organization unchanged, e.g. a new version of a spreadsheet package would be bought when more copies were required, but existing copies of the previous version would not be replaced; change may occur and the ideologies adjusted; or, where there has been an overall organizational non-reaction, the event may be absorbed by a part of the organization and then spread from within – the 'trojan horse' effect. (Laughlin, 1991).

When change occurs, Carnall (1990, p. 141) suggests that it has five stages:

(1) Denial (why change?)

(2) Defence (stall)

(3) Discarding (adjusting to the change)

(4) Adaptation (implementation)

(5) Internalization (acceptance that the new approach is 'normal')

and it would tend to be a somewhat slow and drawn out process: hard to detect initially, but ultimately obvious.

Changes in the use of IT have been commonplace for many years. Increased efficiency – the IT industry has been improving its fundamental technological cost performance at 30% to 40% compound annually for more than 30 years (Benjamin and Scott Morton, 1988) – has contributed to an enormous growth in the use of IT. This growth can be illustrated by findings such as those of Gurbaxani and Mendelson (1990) who found that data processing expenditure in the US (in 1972 dollars) rose from \$30 billion in 1975 to \$59 billion in 1987 which, allowing for a 30% compound technological cost performance growth factor, bought the performance equivalent of \$1375 billion in 1975: a 45-fold purchased performance growth over the actual 1975 purchase. As an example of a specific item of technology – the spreadsheet – Brancheau and Wetherbe's (1990) study of adoption among 500 professionals working in corporate finance and accounting departments showed a rise in spreadsheet usage of over 900%, from a base of 35 users in 1981 to over 300 in 1987.

Information technology has resulted in an intensification of organizational changes which has caused a speeding up of the rate of change (Hopwood, 1990, p. 12–13). It has reduced the costs of the provision of information. As a result, it is now rational to acquire information when previously it may not have been. That information may provide the opportunity to adopt methods and practices that would previously have appeared irrational. In addition, utilization of increased education opportunities (particularly MBAs) has had the effect of increasing the knowledge and skills of management. These environmental changes could have led to changes in organizational practices. Such changes would have occurred first in those organizations which would have been the first to find that the benefits of obtaining the information deriving from such change exceeded its cost. Thus, when computers appeared it was only the largest companies, those who had the greatest economies of scale, who used them. Then, as costs fell in relation to computational power, more and more companies viewed them as being a cost-effective information source. Consequently, IT-led change would tend to start in the largest companies and then trickle down to smaller and smaller ones until, ultimately, all companies had changed. From an accounting perspective, despite the accountant's inflexible and old-fashioned image, accounting has not stood still. As Hopwood et al. (1990, p. 102) state: 'accounting has not just evolved... (it) has responded in a more positive way to external as well as internal pressures and circumstances, internalizing into itself residues of events and disruption in the contexts in which it operates'.

This article considers accountancy's reaction to IT-led pressure for change, specifically in the context of expert systems within the management accounting sub-domain.

## Management accounting

Accountancy comprises a number of sub-domains including financial accounting, taxation, auditing and management accounting. In contrast to financial accountants who prepare accounting information for external use, management accountants prepare accounting information for internal use. More specifically, as Drury (1988, p. 20) states, management accounting ‘relates to the provision of appropriate information for people within the organization to help them make better decisions.’ It is one of the major activities undertaken by accountants (e.g. in November 1992, over 4000 members of the UK Chartered Institute of Management Accountants (CIMA) were employed in that role in the UK (CIMA Statistics Report, November 1992)). Management accounting information is used for (CIMA, 1991, p. 13):

## The suitability of expert systems to the management accounting sub-domain

To achieve these varied objectives, management accountants perform a wide range of tasks, not only covering many different activities and areas, but also varying considerably in their complexity, degree of structurization, repetitiveness and need for the exercise of judgement. Despite this variability, or possibly because of it, Lin (1988, p. 71) suggests that expert systems ('computer programs which store and interpret human expertise (and which are) used to give advice and reach decisions in the light of evidence given to them in much the same way as human expertise would be consulted' – Jackson, 1992, p. 1) could be used successfully in many areas of management accounting. Lin specifically referred to their potential for transfer pricing (i.e. the pricing of items for sale to other companies/divisions/departments within the same organization), performance evaluation, incentives and compensation schemes, planning and budgeting, product pricing, capital budgeting (i.e. long term investment in plant, equipment, buildings etc.), variance analysis (i.e. the comparison of actual data to budgeted/forecasted data), information systems selection, and training of management accountants. Buckner and Shah (1989) performed a similar analysis with broadly similar, if less specific, conclusions. Böer (1989) cited examples of possible management accounting expert system applications which both confirmed and expanded Lin's 1988 analysis.

These theoretical assessments were all based upon a comparison of the tasks performed by management accountants with a checklist of attributes which tasks may/should possess if they are to be suitable for expert systems development; and, secondly, against a list of factors which, if present, could make them unsuitable. (For examples of these checklists see Prerau, 1985; Barrett and Beerel, 1988; Martin and Oxman, 1988; Young, 1989; Sangster, 1991). Attributes which should exist include the task should not be relatively inconsequential for the organization; there should be a substantial return on the investment, which may or may not be financial; and a source of expertise must be available (not necessarily a human expert). Those that should not be present include: the expertise is hard to define or based on common sense; intended users would not use the system; senior management is not in favour of the development; and funds are not/will not be available to ensure completion and implementation of the system. The analyses of Lin (1988), Böer (1989) and Buckner and Shah (1989) suggest that the scope for management accounting-based expert systems is large, though the organizational ethos would be required to be favourable (a point echoed more generally in the context of accountancy's reaction to new technology by Hopwood (1990) who stated that 'the impact of new technologies on accounting is likely to be mediated and influenced by the organizational and cultural terrains into which the technologies are introduced') and sufficient resources would need to be available for any development to proceed.

This is not to say that, subject to management backing and funding, expert systems are suitable every time one of these management accounting tasks is being performed. There will be many occasions when they will fail the essential attributes tests – e.g. many organizations will devote only a small amount of resources to capital expenditure, or may only undertake such activity on an irregular and infrequent basis. There will also be times when the main area of expertise is not viewed as being within the role of the management accountant – e.g. in some organizations, investigation of variances will be a management accountant's task, in others, a manager's task. [See Böer(1989, p. 8) for a case-based example.] Nevertheless, if an organization seeks quality, efficiency or consistency in a management accounting task, there will be many occasions when the task will meet the defined criteria.

## The suitability of expert systems to the overall accountancy domain

A similar viewpoint concerning the suitability of expert systems exists for the accountancy domain as a whole. In 1985, a study commissioned by the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales to examine how accountants in industry and commerce made use of IT found that over half the accountants surveyed (half of whom worked in industry and half for accountancy firms) believed that expert systems would be commonplace by 1990 (Clarke and Cooper, 1985). In 1986, Edwards and Connell conducted a questionnaire-based survey of 600 organizations (mainly large accountancy firms and the accounting departments of large industrial companies) in the UK, USA and Canada (Edwards and Connell, 1989). Of the 21 UK accounting firms and 40 UK industrial companies who responded, 48% and 55% respectively were involved in expert systems development (pp. 189–90).

Supporting these empirically-based assessments, a number of theoretical analyses were published between 1985 and 1990 all of which affirmed the potential of accountancy-based expert systems (see, e.g. Elliott and Kielich, 1985; O'Leary, 1987; Wilson, 1989; Jancura, 1990). The overall message arising from these analyses is that not only should the technology be in widespread use across all areas of accountancy, but that it is essential that accountants adopt it if they are to remain competitive. As England et al. state (1989, p. 62):

it is no longer a question of whether expert systems will be used in accounting. They are already here. And there is great potential for continued, rapid growth of these systems. Accounting professionals who wish to stay abreast of current developments and remain competitive should plan accordingly.

## The level of adoption of accountancy-based expert systems

Drawing back from the accountancy domain to a business-wide level, the take-up of expert system technology has generally been well below anticipated levels. For example, in 1988, Ovum Ltd with Segal Quince Wicksteed (p. 32) estimated that only about 150 UK companies were seriously developing expert systems. Three years later, when 68000 UK directors and senior managers were contacted under the UK Department of Trade and Industry's Knowledge at Work Initiative (designed to counter the low take-up of expert systems technology) only around 500 operational expert system installations were identified (Touche Ross Management Consultants, 1992). While the wide range of application areas identified included some accountancy-based systems [accounting/financial management (12%), data management (6%) and stock/inventory (1%) (p. 16)], the accountancy domain has clearly been subject to industry and commerce's general overall slowness in adoption of the technology and take-up within the domain is at a level well below that anticipated by Clarke and Cooper (1985) and Edwards and Connell (1989).

Nevertheless, there have been a significant number of reported examples of accountancy-based expert systems (see, e.g. Connell, 1987; Humpert and Holley, 1988; Brown, 1989; Brown and Phillips, 1990). Yet, while intuitively it might be expected that management accounting's apparent suitability as an area for expert systems development would have led to a similar level of development in that area to that attained in many of the other accountancy sub-domains, reports of accountancy-based expert systems contain relatively few in management accounting compared with those in those other areas (e.g. McMahon's 1990 world-wide bibliography of expert systems in accounting, finance, and management contains 151 auditing, 85 financial accounting and 77 taxation references, but only 18 relating to management accounting). There is no explanation in the literature for this apparent development imbalance; and consideration of the various expert systems attribute criteria previously discussed (attributes which the tasks may/should possess if they are to be suitable and factors which, if present, could make them unsuitable) do not suggest any obvious general barrier to the development of management accounting expert systems which would not also apply to these other accountancy areas.

## Suggested explanations for the apparent low level of development of management accounting-based expert systems compared with those in other accountancy areas

## The relative lack of reported management accounting-based expert systems

The apparent sub-domain development imbalance may be due to a far smaller proportion of implemented management accounting expert systems being publicized than, e.g. those in the area of financial accounting. This could be the case if competitive advantage was being protected. However, would companies not wish to maintain also some competitive advantage obtained through the use of expert systems in, e.g. financial accounting? Clearly, there is likely to be more to gain by not revealing systems which enhance the way in which a business is run, as opposed to systems concerned with the preparation of information for external consumption. Nevertheless, a successful expert systems development in any area is, by implication, going to result in more efficient use of resources. If the ‘competitive edge’ argument is valid, it should apply to all successful developments, not just those from management accounting.

It could be suggested that there are likely to be more financial accounting expert systems reported because much of financial accounts preparation is quasi-legal and structured, being required to adopt set formats and contents as laid down by Companies Acts and the regulatory framework stipulated by the professional accountancy bodies. In contrast, management accounting has no set presentation and content rules. However, management accounting is based upon well documented approaches which dictate how information is processed. While the presentation style, complexity and detail differs from organization to organization, the basic procedures adopted follow a broadly similar set of generally applied techniques. In addition, judgement is just as applicable to the application of the regulatory framework by financial accountants as it is to the interpretation of budget variances by management accountants.

## The IT ‘champion’ and other proposed explanations

In 1990, King et al. reported on a three-and-a-half year longitudinal study of 16 UK-based companies in which they considered how IT had impacted upon the role of the management accountant, how that role was changing, and the process by which IT changes take place. They found that IT change was generally an incremental process and that existence of technology did not, in itself, act as a major force for change. What did lead to change was the existence of a proactive individual driving the process—an IT ‘champion’—whose effectiveness depended upon his/her power and position (pp. 249–50). Applied specifically to the area of expert systems, which would suggest that no matter how suitable the technology, it would not be used until those in a position to do so know of it, understand its potential, and believe it to be feasible. Without an expert systems ‘champion’, the technology will not be adopted. If this is to explain the apparent relative non-development of management accounting-based expert systems, not only should there be a general lack of management accountants who believe in the feasibility of the technology, there must also be a great many who do not understand the potential of expert systems, and even more who have not heard of expert systems. If this is found to be the case, comparison of similar characteristics of accountants working in other areas may shed some light on the apparent development imbalance between the sub-domains.

Among the other explanations for the apparent lack of management accounting-based expert systems, Humpert and Holley (1988) suggested that it may be due in part to the unavailability of techniques capable of encapsulating the type of knowledge required. Böer (1989) found that the technology could be ignored if users perceived the related task to be trivial. King and McAulay (1991, p. 141) found that the process of developing an expert system could, in itself, lead to new practices which negated the perceived usefulness of an expert system.

However, while these suggestions all help explain why management accountancy-based expert systems have not been developed as much as may have been expected after allowing for the generally low level of expert systems development to date, they could be just as applicable to the other accountancy areas and do not, in themselves, explain the apparent inter-sub-domain development imbalance.

## The nature of management accounting

Consideration of the characteristics of the management accounting domain may help explain this situation. Differences in organizational structure and the way in which information is channelled through organizations mean that there is a lack of transferability of management accounting-based systems between organizations compared with, e.g. financial accounting-based systems. In addition, management accountants as a group lack any cohesive identity: if two management accountants were asked to describe in detail their tasks and responsibilities their responses would probably be significantly different—even if they were both working in the same management accounting area.

## The impact of IT

Having delivered the potential to adjust the balance between the traditional management accounting functions of score keeping, attention directing and problem solving and generally extend the role of the management accountant into new areas like strategic problem solving, IT integration may also be a contributory factor. As King et al. (1991) state:

advances in technology – have increased the type and quantity of financial and non-financial data collection and its diffusion within organisations. The traditional view of management accounting as the core of an organisation's information system is being challenged and the boundaries of management accounting are being questioned. This raises the problem of how management accountants should respond. Should management accounting be concerned with primarily financial data or should it encompass the totality of the management information system? (p. 294)

and,

A powerful financial controller may initiate IT developments to extend the domain of management accounting within the organisation, while a weak or inexperienced one may witness the contraction of their function as other managers develop skills possessed by the (management) accountants (p. 295).

This shifting emphasis in the role of the management accountant and the resulting lack of agreement about what exactly that role is can only contribute to a limitation on the extent of management accounting-based expert systems developments. The more traditional areas of the management accountant's activities are concerned mainly with numerical concepts rather than symbolic ones and are, consequently, less appropriate areas for expert systems developments. The newer areas of activity, while being more suited to expert systems activity are likely to suffer (in terms of expert systems development) because the management accountant is attempting to establish a permanent right to operate in these areas. As a result, until the management accountant's role has been established, expert system developments in these areas may be perceived as being too high risk.

## Interorganizational differences

Another limiting factor may be the differences in interorganizational ethos and managerial decision-making style which mean that any management accounting-based expert system must be customized to match the specific needs and requirements of the organization in which it is to be used. A few generic expert system packages are available which could be used in the performance of specific management accounting-related tasks. [e.g. Management Advisor (marketed by Palladian Software) enables managers to consider alternative approaches to new product development proposals, evaluate major strategic investments, and consider the consequences of cost-reduction plans and buying and leasing equipment (Feigenbaum et al., 1988, pp. 291–2).] Nonetheless, generally, the nearest thing to an 'off-the-shelf' expert system is an expert system 'shell' – an 'empty' expert system into which the domain-specific rules and relationships need to be entered. A user interface and a reasoning mechanism are provided, making the writing and development easier than if, e.g. a computer language were used. However, compared with entering data into a database or text into a wordprocessor, writing a 'shell'-based expert system is a non-trivial task which may be beyond the capabilities of other than truly computer literate management accountants.

## Other factors

Furthermore, while existing expert systems could often be used as the basis for new ones, the lack of management accounting 'role models' means that organizations do not have access to previously constructed management accounting-based systems (other than their own) and every developer is effectively being required to ‘reinvent the wheel’. This is not the case for those developing expert systems for external use (e.g. audit and tax-based systems which can be duplicated and used in numerous different organizations). Consequently, the relative cost of the development if likely to be significantly higher when management accounting-based expert systems are involved. As a result, there is a far lower likelihood that a proposed management accounting-based expert system would pass a cost/benefit analysis test, than one relating to another of the accountancy sub-domains.

Finally, a potentially significant limiting factor in determining the flexibility and robustness of management accounting-based expert systems is the difficulty a management accountant would have in identifying other suitable management accountants with whom to discuss the expertise involved on other than a very general level. This is due to a combination of organizational differences and the need for secrecy (why help your competitors improve their management accounting performance?) The material benefit of such systems is, consequently, likely to be limited, resulting in their being less likely to be developed than, e.g. taxation-based expert systems.

## The survey

Summarizing this analysis, there appear to be two dimensions involved: the profile of the management accountant and his/her environment, and the comparison of that analysis with a similar analysis of other categories of accountants. Concentrating upon the management accounting dimension, a questionnaire-based survey was carried out in July 1992 of the 275 UK-based members of the Scottish chartered accountants institute whose main area of work (as indicated by them on their annual return to their professional body) was in management accounting. Eighty-six replies were received, representing a response rate of 31.3%. Of these, 11 were unusable for a variety of reasons (one had retired, three's main area of work had changed, and six were received too late to be included in the statistical analysis), leaving 75.

If a management accountant was to consult an expert in a field in which the expert was acknowledged by the management accountant as having equal or superior expertise, the expert's advice would usually be accepted (in the absence of other similarly qualified experts from whom a second opinion could be sought). To be worthwhile, an expert system intended to be treated as if it were such an expert (or ‘consultant’) must be trusted by its users. ## Relationships identified

Spearman Rank Correlation tests found no relationship between respondents' attitude towards the adoption of expert systems software and any of: age, length of time since qualification, perceived confidence with computers, and perceived computer literacy.

A number of significant findings (at the 0.10 level) were found when Mann-Whitney tests were conducted comparing a range of respondent profiles:

those who neither themselves nor whose staff developed computer-based systems indicated greater willingness to use expert systems than the rest;

those who did not develop their own systems indicated greater willingness to use expert systems than those who did; and

those whose staff did not develop computer-based systems indicated greater willingness to use expert systems than those whose staff did.

Thus, while characteristics of the individual (e.g. age, literacy) appear not to affect the attitude towards software of this type, an environment in which all systems used are developed elsewhere appears to create a more favourable attitude towards it.

Respondents who gave a satisfactory definition of expert systems used less types of software and worked in larger organizations than those whose definitions demonstrated a lack of understanding of the term; and, when compared with all other respondents, they were younger, more recently qualified, more computer literate, used more powerful PCs and worked in larger organizations. Using a $\chi^{2}$ test, a significant positive relationship was found between having staff who developed systems and a satisfactory expert system definition.

Finally, while no significant differences were found between those with the authority to adopt expert systems and those without, those with the authority to develop computer-based systems were older and had been qualified longer than those who did not.

## Discussion and conclusions

New technologies can be anything from a resounding success to a complete failure. While the spreadsheet has become truly ubiquitous, and the word processor indispensable, technology designed to assist managers in specific decision making tasks has generally failed to live up to its promise. Failure can occur for a number of reasons; the people involved, the development strategy adopted, the organizational structure and culture, the implementation approach, the management style, the nature of the task. Laudon and Laudon (1991, p. 763) refer to an estimated 75% failure rate among large systems installations and to a US Army study which found 47% of the systems incorporated into nine federal projects were delivered but unused and 19% abandoned or redeveloped. However, these failure aspects have, as yet, only minimal relevance to the area of management accounting-based expert systems. While hearing that an organization had experienced a major failure because it used something called an ‘expert system’ could have a dampening effect upon any desire to find out more about the technology, only once expert systems are known of and understood can the failure factors become truly relevant.

This paper has considered the manner in which organizational change develops and describe the background to the ongoing debate on the low level of reported management accounting-based expert systems. It has presented a number of possible explanations for this situation and described, presented and analysed the results of a questionnaire-based survey of practising management accountants.

The survey produced three major findings. It found that despite the previous forecasts and in spite of recommendations like ‘it is vital that the management accountant understands expert systems and the functional applications of such technology in today’s business environment’ (Akers et al. 1986, p. 34) and considerable publicity for expert systems developed in other accounting domains, not only did only 16% of the respondents have a reasonable understanding of what an expert system is, but over 25% had a mistaken understanding of the term. (This finding must also be relevant to the more general issue of why Clarke and Cooper’s 1985 forecast of the number of accountancy-based expert systems was so greatly overestimated.)

The second major finding of the survey was a general reluctance to trust expert systems software. Almost 60% indicated that they would not use it in the most effective manner. This was even more pronounced among those that were reasonably aware of what expert systems are – 67% of whom indicated that they would not trust software of this type.

The combined effect of these first two findings is clearly significant. As the survey dealt only with management accountants belonging to one of the six UK accountancy bodies, it would be wrong to generalize too much; however, if it were reflected across the profession, only one in 18 management accountants would both know what expert systems are and be willing to trust their output sufficiently to contemplate developing and/or using them.

The final significant finding concerns the incompatible relationship identified between knowing what expert systems are and being in a position to adopt the technology. Those who do not develop their own systems are the most receptive to expert systems technology. They are less aware of expert systems and older than those who do develop their own systems. Unfortunately, it is the older management accountant who tends to have the authority to develop expert systems. As a result, those who are best placed to develop expert systems are not in the best position to do so - not only must they convince themselves that the technology is appropriate; they must also convince their superiors. King et al.'s (1990) suggestion that an 'IT champion' is required if things are to change is clearly particularly relevant to management accounting expert systems.

Within the context of organizational change, while there is some evidence of change (in the form of adoption of expert systems within management accounting) having occurred, in terms of the domain such change is clearly still at Laughlin's fourth reaction level – partial absorption – without, as yet, any evidence of a ‘trojan horse’ effect. Indeed, such is the low level of adoption that it would be more appropriate to say that management accounting-based expert systems are generally at the first of Laughlin's (1991) four reactions to pressures for change – ‘happy with the way things are done at present’. In terms of Carnall's (1990) five stages of change, they are generally at stage one – ‘why change?’ and they certainly will not progress beyond that stage until there is a far greater level of awareness of the technology among management accountants.

Temporary barriers like the effect of the expanding role of the management accountant reported by King et al. (1991) and the lack of authority for younger management accountants to develop systems would be expected to disappear over time. Problems of incorporating expertise as referred to by King and McAulay (1991) can be overcome if the domain expert defines and encodes their own knowledge rather than doing so through a third party. Thanks to the latest generation of 'shells', writing expert system programs, while certainly not trivial, is now well within the capability of any reasonably computer literate management accountant, and the survey results suggest that literacy is at an appropriate level for this to take place.

What will not change without considerable effort is the level of management accountants' awareness, understanding of and attitude to expert systems. Clearly, if this is to be achieved, a major educational effort may be required, possibly along the lines of the 'knowledge at work initiative', but targeted directly at management accountants. Only once management accountants are properly informed and sufficiently exposed to the technology will there be any likelihood that the potential benefits will be appreciated sufficiently for the projections of Clarke and Cooper (1985) to become a reality with respect to the management accounting domain. No matter how suitable the technology, it will not be used until those in a position to do so know of it, understand its potential, and believe it to be feasible.
