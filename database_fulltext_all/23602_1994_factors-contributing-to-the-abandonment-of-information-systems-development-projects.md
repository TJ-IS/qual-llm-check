# Factors contributing to the abandonment of information systems development projects

KWEKU EWUSI-MENSAH and ZBIGNIEW H. PRZASNYSKI
College of Business Administration, Loyola Marymount University, Los Angeles, CA 90045, USA

The study identified several factors as contributing to the abandonment of IS development projects. Organizational-related issues which ranged from senior management involvement to end-user participation in the project development process were the most widespread and dominant of the factors. Organizational issues were also found to influence factors dealing primarily with economic and technological matters in project development.

## Introduction

Every year organizations make substantial investments of money and other resources into developing new information systems to meet their information requirements. Unfortunately, a significant amount of all project development undertaken is never completed or not used if completed (Comptroller General, 1979; Gladden, 1982). In spite of this fact, the abandonment of IS projects under development has not gained much attention in either IS practice or research, although it is an important aspect of the general problem of IS failure. In contrast, however, there have been several studies by researchers dealing with IS failure from the usage and operations viewpoints after a system has been implemented (Lucas, 1975; Ives et al., 1980; Lyytinen, 1987; Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987).

In general, there is a dearth of studies on IS project abandonment in organizations reported in the IS literature. McFarlan (1981) presented examples of attempts to develop information systems in three different large companies which resulted in failure. He claimed to have data indicating such failures are quite widespread in organizations of all sizes world-wide including Fortune 500 companies. Glaser (1984, p. 45) also reports, 'In the computer industry . . . [project] Objectives are missed and schedule and cost targets overrun with distressing regularity and, at times with equally distressing results'. Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) also confirm that IS failure continues in organizations despite the enormous progress and strides made in the development, implementation and usage of IS.

In spite of the studies cited, we still do not, in the IS community at large, fully understand why some IS development projects are successful but a substantial number of them are not. What characterizes and distinguishes the successfully completed projects from the unsuccessful or abandoned ones? What factors generally contribute to the abandonment of some IS development projects?

We characterize IS project abandonment as the perceived inability of the IS development project to meet the requirements or expectations of various combinations of organizational stakeholders. The stakeholders (as far as IS project development is concerned) are those claimants within the organization who have a vested interest in the project and its successful completion or termination if necessary. IS project abandonment is likely to occur whenever any group of stakeholders perceive major potential problems in that project's development which may prevent the project from being successfully completed and implemented. In this work, an IS development project is broadly conceived as a group of interrelated activities undertaken in sequence and/or in parallel by some members of the stakeholder groups with the objective of producing a computer-based system to generate information to satisfy some specific organizational requirements. This study attempts to determine a profile or characteristics of an abandoned IS development project and the factors likely to contribute to the abandonment of such projects. We concur with Lyytinen (1987) that IS project abandonment like IS failure is a 'multidimensional' issue with 'several subcomponents: technical, behavioural, political, etc. He attributes this to the fact that 'IS development is fraught with recurrent problems caused by poor, undisciplined and incomplete development practices'. We further suspect that the conceptual nature of IS project work (Brooks, 1975), the inherent risks associated with successful completion, the varied requirements and the need for proper effective management may all be contributing factors to project abandonment, in spite of the significant progress made in

IS development methodologies and tools since the early days of business computing (see Olle et al., 1988; Modha et al., 1990).

## Abandonment of IS development projects

We believe IS project abandonment occurs whenever management decides, for whatever reasons, to discontinue temporarily or retire permanently a project under development. In an earlier work (Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski, 1991) we identified three types of IS project abandonment that occur in organizations.

(1) Total abandonment, i.e. a complete termination of all activities on the project prior to full implementation.

(2) Substantial abandonment, i.e. a major truncation or simplification of the project to make it radically different from the original specification prior to full implementation.

(3) Partial abandonment, i.e. a reduction of the original scope of the project is made without entailing major or significant changes to the project's original specifications, prior to its full implementation.

While all three types of abandonment occur within organizations, the data from the earlier study cited above, suggest that the most prevalent is the total abandonment case. The authors' prior experience suggests that the investigation of IS development project abandonment presents special problems due to the sensitive nature of the subject matter. Consequently, this paper has a twofold thrust firstly in identifying the factors, indeed the conditions and circumstances, contributing to this type of abandonment and, secondly, in refining and evaluating the research vehicle itself. The paper describes an empirical survey of Fortune 500 companies.

## Related/prior research

The abandonment of projects under development is also discussed in the research and development literature (see, for example, Balanchandra and Raelin, 1980). Several authors have identified a number of project management issues that lead to project failure: staffing and managerial and communicational aspects of project management are consistently cited as critical reasons (e.g. Might and Fischer, 1985; Slevin and Pinto, 1986; Pinto and Slevin, 1987). White (1984), on the other hand, suggests that project failures are rooted in the way participants interact with each other and perceive work-related issues.

Although there are similarities between IS failure and IS project abandonment, the two issues are fundamentally different. IS failure deals with the failure of usage and/or operations of the IS and may be the consequence of failing or dwindling expectations of the implemented systems. On the other hand, the abandonment of an IS development project is concerned with the process of IS development and thus deals with the anticipated or expected failure of the project prior to its full implementation. The abandonment of IS development projects may therefore be characterized as an endemic part of the larger organizational issue of IS failure.

The earliest reference to the issue of ‘MIS development failures’ was by Morgan and Soden (1973) in their attempt to understand the common interrelated factors which may be at the root of such failures. They concluded from their rather small sample that ‘almost all the failures were of management and personnel, rather than technology’. Keider (1984) also reported some preliminary evidence obtained from questioning 100 MIS professionals on the single reason that caused most project failures. He also concluded that ‘although some projects fail because of technology or design problems, [the main reasons] are within the control of the project manager’. In addition, the authors earlier study suggests that IS project abandonment can occur when problems arise in perceiving, analysing, designing or configuring the system objectives, when the technological basis for the system and its behavioural, political or organizational issues directly or indirectly affect ways that could bring the project to a successful completion, within the estimated budget and schedule constraints and/or when organizational environment factors combine to reduce the projects’ expected benefits or increase its expected costs (Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski, 1991). Still, we feel compelled to agree with Staw and Ross (1987) that, in general, project abandonment in itself may be a good and acceptable management practice in that it may prevent further investment of scarce organizational resources in a non-productive venture.

## Research design

The research strategy used in investigating this problem was a survey questionnaire of top computer executives of Fortune 500 Companies in the USA. The survey questionnaire has previously been field tested in a study of companies in the Southern California (mainly Los Angeles and Orange Counties) area with a significant concentration of large IS development projects (Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski, 1991). The sample of senior IS executives was selected from the 1987 Fall directory of top computer executives provided by Applied Computer Research, Inc. (1987). The list of IS executives was cross-matched with the list of 1988 Fortune 500 Companies published by Fortune Magazine (Fortune 500 Companies, 1988) to compile the list of IS executives and their senior technical managers who participated in the study.

The questionnaire with a covering letter explaining the nature of the study and the need for each respondent's participation was mailed to a total of 951 senior IS executives (top computer executives, TCEs) and 503 systems managers in Fortune 500 companies. A total of 82 completed usable responses, that is, those with experience of abandoned IS development projects were received. Table 1 shows the response rate of the organizations by industry classification. The combined rate of return was approximately 5.6%. There may be several explanations for the relatively low response rate: the depth and length of the questionnaire (11 pages) which required at least 30 min to complete fully, there was no prior screening of the companies to determine those with abandoned IS development projects so it is possible that perhaps some of the non-respondents had not experienced abandoned projects in their IS projects portfolio within the preceding 5 years and, furthermore, due to the sensitive nature of the study, the questionnaire intentionally had no identifying characteristics so as to preserve the anonymity and confidentiality of the respondents and their organizations, with the obvious drawback that we could not undertake a follow-up of the non-respondents in an effort to increase the overall response rate. In addition, prior experience suggests that discussion of project abandonment has negative connotations and is therefore not readily or easily discussed, even in a completely anonymous questionnaire (Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski, 1991). This experience is independently confirmed by DeMarco and Lister (1987) who assert that there is a code of silence in the industry which prohibits discussions about systems failures among industry specialists. Finally, several letters were received indicating that it was company policy not to respond to any questionnaires or surveys.

The questionnaire was divided into six sections. The first section characterized the company and its IS department, the second section profiled a 'typical' abandoned IS development project and the next three sections assessed the extent to which the abandoned project may have been the result of economic, technological and organizational issues, respectively. The last section dealt with the issue of post-mortems on the abandoned projects but this will be discussed in a separate paper.

## Respondents' characteristics

As indicated above data were collected from TCEs and systems managers from the responding organization. The intent was to compare the views and perceptions of the two groups with respect to the abandonment of IS development projects. Unfortunately, there was insufficient response from the systems managers group (25 usable responses) to permit such a comparison so the decision was made to combine the data from the two groups for the purposes of the analysis. Hence, the subsequent discussion of the results is based on the combined data from both the TCE group and the systems managers group from all the responding organizations.

The majority of the respondents were from manufacturing organizations (47 of the 57 TCEs and 16 of the 25 systems managers). The range of annual revenues of the respondents' organizations had a minimum of at least \$100 million for eight companies to over \$10 billion for two companies, for the period 1982–1986. For the same period, the approximate annual IS budget ranged from at least \$2 million for 16 companies to over \$100 million for two companies. The number of full-time equivalent personnel in IS ranged from at least ten for 14 respondents to over 1000 for two respondents, with approximately one-third of the respondents reporting values in the ranges of 76–300 IS employees. More than half of the respondents described their IS departments as stable and approximately one-quarter each described their IS department as either growing or shrinking in size.

## Factor analysis of the responses

The central question in this study was to identify what factors contribute to the abandonment of IS development projects. The economic, technological and organizational issues described earlier were considered to be potential contributors to IS project abandonment and therefore the questionnaire was developed accordingly. The rationale for the approach taken in the questionnaire was primarily to provide a mnemonic code to stimulate the memory of the respondents to focus on each of these three specific issues (that is, issues which could be considered or at least perceived to be contributing factors to the abandonment decision). However, a broadly conceived, these three issues (i.e. economic, technological and organizational) do not sufficiently reveal any underlying factors which can be associated with any one category. Taken separately they would not reveal any common underlying interacting factors traceable to any of the three broad classifications. Consequently, in the data analysis it became necessary to examine the three issues together in an effort to shed light on any such common underlying factors.

The data was, therefore, factor analysed as follows.

(1) First, the data on the economic, technological and organizational issues were combined and factor analysed to determine the underlying factors and their possible interaction effects with each other.

(2) Second, due to the low response rate a factor analysis was not appropriate for each of the three data groups (i.e. economic, technological and organizational issues) considered individually. So an analysis was performed combining the responses from two data groups at a time. For example, data on economic and technological factors were combined to determine the specific breakdown of the factor loadings and how they might collectively affect the abandonment decision. Similarly, we examined the combination of economic and organizational data and their collective effect on the abandonment decision. Finally, we looked at the combination of technological and organizational data and their collective influence on project abandonment.

The overall results of these two factor analyses confirmed our suspicion that although for the purposes of data collection it was prudent to help the respondents to focus on one issue at a time (in order to determine the contributing factors to the abandonment decision), the data analysis would reveal any hidden underlying factors that may be present among the three main issues that might otherwise go unrecognized. The factor analysis of the data was thus expected to minimize any possible halo effect which the data collection might have introduced into the study and thus help to focus the results on the collective effects of all the underlying factors – including common interacting factors – contributing to the IS development project abandonment decision.

## Discussion of the results

The significance of the factor analysis results is that they shed light on the variety of underlying factors emerging from economic, technological and organizational issues which can all contribute to the decision to abandon an IS development project. The study examined the reasons and perceptions of top IS executives and systems managers in Fortune 500 companies to determine or ascertain the common underlying factors in IS development project abandonment decisions in their organizations. In the interest of brevity the discussion is based on the factor analysis of the data of economic, technical and organizational issues combined. However, whenever appropriate or warranted, references to the other results (i.e. pairwise consideration of economic, technological and organizational issues) are cited for emphasis.

Of the 12 factors that emerged in this analysis, the premier factor which contributed the most to the abandonment decision was 'escalating project cost and lengthening completion schedules'. This factor was also confirmed to be the major contributor in each of the two pairwise comparisons in which the economic issue was considered. This factor may be partly explained by inadequate IS requirements undertaken prior to the beginning of the project. This is supported by the appearance of the variable D11 (i.e. project personnel underestimated the IS requirements problem) which showed up in both Tables 13 and 19 where organizational and economic issues are jointly considered. Thus, at the root of the abandonment problem is the more fundamental problem of IS requirements failure (see, for example, Davis, 1982).

The second factor which is the next major contributor to project abandonment was the 'lack of appropriate technical infrastructure and expertise' within the organization. A plausible explanation for this factor are the two variables, D12 and D20, which dealt, respectively, with project staff turnover and the project as the first of its kind attempted by the IS/user departments. Thus, the combined effect of inadequate technical infrastructure (i.e. C7) and personnel without appropriate expertise (i.e. C2) to successfully tackle the project may eventually cause the project to be subsequently abandoned.

Factor 3 was that the ‘actual project expenditures and completion schedule were below the estimates at the time of project abandonment’. The analysis consistently showed that both the cost of the completed portion of the project (B2) and the time spent on the project (B9) at the time of abandonment were lower than the estimated values. The negative factor loadings for variables B12, B13 and B6 also serve to confirm this fact. We may thus surmise that abandonment may not only be dictated by 'escalating project cost and time of completion schedules' as explained by factor 1, but also in other instances the decision may be the consequence of other problems associated with the project.

For instance, factor 4 was that ‘technological inadequacies and shortcomings’ may be one of such factors which may cause a project to be abandoned. The factor loadings for this factor were all very high. This factor also showed up in each of the two pairwise comparisons in which technology was considered. Thus, in abandonment decisions technological inadequacies and shortcomings are most likely to feature prominently. This finding indicates that even in situations where the project is below cost and time of completion estimates, once deficiencies in the technological issues of the project are uncovered, which cannot be rectified, the project may eventually be terminated. This may indeed be a prudent decision management should take to safeguard the resources of the organization (see Staw and Ross, 1987).

This raises the point which indicates that once a project loses the support of key management there is a high likelihood that, ceteris paribus, the project may eventually be abandoned. This may be explained by the fact that the loss of key management supportive of the project may be associated with the loss of adequate funding and/or loss of interest on the part of top management to even continue with the project. It is therefore clear that variables D16 and D13 indeed are complementary factors which may exacerbate the situation and thus help speed up the project termination decision. Thus, to forestall such an action on a prospective project, it is important that the sponsors of the project seek a diverse and wide support among the senior management of the organization as insurance against any management turnover and its potentially harmful consequence on the project's success.

The sixth factor we characterized as ‘end-user acquiescence’ with a factor loading of 0.72 for the variable D4 (i.e. end-users expected to accept changes resulting from project). This factor showed up in only one of the pairwise issues analysed (i.e. economic and organizational) but not when the technological and organizational data were combined. There was a significant negative factor loading of -0.60 for D5 dealing with end-user participation in this factor. This seems to suggest that if end-user participation is not sought but is taken for granted end-users will accept whatever the final project outcome is, then potential for conflicts, disagreements and perhaps even outright resistance may arise in the course of the project’s development and this may eventually contribute to abandonment. This explanation is supported by the factor loadings on variables D9 (0.64) and D3 (0.66) dealing with end-user resistance and organizational politics and disagreements, respectively.

Therefore, it may be argued that once senior or key management support is withdrawn then it stands to reason that the project is not viewed favourably by management and, consequently, obtaining resources to continue with it may be an uphill battle which may ultimately sabotage the project.

The next factor, the eighth, concerns ‘end-user conflicts and technical disagreements’ with high factor loadings of 0.83 and 0.67 for the variables D18 and D17, respectively. The same factor also appears in each of the two pairwise analyses of the data in which organizational issues were included. Factor 8 clearly confirms this view and demonstrates the need for both senior management and IS personnel to move swiftly to resolve any conflicts and/or technical disagreements associated with the project. Failure to undertake such action may eventually play a role in the termination of the project.

Factor 9 deals with what we called ‘satisfying existing or emerging technology’, with a high factor loading for C9 (i.e. available software package satisfied most of the project’s objectives) and a moderate factor loading for C6 (i.e. emerging or new technology made the project obsolete). It is obvious that once a project’s funding is terminated there is a high likelihood that the project may eventually be terminated. What is more interesting is determining the underlying causes for the ‘lack of funds’ leading to the project’s eventual termination. In searching for the explanation for this outcome some of the earlier factors identified may provide clues for the emergence of this factor as a contributor to the abandonment of IS development projects. For example, factor 1 states that ‘escalating project cost and lengthening completion schedules’ may be a major contributor to project abandonment. In addition, factor 5 deals with ‘loss of critical personnel and management changes associated with a project’. Both of these factors may be the cause of the project’s lack of funds which eventually leads to the project’s abandonment.

The final factor is based on a single variable (i.e. D19) which we characterize as the ‘consequence of merger/acquisition by another company’. It is almost self-evident that in virtually all cases when a company acquires or merges with another company, changes in management and/or reorganization takes place. These sort of changes may involve a review of current ongoing projects and if those projects are not deemed to be in the overall plans of the acquiring organization then termination is likely to follow. We, therefore, find this factor to be clearly reflective of general management practices in organizations. The 12 factors are summarized in Table 21.

The preceding paragraphs have identified various factors which may contribute to the abandonment of IS development projects. Of the 12 factors, half of them dealt exclusively with organizational issues ranging from senior management involvement to end-user participation in the project development process. Organizational issues also contributed to two other factors dealing with economic and technological issues. This shows how widespread and dominant organizational issues are and how much influence they can generate with respect to the outcome of IS development projects. The remaining two issues – economic and technological – each contributed three factors to the overall total number of factors.

Factor 3 is the only one of the three economic-related factors which indicated that actual project costs and/or time of completion may not necessarily exceed the estimates at the time of abandonment. We find this result instructive in that it demonstrates sound management not to have delayed a project's termination until more organizational resources have been further expended on those projects. This view is supportive of Staw and Ross (1987) who argue that it is sound management practice to 'know when to pull the plug' on a project and thus save the organization's resources.

There are times, though, when escalating project costs and time of completion schedules may be indicative of more fundamental problems facing the project, such as erroneous and/or incomplete requirements analysis, which may cause the project to be abandoned. However, irrespective of the underlying economic factors at work, once funding for the project is discontinued or perhaps even substantially curtailed the project is certain to be terminated.

The technology-related factors all offer convincing evidence of the important role of technology in the successful completion of IS development projects. It can be categorically stated that the organization's senior management should not allow projects to be undertaken for which the organization has not demonstrated sufficient know-how in terms of the availability of the appropriate technical infrastructure within the organization, the availability of the needed technology in the market-place and the expertise within the organization to deal satisfactorily with it. The second dictum, simply put, may be not to tackle any project until a thorough IT search has revealed the non-existence of any satisfactory technology which can be suitably customized, if necessary, to satisfy the project's objectives.

Finally, the remaining six factors dealing with organizational issues, can be broadly grouped into two areas focusing on

(1) management and personnel matters (factors 5, 7 and 12),

(2) end-user-related concerns (factors 6, 8 and 11).

Simply stated, when end-user participation in projects is discouraged and their acquiescence is taken for granted or demanded, it may create the basis for potential future conflicts and possibly also contribute to technical disagreements and eventually to the termination of the project. Numerous IS studies confirm this finding that end-user involvement is necessary to the potential successful completion of IS development projects (see, for example, Robey and Farrow, 1982; Ives and Olson, 1984).

The role of senior management and their commitment to the objectives of the project as well as turnover of personnel critical to the project are at the heart of management and personnel matters. These factors it can be argued are related to some of the factors emerging from the economic and technological issues. For instance, lack of management commitment and low perceptions of the value or worth of the project to the organization may lead to the project's loss of support and with it may come perhaps some loss of funds and eventually the termination of the project. On the other hand, critical personnel losses may rob the project team of a level of technical expertise management may feel is essential for successfully completing the project. This may consequently contribute to the management's lack of interest and commitment to the future of the project (especially when project costs and time of completion are becoming related problems) and thus lead eventually to the project's abandonment.

## Conclusions and suggestions for future work

This study sought to characterize abandoned projects and to identify relevant factors which can be culled from the three broad generalizations of economic, technological and organizational issues contributing to the abandonment of IS development projects. We also sought to identify any underlying interacting factors among the major factors from the three main issues. The 12 factors which emerged provide us with insight as to the possible underlying causes of the abandonment of IS development projects.

The major lesson from the current study is quite clearly that the abandonment of IS project development should not, for the most part, be viewed necessarily as a negative outcome for the organization for at least two reasons: firstly, it prevents further expenditures of resources on projects whose successful outcome may be in doubt and, secondly, it provides the organization with a chance to learn from past mistakes in project development and thus minimize making similar mistakes in the future. Nevertheless, the incidence of abandoned development projects cited by McFarlan (1981) raises serious questions as to how some projects are managed and whether projects selected for development are good candidates for successful completion.

The results reported in this study, though illuminating, are limited in important respects. First, the number of respondents (82 or 5.6%) is relatively low for a study on a subject of this importance to the IS community. The small sample size reflects the ambivalence of IS management in most organizations; they would like to know more, in general, about the causes of project abandonment but, on the other hand, feel uncomfortable in discussing details of such abandonment within their own organizations. Second, all the respondents are IS professionals and, hence, may be potentially biased in their responses to statements which they may feel reflect rather negatively on their role in the development project subsequently abandoned. The views of the other two stakeholder groups – end-users and senior management – are not represented in this study. Third, the results reported here are quite broad in that one cannot determine which factors or combination of factors dominate particular phases of the project development life cycle. Finally, the study concerned itself only with those projects known to have been abandoned in the development phase and no inferences can be made about the factors that contribute to the successful completion of IS development projects.

We hope that with further work we may be able to show, for example, whether economic factors dominate later phases of the project development life cycle, whether technological issues perhaps are more influential during the design or middle phases of the development life cycle and whether organizational concerns, though pervasive throughout the whole project development life cycle, may probably be more dominant in the early phases of project development when issues are being negotiated and clarified among the three major stakeholder groups. Knowledge of such issues and concerns, we feel, will contribute to better overall management of IS development projects and perhaps minimize the incidence of abandoned IS development projects in organizations.

We believe this study was useful, not only from the numerical data it yielded concerning the characteristics of abandoned IS development projects and the factors leading to abandonment, but also in highlighting some of the difficulties in researching this area. DeMarco and Lister's (1987) statement that 'there is an inviolable industry standard that prohibits examining our failures' is certainly true in our experience. Nevertheless, the low response rate came as somewhat of a disappointment to the authors. We believe that this was due to a combination of reasons as discussed earlier. Although considerable effort was expended in the design and execution of the survey to boost the response rate, nevertheless, due to the sensitive nature of the subject matter being investigated we now believe that the survey instrument, sent blind to a target population may not be the method of choice for future investigations of this issue.

Since the issue of IS development project abandonment is of great interest to the IS community, as borne out by unsolicited comments both from the anonymous survey respondents and colleagues, the comparative convenience and low cost of the survey research instrument we believe should be traded for an in-depth case study and interview data-gathering techniques to do justice to this important issue. For future researchers who may investigate the issue we offer some specific suggestions, based on our experience.

(1) Some pre-screening of organizations who have experienced IS development project abandonment and who are prepared to discuss their experiences is necessary. This may lead to the introduction of a potential bias in that only so-called ‘acceptable failures’ may be shared with the IS community. However, with appropriate respect for confidentiality on the part of the researchers as well as their investigative skills this bias can be minimized.

(2) In-depth interviews of all three stakeholders groups (i.e. IS personnel, end-users and senior management) should be preferable to surveys (even to pre-screened organizations).

(3) The sample of organizations should be more widely representative of industries in the general economy as a whole and not, for example, only of Fortune 500 companies which are predominantly in the manufacturing sector.

A logical extension of the study will be to examine both abandoned and completed projects, preferably, in the same organization. The data collection will be of a triangular nature, that is, based on questionnaires, archival data on the abandoned and completed projects and interviews of groups of individuals from the ranks of management, IS personnel and end-users associated with the project(s) in question. It will be instructive to know if the views of senior management and end-user groups will be significantly different from that of IS professionals in attempting to explain what issues or factors might have contributed to the abandonment of IS development projects.
