# A product-process model of innovation in software development

PAUL QUINTAS

Centre for Information and Communication Technologies, Science Policy Research Unit, University of Sussex, Falmer, Brighton, BN1 9RF, UK

Software is acknowledged to be crucial in enabling the widespread diffusion of information technology (IT), but is inadequately dealt with in analyses of technological change and often misrepresented in accounts of IT phenomena. The tools that are used to analyse conventional innovation processes are found to be inappropriate for software. The characteristics of software which present conceptual difficulty, and the features which drive change in the software development process are identified. Three lines of technological development in software are discussed: product diffusion, innovation in the organization and management of the development process, and innovation in technical practice. These reflect currently dominant and emergent trends, in particular the shift away from wholly custom-built towards packaged software; concern with process issues, including quality accreditation; and technical developments including CASE (computer aided software engineering) and object orientation. A framework is proposed which supports analysis of the distinctions and interactions between technical practice and organizational aspects of the development process, and also explains the interdependence between product diffusion and process innovation.

## Introduction

The separation of hardware from software confers on computer systems the defining characteristic of being reprogrammable and thus highly versatile. Exponential decrease in the cost of semiconductors has made hardware platforms cheaper, but this alone does not confer on IT the capability of being the key enabling technology of the late 20th century, as identified by Freeman (1984) and others. Software makes possible the diffusion of IT into every corner of economic and domestic life. However, because of long-standing difficulties in improving productivity within the software development process, and problems with the quality of software products, software has tended to control the pace and scope of IT diffusion. It is remarkable, therefore, that software is often ignored in general accounts of technical change, and misrepresented in studies of IT phenomena.

Software presents analytical and conceptual difficulties primarily because its nature is obscure and inaccessible for the non-technical, and the scope and heterogeneity of its application is already so large as to be effectively unknowable. This latter point results from the fact that software is developed across all sectors of the economy, and indeed the majority of software professionals work outside the IT industry.

Software does not map easily onto conventional models of technological phenomena, nor indeed is it readily accessible through conventional language or tools of analysis. The aim of this paper is to address these conceptual and analytical issues, providing a bridge between the esoteric software domain and conventional analysis of technological change.

The paper focuses on the process by which software is developed. However, in doing so, it is shown that the diffusion of software products interacts with and affects the development process. These product-process interactions form one component of the analysis. The second component explores two aspects of the development process, looking at organizational and technical factors.

The paper is structured as follows. It begins by identifying some of the characteristics of software which separate it from conventional analyses of technological change. This raises questions about the appropriateness of existing indicators and analytical tools. We then develop a framework within which software activity may be analysed. The factors driving change in software are briefly identified. Three trajectories of change in software development are explored: the substitution of products and intermediate products for developer activity; innovation in the organization and management of the development process; and innovation in technical practice. The trends within and interactions between these three trajectories of development are analysed, and conclusions for software development are drawn. Finally, the more general implications for the study of technological innovation processes are briefly discussed.

## Software singularities

It is apparent that software is an atypical technology. Until recently it was regarded in intellectual property law as being equivalent to literary works; its transmission across borders, or theft, is undetectable, and it can be reproduced or indeed plagiarized at virtually zero cost. Familiar concepts such as ‘industrial sector’, ‘research and development’ and ‘production’ have questionable relevance to software. Most importantly, the conventional indicators used to analyse technological activity are likely to mislead if applied uncritically to software. For example, consultants’ reports refer to the ‘software industry’, covering traded software products and services, and in some cases the identifiable software outputs of IT hardware manufacturers (OECD, 1989). Such analyses, based on a notional supply-side industrial sector, are only partial, since the majority of software professionals are not employed in the IT sector, but work throughout all sectors of the economy, including manufacturing, transportation, utilities and the public and private service sectors $^{1}$ . Thus the majority of software activity is ignored largely because its outputs are not generally traded, and reliable data do not exist (OECD, 1989; Miles, 1990).

The software industry therefore captures only a minority of software activity, making a conventional sector-based analysis inappropriate. Software development takes place in all four of Pavitt's widely-quoted sectoral types of innovating firms: science based, specialized suppliers, scale intensive and supplier dominated (Pavitt, 1984). Moreover, service-sector organizations, identified by Pavitt as being supplier dominated firms which 'make only minor contribution to their process or product technology', (p. 356) have, in the case of software, a long history of significant and innovative capability. The UK building societies and banks, for example, employ hundreds of software developers, each firm's IT department significantly outnumbering the employment levels of all but the largest UK software houses. Historically, user organizations have developed a large proportion of software, and made important contributions to software innovation, including the general-purpose operating system (Friedman, 1989).

The concept of innovation is central to analyses of technological change $^{2}$ , but its defining characteristics are somewhat imprecise, and there has been no rigorous analysis of innovation in the software field. Paraphrasing Freeman (1982) an innovation is a new or improved product, process or system; it is invention developed through to practical usefulness. Innovations generally represent incremental progress, most are ‘...one step in a sequence of innovations, within a particular technological regime’. (Metcalfe, 1988, p. 562). In contrast, and more rarely, radical innovations represent discontinuous change in that they could not emerge from incremental development of existing technologies (Freeman, 1982). Diffusion describes the process by which innovations spread to many users. Diffusion generally leads to further innovation as technologies are extended to meet new applications (Rosenberg, 1982; Georghiou et al., 1986).

Historically the most celebrated innovations are those radical technologies that are highly visible, such as penicillin, radio or nylon. However, the decision to include or exclude innovations from any list is somewhat subjective; whilst most scholars agree on the major breakthroughs (e.g. the steam engine, semiconductors) at the other end of the spectrum there is no clear division between incremental innovation and routine product development. The question of the relative novelty of technologies has historically been interlinked with the laws on intellectual property rights (ipr) and especially patent law. The conceptual challenges surrounding software are aptly illustrated by the ongoing controversy over the ownership of software ipr. In the US, until the 1980s, the main protection for software authors was trade secret law $^{3}$ . The 1980 US Software Copyright Act amended copyright law to include software on the basis that it is the expression of ideas. This act did not establish firm guidelines for the scope of software protection, leaving this to the courts to interpret, which led to considerable confusion. US lower courts came to contradictory decisions in two 1980s cases, one ruling that software ‘expressions’ could be protected, the other that expressions were ideas and could not be subject to copyright (Schachter, 1990).

US patent law has shifted ground considerably in the last ten years. In 1981 the US Supreme Court ruled that an industrial process controlled by computer program could be patented where the software was the only original feature. In a watershed 1987 case, a patent was granted to Merill Lynch on a trading system program, opening the doors for further US software patents $^{4}$ .

Subsequently, US courts have begun granting patents for software routines and algorithms (Garfinkel, et al. 1991). By the late 1980s the US Patent Office was granting large numbers of patents (estimated at over 1000 a year) for software routines, algorithms and techniques, mainly awarded to large IT-sector companies. Many developers are critical of this; such routines are considered to be commonly-used and thus subject to prior-art claims; programs may contain thousands of algorithms, and checking for patent infringement is impractical. Indeed, the US Patent Office has no adequate system for classifying and cross checking software algorithms, and has already issued conflicting patents to IBM and Unisys for the same algorithm (Garfinkel et al., 1991). There is also disquiet at the patenting of generic knowledge: 'patenting an algorithm or technique is like patenting a series of musical notes ... then forcing composers to purchase a musical sequence licence' (p. 51). The patent authorities' dilemma over software is symptomatic of the inability of current analyses of technological change to come to terms with technology existing solely as information.

In addition to confusion over the definition and identification of innovative activities, software's unique position with regard to ipr leads to a further problem: an absence of historical patent data. Much analysis of innovative activity conventionally uses proxy indicators, especially R & D expenditure for inputs, and patents for outputs. For reasons discussed above, software has not been included in analyses based on patents. R & D data is of only marginal use in relation to software, for three principal reasons. First, the majority of software activity takes place in user organizations, many of which (especially in the service sector) do not have a tradition of undertaking R & D or accounting for R & D expenditure, or accounting for software activity separately. Second, many software developer firms are small, and small firms tend not to have separately defined R & D departments or roles (Wyatt, 1985). Third, it is not clear that software R & D can be separated from other software development activities; for example, the 1981 OECD Frascati definitions of R & D did not deal with software, but the emphasis on design and operating instructions for non-standard products (OECD, 1981) could be interpreted as including most applications developments. Software developers may therefore claim with some justification that all their activities might be considered to be R & D.

Moreover, the products resulting from software development activities are rarely, if ever, finished. This is the case with both custom-built and traded software, for example word processing packages, where a series of upgraded versions may be made available over time – each of which includes modifications to earlier versions of the product. The term ‘maintenance’ is used to cover all post-release activities, including adaptive, perfective and corrective changes. Maintenance has been found to represent between 40% and 70% of overall software costs (Lientz and Swanson, 1980). These authors found that 42% of all maintenance activity was concerned with the development of enhancements in order to provide functions and facilities required by users. Consequently, it is inappropriate to characterize R & D in software as particular forms of effort which end or are reduced once a product is delivered. Because of this, data relating to resources allocated to R & D are inevitably unreliable.

We should also acknowledge, however, that parallels between software and conventional analyses of technical change have been noted in several respects, as for example in the area of technology transfer (Buxton and Malcolm, 1991), the critical factors for project success (Lockett, 1987) and the diffusion of methodologies (Raghavan and Chand, 1989). Nevertheless, the above brief review of software's distinctive features leads to a picture of analytical complexity and paucity of data. Whilst little can be done about the latter problem in the current paper, it is possible to develop a framework within which technological change in software can be analysed. The model proposed here encompasses the three main current trajectories of technological change in software development. Before looking in detail at each of these, models of conventional product and process interactions are discussed, and following that, we identify the principle drivers for change in software development.

## Product-process models

The product-process dichotomy, whereby the activities involved in developing and making an artefact are separated from the artefact that results, is central to economic theory and to the literature on technical change. So too, the interactions between product and process innovations are the subject of much analysis. Although the main focus of this paper is the software development process, it is clear that innovations within this are interlinked with product changes. The product-process distinction is used here, therefore, as a starting point for analysis of innovation in the development process.

In a much quoted paper which built on Kuznets' (1953) product cycle theory, Utterback and Abernathy (1975) proposed a product-process model which suggested a shifting relationship over time between product and process innovation. In this account, in the early stages of an industry product innovation predominates, with new products being produced for new markets and no dominant design. Processes at this stage are unrefined and labour-intensive. Over time, as the rate of product innovation slows, innovative effort switches to the manufacturing process to increase productivity. In the mature phase, products are standardized, production systems capital-intensive, with process innovation requiring greater investment for decreasing benefits.

This model, whilst conceptually useful, is not found to be generalizable across sectors (Pavitt and Rothwell, 1976). Even the sector formerly regarded as being the classic example of a complete product-process cycle – the automobile industry – which was believed to be mature in the US in the 1950s to 1970s, moved into a new phase of product innovation in the 1980s (Altshuler et al., 1984). Another problem is that the distinction between product and process is a relativistic concept. One firm's product (e.g. a software tool) is another firm's process innovation. Simonetti et al. (1992) point out that the allocation of an innovation to either product or process category varies according to viewpoint. For example, the number of process innovations will appear to rise as a consequence of corporate takeover leading to vertical integration, since transactions will occur within rather than between organizations. The authors' analysis of the SPRU Innovation Database suggests that almost half of the innovations regarded by firms as product innovations are also used within the same firm, and thus may be regarded as process innovations. According to the perspective adopted, 97% of innovations on the database could be regarded as both products or processes (ibid).

Barras (1986) proposes a reverse product cycle to describe the historical progression of innovation in service industries based on the introduction and use of IT systems. Three phases are suggested in which IT is applied by service sector 'user' organizations first to increase internal efficiency, second to improve the quality of existing services and third to support new service provision. The historical transition in this case is thus from process to product innovation. Similarly the Japanese motor industry followed a reverse cycle, moving from process innovation in the 1960s and 1970s to product innovation in the 1980s. Significantly, in the context of the discussion which follows on software innovation, Japanese process innovation focused primarily on organizational and management issues rather than technical change (Altshuler et al., 1984).

As Barras points out, whilst the main thrust of the product-process model emphasises the shifts over time between product and process innovation, the interactions and feedback loops between these are also discussed in some of the literature. As an illustration of interaction, the process innovations introduced by banks to replace paper-based clearing procedures with Electronic Funds Transfer (EFT) made possible the provision of new service products including Automatic Teller Machines (ATMs). Also relevant here is the interaction between innovation and diffusion. Diffusion describes the process by which innovations spread to many users and applications. Rosenberg (1972) has drawn attention to the fact that the diffusion of innovations is accompanied by continuous process improvements and modifications which tailor the innovation to specific needs or improve the performance of the original. Thus the diffusion process generally leads to further innovation as technologies are extended to meet new applications (Georghiou et al., 1986).

## The drivers of change

Developing software to meet the huge variety of tasks that IT systems are applied to is inherently difficult. Software development is a mediating function (Friedman, 1989) bridging between users and IT systems; that is, between complex real world problems and binary logic. After a history spanning five decades, software development remains a curiously pre-industrial craft activity; highly labour and skills-intensive with relatively low levels of capitalization. For most of that history, demand for IT applications has exceeded the supply of software. The problems often referred to as the software bottleneck or crisis focus on two elements: the low rates of productivity improvement in software development, particularly relative to hardware; and the poor quality and reliability of delivered software products. Although these problems are long-standing, they have been exacerbated by the increasing size of systems, heightened demand for complex applications and the shortage of skilled personnel. As hardware capability has increased, user expectations have risen and software systems have become larger, presenting difficulties for the coordination and management of development projects.

High profile system failures, such as the collapse of a telephone network, American Airlines' 1988 loss of \$50 million booking fees, Gulf war Patriot missile failures and continued concern over fly-by-wire aircraft such as the A320 Airbus are the tip of the software quality iceberg. Conservative estimates in the late 1980s put UK business losses due to poor software quality at £500 million a year (Price Waterhouse, 1988).

Not surprisingly, software product quality is particularly difficult to assess. Products are tested against reliability and performance indicators, checked for compliance with the specification, and may be assessed for 'suitability for purpose'. The process of finding and removing errors (debugging) can never be exhaustive; hence the aphoristic statement attributed to Dijkstra: 'Testing can show the presence, but never the absence of errors in software; (Buxton et al., 1969). Errors are generally sought up to a limit imposed by cost or delivery date. Even with maximum effort: "Rather than speak of 'correct' software in the unqualified sense, one should rather speak of reasonably 'believable' software. Rather than say software is error free, the most one can usually say is that the conditions under which the next errors will be manifested have not yet arisen." (Macro and Buxton, 1987).

The demand side factors driving changes in the software development process are thus the demand for new, bigger, more sophisticated applications, for reduced costs, and for improved quality and reliability. Developers seek to meet these demands by increasing productivity and improving quality within the development process. We now turn to the first of three trajectories of technological change in software.

## Product diffusion

From the mid-1980s, sales of software packages have grown faster than the overall expenditure on all traded IT products and services, and the total traded software market in OECD countries (Coopers & Lybrand, 1989; OECD, 1989). This growth reflects two trends: the net growth of IT use focused on IT diffusion into ever smaller organizations, largely based on personal computers (PCs); and the substitution of packages for custom-built systems development, including large and small IT systems development for existing IT user organizations. Since our concern in this paper is with the software development process, we emphasize the latter. We discuss how the diffusion of standard software products and also the use of intermediate products (i.e. the re-use of programs and subsystems) affect the development process.

The displacement of developer activity by bought-in packages is, essentially, the substitution of a standard product for a service, whether the substitution is complete or partial. IT user organizations increasingly purchase packages in preference to developing wholly custom-built applications and systems using either in-house developers or external contractors. This trend started with systems software, followed by applications for standard back-office functions such as payroll and accounts, moving into front office and organization-wide information systems in the 1970s and 1980s. The latter focuses on databases and associated database management systems (dbms) products. These are likely to be only a partial fit with user needs, requiring additions and modifications. Application tools which enable customization of the product to meet the needs of the organization are the fastest growing sector of the software product market (Brady and Quintas, 1991). The productivity benefits of product substitution depend on the extent to which the package fits the needs of the user. The more generic the application task, the more likely a standard package will fit and perform well without expensive modification. For an organization's strategically important systems, a high level of customization may be required to enable a package-based system to deliver competitive advantage.

The distinction between packaged products and custom-built systems is, therefore, increasingly inaccurate as a description of how many large software systems are built. Rather than constructing an entire system from scratch, developers may now opt for hybrid application solutions based on customized standard generic packages. Often, more than one package is integrated, purpose-built software providing the 'glue'. So too, software houses build custom systems by tailoring a generic software kernel (i.e. a system core) to the specific needs of the customer. Therefore, whilst the building blocks of systems are increasing in size and are being commodified, it is not the case that products are simply substituting for custom-build development activity. Use of packages and kernels change the tasks developers do, and demand new skills. Large database installation requires that developers in user organizations acquire expertise through training programmes and learning from product vendors, contractors and consultants who have product-specific knowledge.

In terms of system quality, packaged software suffers from problems as much as custom-built software, but has the advantage that many bugs are likely to be identified by previous users, and quality improved in later versions of the product. With established packages, at minimum, the main shortcomings are well known. However, incorporation of standard products within systems carries the risk that the methods employed in their development may be less rigorous than those used in the custom-built part of the system, and the behaviour of the product cannot be predicted under all circumstances.

As IT systems development becomes increasingly interlinked with strategic planning within the user organization, standard packages must be tailored to the individual and changing needs of the organization. In the case of complex industry-specific applications such as manufacturing process control systems, financial services and credit card operations packages, Brady et al. (1991) show empirically that the organizational models built into such complex products mean that users must either commit significant resources to modifying the package, or make changes in the organization itself. Moreover, software products aimed specifically at supporting process modelling and helping organizations re-engineer their business processes are now reaching the market. The process implications of software product diffusion thus goes beyond the effects on software developers.

Substitution of products for custom-built systems is therefore consistent with the historical pattern identified by Friedman (1989) whereby the locus of systems development activity shifts downstream away from the IT system core (i.e. the hardware and systems software) towards the end-user, in this case based upon standard packages as the central building blocks. Whilst the propensity to develop complete systems in-house is decreasing amongst existing IT users and late adopter organizations, packages by no means herald the end of in-house development. Customization and modification to meet specific requirements remain labour and skills-intensive. Product substitution does, however, suggest significant changes in development practice and skills requirements for both internal developers and contractors.

A further aspect of software products requires mention. Unlike most technologies, there is nothing inherent in the characteristics of software which prevents multiple applications or instances of diffusion of a single product. Software products are not consumed by an instance of implementation, only legal restrictions and added-on software security devices prevent purchasers copying a product an infinite number of times without degradation and at virtually zero cost. The rate of diffusion is therefore controlled not by conventional factors of production and distribution but by artificially-imposed restrictions on multiple use. The real costs of acquisition of a software product thus lie predominantly in the costs of assimilation, which include customization and learning expenditure. Within large user organizations, where customization and learning costs may be shared over a large number of applications of a single software product, the real costs of software product diffusion may be negligible. This is not to say that product vendors are not entitled to protect and recoup their development costs by charging customers over and above the relatively trivial cost of software reproduction and distribution. The point is that there is nothing inherent in the technology which prevents very large scale reproduction and diffusion at very low cost. This suggests that conventional accounts of diffusion do not adequately reflect the nature and characteristics of software.

The following two sections explore two aspects of change in the software development process. The first looks at organizational issues, the second at technical innovation.

## Process organization and management

Non-technical issues in software development – principally project management and the organization of work – have received increasing interest over the last decade. The software developer work culture in the US and Europe has always been dependent on the skills and creativity of the individual, and well into the 1980s the management of developers predominantly relied on responsible autonomy, with direct control of software personnel remaining the exception (Friedman, 1989). The work environment is generally informal and often anarchic: 'Managing software engineers is like herding cats' (Norris and Rigby, 1992). In contrast, there is some evidence that large Japanese software producers moved earlier and much further towards formal control and management of the development process (Cusumano, 1991). Partly because of the increasing size of developer teams, there are now indications that US and European organizations are moving towards more formal management regimes for software development.

We first need to provide a rationale for making the distinction between technical and organizational aspects of the development process. The MIT Management in the 1990s research programme (MIT90) points out the difference between process and procedure:

A process is not the same as a procedure. A business procedure defines the actions and/or decisions that workers must take under various circumstances. Procedures reflect detailed work design and leave workers no discretion to interpret what they should do. In a process the detailed execution of certain tasks may be left to the workers, enabling them to plan the work as they wish. Other tasks require very precise and consistent standards of output, and thus need to be defined in detail as procedures, to be followed in all cases. (ICL, 1990, p. 7).

This paragraph is notable for the clarity with which procedure definition is linked to worker autonomy. However, the terminology may be improved upon because both procedure and process as defined above are aspects of process in the conventional sense, meaning all aspects of processes – e.g. tools, methods, production, work organization and management. In this paper we suggest that the key distinction to be drawn is between work organization denoting management and organizational aspects (i.e. who does what and when issues); and technical practice – the tools and methods used by software developers (i.e. 'how' aspects). As will be seen below, this distinction is not only analytically useful, it does reflect identifiable and institutionalized boundaries between different aspects of software development. However, it is also the case that there are significant interactions between the technical and the organizational.

The distinction drawn above clearly reflects the scope of process accreditation standards such as ISO 9001 and BS 5750 which are concerned with work organization and management rather than techniques. Management issues cover control, documentation, checking and signing-off work packages, in contrast to methodological issues such as techniques, tools and methods employed. Quality accreditation schemes provide a framework within which developers apply their tools and methods. However, other forms of quality regime, introduced at the project, department or enterprise level, may specify procedures in the sense defined in the quote above, in some cases leaving workers no discretion in the techniques to be used.

The drive for ‘quality’ as an organizational principal has spread throughout all economic sectors of the industrialized world, principally following the success of Japanese manufacturers in driving down costs and producing reliable products. Software has been the subject of quality improvement experiments for many years; indeed, some companies are in their second decade of quality management programmes. Friedman (1989) identifies early moves towards quality management. Process standards to facilitate direct management control over systems development were proposed by Brandon in the early 1960s (Brandon, 1963). Friedman notes that the application of such management procedures was constrained by the inability of management to set targets and monitor the software process. Even documentation of the system became established practice only in larger organizations.

In the 1970s the (limited) use of structured programming techniques led to the introduction of more formal process management regimes, such as design and code reviews by peers or superiors. Friedman points out that elements of structured methods were only implemented in a piecemeal fashion, and then only in a limited proportion of establishments (Friedman, 1989). Mumford's 1970s survey (1972) showed that responsible autonomy predominated; the few negative responses about supervision were complaints about 'too little supervision rather than too much' (Mumford, 1972).

Cusumano (1991) describes how large Japanese companies begin introducing formal management structures in the mid 1960s, over the following decades establishing software factories in which development became increasingly subject to formal organization and control. The mid-1970s US SDC Software Factory experiment identified the need to introduce standard, repeatable project management procedures for all phases of the development process. The factory was closed after three years, partly because of resistance by project managers. The general push for quality assurance in all manufacturing sectors, particularly the automobile and consumer goods sectors, stimulated interest in software quality management in the late 1970s/early 1980s, following influential work in Japan by Juran and Deming, and in the US by Crosby. In the mid-1980s Total Quality Management (TQM) programmes were instigated in UK based firms such as ICL and IBM (UK).

Friedman's UK research in the early 1980s suggests that elements of direct supervision of programmers' work, especially checking by supervisors (85% of sample), walkthroughs and reviews (56%), had become fairly common practice (Friedman, 1989). Notably, however, 41% of Friedman's survey subjects still did not require documentation after the completion of any stage in the software process before continuation of the project. Friedman concludes that whilst there was some evidence of tighter project management in many establishments by the early 1980s, this was balanced by a concern to retain staff through a relaxed management style and developer autonomy.

The military have played a key role in developing process quality standards. The NATO AQAP (Allied Quality Assurance Publications) standards series is used by the UK MoD and in many other countries. AQAP 13, published in 1981, is a software oriented supplement to the AQAP 1 general standard. A general-purpose civil UK standard, BS 5750, was derived from MoD standards in 1979. This fed into the international standard ISO 9001 and European standard EN 29001. The UK Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) began encouraging non-military contractor software companies to register for BS 5750 in 1986.

The market provided a further impetus. Major customers such as British Telecom began, from the mid-1980s, to stipulate that their suppliers (of all types of equipment) should have BS 5750 accreditation. Even large suppliers like IBM, which had implemented quality management regimes many years before, then had to obtain BS 5750 accreditation in order to comply. Competition in the market place, heightened by the current recession, has put further pressure on suppliers to demonstrate a commitment to quality. The European standard EN 29001 is likely to be made mandatory for European public sector software contracts, again stimulating demand for accreditation.

The push towards quality standard accreditation is now moving from the leading edge software suppliers to mainstream developers. This is being driven largely by customers. User organizations are introducing quality management regimes across their business activities, and want to extend this into software. The difficulties associated with assessment of software product quality make process quality assurance an attractive indicator of suppliers' capabilities. The current recession, and growing concern over corporate IT expenditure each provide an additional impetus.

Conversely, BS 5750 is regarded by many software suppliers as being a good marketing ploy. In-house developed quality manuals are also becoming commonplace. Manuals are demanded by some customers, and are regarded by suppliers as good public relations. These may go beyond documentation and reporting standards, venturing into recommended techniques and tools. Recently a software interpretation of the general ISO standard (ISO 9001-3) has been developed to bridge the acknowledged gaps between software development and conventional technical activity. The UK DTI launched the TickIT scheme in mid-1991, aimed specifically at accreditation for IT systems supply where software forms a significant part. Standards accreditation is currently the central plank of the DTI software quality initiative.

Management standards provide documented and visible project management, generating large quantities of paper which form an audit trail of who did what, when, and who checked it. They demonstrate a commitment to managing the process, and as such, are welcomed by customers. To what extent development managers see quality checks as instruments of control is open to question. What is clear is that BS 5750 or ISO 9001 conformance does not affect developers' technical practice, and there is no established link between such quality regimes and product quality.

Another important recent development is the process maturity assessment work done at the US Department of Defense (DoD) funded Software Engineering Institute (SEI). US Air Force requirements for an objective measure of the quality of their software suppliers' processes led in 1986 to an SEI project on process assessment. There are two interrelated aspects to the resulting SEI work. The first draws on previous work by Watts Humphrey at IBM, which was in turn based on earlier work by W. Edwards Deming, Joseph Juran, Philip Crosby and "... the principles originally enunciated by W.A. Shewhart at the Bell Telephone Laboratories in the 1930s ..." (Humphrey and Curtis, 1991, p. 46). This is the five-stage maturity framework, now referred to as the Capability Maturity Model (CMM), which uses a detailed questionnaire (first released in 1987) to undertake a process audit within developer organizations (Humphrey, 1989). The CMM is a prescriptive assessment that is intended to help developers improve their processes and move to a higher maturity level. Second, the Software Capability Evaluation (SCE), based on the

CMM, is used by the DoD and other government agencies to evaluate the process capabilities of software suppliers (Bollinger and McGowan, 1991; Humphrey and Curtis, 1991; Rugg, 1993). The central principal of the CMM is that the process must be managed effectively and monitored, and that data on all aspects of the development process are collected and fed back to make improvements. The five stages of the CMM are: 1: Initial, 2: Repeatable, 3: Defined, 4: Managed and 5: Optimizing.

In the SEI model, Level 1 organizations are characterized by their lack of formal project management procedures, basic monitoring and control. They focus on crisis management, and their positive achievements depend on the 'competence and heroics' of key individuals (Paulk et al., 1993, p. 22). 'The initial process level could properly be called ad hoc, and it is often even chaotic.' (Humphrey, 1989, p. 6). Where they do have nominal procedures for planning and tracking projects, such organizations lack mechanisms to ensure their use. Activities that are not perceived to be essential to the delivery of a product are easily abandoned in favour of coding and de-bugging. These problems are symptomatic of the failure of senior management to understand the issues. Areas identified by the CMM for improvement focus on basic project management mechanisms, senior management review and approval of plans, quality assurance independent of project teams, and change control procedures to manage the introduction of changes due to alterations to requirements, or prompted by test results (Humphrey, 1989). The presence or introduction of such process control mechanisms enables an organization to be considered to be at Level 2 – the repeatable process. However, this level of process maturity is limited, since the procedures and expertise derive from the experience gained from previous projects. Level 2 organizations are thus vulnerable when facing new challenges such as the first use of new tools or methods, or the development of new types of products.

The higher maturity levels introduce further process improvements based on rigorous collection of quantitative data which is used to improve the quality of the product, and the development process. Table 1 shows the five maturity levels, the second column summarizing the main characteristics of each. Levels 4 and 5 were entirely theoretical at the time of publication of Humphrey's book (1989) because the SEI had found no developers at these levels (Shandle, 1989). SEI-conducted maturity assessments in the late 1980s indicated that 72% of DoD contractors were at process Level 1. When a non-random sample of commercial software developers were included, 84% were found to be at Level 1, 14% at Level 2, and 2% at Level 3. Hughes Aircraft, for example, were assessed by the SEI in 1987 to be at Level 2, which was considered to be ‘... a very strong base. ... in approximately the 90th percentile of all organizations studied’. (Humphrey et al., 1991). Subsequent SEI assessment of 296 projects at 27 sites, conducted up to April 1991, confirm that over 80% of developers remain at Level 1, a classification which also applies to 88% of individual projects, as shown in Table 2. Note that 2% of projects were found to be at Level 5 (Baumert, 1991). It is important to emphasize that these results only concern organizations with which the SEI has been involved, and it would be reasonable to assume that the average maturity level of developers is significantly lower (indeed, the need to create a minus scale has been humourously suggested by Anthony Finkelstein).

The maturity framework is currently being used in the UK and elsewhere as an assessment scheme within developer organizations. The European Community's ESPRIT R & D project 'Bootstrap' has incorporated the CMM together with ISO 9000–9004 within the bootstrap methodology for assessing the process maturity of developer organizations. In Bootstrap, CMM Level 3 is regarded as qualification for ISO 9000 certification (Raju, 1993).

Experience gained over four years of SEI use of the maturity model, combined with feedback and criticism from software suppliers and reviewers, has led to a revized CMM, first announced in August 1991 (Baumert, 1991). A detailed description of the new CMM Version 1.1 can be found in Paulk et al. (1993). The revisions do not change the overall model structure, but provide more consistent wording, new levels of detail and a revised questionnaire, expanded from 101 to 120 questions. The principal addition is of 18 key process areas relating to the five maturity levels. These are shown in the third column of Table 1. Within these key process areas, 343 key practices are identified. Each of these generates one or more key indicator which in turn formulate the questions incorporated in the revised questionnaire. The most significant methodological change is a shift from assessment based on single number scores to the construction of a key process profile. In this new method, the maturity level of an organization is determined by the highest level at which its profile consistently matches prescribed key process profiles.

How far does the CMM extend into technical practice? Humphrey observes that 'Unfortunately, the highest-priority problems of most software organizations are not technical' (Humphrey, 1989, p. 8). Organizations must put rigorous management processes in place in order to use technology effectively. Out of 101 yes/no questions in the first SEI maturity assessment questionnaire, 16 directly address tools and technology issues, and a further five deal with the acquisition and management of new technologies (Bollinger and McGowan, 1991). The SEI CMM therefore differs from ISO and BS quality accreditation in that it addresses issues of technical practice. However, it concentrates mainly on the procedures by which technology is inserted and supported, and falls short of mandating specific approaches. The revised CMM questionnaire, like the first, does not attempt to assess the technological expertise of the developer organization (Baumert, 1991). An example illustrates how this works. SEI maturity assessment at Hughes Aircraft led to the formalization of the management of software development technology and the appointment of a head of technology transfer (Humphrey et al., 1991). The main recommendations relating to technology were for the establishment of a mechanism ‘... to assess tool needs and effectiveness, develop or acquire automation support ... provide ongoing information on CASE availability and capabilities, and make tools expertise available to the projects.’ (p. 20).

Bollinger and McGowan (1991) criticise the related SEI Software Capability Evaluations for not taking the technological sophistication of developers into account. They argue that the SCE would not distinguish between a developer using automated tools from one using manual methods, assuming the latter had good processes in place. Humphrey and Curtis (1991, p. 46) acknowledge that 'Technology has always been a contentious issue in the maturity model and questionnaire.' They continue:

From the outset, the SEI tried to include a range of technology-related topics. However, none of the experts assembled at several workshops could agree on any but the most rudimentary tools as broadly applicable. One of the strongest contentions, for example, was that no organization should be considered at Level 2 unless it used Ada for all its software work. Until there is a broader consensus on the most effective technologies, we have reluctantly concluded that specific technologies should not be addressed in this framework. (Humphrey and Curtis, 1991, p. 46).

This suggests that the only impediment to including specific, mandated technologies within the orbit of the management of the software development process is the absence of a consensus as to which technologies to pick. Let us now turn to developments in technical practice, before returning to these interactions $^{6}$ .

## Innovation in technical practice

The history of software development is one of continuous search for improved tools and techniques to assist the developer. Technical developments come from commercial system developers, commercial and military R & D laboratories and from academic computer science departments. Here we identify but a few. High level languages such as Fortran (developed by IBM) and Cobol (US government sponsored, with pioneering work done by the US Navy) were developed in the 1950s. Computer-based tools, initially developed by and for individual programmers, then becoming tools sold as products, have been used since the 1950s to support an increasingly large part of the development process. From the 1960s structured programming was promoted by computer scientists such as Dijkstra and Hoare as a more formal and rigorous approach to program design and construction, countering the 'arcane and error-prone craft activity' (Hoare, 1982) of the 'hacker'. The (principally academic) formalist computer science approach advocates the application of mathematics for proving the 'correctness' of programs and for writing logically correct specifications. At the other extreme, fourth generation languages (4GLs) which emerged in the 1970s were prematurely heralded as enabling 'end user programming' because they provide commands that are similar to natural language, and avoid the need for knowledge of deeper level system functions.

'Software engineering' is an emotive term which encompasses a wide range of approaches unified by the concept of turning software development into an engineering discipline. This provocatively begs the question as to what is assumed to be meant by engineering. Software engineering does, for example, generally include the project management aspects of good engineering practice (Sommerville, 1982; Macro and Buxton, 1987). Structured analysis and design methodologies provide rigorous (often theory-based) techniques as well as imposing structure on the project tasks. However, much emphasis is also placed upon software tools and the technical aspects of software engineering. Throughout the 1980s, governments including the UK, US, Japan and the EC supported programmes of research and development in software engineering technologies, with the majority of the effort going into tools and techniques rather than into the organizational aspects of software development (Quintas, 1991a).

Computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools are available to support all aspects of development; upper CASE is focused on analysis and design, lower CASE on programming and testing. Integrated project support environments (IPSEs) and integrated tools (i-CASE) aim to provide seamless support for the whole software lifecycle. These generally have a range of integrated tools and a central database or repository which support teams of developers, providing project management, change and version control, and a record of project history. Their effect is to shift effort away from programming and testing towards user requirements analysis and specification.

The most advanced i-CASE tools and IPSEs are based on structured methodologies, and support re-use of specifications as well as code.

Whilst CASE tool vendors often claim that large productivity increases result from CASE use, more circumspect analysis suggests that these are exaggerated. One recent three year study of 45 companies across ten industries showed average productivity gains of 9–12%, and maximum gains of 25% (Myers, 1992). This suggests that the high cost of CASE tools and support must be justified in terms of quality improvements and speed to market, rather than simply on a productivity basis (Reifer, quoted in Myers, 1992).

Prototyping techniques enable developers to present pilot versions of a system to the user at an early stage in order to demonstrate and refine the system characteristics, reducing the likelihood of misinterpretations at the specification stage. Prototyping is an iterative process which results in a development history that diverges from the traditional linear lifecycle model.

Object-oriented design and programming is emerging as perhaps the most significant technical systems development innovation of the 1980s and early 1990s. Object-orientation (OO) has roots in the Simula language developed in the 1960s, and the first fully object-oriented language – Smalltalk – developed at Xerox PARC in the 1970s. Innovative technologies such as the Apple Macintosh operating system were developed using object oriented principles. Bell Laboratories' C++ language is now taking object orientation into commercial development, and OO features are being added to products such as data base management systems. Elements of the object orientation approach correspond to conventional best structured design and programming practice. However, key features of OO, including the encapsulation of data and procedures within objects, the idea of classes of objects which have similar characteristics, the concept of inheritance of characteristics between generations, and the primary concern with real-world phenomena rather than with the way the machine processes data, taken together suggest a revolutionary approach. Object orientation inherently supports the re-use of objects, which has great potential for productivity gains.

How do technical innovations affect the organization and cultural aspects of software development? Object-oriented design and programming techniques are based on a novel conceptual approach to system construction. Whilst this represents technical and intellectual challenges, object-orientation does not appear to be aimed at changing the developer culture or reducing developer autonomy. However, it does render irrelevant the procedural, project-based models of development such as the waterfall model. Stand-alone, product-based tools such as 4GLs and individual CASE tools affect technical practice, but do not generally affect work organization. In contrast, software engineering approaches such as structured methodologies, IPSEs and i-CASE have implications for both technical practice and the organizational aspects of software development.

Elswhere (Quintas, 1991a; b) we discuss the implications of advanced software engineering, showing how tools and methods affect the degree of control and autonomy exercised by software personnel, not only over technical choices, but also over the organization of work. With the introduction of software engineering based on rigorous methods, techniques become prescribed, the process regulated and managed. This suggests implications for developers' skills, careers, and work autonomy, and indeed social factors such as these may control the pace of adoption of software engineering tools and methods. Similarly, Raghavan and Chand (1989) identify a number of factors which inhibit the diffusion of structured design methods such as Yourdon and Jackson methodologies. These include complexity in use, incompatibility with existing practice, and threat to developer creativity. Developer scepticism and resistance to such technologies is therefore not surprising, although the lack of provision for addressing these issues in government software engineering programmes in the 1980s perhaps is.

## Product – process interaction in software development

We have seen that there are useful distinctions to be made and interactions to be emphasized between the three trajectories explored above. Product diffusion affects the software development process, driving changes in technical practices through the substitution of bought-in products for custom development activity. Internally generated software products may be used in-house as process innovations (e.g. tools), intermediate products (e.g. a software house's system kernel) or traded externally as commodities. The growing interest in re-use of software designs, specifications and programs means that internal processes are being embodied in intermediate products or components, to be incorporated again and again into new products. Object-oriented techniques enable libraries of objects to be stored and used in a wide range of systems. At various levels developers are increasing the size of the building-blocks from which systems are constructed, transforming the development process.

We have explored two aspects of the software development process: management or organizational issues, and technical practice. Quality accreditation schemes focus on the management of the process and do not affect technical practice. They address who, what and when issues, leaving developers to decide how. In contrast, the SEI Capability Maturity Model includes reference to the management of technology, although at present falls short of mandating specific languages, tools and methods. Innovation in technical practice may also prompt changes in the organization and management of the development process. Object orientation renders the traditional development lifecycle redundant, suggesting significant organizational changes, but its impact on developer autonomy seems unlikely to amount to a culture change. Structured methodologies, IPSEs and integrated tools based on methodologies have significant impacts on work organization by enforcing strict management practices.

Figure 2 illustrates some of the main product-process interactions in software development discussed in this paper. It shows that the outputs of that process, generic products (e.g. databases), intermediate products (objects, kernels), tools and methods all feed back into, and to varying degrees transform, aspects of development practice. Thus product diffusion inevitably demands process changes. The reverse is also true: object-oriented design and programming techniques leads to re-usable components which feed into new products. Rigorous development methodologies are expected to lead to qualitatively different software, in terms of suitability, performance and reliability. A currently emergent example of product-process interaction is the graphical user interface (GUI) which, to the PC user, is a useful product, and to the developer, is a new kind of programming environment. Increasing use of object oriented technology surrounding GUIs will continue to transform the process of developing applications. The least certain link is that between organizational innovation and product quality. Process quality standards such as BS 5750 do not affect the content of the task. However, it is clear that process standards do affect worker autonomy in relation to project organization and management.

## Conclusions

Software is arguably the most significant and conceptually challenging technology of the current era. The characteristics of software present particular difficulties for the analysis of technological change. On the one hand this confirms the unique features of software; on the other it suggests that conventional preoccupations with analytical tools such as R & D and patent data require re-thinking. Basic concepts such as the notion of a supply side or capital goods sector which produces technology for a recipient demand side are quite simply misleading. Interactions between products and processes discussed in this paper point to the need for a critical approach to these interactions based on disaggregations of both concepts.

Technical innovation has continued both within the software development process (e.g. new tools, methods and languages), and in terms of product innovation. Whilst there is evidence of packaged products and intermediate products substituting for developer (process) activity, there is little indication that this represents a shift in the rate or quality of innovative performance from process to product innovation. On the contrary, process innovations such as object orientation are beginning to have profound effects on software products, such as graphical user interfaces and databases. Conversely, we have seen that software products such as databases carry with them the need for new skills acquisition and may impose new ways of developing software and systems, thus products have process implications.

The discussion of software packages suggests a need to re-examine the concepts of product and diffusion in the software field. We have observed that software products are not consumed by an instance of implementation, that reproduction costs are close to zero, and therefore that is nothing inherent in software which prevents multiple applications or instances of diffusion of a single product. The rate of diffusion is therefore controlled not by conventional factors of production and distribution but by artificially-imposed restrictions on multiple use, as well, of course, as the cost of assimilation.

We have now seen how the two aspects of process – technical practice and organization and management – can be regarded as distinct but interrelated elements. The previous section of this paper summarizes the interactions, illustrating how particular technical approaches affect work organization more than others. So too, process quality management regimes may, as is the case with BS 5750 and ISO 9001, deal with organizational issues but do not directly affect technical practice. The SEI maturity framework and many in-house quality programmes do, however, venture into the area of tools, methods and languages.

The analytical distinction adopted here, between organizational issues and technical practice, raises questions concerning management control and labour process issues. It leads, for example, to an important distinction between control over work organization, and control over technical practice. The relative impacts of different process changes on both of these control-autonomy issues (and indeed the relationship between such process changes) and the key question of the quality of the software products that result, are important areas for research.
