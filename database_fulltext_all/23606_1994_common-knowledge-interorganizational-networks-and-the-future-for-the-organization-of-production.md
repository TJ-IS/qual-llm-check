# Common knowledge, interorganizational networks and the future for the organization of production

MARK BORMAN

Department of Management Science, University of Strathclyde, Glasgow GI 1XH, UK

This paper seeks to investigate the potential for interorganizational networks (IONs) to facilitate a challenge to the current hegemony of integrated production. From a transaction cost perspective the choice between market and hierarchical modes of production depends upon their relative coordination costs. The argument developed here is that uncertainty has precipitated market failure and the consequent rise of large, integrated concerns. IONs, however, by facilitating the cost-effective diffusion of information can reduce the coordination costs associated with the market place and permit the deintegration of production. The greatest benefits are held to be realized where the development of common knowledge between connected trading partners permits the 'free flow' of information. Supporting empirical work consists of a comprehensive survey of the uptake of IONs in the Scottish electronics industry and the development of case studies in a variety of companies highlighting the specific processes at work. Tentative conclusions indicate that while IONs do represent an opportunity for the emergence of new, more collaborative modes of production such an outcome is by no means assured.

'changes consist primarily in a relative decline in the importance of Fordist mass production and an enormous expansion of manufacturing activities based on less rigid and more highly adaptable (i.e., flexible) technological and institutional structures' (Scott, 1988, p. 171).

## Introduction

It can be argued that the existence of transaction costs is a prime contributor to inefficient production. This paper proposes that the introduction of interorganizational networks (IONs) allows for the abatement of much of this inefficiency. Of particular interest is the potential for a change in the nature of the boundaries between firms and a shift in the allocation of functions. Outsourcing of necessary but peripheral functions becomes a possibility as does the general decomposition of firm-based activity. The facilitator in this process is ‘common knowledge’. The argument proposed is that it is the development and sharing of common knowledge between companies made possible by IONs that allows for both the scale and scope of interfirm activity to be extended.

In order to assess the impact of the introduction of IONs it is necessary to examine the precise characteristics of such systems and attempt to quantify changes in interfirm relationships $^{1}$ . This paper therefore while contributing to the conceptual debate seeks also to present the findings of current empirical research. To this end a survey of the Scottish electronics industry charts the uptake and impact of a broad spectrum of IONs in what is generally described as a pivotal sector of the economy (NEDC, 1983; OECD, 1989) while the development of a number of new case studies highlights the specific processes at work and the factors – such as technical and organizational characteristics – that serve to curb or enhance the influence of IONs.

The remainder of the paper is divided into four sections. The first develops the underlying theoretical perspective while the second is a description of the methodological approach adopted. Following this are the results of the empirical work to date while the final section presents some initial conclusions.

## Theoretical perspective

Two primary economic theories concerning the organization of production are the neoclassical theory of the firm (Machlup, 1967) and the transaction cost theory of the firm (Williamson, 1986a). These theories are applicable under different sets of conditions – namely perfect and imperfect competition – and are concerned with production and coordination costs, respectively. The two theories are not inherently incompatible and it is possible to construct a hybrid theory which considers the role of both production and coordination costs, the relative emphasis being dependent upon the prevalent market conditions. Under the assumptions of perfect competition there are no costs associated with market exchanges and goods are transferred between parties at prices that reflect production costs. In such circumstances the firm is considered merely as a production function combining inputs into outputs and the economy is perfectly efficient. The relaxation of the assumption of costless exchange and the introduction of 'transaction costs' has important ramifications with respect to the possibility of achieving purely efficient production. While under the assumptions of perfect competition it may safely be assumed that activities can be distributed between the firm and the market at the lowest level thus ensuring the best possible allocation of production this is not the case with transaction costs present. The incidence of transaction costs means that it is not possible to be concerned with individual activities but rather only with amalgamations.

A common assumption in economics is that an activity is only internalized within a firm if there is some kind of market failure. At the optimum, specialization implies that independent firms will concentrate precisely upon those activities in which they exhibit some form of competitive advantage. In such circumstances there will be substantial value added if the 'components' so produced are combined with one another to produce a higher level product. Thus, components are brought together via the market by specialized coordinators or assemblers into 'final' products. Inefficient production structures however can be seen to be maintained because transaction costs restrict the breakdown of production and its consequent coordination via the market. Traditional transaction cost analysis suggests that in some instances the promulgation of markets is being deterred by the specific nature of the investment that is needed for production (Williamson, 1986b).

The alternative developed here is that it is not just the specific nature of an asset that is important but also the level of uncertainty, for it is uncertainty that permits opportunism. It is difficult if conditions cannot be predicted with certainty for specialization and its associated benefits to be achieved within the market. With asset specificity – whereby an asset has very little value in alternative use – and uncertainty – whereby it is not possible to fully specify a contract – there is the possibility of opportunism if activity is coordinated by the market. It is a relatively simple matter for one of the parties to a transaction to reinterpret the contract in their favour once the other has invested in highly specific assets. However by incorporating the whole productive process within a hierarchical structure – the firm – uncertainty and thus opportunism between the various production stages is eliminated or at least reduced. Typically though, there is a trade-off between reducing uncertainty and maximizing efficiency. A degree of efficiency is inevitably sacrificed in enlarging the unit of specialization (Johnston and Lawrence, 1988; Bowersox, 1990; Prahalad and Hamel, 1990). Under such circumstances that specialization that can occur is centred around particular products or families of products. By focusing attention on asset specificity and taking uncertainty as a given Williamson (1981) misses the significance of the possibility that the reduction of uncertainty can serve to reduce the consequences of asset specificity.

The generation and communication of information have a pivotal role to play in the reduction of uncertainty. After all, by definition, given perfect information there is no uncertainty. As such it is argued that common knowledge and its antithesis – asymmetrical information – play a crucial role in determining the extent to which production proceeds efficiently.

the critical impact of information on the optimal allocation of risk bearing is not merely its presence or absence but its inequality among economic agents (Arrow, 1969, p. 65).

Common knowledge can be seen to exist where all parties to an arrangement are in possession of the same information and attribute a common meaning to that information. Asymmetric information or information impactedness exists when the ‘true underlying circumstances relevant to the transaction or related set of transactions, are known to one or more parties but cannot be costlessly discerned by or displayed for others’ (Williamson, 1975, p. 31). Common knowledge, by functioning as a conduit between distinct organizational information sets, is a powerful construct which eases computations and communications between firms (Cremer, 1990, p. 55). It can be seen to have a number of component parts

(1) Knowledge of facts.

(2) Knowledge of the same facts.

(3) Same knowledge of facts.

(4) Knowledge of rules.

(5) Knowledge of language.

(Borman et al., 1992, p. 6).

It is argued here that it is by influencing the nature of common knowledge and permitting interorganizational leaning that IONs affect the nature of interorganizational activity. IONs and the information flows they support, by permitting a routinization and codification of decision-making processes, can be seen to contribute to the reduction of uncertainty and the interlacing of organizational processes and thus provide an infrastructure for the development of common knowledge across organizations.

What distinguishes such technologies is that they institute systems and as such are shaped through a process of iteration between the organisation and the technology. Thus the adoption and widespread use of such technologies can bring about, and may in fact be dependent upon, organisational change. In effect, the adoption of computer networks and their intersection with the existing transactional structure of the firm provides a set of heuristics with which to reconceptualise this transactional structure and thus deliver organisational change (Capello and Williams, 1992, p. 351).

Common knowledge facilitates the re-emergence of market-based activity and demands a reassessment of conventional views of the firm. Effective communication across firm boundaries permits firms to focus more precisely upon those activities in which they exhibit some distinctive competence. This shift in the unit of specialization from the product to the function when extended across the economy as a whole can be seen to represent an increase in overall productive efficiency. Furthermore, it permits the development of skills that are applicable across the whole industrial spectrum thus freeing company success from the fortunes of individual or closely related sets of industries. This move toward functional specialization questions current understandings of the firm not only in terms of definition but also with respect to the appropriateness of industrial and regulatory policy.

readers should keep in mind that the ‘borderless’ idea represents a fundamental paradigm shift in our conception of the modern organization. We are moving from a ‘pre-Copernican’ business world, where the individual company is at the center of the universe to a far more fluid ‘post-Copernican’ environment where each company is but one point in a extended network of equals (Peters, 1991, p. 98).

When writing about firms, markets and even non-market-non-hierarchical institutions reliance can be placed upon legal distinctions and references to structures that may lack credence. While the firms and markets of today are increasingly seen to be fluid in that they can be broken down and reconstructed into new forms the essential characteristics appear to be sacrosanct. Size and structures may be varied but the principle of being able to characterize firms in terms of such attributes appears to be sacrosanct. The amorphous ‘functional’ firm seems to transcend and render inappropriate many of the guidelines that are used to delimit firms and distinguish firms from markets.

## Methodology

A criticism that can be levelled at work in the field of information systems and organizational activity is that it often appears to be building castles in the air with many proposed theories remaining unsupported by empirical work. Furthermore, it appears that much of the investigation that does take place remains firmly wedded to the case study approach. While this is valuable in that it highlights what can be achieved a major failing is that it implicitly points to a widespread diffusion of IONs and yet consistently relates to the same small groups such as American Airlines and American Hospital Supplies – some of which have been in operation for 10 or more years. Systems such as Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) are portrayed as heralding the dawn of a new era – a new era that is a long time coming. EDI as a technology is over 25 years old (Pederson, 1993) and may still not be in widespread let alone universal use. Where attempts have been made to quantify the diffusion of IONs across the economy they have typically restricted their coverage to very large companies or very specific technologies. This paper seeks to differentiate itself by the inclusion of a comprehensive industry-wide survey and a number of associated case studies that seek to uncover the actual uses to which IONs are put. The overall objectives of the two strands of empirical work then are to chart the diffusion of IONs, to determine organizational policy towards them and to examine their actual role within the organization.

## Diffusion of IONs

Given the heterogeneous nature of information it becomes apparent that no single ION will be capable of meeting all the different information needs of a given organization. Different applications often have incompatible requirements in areas such as security and coverage. There will be different levels and characteristics to any common knowledge developed between organizations dependent upon the functionality and, more importantly, the limitations of any given ION. There are undoubtedly a very wide number of IONs in existence if one considers them from an application perspective, drawing distinctions, for example, between banking and travel reservation systems. The focus of this research, however, is on the functionality embedded within an ION and as such a distinction will be made between three main classes of ION – electronic data interchange, electronic mail and structured external information sources $^{2}$ – which differ primarily in the nature of the information that is transferred.

## Organizational policy

It is useful to examine the extent to which IONs function as a straightforward substitution for alternative traditional communications media since this determines in part the possibilities for organizational change. IONs which offer broadly similar capabilities may generate radically differing outcomes depending upon the particular circumstances surrounding their introduction. In the context of this paper innovative companies are those that have sought to use IONs not to perpetuate existing communication structures but to radically restructure them. In such companies IONs are not considered in comparative terms with what has been achieved historically but are looked to for the new and innovative opportunities that they offer.

## Information flows

While the technical and organizational aspects of an interorganizational network do impinge upon the potentialities of the network acting to restrict or enhance them accordingly it is the actual information that flows over the network that is of import from the perspective of developing common knowledge and restructuring production. It is thus necessary to look beyond the technological system itself to examine the nature of the information flows supported by the network.

The work of Mingers (1992) and Liebenau and Backhouse (1990) has incorporated the field of semiotics into the study of computer-mediated information systems. Semiotics provides for the disaggregation of communication into its primary components, namely empirics, syntactics, semantics and pragmatics.

Communication consists of elements which we can analyze in terms of a continuum from context, through meaning, grammar and code (Liebenau and Backhouse, 1990, p. 11).

It is argued here that the focus of work in the field has to date lain with the act of passing information between trading partners rather than with the actual content and use of that information.

Within the framework of objectives as outlined above the electronics industry in Scotland was chosen as the specific area of study for a number of reasons.

(1) The European industry has experienced a growth rate of approximately 15% per annum in the 1980s, a figure well in excess of the GDP growth rate and has now caught up with other major European Community industries such as the chemical industry and the motor industry. Between 1984 and 1989, for example, the turnover for the industry as a whole more than doubled rising from ECU 55 bn to ECU 130 bn.

(2) Despite the overall trade deficit within the UK the Scottish Electronics industry is significant at a European level. For example, Scottish Enterprise data estimated that by July 1993 Scotland would be producing $35\%$ of Europe's PCs (Financial Times, 1993).

(3) From a national perspective the vitality of the electronics industry is of importance to Scotland as a whole. In total the Scottish electronics industry employs 45 000 people and in 1991 accounted for

$13\%$ of Scotland's employment in manufacturing and $21\%$ of its manufactured output (Financial Times, 1993).

## Results

## Survey

The population upon which the survey is based corresponds to the Scottish Enterprise Electronics and Support Companies in Scotland directory (Scotland Enterprise, 1991). This contains details of 471 electronics and support companies operating in Scotland as of 1991 $^{3}$ . This particular definition of the industry was chosen as opposed to any other corresponding more narrowly to sector codes in order to capture as much of the potential trading milieu as possible. Given that it is trading relationships that are of interest it is self-defeating to focus on a single element of the industry particularly where as in the case of electronics that industry is increasingly interconnected.

In the current situation the dividing lines between component and equipment makers may have become progressively more blurred. If we add the assembly subcontractors and makers of modular parts to the equation, today's industry becomes even less definable. (Boswell, 1993).

A random sample of 74 sites representing over 15% of the population was selected $^{4}$ . Fifty of these subsequently participated in a telephone survey during March 1993. In each instance the person spoken to was the person in charge of information technology at that particular site and so can be deemed to have most knowledge about its operations. Of the remaining 24, two refused to answer any questions, 16 had the phone disconnected and six were uncontactable for reasons ranging from holidays to illness.

Of the companies contacted 32% had an external computer link of some description $^{5}$ (see Figure 1) and 18% had two or more, the most popular combination being electronic mail and an information source of some description. At a prima facie level these findings are interesting in a number of respects. Firstly, given that the survey covers the full range of companies in the industry they show a relatively high uptake of the technology. Since over 70% of companies in the population have fewer than 50 employees (see Appendix 1) IONs would seem not to be confined to just the largest firms. Secondly, the results suggest that the overwhelming emphasis in the literature on EDI may be somewhat misplaced since only one in five sites with IONs classify them as EDI $^{6}$ . Thirdly, companies appear to appreciate that a single class of link can only play a limited role and are installing multiple types of IONs to support the different aspects of the trading cycle.

With respect to whether IONs are used by companies in an upstream or a downstream direction of the ION a majority, 81%, of sites with IONs had connections with a supplier of goods or services to the company while only 38% had links to customers. Concerning changes in trading patterns amongst sites that have implemented external computer linkages, 21% said that the number of trading partners had increased while 71% remarked that there had been no change. In a similar vein all companies said that the linkages were in effect solely being used to support established business by, for example, operating as an extra service, rather than to help the company generate new business opportunities. Sixty-nine per cent of companies see IONs as facilitating close, working relations with trading partners through fostering a more timely, accurate and appropriate transfer of information. This finding appears to be borne out if the reasoning behind the introduction of IONs is examined (see Figure 2). Only 27% of the companies considered reduced cost to be a very important consideration while 86% thought the availability of more information and improved quality was.

Taken together these findings tend to suggest that while companies are not seeking pure efficiency gains they may be looking to alter trading relationships within the existing framework rather than to break out and instigate a radical transformation.

## Case study 1: Computer Co. $^{8}$

Computer Co., a world-wide company, provides a full range of desktop, client/server, production and mainframe systems for multivendor computing environments. The company is currently experiencing trading difficulties, a reflection of the degree of competition in the sector and the pace of technological change. While in 1992 sales revenues were \$13.9 bn the company recorded a loss in excess of \$2.5 bn. Costs including research, sales and administrative costs are rising and the company sees a need to realign and streamline its business practices and develop a focused investment programme if it is to maintain a competitive position in the world market-place.

The Computer Co. site at Ayr is primarily concerned with the production of small and mid-range systems and has recently benefited from the ongoing rationalization within the company which saw it take over workstation production that was previously performed at Galway, Ireland. It is necessary to draw a distinction between communications with suppliers and those with customers. The primary difference is that on the customer side the Ayr site deals with sales subsidiaries of Computer Co. while suppliers are predominantly independent companies.

## Case study 2: Camera Co.

Camera Co. supplies a variety of products primarily in the instant image recording field including instant photographic cameras and films, electronic imaging recording devices, conventional films and light polarizing filters and lenses. The company had sales of \$2.15 bn in 1992 and reported a profit of \$213 m. In the 1990s Camera Co.'s policy is to move outwith its traditional markets and compete throughout the 'imaging industry'. Currently the company is active in less than 20% of this \$155 bn industry.

Camera Co.'s manufacturing plant at Renton produces a broad range of goods for the corporation – cameras, films, lenses and X-ray equipment. Approximately 80% of the Renton site' suppliers are internal to the Camera Co. group though there are also between ten and 12 major external suppliers. These suppliers are evaluated continuously with respect to a number of performance measures such as quality and delivery.

## Case study 3: Systems Co.

Systems Co. is a small, independent Scottish company based 10 miles south east of Galashiels. The company designs and produces hardware and software peripheral equipment (particularly for games consoles) had a turnover of £218k in 1991 and declared profits of £10k.

There are two strands to the company – commercial/retail and development. The former involves the production and sale of products directly to the market-place while the latter essentially concerns the production of software or hardware under contract.

The case studies revealed a number of themes that appear to be limiting and shaping the application of IONs.

## Limitations

At both Computer Co. and in particular Camera Co. intracorporation communications are viewed as unproblematic and transparent because of the prevalence of standardized operating procedures and technologies. At Camera Co. the adoption of EDI for communications between divisions was a natural migration from the pre-existing paper-based system given the corporation wide use of a standard materials requirements planning (MRP) package that came complete with in-built communications facilities. The move was further simplified by the existence within Camera Co. of a comprehensive internal communications infrastructure and a common informational structure, for example, there is a common part numbering system throughout Camera Co.

When it comes to external communications and in particular EDI which is viewed as holding most potential, evidence suggests that there are two main constraining factors: cost and technical inadequacies.

On the technical side the issues are reliability and problems associated with the diversity of incompatible standards and networks. Computer Co., for example, has numerous suppliers connected to different third-party EDI suppliers. Yet as the situation stands there is limited interconnection between the different networks and issues relating to message audit trails and the allocation of responsibility have yet to be resolved. Turning to reliability, while EDI is viewed as being of primary value to support a move to more efficient 'pull driven manufacturing systems' such as just in time (JIT) and MRP, its implementation has been largely restricted to peripheral areas such as the processing of financial transactions. Considering Computer Co. once more there is at the present time no genuine EDI in operation supporting manufacturing – messages are invariably pulled off the machine and manually checked. There is sufficient trust in the technical capabilities of EDI to allow the data to be directly input into production schedules or computer numerically controlled (CNC) machines.

Cost concerns are at least partially related to technical limitations. Despite intent at the strategic level, at an operational level there is a vicious circle operating to inhibit the introduction of EDI. The lack of trust in the technology means there is little prospect of introducing EDI in areas where there is a possibility for transforming business processes and contributing a significant level of value added. On the other hand, the use of EDI in peripheral areas results in low payback on the initial investment making it hard to justify.

Turning to the use of other IONs the use of electronic mail seems also to be constrained not only by shortcomings in the technology but also by the limited range of information that can be communicated. At Computer Co. the use of electronic mail, a media reserved for the transfer of textual data, is largely restricted to technical applications. The absence of contextual clues leads to problems in areas such as marketing where presentation as well as content are seen to be important and the impersonal nature of the information transferred limits its usefulness for discussion and negotiation purposes. These areas remain respectively the preserve of written and telephone or face to face communications which are perceived to be much richer media. At Camera Co. the actual system employed is endowed with only a limited degree of functionality and is, for example, incapable of incorporating files into messages. There is consequently a limited integration with the site's other information technology resources and its primary role is restricted to the communication of short notes.

Systems Co., in contrast to Camera Co. and Computer Co. who have opted for sophisticated and expensive implementations of IONs with little impact on existing business processes, uses the simple technology of electronic file transfer to manage the transfer of the company's software products to and from suppliers and customers. This is implemented via direct dial-up links which were chosen because communication in this manner is faster and cheaper than using the services of a value added network services (VANS) supplier although there is typically the disadvantage of both parties having to be ready at the same time for transmission to be possible. To circumvent this inconvenience some large customers have established bulletin boards which can be dialled into to deposit or retrieve information at any time. Other companies have installed software that automatically establishes connections when necessary. In other words a simple but effective ION by being applied with a little initiative to a suitable aspect of the company's business has shown itself to be much more productive than the quest for applications for sophisticated but unproven technologies.

## Internal-external distinction

At both Camera Co. and Computer Co. there can be seen to be a clear difference, in terms of the nature and role of the information transferred, between internal and external communications with the strategic emphasis lying squarely with intracorporation communication. At Computer Co., for example, with respect to internal communications concern lies with discussion and planning while external communications are more akin to the presentation of final documents which then have to be acted on. At the intracorporation level electronic linkages allow for a larger more dispersed pool of skills to be tapped. For example, a consultant in the South of England is helping in the development of software for use at the Ayr site. External collaboration, however, is only occurring within very narrowly defined limits. Thus, while Computer Co. view their business as changing from the supply of hardware to being a service provider they do not seem to view this as being the case for their own suppliers; more is being demanded of them in price and performance terms yet there is little indication of significant responsibilities being divested to them. Product design and manufacturing decisions seem to be taken within Computer Co. with little or no outside consultation. It thus appears that although there is a recognition that self-sufficiency is no longer possible the solution is being implemented within a hierarchical rather than a market-based framework. While accepting that in an age of specialization it cannot be expected that a single plant or subsidiary will possess all of the required specialisms it is still believed that they will be found somewhere within the corporation. Relationships with suppliers remain essentially transaction based, there has been no qualitative change in the nature of the information exchanged and there is little prospect of joint business development. Suppliers are still responding to the demands of Computer Co. rather than helping to define them.

Systems Co., a much smaller company, has moved in the opposite direction toward tapping external resources. Use is made of the technical conferences on Cix, an information service. These can best be described as an 'experience system' which enables expertise from outside the company to be called upon in response to particular problems that are encountered. The conferences accessed by Systems Co. tend to be used by software producers rather than end-users and there tends to be a relatively small number of active contributors facilitating the development of virtual relationships. Systems Co. also uses another information service, CompuServe, for customer support though the service is no longer used as much as in the past. During the late 1980s Systems Co. had a large number of commercial customers in the US and CompuServe was invaluable in maintaining relations – time zone differences rendering the telephone a rather inadequate communications medium. However, there is not the same requirement or market for support via electronic communications in the British and European markets.

At a more general level collaboration and specialization have been inherent features of the sectors Systems Co. operates in virtually since their inception. Specialization has, however, increased in recent years, aided by improvements in communication facilities. Previously it was only self-contained tasks such as draughting that were 'put out' while now the practice has been extended to include software modules that have to interact with one another. Relationships are genuinely collaborative in the sense that Systems Co. does not simply respond to the demands of others but helps define their needs, for example, providing input both with respect to what is technically feasible and what the market wants. Electronic communications are viewed as invaluable in facilitating the collaborative process. Software development, for example, is evolutionary with different versions of the software travelling back and forth being updated and improved upon on almost a daily basis. Where 'bugs' are found in the software used by the company these are reported and rectified electronically. It is viewed as being much easier and more effective to transfer the relevant code than attempting to explain the problem over the telephone – the problem is in effect transferred.

## Reshaping of organizational activity

This is clearly connected to the previous section but examines the processes at work in more detail from an organizational perspective. An example of the potential for IONs to facilitate a redistribution of tasks and responsibilities is provided by Camera Co. The Renton site is currently introducing computer-aided design (CAD) for the electronic transfer of drawings both within the corporation and externally. At the moment drawings are shipped on paper from the US into Renton and if necessary then copied to suppliers. This hard copy system leads to complications if amendments or alterations are necessary which is often the case because of differences in US–UK specifications and tooling constraints. With CAD it will be possible for all parties to see the most current drawing. Perhaps of more interest is that in the medium term the interviewee thought that the move will also foster a greater integration of operations, the increased immediacy of information allowing Renton to take a more active role in the design process than is currently the case.

## Conclusions

It is possible at this stage to draw a number of tentative conclusions. It would appear that the use of IONs is of significance to the economy and further that this is set to increase over the next few years. It may also be the case that in addition to an impact in their own right the introduction of IONs will serve as the driving force behind a general reassessment of the role of communications within firms. As one interviewee commented ‘once communications are made more effective a greater reliance is placed upon them’.

At the present time the redistribution of activities seems largely to be limited to technical functions and be under the umbrella of common ownership. Internally there is evidence that IONs are reducing the reliance on proximate resources and allowing the utilization of the most appropriate competences regardless of where they are located within the firm. Common knowledge would appear to have a significant role to play in this process witnessed by the distinction between technical and managerial activities. In technical areas a combination of accepted terminology and precise definitions renders information readily transferable. In software development, for example, work proceeds within a precisely specified 'language'. In management, however, meaning is much more esoterically defined fostering a greater degree of ambiguity.

At the interfirm level the absence of common knowledge would seem to be a major contributor to transaction costs in the market-place. Uncertainty with respect to the meaning of information communicated between firms and the consequent risk of opportunistic behaviour leads to the internalization of production. Empirical work suggests that the limited interfirm collaboration occurring is restricted to technical areas where there is a foundation of common knowledge.

It is difficult to determine at this stage whether existing hierarchical structures will indeed ultimately be overshadowed. There are a number of factors which impinge upon the development of common knowledge and will thus serve to determine whether there is scope for the use of resources that currently lie outwith the firm's boundaries or merely the more complete exploitation of internal resources. Furthermore, it is necessary to match appropriate IONs to identified needs; IONs themselves do not serve as a panacea. At the current time a major constraint to the diffusion of IONs is held both in the literature and in the field to be technological limitations. Yet of more significance are the absence of coherent implementation strategies and organizational policies to foster the development of common knowledge. In this sense it may be more expedient for firms to introduce IONs in piecemeal fashion using simple, proven technologies as building blocks and so generate enthusiasm and commitment than to take a single giant step to electronic trading.
