# Computer capacity planning management: definitions and methodology

MAGID IGBARIA and SNEHAMAY BANERJEE
Department of Management, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

Information systems (IS) managers are asked to provide services and cut computer costs. Computer capacity planning (CCP) provides them with a tool to assess the future computing needs and new technologies. Effective CCP is an important step in providing users with the lowest cost solution that meets their computing requirements. This paper discusses the definition and methodologies of CCP. The importance of CCP for IS people as well as business people is emphasized. Five phases for developing a capacity plan are presented and a few examples for illustration are provided. A real life example is also presented and suggestions and recommendations are offered.

## Introduction

Computer capacity planning (CCP) is the task of ensuring, in a cost-effective way, that an organization will have adequate computing resources to meet its future computing needs. The meaning of CCP has changed over time with changes in the computing practices of organizations and the costs of computer-related products (Cady and Howarth, 1990). However, experts agree that the main goal of CCP is to maintain a balance between business growth and needs and explicit or implicit service level objectives of computing support (DEC, 1990a). In other words, CCP is a method used for projecting computer workload and planning to meet the future demand for computing resources in a cost-effective manner.

Information technology (IT) is an expensive resource with many hidden costs and these costs are growing rapidly. Investment in IT grew from \$55 billion to \$190 billion in the 1980s, an annual growth rate of just under 15% (Keen, 1991). On the other hand, companies are becoming increasingly dependent on their IT resources as new services, e.g. electronic data interchange (EDI), are introduced for handling routine business transactions. Information systems (IS) managers whose budgets are being cut must find ways to provide more service while spending less money. CCP can help them determine the best way to balance capacity with cost.

Capacity planning needs involvement of the IS managers and the key executives from all segments of the organization. Keen (1991) urges for an effective dialogue between the two sides of management who determine the very basics of business choices and technology consequences and the technology choices and very basics of business consequences. Such a dialogue between key executives is a must because technical, organizational, economic and managerial choices have become so interdependent that they can not be handled in isolation. Any technical strategy that ignores organizational or economic issues is likely to fail. CCP methodology provides a forum for continuing such a dialogue between IS and other key managers to create a corporate blueprint for IT infrastructure of the future. Proper planning for future computing needs can produce substantial benefits for an organization that include

(1) reduced risk in acquiring computing resources through better and more timely use of available information;

(2) pro-active management of computing resources in a changing business environment through quantitative and validated methodologies;

(3) sound foundation for investment justification and cost control of computing resources;

(4) improved use of computer resources due to better business planning;

(5) investment protection through accurate determination of computing needs;

(6) increased user satisfaction through assessment and coordination of user needs;

(7) avoidance of degraded services to the user community.

Capacity planning is a difficult task because no clear economic framework exists to do a cost–benefit analysis of IT. Even if investment costs can be estimated it is almost impossible to measure the economic benefits resulting out of the investment. However, rapid growth and innovations in IT, internationalization of business and markets and competition have made CCP a frequent exercise in many organizations which is an ongoing and iterative process. It is therefore necessary to have a comprehensive framework for CCP that can encompass every aspect of an organization for effectively managing the future growth of IT. In this paper we discuss a comprehensive methodology for capacity planning with a real life example illustrating the merits of the methodology. Details of the methodology are discussed below.

## A capacity planning methodology

The proposed methodology is adapted from Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC 1990b). It consists of five phases: business assessment, systems assessment, capacity analysis, evaluation of alternatives and systems growth plan. The relationship between these five steps is shown in Figure 1. It is important to remember that these phases make up a process that provides the business with an ongoing capacity plan for any type of system. To illustrate the process we have used examples from the DEC system. However, availability of similar products from other vendors makes these steps applicable to other computer configurations, such as IBM (for more details on IBM systems, see Armstrong (1982)). A brief discussion of each of these five phases is given in the following subsections:

## Business assessment

This phase assesses future growth of business and expected changes in the organization and is conducted in parallel with systems assessment. It provides insight into the following areas.

(1) Strategic and competitive advantages obtained through information technology. Such advantages can be further classified as direct and indirect. Direct advantages include improved service level, better cost control, faster product development and increased production level. Indirect advantages include increased productivity and operational efficiency and effectiveness of shaping the future of the company (Keen, 1991).

(2) Flow of information within the organization. Such understanding helps managers to define the purpose information technology serves within and across functional groups, reveal redundancy of different organizational efforts, highlight existing information links and identify need for new links, identify informational dependencies across and within groups, create a profile of the usage of computing resources at a departmental level and identify areas in computing usage where upgrade may be required.

This phase is driven by the information systems (IS) department and requires information from management and user groups. Top management commitment and involvement are critical for the success of this phase because it requires cooperation from diverse groups. This phase consists of six steps which are described below.

(1) Define the scope of project: select team members and choose a team coordinator. Define roles and responsibilities for each team member. Develop a preliminary project plan and select the participants for the study that represent a diverse group of users.

(2) Identify data collection methods and tools: create a detailed project plan, identify data requirements and identify data collection and analysis methods.

(3) Collect data: meet with the team members as per established schedule and collect data using the methods determined in step 2. Performance and workload data are either collected continuously or sampled.

(4) Analyse data: summarize the data collected in step 3 and interpret the same using qualitative and quantitative methods (e.g. statistical or subjective approaches). Draw conclusions from the results of the analysis and develop solutions to address the problems and opportunities.

(5) Write and present report: summarize the findings, conclusions and recommendations into a final report and present it to the management.

(6) Factor into other systems growth planning activities: translate the results of the business assessment into additional resource requirements and incorporate it into the systems growth planning process.

The business assessment is an ongoing process. In the ideal situation departments provide information about additional users, natural growth of existing applications, new applications, new applications to be developed over the planning period and other expected changes on an ongoing basis.

## Systems assessment

The goal of this phase is to understand current and future systems performance needs. Understanding current level of systems performance provides a foundation for estimating the impact of changes in the business environment on the computing resources. Successful completion of this phase provides the following: an understanding of the current workload, an understanding of the impact of workload changes on systems performance, clear justification for purchase of any additional computing resources, a mechanism for costing and pricing of computer services (charge-back system) and an early warning for costing and pricing of computer services (charge-back system) and an early warning for possible bottle-necks. Four essential steps for this phase are as follows.

(1) Establish a tuned system: this step is the foundation for predicting future system requirements. A tuned system allows IS managers to improve the efficiency of an existing computer system. Recommendations based on an improperly tuned system may not meet future computing needs. Performance management tools to be used for this process are presented in the next subsection.

(2) Understand reasons for changing demand: monitor workload changes, e.g. changes in number of users, number of software applications, application workload mix, computer time usage, user storage allocation, memory and I/O usage and peak processing periods. This can be done by monitoring performance, plotting trend graphs and surveying users. Develop a profile of the changes that are taking place in the computing environment.

(3) Establish service level objectives: service level objectives (SLOs) represent the best estimates for minimum acceptable performance levels for different system components, e.g. response-turnaround time. SLOs also include thresholds for particular resources, e.g. CPU utilization should not exceed 75% or memory utilization should not exceed 90%, response time should not exceed 3s and batch turnaround time should not exceed 30min. Monitor and compare current systems performances with appropriate SLOs. For example, run a benchmark at predetermined intervals and graph the result to track peak processing periods. Prioritizing jobs, adjusting user priorities, restricting log-on times, limiting access privileges, etc., can improve systems performance if it is approaching SLO. Chart the results to predict the speed of systems growth.

(4) Monitor and define limiting resource: monitor three primary resources namely, CPU cycles, memory utilization and disk I/O to identify the primary limiting resource when the system is running at or near peak utilization. There are several components to each of these resources such as page faults, lock activity and the time the processor spends in the different CPU modes. It is important to monitor each of the three primary resources separately and in relation to each other. This is important because independent examination of resources by themselves can lead to erroneous results. For example, memory bottlenecks can be masked by an increase in CPU utilization which can lead to buying more CPU power when the actual need is for more memory.

To identify primary limiting resources collect the necessary data on the performance of an existing system through a routine monitoring process. We have illustrated the method using the DEC/VAX system which provides monitoring processes such as SPM, VPA and MONITOR. Establish 'rule of thumb' thresholds for these resources and plot the current utilization of these resources against the threshold values. These thresholds are site dependent as these are based on the workload and service level objectives. After detecting an actual or potential bottle-neck formulate a strategy to eliminate the bottle-neck. Several factors, e.g. severity of the bottle-neck, user or application importance, availability of alternative resources and the environment (stand-alone, clustered and networked), must be considered for developing this strategy.

A potential strategy for eliminating bottle-neck will be to move processing to off-peak hours. Other strategies include better balancing of usage in a clustered environment, reduction of CPU interrupt processing (such as memory paging or swapping) by increasing memory allocation for critical functions and frequent defragmentation of disks. The strategies are context dependent and there is no cookbook answer for a problem. Therefore, it is important to understand the relationship between different resources and characteristics of the current system(s).

## Performance management tools

The performance management tools are generally used to locate the bottle-necks that limit systems performance. We discuss two such tools for the VAX/VMS environment. MONITOR is a standard utility for the VMS operating system, whereas the VAX/Performance Advisor (VPA) is a layered product which may be purchased at an additional cost. Similar tools are available for other computing environments. Details of these tools are discussed below for illustrative purposes.

## Monitor utility

The monitor utility provides information on operating system performance and is very useful for analysing a running system. It displays different classes of data including I/O statistics, page management statistics, locking statistics and process statistics. These displays can be used in real time to identify and resolve problems such as runaway processes quickly. Such information can also be recorded and played back later for analysis. However, these recording/accounting files can become very large within a short period of time. A summary file can also be created containing information on each of the classes by time of use which is useful for understanding system utilization for a given time period.

## VAX performance advisor (VPA)

VPA is a set of data collection and reporting software. Data is collected at pre-specified intervals for a given time period, e.g. at 2 min intervals from 7 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays only. This data summarized using different reports. An analysis report compares collected data against a set of performance rules. In other words, it compares the current utilization with the maximum capacity available to the user community. Any specified rule violation (e.g. exceeding 90% memory utilization) will trigger a brief report to inform IS managers of the potential problem. If requested, a detailed report will go into more details and suggest remedies to the problem. Figure 2 presents an example of a brief report. A performance report details the performance of the system/cluster over a requested period of time. VPA can also create custom graphs of any metric (VPA collects data on over 100 metrics). Figure 3 presents a graph on the top I/O rate of disks for a given time period. Using VPA's modelling capabilities IS managers can create a baseline model which depicts current system utilization. This model is used to estimate changes in system usage for potential system upgrades. It can also be used to predict the effects of adding a new group of jobs/users to an existing workload and to assess the effects of changing technologies and user needs. In summary, VPA makes understanding, reporting and resolving performance bottle-necks much easier. It provides an early warning of possible bottle-necks. However, it needs a lot of storage and computing capacity.

## Capacity analysis

Capacity analysis is the process of predicting the impact of changes in user needs or technologies on system performance. This prediction is based on the user needs (utilizations) and performance (capacity). This phase consists of the following five steps.

Figure 3 VAX performance adviser 1/10 performance group

(1) Workload characterization: workload characterization involves grouping similar applications or functional groups into resource consumption groups. We need to identify a number of homogeneous groups of applications or users. Different techniques have been developed and discussed to cluster groups based on their computer consumption (Ahituv et al., 1988; Benjamin and Igbaria, 1991). For example, all student jobs could be in one group and all researchers in another or life insurance in one group and elementary insurance in another (Igbaria, 1990). Such grouping helps increasing the accuracy of future workload estimates and provides a better prediction of computer resource utilization. Tools such as VPA allow IS managers to define homogeneous groups and report resource consumption statistics for these groups.

(2) Model validation: workload characterization forms the basis for developing a model that will generate future workload estimates. This model is validated by comparing the actual workloads of the system against predicted workloads for a given period of time. It is an iterative process and the model improves with each iteration. It is important to investigate the reasons for inaccurate predictions of the past so that corrective measures may be taken for estimating future workloads.

(3) System balancing: system balancing involves distributing different workloads across CPUs in order to obtain similar response times or distributing files across different disks to obtain similar access times. The objective is to determine whether or not it would be worthwhile for the current system usage.

(4) 'What-if' analysis: what-if analysis provides an early warning for possible bottle-necks. After a bottle-neck is detected the What-if? analysis may also be used to find a good solution to the problem. For example, to identify the number of files that can be added to a particular disk before it becomes fully loaded, successively bigger loads are added to the disk until the disk becomes I/O bound. This is called saturation analysis. This indicates how far in the future one should plan on choosing between alternatives, such as purchasing an additional disk or upgrading and/or tuning the current disk.

(5) Alternative development: once the potential bottleneck for the projected workload has been detected, it is necessary to identify potential solutions to the problem. Results of What-if analysis may be used for this purpose.

These steps are not always distinguishable and their use is dependent on the technique used for conducting the capacity analysis. Several techniques exist for conducting capacity analysis and these techniques have evolved over the years. Such techniques include, in ascending order of accuracy, manual estimation, linear trend analysis, statistical modelling, analytic modelling, simulation and benchmarking. As the degree of accuracy goes up so does the associated cost and complexity. Manual estimation uses IS managers' assessment of future workload, without the aid of a computer program, to estimate future computing needs. This is inexpensive but risky at best. Linear trend analysis involves making straight line (i.e. linear) projections to determine future capacity needs. This can be done manually or using a commercially available spreadsheet or statistical package. This analysis is very sensitive to workload variation and it is difficult to factor in changing business needs. It implies constant usage growth. Therefore, this technique may not provide an accurate forecast. Statistical modelling is a forecast technique which bases the projection of future demand on actual current usage data and computer actions taken, e.g. upgrading, etc. It is used to determine how well a specific system is meeting current or may be expected to meet the future demands. Analytic modelling is probably the most cost-effective method for performing capacity analysis. It can make capacity requirement estimates where workload and resource conditions are changing. It is important to note that this holds a greater potential benefit provided that the analyst understands the true interaction of hardware performance factors with those of software. Most of the discussions of analytical modelling are based on queuing models. Simulation is another useful method. This method allows IS managers to mimic actual future usage and measure the results. However, this method requires substantial computing resources. Bench-marking is the most accurate but is very expensive. It is time-consuming and a nuisance. It allows IS managers to run the actual applications on the proposed hardware and software. Expenses can rise even higher if one scenario does not work and IS managers are forced to acquire and set up something new. Additionally, the entire workload or a portion of it should be specifically taken off the existing system and loaded on the proposed system.

The choice of a technique for performing capacity analysis is dependent upon many factors that include time and resource availability, size of the system, predictability of workload variation, etc. The choice is usually based on a trade-off between the cost of performing the analysis and the accuracy of the analysis. Most IS departments find that the most useful method involves a combination of intuition, simulation models and linear analysis.

## Evaluation of alternatives

Many factors influence the evaluation of potential alternatives for meeting future capacity needs. Such factors include future growth of business, system cost, compatibility with existing resources, computing style of the environment including competitors, growth potential of the system and maintenance cost. Potential alternatives can be grouped into three categories: upgrading or replacing existing systems, off-loading or networking applications and clustering systems. Each of these categories is discussed below.

(1) Upgrading or replacing existing systems: whenever any part of a system becomes a bottle-neck it can be either upgraded or replaced. Replacement is beneficial if the cost of maintaining the older system no longer justifies its existence. A newer system has some obvious advantages but replacing hardware may require additional investment for software. For example, DEC software is controlled by licences that prevent software 'licensed' for a certain computer type from working on a faster machine. A new system must be purchased or leased. The biggest advantage is upgrading existing system is that the users are not effected by the change. User productivity is maintained and the investment in existing peripherals is preserved. However, obsolescence becomes a potential problem for the system. In addition, organizations should consider whether tuning the system can remove the bottle-neck and the cost associated with it.

(2) Networking: this provides an ability to spread the workload to both local and remote resources. Additionally, it increases productivity through communications, provides access to multivendor installations and increases resource sharing. Through networking any computing resource can become a resource to the entire organization by simply connecting it to the network and therefore IS managers can shift a few applications to other computer systems.

(3) Clustering: this is a method of interconnecting systems that allow sharing of both data and resources that might normally be accessible by only one system. Additional benefits of clustering include centralized management of resources, reduction of duplicate databases, increased flexibility for future growth and better utilization of resources due to load balancing.

Each of the potential alternatives has some advantages and disadvantages. Financial considerations also for evaluating different alternatives include depreciation, trade-in value, leasing options, taxes, capital outlay and the corporation's long-term objective.

## Systems growth plan

The final stage of the capacity planning process is the development of a systems growth plan. This plan details what resources will be needed when and its associated costs for the next 1–3 years. This plan is an ongoing process and it should be continually monitored, reviewed and updated with input from each user group and management to make sure that resources are removed or acquired at the appropriate time. This helps to assure that equipment won't be sitting around waiting to be used (unnecessary expense) or productivity won't be reduced by not having the resources when they are needed.

The plan should be communicated to all of the affected groups within and outside of the IS department. This serves two purposes: first, it makes others aware that this process exists and that they had some input into the resulting configuration. This may make it easier to get the required information in future. Second, those that make decisions based on this plan are constantly aware of the resources that are going to be available to them. The results of the capacity plan must be fed back into the process. This helps in determining the accuracy of the planning process which in turn helps in improving the process in future.

It is important to mention that many factors are considered in the capacity planning process. Some of these factors result from changing business conditions within the firm and are usually reflected in the business plan. Other factors such as volume change in either direction, changes in financial conditions and budgets within the firm and changes in the number of people using IT can be the result of changes in external conditions. Information on such factors that change workload projection are essential for IS managers for validating the capacity plan. Effective capacity planning is a continuous process and it must be accompanied by validation procedures based on updated information. ## Example

This section presents an example of the problems faced by IS managers in conducting capacity planning based on a case study conducted at a large international corporation in the USA. The computing division (CD) of the company employs more than 100 people who support approximately 5000 scientific and technical users world-wide. The CD is divided into four sections: systems and operations, technology planning and support, training and support and science, engineering and mathematical consulting.

The systems and operations section is responsible for the day to day operations of CD's four VAX clusters, the Cray YMP supercomputer and the supporting network. Their major responsibilities include performance and capacity management, network support and configuration, hardware maintenance, back-ups and operating systems support. The technology planning and support section provides product customization and support for over 130 layered products. These include, but are not limited to, office automation products, statistical and molecular modelling packages and compilers and fourth generation languages. The training and support section includes a help line that answers approximately 300–400 calls per day and year-round training for users of all levels for the various layered products. Lastly, the science, engineering and mathematics consulting section is contracted out to provide high-level consulting in areas such as statistics, data analysis and simulation. This group also consults on applications for personal computers, including Macintosh's and the various IBM PCs.

All of the CD's 5000 customers have accounts on one or both of the two production DEC/VAX clusters. Each of these clusters includes a front-end to the CRAY YMP supercomputer. In addition to the two VAX clusters just mentioned, there is a test cluster, which is used to test new software and bug fixes and a print cluster. The print cluster serves approximately 400 print queues for the other three clusters. The four DEC VAX clusters are made up of 22 DEC VAX minicomputers. Most are multiprocessors and all of them run under the VMS operating system. Connected to the four VAX clusters are approximately 250 disk drives and 30 tape drives. Each of the four VAX clusters is monitored and can be operated through two centrally located MICROVAX class machines. The supporting network is comprised of mostly Ungermann–Bass equipment and provides access to all the company's sites world-wide.

## Capacity planning in the CD

Presently, capacity planning is not a regular process in the CD. Evaluation of the need for additional resources is done in response to either the expiration of a lease or the growth line of a single graph. Until recently even lease expirations were not monitored too closely. Frequently, leases would be allowed to roll over on a month to month basis. However, the recent heightened attention toward cost cutting has shown this to be very expensive, since most 3 year leases can be negotiated at a much lower cost. Lease expirations are now viewed as an opportunity to make capacity changes and reduce costs at the same time. Although this is an improvement, it is still very reactionary and is based on a narrow view of future computing needs. Capacity needs are re-evaluated each month based on a graph that tracks interactive CPU utilization as a percent of the CPU capacity available. New capacity is purchased when system utilization crosses a given threshold. In general, this means another node is added to the cluster. By approaching capacity planning in this way, the corresponding capacity plan is frequently invalidated within a few months and often times as short as weeks.

There are several problems with this method. First, CPU is only one of several critical resources and, in fact, is the most expensive one. By not monitoring other critical resources (memory and I/O at a minimum) the CD is spending more money than may be necessary. Because an increase in CPU utilization can sometimes be attributed to insufficient memory or I/O capacity, the CD's policy of buying new computers each time masks this problem since additional memory and I/O capacity is purchased with the new machine. Second, the graph is based on accounting data from each machine. Since records are written into this accounting file only when a process terminates, data for system processes and other processes which run forever are ignored. This has potential harmful side-effects should the workload for these processes change. Lastly, this graph does not consider the CPU usage of batch or network processes. This is especially important because CD customers have taken a special interest in cutting their own costs. One way to do this is to run more jobs in batch after hours. By not including batch utilization in their calculations the CD is ignoring possible workload shifts.

When the need for additional computing resources is recognized the CD forms an ad hoc group of people to make capacity decisions. This group generally consists of people from systems and operations, technology planning and the science and mathematical consulting group. This group examines existing performance data then uses manual estimation or linear trending to determine how much capacity should be purchased. This process does not involve line managers and the business plan does not provide any input to the capacity planning process. As a result the capacity planning process and the corresponding plan is not taken seriously by the managers.

## Present plan

A tight budget forced the CD to re-evaluate its capacity planning process and it made great strides in improving performance management techniques. It has gone from being very reactive to actively identifying potential bottlenecks and areas where performance can be improved. By using products such as MONITOR and VPA, patterns of bad performance are more easily identified and corrected. However, in order to enhance the capacity planning process several improvements are being implemented. These are as follows.

(1) Generating reports from a common source of data. Currently such data are generated from many performance management tools. This causes confusion when explaining them to management.

(2) Defining service level objectives. Currently, the only measure of customer satisfaction is the number of help line calls.

However, much more needs to be done in implementing an effective and ongoing capacity planning process. The CD has sustained continual growth since 1984. This has made it much simpler to plan for future computing needs. However, growth has levelled off and there are demands from customers to cut costs. To keep costs to a minimum and to plan for changing technology and a changing workload, the CD must improve its assessment of future business needs and use a better capacity analysis method.

To do this CD is planning to use it's customer representatives (one for each major department) to actively interview key customers in order to get a better understanding of future business requirements. These will be translated into computing resource requirements. Additionally, the same is planned to be done with corporate initiatives and plans for future technology. However, this is considered to be the first step towards developing an effective capacity planning process for the future. The haphazard method of planning for future computing needs are coming under increased scrutiny from top management as such plans fail to meet computing needs in a cost-effective manner. In this case, the CD cannot survive doing business as usual and is therefore looking for a better alternative to carry out the capacity plan. The methodology outlined here is a potential method for developing such a plan in the future.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we have discussed the definitions and methodology of computer capacity planning and management. IS managers are struggling with tighter budgets, deteriorating infrastructures and escalating facilities costs. Therefore, they need to provide more service while cutting their costs. CCP is described as a tool to help IS managers to assess current and future business needs and existing workload and future technologies. They need to determine the best way to balance capacity with cost.

IS managers need to analyse the existing system (business assessment and service levels), determine future requirements (data collection and analysis, workload forecasting, capacity analysis including comparison of actual and predicted workload), evaluate different alternatives including financial analysis and develop a capacity plan for the management.

Finally, this paper provides IS managers and capacity planners with a methodology to assess the existing and future workload and performance of computer systems. This methodology can be easily followed and implemented. All the data necessary to develop a capacity plan are typically available from accounting files, business documents and interviews. This methodology can be implemented and reviewed on ongoing bases and may serve as a basis for developing a DSS to support IS managers for forecasting the impact changing technologies and user needs. It will also help IS managers to predict the effect of adding a new group of applications or transactions on the current system.
