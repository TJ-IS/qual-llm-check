# Matching an IT project with an appropriate method of evaluation: a research note on ‘Evaluating investments in IT’

BARBARA FARBEY and DAVID TARGETT
University of Bath, Bath, UK

FRANK LAND

London School of Economics, London, UK

This note addresses the problem of choosing an appropriate method when evaluating an IT investment as set out in a previous work. The problem arises immediately because there are many methods on offer for performing the evaluation and a great number of different situations in which those methods could be applied. There is thus a problem of matching the method to the situation across many dimensions. In the previous paper the authors spelled out some of these dimensions on the basis of empirical work and proposed a matching method derived by trial and error which had, at the time, no theoretical foundations. Further research has suggested a possible rationale and it is that which is presented here, together with a further dimension on which the matching may be carried through.

## Introduction

In a previous work (Farbey et al., 1993) we argued that

the search for a single technique that can deal with all IT investment projects is proving fruitless. The range of circumstances to which that one technique would have to be applied is so wide that no single technique can be applied. This wide range of circumstances comes about because every IT project has characteristics which point to the set of circumstances in which it could be applied. At the same time every evaluation technique has characteristics which point to the circumstances in which it could be applied. A first step in evaluation is therefore to understand more about the context in which the evaluation is taking place.

We went on to describe in some detail both the various circumstances which we met in the course of that research and features of the various methods.

We further attempted a simple matching procedure. The procedure was based on a series of $2 \times 2$ matrices which captured the relevant characteristics as we saw them, superimposed the matrices on each other and compared the result with a similar $2 \times 2$ classification of evaluation methods. The individual matrices and the summary are shown in Figure 1. The methods classification is reproduced in Figure 2.

At the time, the matrices describing project characteristics were derived empirically, abstracting from interviews and the literature. The evaluation methods were assigned to cells according to their dominant features following our definitions of the two axes as 'well defined versus fuzzy' evaluation constraints and the 'conservative versus radical' role of IT.

The method proved to be effective for the projects we were studying and also for other researchers working independently. However, it lacked theoretical underpinning and was presented simply as a speculation. Since then we have found a classification which we think explains why the method works. The classification, which is due to Hellstern (1986), comes from evaluation research, a body of work to which we made reference in earlier work and which we have continued to explore. It is also available in the information systems literature in an article by Earl and Hopwood (1987) where it is discussed in terms of strategic decision-making. Taking all three classifications together, it becomes clear that they are essentially the same. In particular, the logic which underpins the Hellstern (1986) categorization and the language which he uses provide a rationale for our empirically discovered method.

## Two dimensions of uncertainty: the classifications of Hellstern, and Earl and Hopwood

Figures 3 and 4 show the Hellstern (1986) and Earl and Hopwood (1987) matrices, respectively. The axes are identical and, therefore, despite the differences in terminology and context, so are the contents of the individual cells.  
Hellstern wrote about the problems of evaluating programmes in the context of public policy. The sorts of programme with which he is concerned are major governmental programmes, such as Head Start in the USA. They are social interventions and as he wrote:

prove to be successful for well defined problems with clear goals and clear causal relations, most problems do not fall into this category. They are not well-structured and cannot be broken down easily into their components. Most problems for which evaluation research could be useful for policymakers and clients are ill-structured problems, with substantive problems varying from actor to actor and over time; they are dynamic. Such problems are not mechanical and cannot be separated from the context.

This adds a new dimension to the argument which we have been making as follows. We have argued for consideration of different methods principally on the grounds that a variety of methods was necessary to match the variety of situations encountered in evaluating IT. Following Hellstern though, it is possible to argue for variety on the grounds that the problems are not only often ill-structured, as is suggested in some of our descriptions of situations, but also that the lack of structure itself has two bases: lack of structure with respect to objectives and lack of structure with respect to knowledge of cause and effect.

The argument can be expanded: if information systems are complex and pervasive sociotechnical systems whose life extends over several months or even years then investment in information systems can be understood as a programme of social action, based on a complex technology and taking place over a substantial period in time. Such investments are in many ways like the programmes of social action which are the subject of evaluation research. In particular they generally present problems to the evaluator which are poorly structured. The lack of structure has two bases: lack of clear objectives and a lack of knowledge as to the potential impact of the systems and, hence, a lack of knowledge as to cause and effect. These two ‘dimensions’ are largely orthogonal in the sense that it is possible to envisage a situation in which the objectives are perfectly clear, but the impact of a new system of achieving them is poorly understood and equally, the potential impact of a system may be obvious, but the value of achieving it may not be commonly agreed.

Looking at the axes of matrices we have used to describe the situations as we met them, it can be seen in most cases the distinction being made follows this same split. The vertical axes (constraints on evaluation) of the individual matrices represent situations where the objectives are not clear, whether the objectives of the system or the objectives of the evaluation. The horizontal axes reflect uncertainty about cause and effect. By definition, conservative action is action undertaken in known circumstances; radical action is not.

What we have done by elaborating the circumstances in which the evaluation might take place and the factors which affect evaluation, is to tease out the factors which influence the choice of technique. These form, as it were, a third dimension. By superimposing them we show where the balance lies across the range of factors. Taking the example given in Farbey et al. (1993), the fictional project described there is basically unstructured but the management, by being willing to consider a sophisticated, possibly costly technique, is signalling a willingness to learn. There is an implicit assumption here, which is that the factors are independent. This would have to be justified in any practical case. If there were significant interdependencies, this would in and of itself add to the loss of focus on objectives.

Looking next at the matrix of methods against which the descriptions are to be matched and using Hellstern's (1986) description of the contexts, we now assert that the critical features of each method are those which determine

(1) how far they contribute to or are based upon a knowledge of cause and effect;

(2) how far they assume or contribute to the formation of clear and agreed objectives.

## Examples

Taking some examples, Return on Investment (ROI) methods are most useful in situations which are clear-cut on both dimensions. Methods which are principally concerned to establish a consensus, Multi-Objective, Multi-Criteria (MOMC), for example or Delphi methods, belong in the bottom left-hand cell.

Experimental methods such as prototyping probably belong here too, since the primary purpose is to establish agreement rather than to provide experimental evidence for anything. On the other hand, certain simulations are used experimentally in the scientific sense of the word, to establish knowledge of cause and effect and they belong in the top-right cell.

Value analysis involves asking experts, for example, the managers for whom the system is being designed, to speculate on their use of the system and to suggest the value of any improved performance they may achieve. Its primary use is to reduce uncertainty about the impact of the system, i.e. of cause and effect. In so doing it also aims at achieving a consensus, i.e. it aims to reduce both kinds of uncertainty so that it belongs in the bottom-right cell. A similar logic would apply to information economics. Critical success factors by contrast, is primarily about clarification of goals. Although it too reduces uncertainty, it probably belongs in the bottom-left cell.

It is evident that these classifications are not rigid. Most methods will go some way to reducing uncertainty about objectives as well as uncertainty about cause and effect. Perhaps blobs overlapping the cell boundaries would give a truer picture. What we have used to classify the methods is the primary purpose – answering the questions: 'Does this method pre-suppose well structured objectives?; Does it have as its main purpose the clarification of objectives?'; 'Does this method assume that we know how the proposed system will impact the organization and therefore its value? Is its main purpose to illuminate the relationship?'

## Testing the classification

By way of probing the classification we can do three things: review our original matrices and try the classification out on some new matrices and on new methods.

First, although as we have said, the original description largely fits the two dimensions described here there is, arguably, one exception. On the argument we are making here, the matrix we have labelled ‘industry situation’ and which has a ‘stable-turbulent’ axis set against a ‘follower-leader’ one, ought probably to be reversed. This would place the stable-turbulent axis, which would be more likely to contribute to uncertainty in cause and effect, across the top.

Second, work which we have done since that previously reported, suggests a further dimension in the evaluation of projects. Several of the projects we are currently investigating are not stand-alone projects, but part of a series of interdependent projects. To a greater or lesser extent, these projects stand or fall together and the valuation of the 'project in itself' has to come to terms with this phenomenon. We are also meeting situations where there are problems of evaluating 'legacy' projects as opposed to 'new/radical' ones. Gathering these together in a new matrix, 'portfolio position' as shown in Figure 5, the legacy-radical axis goes across the top and the independent-interdependent axis goes down the side on the argument that it is likely to be easier to crystallize the objective of a single, stand-alone project than for a multiplicity of interdependent ones.

A third way of probing the classification is to look at other methods of evaluation, which are new to us and not often considered in the context of IS evaluation. As illustrations we can consider three such methods as reported in House (1983), again in the context of evaluation research. They are

(1) art criticism;

(2) accreditation;

(3) adversarial methods.

The first two are similar in so far as they rely on expert knowledge. Presumably in the context of information systems, the nearest equivalent method to 'art criticism' is 'business judgement'. It is an estimate of the worth of the system based not only on explicit, articulated formal knowledge, but also on tacit knowledge acquired through experience. Accreditation too relies on knowledge, knowledge of how, in general, these things are done. In this it is similar to boundary values. All these methods are in effect saying that there is no help to be had, either from trying to reach common objectives or from experimenting to discover how the proposed system will impact on the organization. All that is left to go on is the common sense of the thing. They belong firmly in the bottom right-hand cell. Finally, adversarial methods, by which are meant methods that rely on formal presentation of arguments by two sides, as in a court of law, would we guess be most likely to be valuable in clarifying objectives, rather than providing evidence for cause and effect. They would therefore belong in the bottom left-hand cell.

A key point made above is that the problems associated with information systems are often ill-structured. In identifying two distinct sources of lack of structure and thus creating four types (cells), Hellstern (1986) has also signalled the dominant activity in each cell:

(1) goal monitoring;

## Biographical notes

Barbara Farbey is currently a research fellow in the School of Management, University of Bath. She is working with Professors David Targett and Frank Land examining how organizations go about evaluating IT investment. The research is sponsored by the Economic and Social Research Council, UK.

Prior to that, Dr Farbey was a research fellow at the London Business School. Her work there included a study of 'IT and Professional Effectiveness' and a second on 'IT Business Metrics – the Business Contribution of the IT Department', both for the Digital Equipment Company. She has worked as a researcher and consultant for many years, in industry and in academia.

Recent publications include How to Evaluate Your I/T Investment: A Study of Methods and Practice (Butterworth Heinemann, Oxford, 1993), Measurement Issues in Information Systems Appraisal, IMEKO TC1 AND TC7 COLLOQUIUM: State and Advances of Measurement and Instrumentation Science (The City University, 8–10 September).

Previously at the London Business School, David Targett joined the University of Bath in 1990 as ICL Professor of Information Systems. Before becoming an academic he was an industrial engineer with British Leyland.

His work has been concerned with the impact of information systems on general management. He has recently completed an ESRC project which investigated competition and collaboration within value chains. He is currently working on another major funded project to examine the way organizations evaluate and take decisions about IT investments.

He has regular consultancy contact with organizations in many sectors of business including retailing, banking, transportation and the whisky industry. His paper 'Designing an Executive Information System for a Bank' won the Pergamon Prize. The most recent of his eight books is 'How to Assess Your IT Investment' (Butterworth Heinemann, Oxford) which was published in 1993.

Frank Land is currently Visiting Professor of Information Management. He has worked with information technology and information systems for almost the whole of his working life. He started his career with J. Lyons and their LEO computer in 1953, first as a programmer and later as a systems analyst. He remained with the British Computer industry until 1967 when he joined the London School of Economics (LSE) to establish teaching and research in systems analysis. In 1982 he became Professor of Systems Analysis at the LSE and in 1986 Professor of Information Management at the London Business School. He has been visiting Professor at the Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania, at Sydney University, Australia and at other institutes in Australia, Egypt and India.

Frank Land has published many papers and books on Information Management. He has acted as technical advisor to the House of Commons Select Committee on Trade and Industry during their investigation of IT in the UK. He is currently taking part in a research project investigating how companies evaluate information systems.
