# Business process redesign: hype, hope or hypocrisy?

C. EDWARDS and J.W. PEPPARD

Cranfield School of Management, Cranfield, Bedford MK43 0AL, UK

There have been few management concepts, even including 'Total Quality', 'World Class Manufacturing' and 'Excellence' to have generated such enormous media interest so quickly as the phenomenon of Business Process Redesign (BPR). The IT community in particular has latched on to the concept, although since its inception it has broadened its appeal. This paper discusses the significant issues relating to BPR and makes a distinction between business re-engineering and business process redesign. Based on a study of a number of organizations who claim to be active, the essential ingredients of BPR are presented. The paper then identifies how organizations are managing their redesign initiatives and presents emerging themes. The paper concludes with some of the lessons learned from the research to date and addresses the vital issues of whether BPR is hype, hope or hypocrisy.

Management are constantly striving to give their organizations competitive advantage in the market-place. They develop strategies which they hope will enable them to outperform their competitors and are constantly scanning the environment for opportunities and new ways to compete. They often look to academics and consultants for prescriptions: anything which will give their organizations an edge. Yet the history of management is full of inconsistencies, even apparent contradictions. Henry Ford introduced the concept of mass production and proclaimed that a customer could have any colour automobile as long as it was black. Yet today, while the focus is still on meeting the demands of a mass market, the emphasis is on customization. Global auto makers now manufacture individual models in many varieties. When Michael Porter (1980) introduced his generic strategies he made a specific case for not being ‘stuck in the middle’, between cost leadership and differentiation: but what about Sainsbury’s, Toyota or Dell Computers, are they not very successful organizations stuck in the middle?

Management prescriptions and theories come and go like waves on a beach and tend to reflect the dominant paradigm at the particular time. Every student of organization theory is only too aware of the eras through which thinking on organizations have passed. First we had the classical management school with its emphasis on the anatomy of organizations and scientific management which treated people as merely adjuncts to machines. Then there was the neoclassicists or the behavioural school which focused on the human dimension of organizations. This was then followed by the systems approach and operational research (for some reason called management science in the US) which focused upon unification and quantification, respectively. Formal planning and strategy, structure-in-five's, industry and competitor analysis have all been prominent at different times. Structure following strategy was revised to treat them as interdependent. Culture was suddenly important during the 1980s. Mission statements became vision statements and have lately been promoted to strategic intent; core competencies have been superseded by organizational capabilities. Recently, there have been calls for new organizational forms: the hollow organization, the networked organization, the learning organization, the modular corporation, the virtual organization and the horizontal organization, have all been the subject of debate. $^{1}$

If we examine information technology a similar picture emerges. Early implementations were piecemeal and stand-alone. During the 1970s integration and information systems planning were popularized together with the concept of a corporate database. IT and competitive advantage was suddenly the fashion during the 1980s. Now in the 1990s it is improved products and services through IT, flexible infrastructure, downsizing through outsourcing and, latterly, re-engineering business processes through IT which have become the dominant themes.

Like the surfer in the 'pipeline', $^{2}$ business management are constantly spotting the next wave. Decisions regarding the applicability of the wave to the organization and which wave to ride takes up considerable management time. However, like many theories and management prescriptions, these waves often prove nothing more than light swells. Excellent companies, the one minute manager, the renewal factor and 'making elephants dance' have all had their 15 minutes of fame and are now, by and large, discarded to the history book, often referenced but seldom read. Is it any wonder that management is sceptical? Deciding which wave to ride is a crucial decision. Riding the wrong wave may cost a surfer the competition; for management it can destroy careers. Much worse, it can be detrimental to the organization. Managers seldom admit failure with new management initiatives, being both instigators and judges of success. Is there not a hint of hypocrisy here?

The surfer may get their highs by taking risks, but managers as a whole tend to be risk averse. Yet there are many successful organizations continually riding new waves in a professionally managed way. Some organizations even create their own waves: just look at Toyota and the now famous Toyota Management System. Others are looked upon as role models, such as Marks & Spencer in retailing.

There is a new wave forming in the sea of management prescription. It has the label Business Process Redesign (BPR), although other ‘management wave spotters’ refer to it as business re-engineering, business redesign, core process redesign, business transformation, process innovation or business process transformation. Albeit different names, they seem to be talking about the same wave. The vital question is: ‘is this a significant wave or just another ripple?’ Is it just hype developed by management consultants to make a quick ‘buck’ but soon to be banished to history? Is it hype in being merely a new name for an old concept? Does it represent tomorrow’s hope for European organizations enabling competitiveness in the global market-place? Or is it merely hypocrisy: flotsam to grasp on to while we wait for the next wave; an excuse to be seen to be doing something different?

This paper discusses the significant issues relating to business process redesign. We first look at what seems to be the essential ingredients of BPR based on our examination of a number of organizations who claim to be active. We then review how organizations are managing their redesign initiative and identify emerging themes. We conclude with some lessons which we have learned as a result of our research to date and address the vital issues of whether BPR is hype, hope or hypocrisy.

## What are the essential themes?

Researchers in BPR are faced with a dilemma from the outset. Not only is their confusion over what exactly constitutes BPR, but there is not even an agreed name for this ill-defined idea. Further, many disciplines are talking about BPR – information systems, organization design, strategy, quality, operations management, organization behaviour, to name a few – but are they talking about the same thing? Is it an objective like zero defects was for total quality? Is it a theory like systems theory? Is it a methodology to be rigorously followed like the structured systems analysis and design method (SSADM)? Perhaps it is the application of organization learning? Is it an umbrella term for a variety of things? Like the goal of the systems movement which sought the unity of sciences, is BPR the beginning of the convergence of management disciplines, a sort of unifying theory? Perhaps it is too early to say, yet something is certainly happening.

Ask any surfer to describe a good wave and they are stuck. Similarly, BPR is difficult to define yet somewhat easier to recognize. The surfer must decide if they are going to ride a wave without knowing if it is a good choice until they have reached the shore. However, the BPR wave has been surfed by a number of organizations over the last few years and, hence, organizations can learn lessons from the experiences of others. There is little reason to replicate failure, especially when figures of 70% have been touted.

This article is based on the research which we have been conducting into business process redesign for the past 3 years. During that time we have examined many organizations that have claimed to be ‘doing’ BPR. Sometimes our involvement has been as consultants providing advice. At other times our interventions have been as facilitators more akin to the notion of process consultation (Schein, 1988). We have also engaged in more systematic data collection through action research and tracked the literature and research reports with interest (Preece and Peppard, 1993; Shah et al., 1994).

This research has identified a number of emerging themes in relation to BPR. These we now outline.

## Theme 1: focus upon stakeholders

Customers do not recognize much of what goes on within an organization, nor should they. For example, whether a telephone repair-man comes to fix your phone and travels by bus and buys his spare parts in the local hardware store is immaterial to the customer, as long as the phone is repaired when promised. In a fast food restaurant, the customer is only interested in quick service with good quality food at a reasonable price. How the kitchen is organized or the qualifications and people skills of the shift manager is irrelevant to them.

It seems that despite a business's total dependence on the customer, we are only now designing organizations with them as the primary focus. In BPR, the task and how it is organized is subservient to the customer requirement. This new focus on the customer has seen a number of businesses name organization units and management positions to reinforce this view. BT talk about end-to-end processes and customer facing divisions. National & Provincial Building Society have directors responsible for customer facing processes.

Indeed the interface with the customer is often simplified and unified to demonstrate the critical importance of customers. For example, Reuters had been experiencing an increasing number of complaints from customers. The problem was identified as resulting from several departments dealing independently with subscribers to Reuters' information service. Too many processing errors occurred and Reuters was losing market share. The separate marketing, sales and installation departments were reorganized into a multitude of three-person teams focusing upon the customer. Today, new orders are processed in less than 2 days, down from 5 and customer terminals are installed and running in a matter of minutes (Business Week, 1993).

In the healthcare sector, the patient is the customer. Kingston Hospital, in a review of its hospital service, revealed that there was room for major improvement. It saw the traditional way of organizing hospitals based around the needs of services, staff and technology as contributing to inefficiencies. With its patient-focused approach to care, it put the needs of the patient as the focus of its redesign (Langan and McIntosh, 1993). Similarly, Sweden's Karolinska hospital has reorganized itself around the 'patient flow' (Brown-Humes, 1994).

Many processes extend into customer and supplier organizations. With electronic data interchange (EDI) an attempt is made to integrate the ordering process of one organization with the sales and delivery processes of another. The focus of EDI is traditionally on data transfer and providing an IT solution to an activity that has been paper based. With BPR an organization would attempt to integrate the customer ordering process into its own fulfilment process so that they are now performed as one process – not just interfaced but integrated.

The term value-adding partnership (Johnston and Lawrence, 1988) has been coined to describe such relationships. Success with such relationships depends largely on the attitudes and practices of the participating managers. Customers do not necessarily have to be external: in many instances a process provides an input to an internal customer. For example, recruitment and management development support the strategic processes of the business. In such situations the same single principle is applied.

However, today's interorganizational systems provide limited support for the full trading cycle. This cycle is composed of the search for partners, offers, bargaining, contracting and settlement. EDI facilitates the exchange of business documents but leaves the coordination-intensive activities to participants; for example, in the search for trading partners or negotiation of prices. In the future, electronic markets (EM) are likely to play a central role in the conduct of business enabling new ways of coordinating exchange relationships among business entities (Ritz, 1993). This development will have profound implications for organizational processes.

## Theme 2: a view that integrates specializations

The word ‘redesign’ assumes that many organizations were designed in the first place. In fact most exhibit a form which has emerged through time. Their evolution has more to do with tradition, style, habits, culture, politics and behaviour than with engineering. Today, the vast majority of organizations are built around the concepts of division of labour, functional specialization, authority and hierarchy. The central feature is to facilitate control and coordination. Boundaries and professions spring up to delineate different aspects of organizational work.

Specialization is respected and propagated by rewards and promotion which are based on excellence at performing in a particular area of speciality. This specialization usually results in incongruent goals and causes functions to be in competition with each other: the accountant wants to increase inventory turns with low inventory levels while the marketing department wishes to reduce delivery times to customers and therefore maintain high inventory. Employees often see themselves working for a function not the organization, particularly if functions become very large. One large UK insurance company has claimed that its actuaries have more loyalty to the profession than to the company who pay their salary.

Indeed, one of the reasons for having a corporate strategy is to provide consistency for functional goals. Interestingly, most universities and business schools actually propagate functional thinking with subjects being taught along specialist lines: accounting, marketing, operations management, etc.

Metaphorically speaking, BPR projects turn the organization on its side. The message being that functional units, as we know them today, which are designed for internal purposes should cease to be the primary focus of attention. Organizations will take on a more horizontal structure constructed by reference to the processes which describe what the organization exists to satisfy (usually a customer) and how it satisfies the customer.

However, BPR does not mean that an organization changes what it does (although for many organizations this may not be a bad thing and may be a natural extension), rather how it is organized and managed. Figure 1 illustrates the notion of process transcending traditional organizational functions.

At British Aerospace (BAe) they recognized that their individual functions were stifling the organization and having a detrimental effect on its competitiveness. While recognizing the role of functions in cultivating and fostering expertise, they sought to keep this positive aspect yet become more process oriented. Multifunctional teams were set up with changes being initiated, such as reward systems, to propagate this new perspective. Today, this multifunctional team orientation has become an accepted part of the BAe organization structure.

Processes have always existed in organizations. However, very often they were not recognized as such and were merely superimposed over the traditional functional organization structure. Unproductive work exists because of functional boundaries which result in many tasks being done simply to satisfy the internal demands of the company's own organization. This is due to checking, reconciling, monitoring, tracking and even replicating activities. Further, the processes themselves when identified may be in need of fundamental redesign: there may be better ways of carrying them out or they may be totally unnecessary.

A key issue often debated is whether or not processes must cross functional boundaries for the redesign initiative to be classified as BPR. We believe this to be problematic and that this distinction is artificial. It depends on how functions are defined within an organization. More importantly, we're not sure it matters.

We find that it is more fruitful to examine the level of granularity at which the redesign is taking place. BPR can be conducted at the highest level in an organization with the organization itself considered as a process which converts capital, labour, information and energy into certain outputs. The organization's strategy, which outlines the relationship of the organization with the external environment, can be conceptualized as the blueprint of this high-level process (Edwards and Peppard, 1994). It is then operationalized at a lower level in terms of the processes that cross functional boundaries that are critical to meeting business objectives. These processes can be further subdivided into subprocesses some of which may be totally within a single function. Some refer to these subprocesses as tasks or activities or procedures. Alternatively, these single-function subprocesses could be considered as processes in their own right which cross subboundaries within the function.

Hence, one organization's subprocess that does not cross functional boundaries is another's process that does cross subboundaries: it all depends upon the level of granularity that is employed when undertaking the analysis.

Consider for example customer order fulfilment. This can be considered as a process that has the objective of satisfying customer needs promptly and efficiently. It contains many subprocesses, customer reception, contract negotiation, order entry, etc., all the way to delivery to the customer, billing and accounts receivable. At the organizational level of analysis accounts receivable may not be seen as a process as it does not cross functional boundaries, yet at the function level of analysis accounts receivable demand actions by the cashier subfunction, the computer input subfunction and debtor control clerks and, hence, it may be viewed as a process. The case of Ford achieving considerable benefit by redesigning its accounts payable is well documented and is proposed as an example of BPR (Hammer, 1990; Butler Cox Foundation, 1991). Surely the distinction is artificial and unimportant.

Yet it is possible to take any process in isolation and redesign it. It is also possible to take a high-level view of the business and first decide what are the processes that it should have in place which are going to contribute to the delivery of the business strategy. It may therefore be fruitful in distinguishing between business process redesign, which is concerned with redesigning any process and business re-engineering, which is concerned with identifying the processes which the organization needs to have in place in the first instance in order to meet the strategic objectives. Table 1 distinguishes between business re-engineering, business process redesign and the concept of continuous improvement, popularized by the total quality management (TQM) philosophy.

The notion of granularity clarifies the distinction that is often made between BPR and business network redesign (BNR). BNR is seen as focusing upon the way in which organizations do business with external entities. The underlying assumption is that the sources of competitive advantage lie partly within a given organization and partly in the larger business network. Is this distinction really necessary? Is not BNR an example of BPR at a high level of granularity with organizations undertaking joint BPR?

Some organizations have only gone half-way in their movement towards a horizontal organization. BT seeks the best of both worlds by having customer facing divisions, but yet still retaining its functional organizational structure. Some organizations have instigated performance contracts (or service level agreements) between functions now contributing to the new process. Process owners 'buy' services to a specified contract from functions. An interesting aside is that if we can specify performance contracts, is this not the first step towards outsourcing?

The majority of the BPR initiatives we have observed have been conducted at the business unit level. However, processes can be also discerned at the corporate level. For example, the process by which investment or divestment decisions are made. If this process is redesigned this could be argued to be BPR at the corporate level. However, corporate restructuring, divesting and investing in business units could hardly be described as BPR: portfolio management would be a better term. The restructuring of ICI into two separate companies is not BPR. Similarly, financial restructuring of a balance sheet, such as changing depreciation policy or tax liability is not BPR.

Belfast-based aircraft manufacturer Short Brothers is an example of a company which transformed itself in the late 1980s, when it decided to cease aircraft manufacturing – which accounted for more than half its revenue – and become a supplier of components to other aircraft manufacturers. The CEO (Chief Executive Officer) of Intel has said that they made a conscious decision to move out of DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory) production in 1985 despite the fact that the company had invested heavily in R&D activity in that area (Sherman, 1993). These are clearly major transformations of product lines and markets: what an organization produces, not transformations of how the organization operates and, hence, not BPR.

## Theme 3: continuous improvement versus redesign

Debate rages whether BPR involves evolutionary or revolutionary change. Some argue that Japanese companies do not engage in revolutionary change but rather continuous incremental improvement. They point to the Japanese word Kaizan as being a key philosophy underlying the performance of Japanese organizations:

many small steps make a journey. However, consideration should also be given to the results; that by focusing on changing the things in an incremental fashion, Japanese companies are so far ahead of many Western competitors that to compete with them only a total redesign, a 'step change', on the Western part will suffice.

Sight should not be lost of the fact that BPR seeks radical performance improvement. Some organizations cannot achieve this objective incrementally: they have to transform themselves. For them BPR will involve significant change, not merely structural but relating to the total organizational form. The extent of this change will depend on where the organization is coming from, its existing structure, its culture, management style, reward systems, etc. and upon the driving forces for change. Some organizations have to make a bigger leap than others. However, many of the reported instances of BPR involve step changes.

Quality management with its emphasis on quality circles seeks improvement through refinement of each activity involved in a process. BPR goes beyond traditional process improvement methods and seeks to radically overhaul and dramatically improve business processes. Small projects are merely fiddling around the edges. While small gains may be achieved, these will not be of the magnitude which BPR seeks.

Western Provident Association, the UK's third largest health insurer has made productivity improvements of 38% from BPR by radically changing how it processes applications for health insurance. Previously the process involved seven staff and took 28 days yet now it takes one person only 4 days. This includes reviewing the medical history, setting up the policy and arranging to collect the money from customers' bank accounts (Financial Times, 1992). US bank Banc One has re-engineered it mortgage process at both the front and back ends. It first replaced desk-to-desk processing with a 'work cell' or team approach that gets the job done weeks faster. On the front end, the sales force now use laptops to transmit the loan application to the regional production centres, where groups of loan analysts with various skills work as a team to approve the mortgage (ComputerWorld, 1992). Both these examples involved significant changes in their respective organizations.

For some organizations, change of the most extreme form is the only way forward. Having determined the organizational success factors for home banking, Midland Bank decided that their existing organization was not an appropriate vehicle, hence they created First Direct. Acting ‘independently’ of the parent it articulated a vision of what was required to deliver this new service.

The benefits of BPR must be considered within the context of the process being redesigned. For example, reducing the number of employees at Ford at 375 as a result of redesigning the accounts payable process is not a great reduction in terms of the total Ford organization. However, when this reduction is considered in the light of the number of employees engaged previously in the accounts payable process then it is very significant.

The message from the BPR projects which we have examined is that it challenges existing ways of operating and conducting business. It is a creative process requiring managers to challenge assumptions which underlie the performance of work. Usually they need to break out of the mind set which influenced their vision of the organization. If we look at the early aviators, they attempted to build planes modelled on how birds fly, flapping wings and all. They were constrained by their mind sets of the birds and therefore their solution space was limited. In discovering the structure of DNA, Watson and Crick had no precedent from which they could logically derive its structure. They, as is required of today's managers, challenged not only existing assumptions but also engaged in a new way of thinking.

## Theme 4: the role of information technology

Generally, the perceived benefits from IT implementations have been poor. Many senior executives feel cheated, not only by IT vendors but also by their own IT staff. Perhaps their expectations were too high, although we doubt it. Many organizations have sought competitive advantage through IT and were disappointed. Examples such as American Airlines' SABRE system, Thomson's TOP reservation system or American Hospital Supplies order processing system are just as difficult to find in the 1990s as they were in the 1980s. Many organizations have learned the hard way that technology itself does not generate sustainable competitive advantage. Rather, advantage comes from the combination of IT, appropriate business processes, effective human behaviour and a strategy to implement the necessary changes.

Most business processes have evolved over the years without the advantage of modern technology. By implementing IT, organizations merely ‘paved the cow path’. With such automation, fundamentally the same job is being done in the same way perhaps resulting in limited efficiency benefits.

BPR actively searches for opportunities to harness the power of information technology in the redesigned process. This makes possible many configurations which would otherwise have been unattainable. However, it is not necessary for the technology which supports the redesigned business processes to be leading edge.

For many of the BPR initiatives which we have examined IT is a key enabler: the revised process would not be possible without IT. The tasks involved in installing a new telephone line at one European PTT used to be handled by many different departments. These tasks included recording a new subscriber, checking whether or not they are financially sound, checking if there is a telephone line available, selecting a free telephone number, confirming the delivery to the customer, connecting the new line in the telephone exchange and, finally, the billing procedure. Using IT and computerized switchboards all these actions can now be completed by just one person behind a single terminal. Ford's accounts payable system is only feasible through the power of IT. Mutual Benefit Life's reduction in the time taken to process a mortgage application from 17 to 3 days is only possible through IT (Cliff, 1992) as is Banc One's previously discussed initiative.

The argument that many new technologies are solutions looking for a problem has been presented many times. But what is wrong with this? Remember what we said earlier about mind sets and the need for challenging existing assumptions. If IT can assist in this challenging process then surely this is good. Neural networks and virtual reality are examples of technologies developed before a particular business need was identified.

As communication costs decrease, new media, such as electronic mail, electronic bulletin boards and computer conferencing can make coordination easier and, therefore, enable the adhocracy to work more effectively. Computer networks can help find and coordinate people with diverse knowledge and skills from many parts of the organization. The duality is that while IT is a key enabler of BPR it can also be the driver with technological developments providing redesign opportunities.

## Theme 5: the critical role of human resources

The history of human resource management has had the same chequered history as information in organizations. Like information, only recently have people been looked upon as a strategic resource. Indeed, it changed its name from personnel management to human resources management (HRM) to give greater emphasis to the human dimension and its critical importance to the organization, as opposed to the personnel management focus on people as merely factors of production.

Drucker (1988) proclaimed some years ago that the organization of the 1990s would be an information-based organization which would be more akin to an orchestra or hospital than the traditional command and control of the military style organization. Crucial to the functioning of this visionary organization is the emphasis on teams and team-work. These teams will normally be composed of members from different specializations. BPR emphasizes the team-working dimension of organizations and seeks to integrate it within the overall organizational form. A commonly reported reason for lack of success is the inability to change traditional work patterns. The companies that have succeeded are those that have made compelling arguments for profound change. The risk of total organizational failure and, hence, unemployment is possibly the most compelling of all reasons.

Prudential Insurance saw cultural change as a necessary first step to BPR. British Aerospace have indicated that one of the lessons they have learned from their programme was that they should have addressed cultural issues earlier and not just at the implementation phase. Xerox contend that at the heart of the changes which it had to make was changing the management philosophy (Rank Xerox, 1992).

Appraisal will need to be based on team performance rather than individual merit. Measurement also moves from an activity focus to an output focus based upon results. The new metrics focus not merely on financial measures, but on performance measures such as quality, time elapsed between order and payment, new product development time, cycle time, time to market, percent of sales from new products, numbers of cooperative efforts and on-time delivery.

BAe credit it new multifunctional team approach for recent success in on-time job completions with employees working beyond their traditional roles to ensure deadlines are met. The associated changes which the company had introduced enabled these new work practices to occur. Performance measurement and appraisal changes also contributed to this behaviour.

Empowerment to a lower organizational level is a key issue for many BPR initiatives. This is necessitated by the move to customer-focused processes which will generally involve moving design-making authority closer to the customer once again speeding up processing in the eyes of the customer.

## Theme 6: management involvement

Given the scale and scope of BPR for most organizations, it requires commitment and involvement from the top. Without both the support and involvement of the top management team any redesign effort is likely to fail. Possibly this is the most often-reported research finding. However, we are somewhat sceptical of this blanket statement. Surely the CEO of Ford need not be involved in redesigning the accounts payable process? Our research indicates that management involvement should be appropriate to the level of granularity of the change. The changes should be supported by the appropriate level of management which may not always be at the apex of the organization.

## So is it hype?

Like the surfer constantly seeking the ultimate wave, we pose the question is this the wave to ride? Or is it going to end like expert systems, the paperless office and current cost accounting, to name but three much-hyped phenomena of the 1980s? In this section we look at what is the evidence that BPR is actually taking place and what is driving organizations to redesign. We also look at the reported success rates and address the question of whether or not BPR is anything new.

## What's the evidence?

If the popular management literature is a barometer of topical issues, then BPR is certainly active as Business Week, International Management, the Economist, Fortune and the Financial Times have all run articles. Noticeably, all were favourable in their comments! We have already drawn on evidence that many organizations are indeed 'doing' BPR. Some of this is based on case reports in the public domain and some is from direct studies which we have undertaken. From press reports and presentations at conferences, many more are claiming to be engaged in BPR initiatives.

The recent European IS conference had a number of papers dealing with the IS aspect of BPR (Whitley, 1993). The Strategic Planning Society has a special interest group on BPR, there is now a Journal of Business Change & Reengineering and an Institute of Business Reengineering. The US publication ComputerWorld initiated an award 'Excellence in Reengineering' in 1992. This award is to recognize an organization's outstanding use of information technology to improve business processes or dramatically transform the way it does business.

A survey conducted by Cranfield School of Management in 1992 sent to over 3000 UK companies in 52 SIC groups revealed that 36% of respondents are either undergoing or have undergone a BPR programme (Edwards and Preece, 1994). The survey reported that 'BPR actually exists as a named phenomenon with UK companies'. A survey by Bristol University and Higham System Services Group of UK financial services companies reported that 65% were already involved in some form of re-engineering activity (Watkins et al., 1993). A recent survey by ComputerWorld reported that 72% of respondents were engaged in business re-engineering (ComputerWorld, 1993). In a critical look at BPR, a report by the OTR Group concluded that BPR is based on real principles and there can be genuine value in applying them (OTR Group, 1993).

There is certainly something out there called BPR.

## What are the driving forces?

The success of European manufacturing and service organizations depends on their ability to satisfy their customers' requirements. However, the market-place within which they operate is substantially different from the less severe competitive market conditions of the past.

The 1990s are characterized by the globalization of markets, intensification of competition, acceleration of product life cycles, disinflation and growing complexity with suppliers, buyers, government, etc. The challenge from Asia and the Pacific Rim countries has become particularly intense, especially in manufacturing.

Technology is continually changing, leading to greater shrinkage of time and distance effects and greater interrelatedness within an industry and organization. It is estimated that over the next 10 years IT performance per pound will improve by over 20% a year. This improvement is not just in reducing the cost of hardware, but the average of all aspects of IT.

To attend to such competitive conditions the focus of management attention has been on strategy. Global marketing, strategic alliances and outsourcing are just some of the strategies which organizations have pursued. While some success has been achieved, it is clear that strategic initiatives have far outdistanced organizational capabilities to implement them. If the 1980s were concerned with strategy we believe that organizational initiatives to action strategy will dominate the 1990s. BPR is one of these.

BPR is not just the domain of the commercial sector. In the public sector the pressures for improvement have been almost as severe. In many OECD nations, governments have begun to inject a 'free market' environment into the public sector as a means of inducing operational improvements, both in terms of cost and service.

## Is BPR risky?

Venkatraman (1991) cites BPR as being one manifestation of the five levels of IT-induced business reconfiguration. Illustrated in Figure 2, he mapped the extent of change against benefits, to derive this framework. However, we believe that this framework is incomplete and that a crucial third dimension needs also to be considered: that of risk. As one moves from level one to level five the risks (as well as the potential rewards) also increase.

BPR is risky and the evidence points to a high failure rate especially among projects which are seeking gains of orders of magnitude: 70% reductions or 10-fold increases. Surely this is to be expected given such high goals. Hammer and Champy (1993) estimate that between 50 and 70% of organizations that undertake re-engineering efforts do not achieve the dramatic results they intended. We would caution against reading too much into these figures: if 80% improvement is sought and only 70% is achieved, is this failure? Perhaps 80% is unattainable.

Yet despite the high level of failure, BPR has been likened to a game of chess rather than roulette. You lose not because of bad luck but because either you don't know the rules or do not have the capability to put them into action.

## Did we not do it in the past?

A question which inevitably arises when management begin considering and investigating BPR is 'did we not do this in the past?' While the words BPR may be new, many firms began 're-engineering' parts of their business before the term was popularized by Hammer (1990). Just look at McDonald's and its service delivery system. However, by giving it a label we have highlighted a particular manifestation and made it more generally known.

Despite some attempts at early redesign, most notably via quality initiatives, organizations still operated with thousands of specialists who are judged and rewarded by how well they perform their separate functions with little knowledge or concern about how they fit into the complex process of turning raw material, capital and labour into products and services. Further, questioning everything a company does is usually too risky and confronts too many entrenched interests among managers and employees to be worth doing unless a firm is in dire trouble. Then it may be too late.

BPR is sometimes seen as organization and methods (O & M) repackaged and renamed for the 1990s. However, while the objective of O & M was to improve efficiency, BPR challenges the very fabric of how work is performed. O & M claimed to challenge existing practices but in reality was usually undertaken by junior people in the organization; most often O & M focused on process improvement rather than on redesign.

BPR is also different from total quality management (TQM), although BPR projects often evolve out of a TQM initiative. Edwards and Preece's (1994) survey indicates that 84% of all companies doing BPR also engaged in

TQM and that only 6% of companies doing BPR have no links with TQM. Fifty-six per cent of those engaged in both TQM and BPR treat them as the same or similar programmes.

TQM generally works within a framework of an organization's existing processes: it concentrates on output and not on how the output is achieved. It also tends to focus on incremental change; it is a performance-enhancement method and not a careful re-examination of basic assumptions. Further, BPR places considerable emphasis on exploiting IT opportunities.

Rank Xerox established its Leadership Through Quality programme in 1983 to move towards a total quality company (Rank Xerox, 1992; Williams, 1993). This strategic initiative led to fundamental changes in the culture of the corporation and has become a way of working for all employees. The programme integrates all employee efforts into a comprehensive method designed to enable them to reach goals, meet or exceed targets and achieve objectives. Late in 1990 they recognized that a functionally aligned hierarchical organization was suboptimal when viewed from the perspective of the customer because it had delays and, when compared with a business process aligned structure, was more expensive and less efficient to operate. As result of BPR, many of the company's processes now take substantially less time; hence now satisfying the customer. For example, specialized individual contracts (for customers requiring, say, special payment or leasing terms) take less than 2 days to process instead of 100.

## Focus on IT

The early conceptualization of BPR in the US had an IT bias. Hammer (1990) called for obliteration of existing ways of working rather than just automating the existing, placing considerable emphasis on IT enabling new ways of performing work. Davenport and Short (1990) related BPR to how companies they studied implemented IT in a way which differed substantially from traditional IT projects. Venkatraman (1991) viewed BPR as one level of IT-induced business reconfiguration. A key feature of BPR is the active search for opportunities to harness the power of information technology in the redesign process.

Given this strong focus on IT some organizations have initiated their BPR project under the auspices of the IT department. A reason often suggested for this is that the IT department is the only group of individuals who frequently operate across the organization, namely, between functions. BPR may be looked upon as a new way to deliver IT systems. John Laing, the UK build construction group, became involved in BPR because many of the systems developed by the IT department failed to meet business requirements. They saw a wall between the IT department and the business.

While IT may be crucial for BPR, it should be remembered that IT is merely an enabling mechanism. Often when BPR is driven by the IT department the focus is on utilizing IT: IT becomes the end as well as the means. This may alienate other departments as they see BPR as a ploy for increasing the IT spend or as a means of lengthening the IT department's backlog, hence defending itself against downsizing. A key obstacle is that the IT department tends to be tarred with the brush of the past which as already explained is not always positive.

A critical problem is that once responsibility is abdicated to the IT department, there may well be little business involvement in the redesign. Business backs away from BPR and it becomes a matter of harnessing technology. The cultural and operational differences between IT and the business has been explored elsewhere but are relevant here also (Grindley, 1991).

## Focus on delivering business strategy

We have observed a small number of organizations that have used business strategy as a key driver of the redesign process. However, we have also seldom found an organization that does not claim this is the case although on deeper probing one becomes a little sceptical. Few organizations have individuals dedicated exclusively to implementing strategy and, hence, the laudable BPR objectives, which are set by senior management, are lost when implementation is attempted.

Within Grand Metropolitan, a process re-engineering cadre of a dozen individuals was established to act as a catalyst for effective change. The team members are highly respected non-IS professionals drawn from other functional areas of the company such as finance and marketing. The group's modus operandi is to act as proactive agents of change: to challenge the status quo. Projects are only undertaken by invitation from business unit management and have to be tangible, benefits-oriented projects. The concept was to build an agenda for important change through a succession of concrete projects. In order to weave this notion of continuous improvement into the style and behaviour of the company, the skills and philosophy of this group would have to be incorporated, through time, into business units. This demands moving the experienced process engineers from the core cadre to the business units and providing attendant staff rotation.

Grand Metropolitan uses BPR to implement what they call a competence-based strategy. This is to be distinguished from the traditional 'structural strategy' which addresses issues such as the composition of the product portfolio, market selection, logistics, acquisitions and divestments. Such strategic decisions are easily copied by competitors. The competence-based strategy deals with establishing excellence in the core competencies necessary to operate effectively in its chosen market-place. For Grand Metropolitan, critical competencies include things such as managing individual brands, product launching, market penetration and manufacturing and operational excellence. They are not readily recognized by competitors as key strengths and they are neither easily nor quickly copied. But it is these competency-based strengths that enable to the company to react, adapt and prosper in such a volatile and competitive environment. Once the key competencies are recognized they become the focus of redesign effort (Grindley, 1991; Ward and Peppard, 1994).

## Focus on quality

Despite the distinction made earlier between BPR and quality management, many BPR projects do originate out of quality initiatives. Cynically, one could argue that if a quality management initiative is proving difficult to maintain then a change of name could prove advantageous. As Table 2 illustrated there are a number of common themes between the two although there are also fundamental principles of differences particularly in relation to continuous improvement versus redesign (see theme 3 earlier).

Through their quality management initiatives many organizations have recognized the need for BPR if substantial objectives are to be met. An example of this would be BT where the process management initiative arose out of the quality programme. One can only speculate at this stage if the methods for quality management which BT are generally employing will prove ineffective for BPR. Similarly, the BPR initiative at Rank Xerox arose as a result of their TQM project when they decided that process redesign was necessary to give the substantial benefits they were looking for.

## Focus on operations

A number of organizations, especially pharmaceutical, engineering, automotive and aerospace companies, have adopted concurrent (or simultaneous) engineering approaches. This seeks to ensure that marketing, design and manufacturing work closely together on new product development. The object is partly design for manufacturability but also to reduce the time it takes to bring a new product to market. There is a clear emphasis on cross-functional teams.

British Aerospace recognized that if they were to survive they needed to change attitudes and values in the organization to reflect the importance of cost-effectiveness and performance as the key competitive drivers. To achieve these, they foresaw a move towards focused teams supported by improved resource management. They examined a number of initiatives such as TQM and simultaneous engineering but saw the TQM message being difficult to communicate and not very relevant to their business. They saw simultaneous engineering as being the only route forward. Apart from being relevant to engineering, it requires integration with other functions and forces changes in processes. Simultaneous engineering is BAe's interpretation of implementing BPR.

They realized that existing processes had just evolved rather than having been the result of any conscious design effort. The initiative began by identifying key processes: marketing, selling, development, making standard products, customization, support and new product introduction. With their existing planning approach there was a strong focus on functions, dependencies were critical, the scope of the top level plan needed to be 100% accurate and the plan was thus fragile, requiring constant revision. This led to what they termed 'the blame culture'. Such an organizational form required project managers coordinating activities across functions.

With the simultaneous engineering approach multifunctional teams are key. There is a focus on goals, the top level plan is based upon the need to change, dependencies are less critical as they are dealt with by the team and members develop ‘mutual role acknowledgement’ in which each understands the contribution of others and this generates what BAe call ‘an achievement culture’.

## Focus on human resources

Some organizations, like BAe, have indicated that if they were engaging in a BPR exercise again, they would address the people issues first. Some have done this from the outset and attempted business process redesign from the HRM perspective. The emphasis of such programmes is on creating a culture for change whilst leaving existing processes intact.

The Prudential Insurance Company commented that changing a mind set is always a problem, but when existing processes are left intact it proves even more difficult. They concluded this after having attempted both TQM and customer care programmes. With hindsight, they now proclaim that both should have been preceded by a cultural change programme with the strong emphasis upon human resources.

## Focus on creating a new organization

Some organizations recognize that change is considerably more difficult than creation and, hence, they attempt to achieve change by creating a completely new 'son of' organization. This is one way an organization might attempt a fresh start without having to unfreeze the cultural and technological legacy of the past. This is not a new manifestation of organizational life: IBM used this philosophy when they undertook the development of the PC.

Midland Bank is an example of an organization who set up a new subsidiary, namely First Direct, to implement telephone banking. Previous attempts by a number of organizations to set up similar operations were less successful than envisaged. One of the reasons for this failure may be the vested interest of branch management which is in direct competition with telephone banking.

## Lessons

What are the lessons to be learned from the work which has been undertaken so far in BPR? What conclusions, if any, can be drawn? Bear in mind that BPR is a recent phenomenon and, hence, the lessons are tentative yet instructive.

## BPR and business strategy's mutual dependency

BPR must be underpinned by business strategy and BPR will itself underpin business strategy. However, the relationship between BPR and business strategy is not straightforward. Accepted wisdom suggests that the organization vision drives business strategy. Yet the content of the business strategy is usually expressed narrowly in terms of products and markets. Scant attention is paid to defining and understanding key skills which underlie current success and how the business relates to its environment, yet the evidence seems to point to these as providing the key link between BPR and business strategy (Edwards and Peppard, 1994).

This perspective is consistent with the emerging thinking on strategy which emphasizes the ‘behavioural’ aspects of strategy. The notion of core competencies and organizational capabilities embody this new behavioural focus. Hamel and Prahalad (1990) define core competencies as the combination of individual technical and production skills that underlie a company’s myriad of product lines. Stalk et al. (1992) argue that competencies emphasize technical and production expertise at specific points along the value chain and that capabilities are more broadly based. They see capabilities as core processes that underpin the business strategy. So, for example, a strategic capability of Grand Metropolitan is its ability to take an unknown brand, invest in it and create a need in the eyes of the customer for this product. The central element of Grand Metropolitan’s BPR is to enhance those vital processes that form part of their organizational capabilities. The link from business strategy to BPR is very clear in Grand Metropolitan.

United Parcel Services have adopted a similar perspective. They determined in the early 1980s that the leader in information management would be the leader in international package distribution. Giant US mass retailer Wal-Mart took a close look at its industry value chain and at its own comparative advantage and decided to reframe the competitive challenge. They concluded that the business they were really in was not retailing at all, but communications and transportation logistics. They then focused on redesigning and improving these processes which enabled them to catapult themselves to be the leading retailer in the US.

## Understanding the context of the process being redesigned

Undertaking BPR demands an understanding not only of the purpose of the process under review but also an understanding of the purposes and methods employed by each of the processes at higher levels of granularity. For example, to redesign a process within a function demands understanding of that function as well as of the organization as a whole, the industry within which the organization competes and society in general. Redesigning a process without reference to higher levels of granularity can result in suboptimality. Examining a process in the light of higher level processes may reveal that the process which is a candidate for redesign should be obliterated (to use Hammer's (1990) terminology).

## Recognizing the interdependency of organizational elements

The interdependency of organizational elements is well recognized: the McKinsey 7-S model with structure, systems, management style, shared values, roles, responsibilities and skills, illustrates this. Changing one element in isolation while leaving the others unchanged results in dissonance. For example, setting up teams but still appraising and rewarding people as individuals is very likely to mitigate against the required change. Similarly, setting up teams without giving members the skills to work as a group is inconsistent.

To illustrate, a number of organizations we have examined have attempted to implement a process-oriented organization yet still maintain the traditional accounting control system. One financial institution in particular redesigned a number of processes but retained its budgeting system. To reduce the costs of running branches, an instruction was issued to the effect that no unnecessary phone contact between branches and headquarters was to take place. This resulted in phone calls being replaced by paper memos which not only resulted in overflowing the internal mail system but critically increasing the time customers had to wait for a response from requests made via branches. The functional budgeting system had defeated the purpose of the BPR.

## Acceptance of change as an important prerequisite

Probably one of the greatest barriers to change is the assumption that it simply happens or that people must simply change because it is necessary to do so. Our research would seem to indicate that organizations which have TQM, JIT or customer care programmes in place prior to BPR have more success. This observation does not mean that these are necessary prerequisites but rather suggests that the acceptance of change is a necessary precondition. If an organization is about to engage in BPR considerable attention must be given to cultivate the need to change.

## Recognizing that benefits take time to manifest themselves

The major benefits of BPR take time to appear. The radical changes which BPR entails do not happen immediately and the necessary changes take time to appear. Culture, employee behaviour and attitudes are not something which readily change. As such, managers should be insulated from any short-term penalties which may arise while waiting for the bigger benefits. The necessity of quick hits to keep the enthusiasm and momentum going should not be disregarded. However, be aware that these are not necessarily the longer-term benefits.

## Being clear about the purpose of the redesign

It is self-evident that clarity in purpose is useful, however in the area of BPR this is vital. That this occurs as BPR, to some, is synonymous with quality and, hence, the vision and techniques are those of process ‘tinkering’, indicating optimization of an existing process design but not redesign. Others clearly focus upon redesign. The benefits, the tools and the management interactions differ substantially between tinkering and redesign and clarity of purpose is therefore not only desirable but essential.

## Being aware how the purpose is conveyed

A number of projects which we observed were less than successful because of the means by which the purpose was conveyed. The need for business change should be generally recognized and then success is more likely. A convincing argument for change must be made and conveyed in the appropriate manner. Communication is therefore of crucial important.

The evidence suggests that if the objective of BPR is short-term cost reduction then it is likely to be unsuccessful. In fact if short-term cost cutting is the objective a 'tinkering' approach is likely to be successful. Further, if BPR has an employee head count reduction purpose in which BPR means redundancy, then subtlety in conveying purpose is required.

## Having the redesign driven by the 'business' as a whole

The argument that IS strategy should be driven by the business (Earl, 1989; Ward et al., 1990) does not hold true for BPR initiatives. To explain, BPR initiatives should be driven by the management of the unit to the appropriate level of granularity for the process under study. The redesign of Ford's accounts payable illustrates this point.

Although IT is an enabler of business redesign the evidence suggests that successful BPR projects are driven by the business and not the IT department. While IT traditionally looks across the business, it generally concerns itself with process automation not redesign. IT seeks automation from the point of view of processing efficiency rather than business effectiveness. It is debatable whether IT departments have the authority, reputation or motivation to conduct redesign. Even if they do have authority, whether they can implement the redesign is questionable.

Clearly the IT department does have a part to play but this should be supportive not directive. IT professionals may well facilitate the inductive thinking involved in redesign and should therefore be part of the redesign team. The evidence suggests that successful BPR is driven by the unit undergoing the change and that IT is involved.

## Being careful of the assumption of a single redesign

Being aware of the limitations of the Lewin (1951) model of change, unfreeze, change, refreeze, is widely referred to in the change management literature. While unfreezing is necessary for change, once refreezing has occurred, it is very difficult to unfreeze again within the immediate future. The desirable approach is to unfreeze, change and then create a state ready for more change, but not frozen, which removes the possibility of more change. The BPR initiative of one building society floundered as they could not unfreeze the second time once refreezing of the first phase of their project had occurred.

## Don't forget the 'son of' strategy

Sometimes the necessary degree of change is deemed not to be realistically possible. In such circumstances the creation of a new organization without the legacy of the past may be an appropriate strategy. However, this should be balanced against the loss of expertise and skills in the old organization which may prove difficult to transfer.

## IT provides opportunities for new designs

Conventional wisdom suggests that the new organizational blueprint should be developed first and then the role of technology in supporting this form should be articulated. This, however, is focusing on implementation rather than on the enabling role of IT. It is often desirable to employ inductive thinking when developing creative new designs. This also suggests that IT professionals should be members of the BPR team.

## Building ‘walls’ around processes to give customer focus but creating ‘windows’ between processes for efficiency

The general argument underpinning BPR is to break down the walls between functions and that such walls should cease to exist. The argument runs that building walls around processes, like putting blinkers on horses, enhances the customer focus. However, such an approach greatly reduces functional efficiency unless there is commonality of design between related elements of different processes. Specialist expertise may be needed across the various processes and this needs to be retained and cultivated. This grouping of specialists is necessary for the sharing of knowledge and expertise. Effectively, BPR demolishes the walls between functions and builds windows between processes.

## Using appropriate metrics

Many organizations have adopted new strategies and innovative operating processes intended to achieve breakthrough performance, but then continue to use the same short-term financial indicators they have used for decades.

Where a function supports numerous processes money was used as the common measure of performance and adherence to standards of input (i.e. budgetary control). However, with BPR processes are the focus of attention and measures which describe what the process achieves are more appropriate. For example, measures of time to market, customer satisfaction, cycle time, throughput time, defective rates as well as cost are process-oriented measures.

## Being wary of the title

BPR has taken on many meanings to many people. Sometimes to the detriment of the programme at which it is aimed. Be aware that the title BPR can of itself be negative and if necessary a title more appropriate to an organization or one that reflects its purpose should be employed.

## BPR and defining service level agreements are essential prior to outsourcing

Outsourcing has become a fashionable strategy for many organizations. Some report satisfaction with such a strategy while others are less positive. A sensible prerequisite of outsourcing any activity is to develop an understanding of where and how that subprocess supports the process and possibly to begin with outsourcing the subprocess to an existing internal function for a service level agreement (SLA) to be developed and honed. The SLA can subsequently form the basis of the contract with the external outsourcer. Engaging in high-level planning for BPR also gives a better understanding of the business and where all the processes contribute towards delivering the strategy.

## Involving stakeholders in the redesign process

As one company put it ‘wear the customer’s shoes’. Customers should be involved in the BPR programme, especially where the process has direct relevance for them. They can give valuable insight and suggest ways in which the process could be redesigned. This may also prove useful in cultivating a closer relationship and have a positive effect on the volume of business conducted with the customer.

## Recognizing the changing role and characteristics of the managers

The characteristics of a process-oriented organization demands a new type of management style. The manager is more of a consultant or coach than has typically been the case. The move towards a customer focus and team organization with specialists working closely together requires a new managerial model. ## Business schools and education

Many business schools need to re-engineer their own processes. This is not just a case of redesigning administrative procedures but a much deeper re-engineering which challenges the very fabric of the traditional business school. The idea of a school grouped around functional specializations, each existing both to promote and preserve their domain of knowledge is very questionable for the future. If we recognize that process is the cornerstone of the organization, why do business schools generally not teach subjects or themes which cross traditional functional areas?

## Hype, hope or hypocrisy?

Many of what are seemingly management fashions do have fundamental messages which can be missed because of hype. When we blow away the spray enveloping the BPR wave we see that its fundamental principles are sound.

A number of organizations have recognized the problem of a fundamentally good idea being surrounded by hype: however, there is something out there called BPR. It does offer hope for European industry to improve its performance dramatically. We have also observed the hypocrisy surrounding BPR, with some organizations claiming to be doing it, yet they are merely engaging in minor incremenetal improvement. They are paying lip service to what BPR entails.

There is a distinct possibility that the three letters 'BPR' may fade away in the next 3 years. However, the fundamental rethinking which organizations have been forced to engage in is here to stay. While BPR as a label may disappear the driving forces for BPR are likely to remain with us and possibly intensify.

BPR changes not only the way we organize but also how we think about organizations. However, it does not offer a miracle cure; considerable effort is needed to make it work successfully. Like the surfer, one must be careful with the terrain which lies underneath. Yet, we think that this is a wave to ride!

## Biographical notes

Chris Edwards worked in industry for 12 years, involved in developing business systems. During that time he qualified as a chartered management accountant and, hence, tended to specialize in financial information systems. He joined the Scottish Business School as a Lecturer in 1971. After receiving a PhD he moved to Carnegie-Mellon University, Pittsburgh and then to the Chair of Management Information Systems at Cranfield.

He is particularly interested in the application of executive support systems and the use of information systems to gain competitive advantage. Books published recently include The Essence of Information Systems, two books on the Data Protection Act and two editions of Information Technology and the Law. He has published extensively in journals and magazines both in the UK and the USA.

He is retained by a number of international organizations advising them on strategic information systems issues.

Joe Peppard is EC Management Fellow at the Cranfield School of Management. An honours Business Studies Graduate at Trinity College, Dublin, he previously spent 5 years lecturing at his Alma Mater. He has considerable expertise in the areas of information systems strategy, business re-engineering, electronic data interchange and executive information systems and has consulted with many European organizations in these areas.

Joe was the leader of the pan-European business re-engineering research project at Cranfield. His publications include IT Strategy for Business (Pitman, 1993) as well as articles in both academic and general business journals. He is joint author of Business Process Re-engineering (Prentice-Hall, 1995).
