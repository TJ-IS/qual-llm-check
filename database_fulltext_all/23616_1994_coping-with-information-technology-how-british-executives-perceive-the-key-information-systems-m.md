# Coping with information technology? How British executives perceive the key information systems management issues in the mid-1990s

ROBERT D. GALLIERS, YASMIN MERALI and LAURA SPEARING
Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

Survey research amongst senior information systems (IS) and non-IS executives in UK organizations was conducted in order to identify their views as to the most important and problematic issues they face in managing information systems. The survey mirrored similar research that has been undertaken in the USA and elsewhere, thus enabling international comparisons in the future. The aim of this paper is to provide comparisons of (i) the views expressed by IS executives vis à vis those with a 'non-IS' role and (ii) the findings of this study with those of a previous similar British study undertaken in 1987.

## Introduction

Organizations in the 1990s are heavily committed to investments in information technology (IT) and communications technology and strategists are acutely aware of the need to exploit IT effectively for business advantage. The rapid pace of technological advances and IT's impact on the changing competitive environment is causing organizations to evaluate critically their management of information and technology resources in order to meet their strategic objectives.

Over the past decade various issues such as IS strategy formulation, IT for competitive advantage and business process redesign have captured the attention of writers and practitioners in the IS and business strategy arena. Often because of their novelty, such issues have occupied centre stage in deliberations about organizational development and management of IT resources, overshadowing older, more pressing issues which stand in the way of organizational development.

The survey-based research reported in this paper aims to collate the views of British executives regarding the relative critical status that they attach to a range of issues in IS management, both currently and in five years' time. It is recognized that the historical issue of the 'culture gap' between IS and non-IS managers may influence the views of the responding managers with regard to the relative importance and problematic nature of the various IT and organizational issues. The survey responses are analysed with a view to identifying areas of convergence and divergence for the perceptions of the two groups.

The responses of the IS managers in the current survey are compared with those of the previous similar British study undertaken in 1987 (Parker and Idundun, 1988)

with a view to gaining some insight into trends associated with the various issues over the ten year period 1987–1997.

## Methodological considerations

Given the desire to obtain a reasonably large sample and to provide direct comparisons with the previous UK study (Parker and Idundun, 1988), a decision was made to undertake a postal survey. Times 1000 companies were surveyed, together with a small number of not-for-profit organizations. A total of 98 usable responses was obtained, but the limited number of not-for-profit organizations responding precluded any reasonable comparison with the private sector.

Clearly, there are a number of limitations associated with this research method (Galliers, 1992a), not the least of which is the fact that the sample is self-selecting (in that only those who are inclined to participate do so). In addition, the views expressed by a single individual in a particular organization cannot be interpreted to be those generally held in that organization. There may also be different interpretations of the questions posed.

The latter concern can be alleviated somewhat by piloting the survey instrument in order to identify potential problems of this sort. This was the case in this research study and amendments were made to the initial research instrument following feedback from a small number of IS executives. In addition, each of the issues included in the questionnaire were explained, as in the case of other similar survey instruments (e.g. Watson, 1989; Niederman et al., 1991).

Despite the shortcomings of the postal survey method, it is argued that the approach does provide some useful data in the context of theory building (Galliers and Land, 1988; Jarvenpaa, 1988), the idea being that the resulting conclusions will be tested in later research. It is anticipated, therefore, that follow-up research will be undertaken with a view to

(1) testing the results of the postal survey;

(2) providing more qualitative data through follow-up interviews and case study research of the kind reported by Clark (1992) in his US study.

As indicated above, the researchers were concerned with retaining as much as possible from earlier similar research (e.g. Niederman et al. (1991) in the USA) to enable international comparisons and comparisons with the only other similar survey to have been undertaken in the UK (Parker and Idundun, 1988). In addition, however, we were at pains to avoid some of the limitations of this prior research.

For example, many of the American surveys (e.g. Ball and Harris, 1982) have relied on responses from members of the Society of Information Management who, through their membership, would be apprised of the key issues as seen by leading academics and professionals through seminars, conferences and the written word. As a consequence, there may well have been an element of self-fulfilling prophesy in the responses thus obtained! Furthermore, these surveys adopted the Delphi method with a view to obtaining a consensus as to the key issues amongst senior IS executives, with no comparison being made of the viewpoints of their non-IS colleagues.

In our survey this problem was avoided by not relying on responses from any particular interest group. Furthermore, we believed that a crucial finding might be the differing views of not just IS executives but also managers from other functional areas/backgrounds. As a result, we polled opinions from both IS and non-IS executives and were satisfied that responses from a single round of questioning would suffice, given our desire to identify differences of opinion rather than to attempt to construct consensus. This is not to argue that the Delphi approach is not appropriate. Indeed, it has the benefit of enabling respondents to add new issues, which can be incorporated into later rounds of questioning. In this instance, however, we were interested in identifying the differing opinions of British executives, as well as those issues about which there tended to be similar opinions being expressed.

Many of the previous surveys asked respondents to identify the critical issues they believed they faced $^{1}$ . One might question whether the respondents interpreted the use of the word critical to mean important or problematic or both. Our survey instrument was designed to distinguish between the perceived importance of each issue and the problematic status accorded to it. A total of 26 issues were identified from previous studies, primarily those of Parker and Idundun (1988) and Niederman et al. (1991) and from recent literature (e.g. Earl, 1989; Davenport and Short, 1990). Respondents were asked to rate each issue in terms of its importance and the extent to which it presented problems, on a scale of 1–10. The issues are presented and defined in the Appendix.

In analysis, we hypothesized that those issues that were perceived to be both important and problematic were indeed critical. Those that were important but relatively less problematic were singled out as issues requiring a watching brief, in case problems did arise. Those that were problematic, but relatively unimportant were labelled irritants and those that were neither important nor problematic were clearly non-critical.

The grid illustrated in Figure 1 was used to discern those issues believed to be either critical or non-critical, those which are irritants and those over which a watching brief is required. The four quadrants were divided by calculating mean scores on the x- and y-axes ( $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ , respectively).

The confidence intervals $(\mu = \bar{x}1.96S/\sqrt{N}$ where S is the sample standard deviation, $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean and N is the number of observations in sample) were also calculated and are shown by the dotted lines on either side of the mean scores. The confidence intervals are provided to indicate that one cannot be confident that any issue falling between the dotted lines either side of $\bar{x}$ should appear above or below $\bar{x}$ – similarly for $\bar{y}$ . For example, any issue outside the vertical dotted line on the left of the grid, but inside the horizontal dotted lines, might be seen as falling in the watching brief or the non-critical category. One could have no confidence in labelling any issue that falls within the dotted square surrounding the intersection of the two means at the centre of the grid.

## Key issues for IS managers in 1987

The results of the 1987 survey of IS managers (Parker and Idundun, 1988) are displayed in Table 1. As stated earlier, that survey was confined to IS managers who were asked to rate the importance to their jobs of the 23 issues shown. Managers were not asked to define the relative problematic status of these issues.

It is clear that in 1987, IS managers across the board considered issues related to IS strategy to be the most important ones, with goal alignment, strategic systems and planning heading the table of important issues. The thrust of strategists and planners was very much towards the exploitation of IT for competitive advantage and in this context the education of senior management was ranked as the fourth most important issue.

In relation to the IS function, the management focus was on the recruitment and training and productivity of IS developers, with end-user computing, information centres and fourth generation languages being viewed as important developments for the management and organization of the IS development activity in this context.

Issues such as data utilization, data security and telecommunications were also ranked highly. These issues may be viewed as precursors of the 1990s concepts of information infrastructures and integrated systems development. The focus was on internal systems, and external data was considered to be the least important issue.

## The 1992 survey: results and discussion

## Key findings

The most critical IS management issues both now and in five years' time for all respondents and the contrasting views expressed by IS managers and non-IS managers are summarized in Tables 2–5, respectively.

For all respondents, it would appear that, in the majority of cases at least, the same issues are ranked highly both in terms of importance and the problems currently being experienced, with each of the following appearing as one of the top five issues in both cases.

\- Improving IS strategic planning.

• Data as a corporate resource.

• Business process redesign.

\- Developing an information architecture.

• Quality of software development.

An exception to this general rule is the appearance of the role and contribution of IS and decision support systems for executive decision making appearing as top ten issues in terms of presenting a problem currently (ranked eighth and tenth, respectively), but being seen as somewhat less important relatively, being ranked thirteenth and eighteenth in this respect. Conversely, while information security and control is seen as being relatively important (ranked sixth), it is (apparently) not a cause for current concern (ranked nineteenth).

Similar issues appear likely to remain with us in five years' time according to our respondents, but a new issue to appear on the scene, both in terms of its importance (ranked third) and the problems it is likely to present (ranked first), relates to the management of distributed systems. An associated issue, that of managing telecommunications, rises to ninth place in terms of its perceived importance, but interestingly appears quite low in the rankings in terms of being problematic, ranking only twentieth.

Generally speaking, our respondents show a degree of confidence in rating the issues: in practically all cases they rate each issue higher on the importance scale than on the problematic scale and they see the issues giving them less cause for concern in five years' time than now. Two issues that buck this trend are managing distributed systems and security and control, both of which are perceived to retain their problematic rating (the changes being just +0.02 and -0.03, respectively). Conversely, many of the issues are seen to be relatively more important in five years' time than they are currently. This is particularly true in relation to managing distributed systems and decision support/executive information systems, the changes in the ratings being +1.25 and +1.06, respectively. Quality of software development is one of the very few issues perceived to decline in importance over the five year period: down 0.38.

There are, of course, a number of issues over which there appears to be a measure of agreement between the IS and non-IS executives who responded to our questionnaire. These include the high ratings, in terms of their perceived importance currently, given to

\- business process redesign;

\- improving IS strategic planning;

• data as a corporate resource;

\- security and control;

• quality of software development.

Three of these issues are also to be found high in the ratings of problematic issues of both groups namely

• quality of software development;

\- improving IS strategic planning;

\- data as a corporate resource.

Note, however, that in the case of two of the issues – improving IS strategic planning and quality of software development – the non-IS executives rate these as being of greater importance and presenting greater difficulties than do their IS colleagues.

Interesting differences between the two groups include the greater emphasis currently given by the IS executives to business process redesign, the education of senior management and the role and contribution of the IS function, as compared to their non-IS colleagues. Conversely, greater emphasis is currently given by the non-IS executives to developing an information architecture and managing distributed systems. On this evidence, it would seem that the non-IS executives are concerned with having an overall picture of corporate information requirements and see the advent of distributed data processing as causing certain control problems which they believe may not be adequately dealt with. IS executives continue to place IT centre stage in terms of enabling radical business change (cf. business process redesign), but perceive a non-educated senior management as an obstacle to overcome if this is to be achieved. Their concern with reviewing the role and contribution of the IS function is understandable in this context, but this is a concern that is not shared to the same extent by their non-IS colleagues.

A somewhat more confused picture emerges when considering the difference in opinion between the two groups in terms of what they perceive to be the most important and problematic issues in five years' time. For example, while business process redesign is ranked by IS executives as equal third amongst the important issues, it falls to tenth place in the estimation of their non-IS colleagues. Similarly, IS's role and contribution and managing end-user computing, while further down the rankings, are seen to be relatively more important by the IS group.

When it comes to the problematic issues, the IS group place managing distributed systems, measuring IS effectiveness and IS's role and contribution relatively higher in the rankings, while the converse is true for data as a corporate resource and determining IS funding levels.

The grid illustrated in Figure 1 was used to discern those issues believed to be either critical or non-critical, those which are irritants and those over which a watching brief is required.

The results of the analysis based on this grid are presented in Figures 2–4. As will be seen, it is generally the case that the respondents tended to classify the issues in either the critical or non-critical quadrants: there are only a very few issues to appear in the watching brief or irritants sectors. Improving information systems strategic planning is viewed as a key issue for the next five years by both groups of managers. The major changes for IS strategists to accommodate over the next five years include the increasing strategic importance of external data and the actualization of effective organizational information infrastructures.

With respect to the establishment of information infrastructures, management of distributed systems is emerging as a critical issue. This is consistent with the generally observed move towards decentralization and distributed systems. As technological advances enable increasingly distributed processing, it is clear that the executives responding to our survey identify with the opportunities and challenges associated with this issue, such as utilization of external databases, electronic data interchange (EDI), ensuring consistency of data, physically connecting computers linked by different networks, controlling wasteful duplication of effort and establishing guidelines to enable access to a variety of users in disparate locations as requiring their attention.

Whilst they perceive that the management of distributed systems will be a critical issue in five years' time, both groups of managers appear to be relatively confident of organizational competencies in technical areas: telecommunications is expected to continue being important but not problematic and the integration of systems will not be critical. This suggests that the organizational, structural and information management issues are thought likely to be more problematic than technical ones in this respect. Consistent with this is the increasing perceived importance of security and control.

With respect to information management, non-IS managers rate the development of an information architecture as the second most critical issue now, but becoming relatively less critical in five years' time. This suggests that managers feel confident in their ability to begin to deal effectively with this issue within the five year time frame. Consistent with this observation, data as a corporate resource remains a critical issue over the next five years for both IS and non-IS managers.

Whilst these observations suggest that there is a consistent view regarding the importance of the concept of corporate information infrastructures, a degree of incongruence in perception is suggested by the low importance accorded currently to enabling mechanisms (such as the integration of systems) and the utilization of external data for the realization of this concept.

IS for competitive advantage remains a critical issue for the next five years whilst the role and contribution of the IS function is considered to be non-critical by both groups of managers. This implies an existing degree of stability regarding the corporate role of IS, despite the recent increasing use of outsourcing services in the UK. However, there are some interesting differences with respect to the issues the respondents believe to be important and problematic for the exploitation of IT in their organizations.

For example, whilst IS managers rate business process redesign as the most critical issue for the next five years, non-IS managers indicate that their most critical issues will continue to be ones related to the establishment of stable IS infrastructures, with business process redesign becoming relatively less critical in five years' time. It is possible that non-IS managers perceive that business process redesign is contingent on the establishment of flexible organizational information infrastructures, whilst IS managers are more inclined to focus on the pro-active role of IS/IT in generating organizational and business change. In this context, it is interesting to note that issues related to organizational growth and development (such as organizational learning, organizational alignment and education of senior management) are currently considered to be more important by IS managers than they are by their non-IS counterparts, although there is a convergence of views regarding the critical status of the first two of these issues in five years' time. The finding in regard to education of senior management may be symptomatic of a situation where the willingness of IS managers to integrate the IS function within business functions exceeds the preparedness of non-IS managers to integrate IS in their corporate decision-making.

The quality of software development is perceived to be a critical issue for the next five years by non-IS managers. The fact that the IS managers, on the other hand, predict that this issue will become less problematic over time suggests that they have a relatively greater measure of confidence in their own ability to manage the problem successfully. In connection with this issue, it is disturbing to note that measuring IS effectiveness is considered to be non-critical by non-IS managers. Issues of quality cannot be divorced from issues of effectiveness if improvements in the IS development process are to be evaluated, monitored and managed.

Similarly, it is interesting – and perhaps a little worrying – to note that human resources for IS is a non-critical issue for both groups of managers. This suggests a failure to recognize the importance of staff development for stakeholders ('users' and 'developers') in order to empower them to become more effective actors in the utilization of IT and in shaping the outcomes of the IS development process. This is particularly important in view of the rapidly changing role of IS in organizations. This also implies that the predominant management culture is one where considerations of human resources for IS development are confined to issues of the availability of technically qualified systems specialists. These findings, especially when combined with the notion that determining IS funding levels is becoming an irritant to non-IS managers, suggest a somewhat narrow focus in managing IS in British companies.

The concern with quality issues coupled with the move towards distributed and integrated systems may be responsible for the waning importance of information centres and managing end-user computing. It is possible that these options are becoming less attractive as a basis for the management of systems development in view of the need to establish more integrated infrastructures and the problems in managing the ownership, responsibility and control issues for end-user developed systems. The fact that IS managers have traditionally been responsible for the support of end-user computing and their wish to retain control may account for the relatively higher importance they accord to it when compared with their non-IS colleagues. Compared to other issues, managing end-user computing is considered to be more of an irritant than anything else, however.

Both groups of executives appear to be relatively disinterested in specialized application developments such as expert systems and artificial intelligence and computer integrated manufacturing (CIM). This may be an indication that applications of this type currently have clear roles within organizations and have been absorbed successfully into business practice or that British managers hold out little expectation as to the opportunities that such technologies provide to their organizations. One would expect CIM to be rated as considerably more important by executives in the manufacturing sector, of course.

Conversely, both groups of managers expect the role of decision support systems to increase significantly in importance, emerging as a critical issue for non-IS managers in five years' time. There is an expectation, in other words, that IT will at last deliver on its promises to provide support to executive decision-making. The advent of executive information systems has clearly had an impact in this regard.

## Key issues for IS managers in 1992: a comparison with results from 1987

Figure 5 illustrates the changing relative importance of various issues over the period 1987–1997, as perceived by IS managers.

In the 1987 survey (Parker and Idundun, 1988) IS managers across the board considered issues related to IS strategic planning to be the most important ones, with goal alignment, strategic systems and planning heading the table of important issues for IS managers. In 1992, IS strategy formulation and goal alignment are not viewed as distinct issues that can be considered in isolation and have become integrated into business strategy formulation. Information systems strategic planning continues to be the most critical issue for IS executives but the focus of planning has evolved considerably over the past five years.

In 1987 many organizations, emerging from the contagion stage of their use of IT (Nolan, 1979), were beginning to harness the computing power and IS expertise in their organizations to support corporate goals. The formulation of IS strategies and the alignment of IS portfolios with business requirements were key issues for IS managers, not only because of the business importance of these issues but also because organizational experience in dealing with these issues was relatively low. IT for competitive advantage was ranked as the second most important issue by IS managers in 1987 and organizations were engaged in trying to identify strategic systems that would lead to sustainable competitive advantage (Porter and Millar, 1985). In the mid-1990s, IT for competitive advantage is given a lower ranking by IS managers, possibly because organizations are realizing that it is becoming increasingly difficult to achieve sustained competitive advantage through innovative use of technology alone (Clemons and Row, 1991).

In 1987, issues related to the management and integration of organizational information resources were emerging as important (data utilization ranked ninth and data security ranked seventh). With respect to communications technology, Parker and Idundun (1988) asserted that the merging of ‘telecommunications and computing technology has made telecommunications a more urgent concern for IS departments’. The integration of systems and new telecommunications options were ranked relatively low in terms of their importance and the concept of distributed systems did not appear in the set of ‘here and now’ issues. The focus of planners was on internal information resource development and exploitation, with external data coming at the bottom of their list of important issues.

In the mid-1990s organizations are committed to the concept of developing integrated information infrastructures (Galliers, 1993) and issues such as developing an information architecture, data as a corporate resource and security and control are featuring as important ones. Communications technology has evolved rapidly over the last five years, becoming integrated into business information systems and has resulted in the appearance of new issues on the management agenda. In contrast to the 1987 survey results, management of distributed systems is a critical issue for IS managers today, with external data rising in the rankings in five years' time.

It is interesting to note that integration of technologies is not ranked as highly important in either of the surveys. The reasons for the low rank may be very different for the two cases, however. In 1987, the focus of IS professionals was on the development of corporate databases and using telecommunications technology for providing local area networks (LANs), often for compatible PCs. The concept of developing integrated information infrastructures has become a popular one in the 1990s and as discussed later, the technical task of integrating systems appears to be less daunting for managers than the related information management and control issues. This observed confidence in dealing with technological integration may be due to the popular acceptance of the concept of open systems and international standards implementation together with the fact that many organizations have implemented IT strategies with a view to alleviating compatibility problems.

For issues related to the implementation of IS strategies, the 1987 survey presented a scenario where the skills shortage for IT professionals was acute and productivity of IT staff was a key issue. End-user computing was becoming established within organizations and managers were generally optimistic about the potential of fourth generation languages for improving productivity significantly. There was significant movement of companies along the centralization–decentralization axis for the organization of the IT function.

In 1992, following a deep recession in the UK and with senior executive resistance to IT still being common (Galliers, 1992b), the IS skills shortage is not perceived to be acute, and whilst productivity of IT professionals continues to be important, the critical focus has shifted to quality issues. Quality of software development and measuring IS effectiveness are viewed as issues of major importance for the mid-1990s. End-user computing appears to have reached a 'steady state'. In general, it would appear that organizations have been successful in overcoming the teething problems that they were facing in 1987. However, as the need for integration of systems increases, new problems related to the control and management of end-user computing make it less attractive as a basis for providing a resource for systems development expertise. As in 1987, specialized systems such as office automation systems, CIM, and expert systems and artificial information remain unimportant in the eyes of those surveyed.

Education of senior management remains an important issue for IS managers in 1992, ranking sixth. It is likely that, as in the 1987 survey, IS managers view this as an enabling mechanism for improving communications with their non-IS counterparts and for engaging them in a proactive role in the exploitation of IT for business advantage. Related to the exploitation of IT for business advantage, there are two additional issues that did not appear in the 1987 survey: organizational learning and business process redesign. The appearance of these issues on the management agenda is perhaps a reflection of the advances that organizations have made over the past five years in integrating IS within business functions. As stated earlier, in 1987 the focus was primarily on the alignment of IS with a priori business goals, whilst in the mid-1990s, organizations that have good alignment are focusing on the exploitation of IT to change how they do business.

## Concluding remarks

It is hoped that by publishing the results of this survey to both sets of executives, they will begin to see more clearly how their views tally with colleagues in their own group and how in general their opinions differ from their colleagues in the other group. By discussing the apparent reasons for these differences of opinion, one might hope that the so-called culture gap (Grindley, 1992) between the two groups might diminish over time. This would appear to be necessary if British companies are to achieve the potential benefits from their investment in IT and the integration of IS staff into their respective business communities.

Further analysis of the data to compare and contrast opinions in the service and manufacturing sectors is currently being undertaken. In addition, the results of the UK survey will be compared with those of similar surveys that have taken place recently elsewhere in the world.

Further research will also be undertaken by means of structured interviews with a view to gaining further insights into the key IS management issues in a manner similar to that adopted by Clark (1992) in the US.

## Appendix 1: key issues in the 1992 survey (Source: amended from Niederman et al., 1991)

1. Developing an informative architecture. A corporate/global information architecture is needed to identify the major information categories used within an enterprise and their relationships to business processes. It is necessary to guide applications development and facilitate the integration and sharing of data.

2. Improving IS strategic planning. It is increasingly critical to an organization's success that it align its long-range IS plan with its strategic business plan. Rapidly changing business environments, increased involvement of end-users and accelerated technological change underscore the need to continue improving strategic planning skills.

3. Data as a corporate resource. The organization's data resource is growing in size, complexity and value. Despite this, it remains largely unrecognized, inaccessible and underutilized. IS must develop a climate within its department and throughout the organization which values the data resource as a corporate asset.

4. Human resources for IS. Current and future shortages of qualified information systems personnel threaten the IS department's ability to keep up with the information needs of its parent organization. Career paths need to be clarified. More emphasis needs to be put on developing business skills.

5. IS for competitive advantage. In many businesses, long-term survival is dependent on using information systems to gain competitive advantage. The business can be lost without it. Competitive advantage results from recognition of opportunities through creativity and innovation, followed by rapid implementation. These are historical weaknesses of IS.

6. Organizational alignment. The effectiveness with which IS can support the enterprise's information needs is dependent on the IS department's position within the enterprise. Too often IS is not located appropriately within the organization.

7. Organizational learning. Organizations that prosper will be those that make use of appropriate new IS technologies in their entire operation. Business practices and organizational structures will need to be modified in many cases. IS also must demonstrate its own ability to learn and use new technology.

8. IS's role and contribution. IS is sometimes viewed as an overhead expense with little appreciation of its contribution to the organization. This can lead executive management to make infeasible demands and to cut funding, resulting in missed opportunities for the organization.

9. Telecommunications. Communication is the life-blood of the organization. Using IS for competitive advantage often depends heavily on telecommunications. Rapid and major changes in the industry complicate this task.

10. Quality of software development. The application development backlog remains at unacceptably high levels. Users are getting impatient. Add to this the increasing costs of human resources and the need for improved effectiveness in systems development becomes clear.

11. Managing end-user computing. The proliferation of end-user comptuing through personal computers offers the promise of improved productivity but also the dangers of inadequate management control. Information system management must balance control against the need for slack. Clarification of end-user roles is a necessity.

12. Integrating technology. The capability now exists to integrate systems that are based on different technologies. As organizations try to integrate them, organizational and managerial problems will need to be solved.

13. Measuring IS effectiveness. The measurement of IS performance is crucial to its effective management. This is becoming more important as organizations invest more and more money in information systems.

14. Security and control. As organizations increase their dependence on information systems, there is a greater risk from destruction and alteration of data, disclosure to outside sources and disruption of information services. Tight security controls and fault tolerant information delivery are becoming a necessity.

15. Determining IS funding levels. There is no generally accepted way of establishing the level of IS funding relative to the other funding needs of the organization. This can put both IS and general managers at a disadvantage.

16. Education of senior management. The education of senior managers in an organization will lead to more enlightened resource allocation for IS, a more strategic view in IS planning and an understanding of IS's role in the organization.

17. Quality assurance. Very often, reports are compiled using data from several sources. Frequently, there are discrepancies among these different data sources due to lack of controls in IS and user departments. Too often computer print-outs are assumed to be accurate. Such assumptions can lead to faulty business decisions.

18. Information centres. Information is the key to an organization's success. Information centres are now being set up to centralize resources, provide guidelines for the organization and give support to end-users.

19. Fourth generation languages. As end-user computing increases, fourth generation languages are helping to ensure that the application specified can be translated into a working system, without advanced knowledge of programming languages.

20. Decision support systems. Improving managers' ability to make effective decisions is an important objective for IS. Decision support systems help them to model different futures and more informed choices.

21. Office automation. Office automation is being implemented by many organizations to improve 'white collar' productivity. Problems arise in determining how this should be done and what the role of IS should be.

22. Expert systems and artificial intelligence. Although the use of expert systems and artificial intelligence technologies have been confined to specialized applications, it is possible that they may impact on general organizational applications.

23. Computer-integrated manufacturing. Factories are being automated to establish an internationally competitive position. Coordination of automation efforts is critical. What should the role of the IS function be?

24. External data. Information on external forces and factors is required to make operational, tactical and strategic decisions. External data can be acquired through paper transfer or advanced telecommunications such as electronic data interchange.

25. Managing distributed systems. Distributed systems present a variety of challenges including establishing and maintaining consistent data, physically connecting computers linked by various networks with seamless gateways, responding to increased demands for joint development projects with users, controlling wasteful duplication of efforts and establishing guidelines for providing data resources to multiple versus local users.

26. Business process redesign. It provides opportunities for organizations to rethink key business processes for improved efficiency and effectiveness.
