# The effectiveness of Decision Support Systems: the implications of task complexity and DSS sophistication

RICHARD WEBBY and MARCUS O'CONNOR
School of Information Systems, University of New South Wales, Kensington 2033, Australia

All Decision Support Systems (DSS) are, by their nature, designed to improve decision making effectiveness, yet a review of the experimental literature reveals that achievement of this objective is mixed. We propose that this is because DSS effectiveness is contingent upon a number of factors related to the task and DSS under investigation. This paper reports a longitudinal experiment designed to evaluate the relationship between DSS effectiveness and two such factors: DSS sophistication and task complexity. In comparison to unaided human judgement, two levels of DSS were evaluated: a deterministic spreadsheet model and a probabilistic model with a graphical risk analysis aid. Our subjects made decisions in a business simulation providing two successive phases of increasing task complexity. Initially, when task complexity was low, we found that neither DSS affected subjects' performance. In the more complex phase, both types of DSS users performed significantly better than unaided subjects. However, risk analysis users performed no better than model-only users. Interestingly, DSS users performed less homogeneously than unaided subjects in the complex phase. DSS users had greater confidence and considered more alternatives than their unaided counterparts. Risk analysis users took longer making decisions in the early stages, while model-only users became the most efficient in the later stages.

## Introduction

A number of researchers have attempted to evaluate the effectiveness of various decision-aiding techniques in different problem domains. We review this literature and find that Decision Support Systems (DSS) effectiveness varies across studies. These mixed results could have occurred because DSS effectiveness is contingent upon a number of environmental factors, such as the task or decision problem, the type of DSS, and the experience of DSS users.

Accordingly, our review of the literature proceeds along a number of dimensions related to the sophistication of decision support and the task environment in each study. This is done to see whether it is possible to draw any inferences about the theorized contingent relationships based on current research. Unfortunately, we find it difficult to do so since most studies comprise isolated points on the DSS and task continuums. Very few researchers have varied either the task or the DSS investigated in their studies (cf. Eckel, 1983).

Our vision is of a future where DSS developers will be able to select decision aids appropriate for their task environment based at least partly upon empirical research, rather than solely on supposition and trial-and-error. In moving towards this vision, we have conducted a longitudinal business simulation experiment in which two contingent factors are varied: DSS sophistication and task complexity.

## DSS effectiveness: literature review

Our purpose here is not to review a broad range of empirical studies on DSS effectiveness in great detail, but rather to focus on the effects of various levels of DSS sophistication and task environment on DSS effectiveness across different laboratory experiments. Refer to Sharda et al. (1988) and Benbasat and Nault (1990) for two recent, more comprehensive reviews. Sharda et al. (1988) also covers representative case and field studies which we have chosen not to review for sake of brevity.

We shall attempt to categorize the research from the perspective of DSS and task differences among studies. Unfortunately, it is impossible to conduct anything approaching a meta-analysis of the studies, due to their methodological variations. However, it is hoped that this review may help synthesize past findings within a more unified framework, enabling improved comparison of studies.

## DSS differences

In practice and in research, DSS vary significantly in terms of their sophistication along a number of dimensions (adapted from Benbasat and Nault, 1990)

Database - This refers to techniques concerned with the storage, retrieval, and manipulation of information for decision purposes, rather than the quantity of data accessible. These techniques range in sophistication from sorting algorithms to powerful relational database management systems.

Process model – Computational models which predict the behaviour of the real-world. They may range from simple additive accounting models allowing ‘what if’ analysis to intricate models utilizing complex, powerful analytical techniques.

Representational aids - Concerned with the translation of data into information. These range from tabular data displays to graphs which, for instance, show uncertain data as a probability distribution through the technique of risk analysis.

Choice models - Allow alternatives to be evaluated based on multiple criteria in order to maximize some notion of utility. These range from simple linear programming routines to interactive, graphical multi-attribute utility decomposition.

## Task differences

In comparing studies, differences in decision settings and problem complexity usually present the greatest obstacles to interpretation. Moreover, subjects with different levels of ability and task familiarity have been used across studies.

Many DSS effectiveness studies have only examined the effect of DSS in a single time period rather than over a number of periods. However, as Vitalari (1985) points out, it is important to consider use of the DSS longitudinally to gauge a feeling for the long term success of a DSS, together with an understanding of their use (or disuse) over time.

## Dependent variables

Depending upon the type of study conducted, various criteria have been used to operationalize the concept of 'DSS effectiveness'. It is possible to categorize six main dependent variables from these studies, although most studies only report the outcome of one or a few of them:

\- Performance, or decision quality, is the key measure of DSS effectiveness. Performance may be assessed ‘objectively’ using internal algorithms in a business simulation to generate a profit or net worth measure, or ‘subjectively’ by employing expert judges to evaluate the performance of subjects in case-problem based research.

\- Homogeneity measures the stability or variance in performance across subjects.

\- Confidence is typically a perceptual, self-reported measure of the degree of confidence a decision maker has in the solution to a problem.

\- Alternatives refers to the number of alternative solutions to a problem the decision maker has considered. It may provide some guide to the completeness of the solution and the effect of the DSS on creativity.

\- Prediction measures whether estimates made by DSS users are more precise than intuitively derived estimates.

\- Efficiency reports whether the DSS reduced the time taken to reach a decision.

Clearly, Figures 1a and b indicate fairly mixed results. The case problem-based research has investigated a wide variety of DSS techniques in an equally wide variety of tasks. DSS range from simple comparative reporting systems (King and Rodriguez, 1978) to sophisticated multi-attribute choice models (Sainfort et al., 1990). Tasks vary from ill-structured business cases being attempted by students (e.g. Aldag and Power, 1986) to semi-structured budget planning by university administrators (Dickmeyer, 1983). While some studies found DSS impacted positively on performance, the majority concluded there was no discernable effect. It is difficult to trace the reasons for these mixed findings.

Functional business simulation studies also indicate mixed results. Within this body of research, DSS have generally taken the form of process models, while the tasks range from marketing to production simulations. Most business simulation studies have measured a number of dependent variables to give us a broader appreciation of the effects of DSS. However, results are too sporadic to reach conclusions. Although the DSS and tasks are more homogenous in the functional simulation studies than in the case-based research, we cannot sensibly draw any inferences about contingent relationships.

The third category of research uses total enterprise simulations to provide the task setting. DSS and tasks again vary in complexity, as do the findings, although results have been more positive in this research framework than in the others. Sharda et al. (1988) found that DSS benefits emerged, especially in later periods, presumably as DSS learning took place. Their study focused on DSS use by groups rather than individuals which, although useful, is another barrier to comparison. Eckel (1983) was one of the few researchers to vary his DSS treatments within a common task environment. The results of our study will be discussed in the proceeding sections.

## Research objectives

Our study investigates the effects of varying two factors in an exploratory experimental setting:

\- DSS sophistication Two levels of decision support are examined to determine whether more sophisticated DSS (representational aids) improve decision making performance. At the basic level, a spreadsheet-based financial model enabling 'what if' analysis was provided to one group. Another group used a risk analysis simulation package in conjunction with a spreadsheet model of the same structure as the model-only group. The decision outputs of the two DSS groups are compared with those of an unaided control group.

\- Task complexity The effect of task complexity on the relative effectiveness of the two DSS features is also investigated. Two complexity levels within the same experimental setting were presented to subjects. This enabled analysis of the effects of changing complexity levels for each individual.

## Research method

## Research design

To investigate the effect of DSS sophistication, three experimental groups were created:

\- Control group, who worked with paper-based worksheets in making decisions in a business simulation.

\- Lotus group, who used a 1-2-3 model created by the authors to make and record their decisions. The format and structure with which they were to make decisions was the same as the control group; the only difference was that the spreadsheet conveniently calculated the results of their decisions and facilitated 'what if' analysis.

\- @RISK group, who received the same 1-2-3 model and conditions as the Lotus group, the difference being that they had access to the @RISK package to perform risk analyses. @RISK is a 1-2-3 'add-in' which allows users to specify the uncertainty associated with any of the input variables. It is then possible to run Monte Carlo or Latin Hypercube simulations and plot the distributions of key variables such as cash balance, net profit, etc. From these graphical plots, users can understand the uncertainty surrounding their decision and assess the likelihood of catastrophic consequences (e.g. bankruptcy).

## Task

It was decided to use a longitudinal business simulation to measure the performance of the three experimental groups. Business simulations have been used extensively in IS research (Courtney et al., 1983). For example, the Minnesota experiments (Dickson et al., 1977) used simulations to examine the significance of various IS characteristics on design activity and their interaction with individual differences. Davis and Grove (1986) used a production simulation task to test Ackoff's (1967) propositions about MIS design. Yaverbaum and Sherr (1986) used an experimental business simulation to elicit requirements for MIS design.

Although business simulations cannot truly capture the reality of the business situation they purport to represent, they can come close enough for participants to recognise them as relevant and worthwhile. Keys et al. (1988) reported that business simulations

\- Use decision environments which are very similar to corporate environments,

• Exhibit high face validity,

\- Provide meaningful, lifelike roles for participants, and

\- Reward intelligent, rational, and well-planned executive-type decision making.

The Business Simulator was selected for use in this study. It is a total enterprise business simulation that focuses on issues of corporate policy and strategy rather than operational matters. This simulation or versions of it are widely used in business policy and management courses throughout the world.

Business Simulator required each person to assume the role of chief executive officer of a start-up robotics company. Subjects participated in the experiment on an individual basis, not in teams. The overall objective for each participant was to maximize their net worth as calculated by the Business Simulator. The sum of A\$200 was offered to the winner of each of the three experimental groups whose net worth was the greatest at the end of the task.

Subjects in the two DSS treatments gathered in a computer laboratory at a set time each week. Each subject used an IBM PS/2 to access the DSS. Control group subjects gathered in a nearby classroom, at the same time each week. One of the authors was present in each room as supervisor. Subjects were asked not to communicate about the task among themselves, either during decision sessions or between sessions.

In the first period, all subjects were given an identical briefing and information package describing their task and their hypothetical competitors. Each participant competed against four different hypothetical companies, simulated on computer, not directly against each other. The character of each competitor was described to enable subjects to anticipate how they might act. To avoid end-of-game behaviour, subjects were told the simulation would end in 12 'years' when in fact it ended in six. No formal training was given in the use of the Lotus-based DSS since all subjects had previous experience with spreadsheets. Instructions were given to the @RISK users and the supervisors gave some help with this package, especially in the early sessions.

In subsequent weeks, all participants were given feedback on their financial performance for the previous week in the form of a printed income statement and balance sheet.

A key concern when making decisions each week was to maintain a positive cash balance. If cash fell below zero in any week, a memorandum warning of possible bankruptcy was issued and a once-only inflow of capital was injected into the company, diluting the subject's percentage shareholding. Any remaining inventory was sold off at a discounted rate. If the subject erred again, bankruptcy was the result, and that person was out of the simulation.

Business Simulator provides three phases of increasing complexity simulated over a 15 year period. For research purposes, however, only the first two phases were used. The decisions required in the respective phases are as follows:

\- Start-up phase, in which three decisions in each 'year' are made over a variable number of years (which varied from three to six depending on a random element in the business simulation): price, advertising, and units purchased. In this phase, the simulated company had an importer/distributor role, and

\- Growth phase, in which six decisions are made over a maximum of three years: price, advertising, factory construction/expansion, factory production levels, long-term debt levels, and common stock issued. In this phase, the simulated company was analogous to a small manufacturer.

The task environment could be characterized as uncertain, with economic conditions fluctuating each year, similar to a real economy. Subjects were told the economic growth rate, inflation, and prime interest rate. The market environment was also highly volatile, being that of a start-up industry.

Subjects could generally be said to be unfamiliar with the task environment. Forty-eight subjects were taken from a Masters and a senior undergraduate course in IS at the University of New South Wales. The Masters students were randomly allocated across the three treatments. However, it was impractical to allocate all undergraduate subjects randomly to the three groups. As a consequence, the control group was larger than the other groups and had a higher proportion of undergraduates than the other treatments. While it would have been possible to remove the undergraduates altogether from the analysis, subsequent analysis (one-way ANOVA) showed there to be no significant difference in performance (net worth) among the Masters and undergraduates in the control group. Hence, it was decided for reasons of sample size to leave them in the analysis. Table 1 shows the number of subjects in each group at each stage of the simulation.

A series of one-way ANOVAs was conducted to test for differences in net worth across the three treatment groups, over each period in the business simulation. Differences in net worth over the different phases of complexity in the business simulation were similarly tested.

Table 1 presents the statistics of net worth across the three treatment groups. The figures in parentheses indicate the numbers of people in the particular sample, for that year or grouping of years. With respect to these sample sizes, the first point that needs to be noted is that some participants did not complete the task. This subject attrition is due to a couple of reasons. Firstly, some people dropped out of the course in which the experiment was run. This largely accounts for the loss of numbers in years 2 and 3. Secondly, 11 people went bankrupt in the business simulation. Overall, however, bankruptcies were evenly spread across the three groups (five in the control group; and three each respectively in the two DSS groups), due to the higher initial numbers in the control group. The data presented and analysed includes all subjects that were lost during the course of the experiment, up to and including the point of bankruptcy or drop-out.

As Table 1 shows, no significant differences in net worth were found between the three groups, except for the growth phase (p < 0.09). This reflects the upward trend, shown in Figure 2, in the performance of the DSS users relative to the control group. It could be suggested from this result that increased decision complexity had an effect on the relative performance of DSS users versus non-DSS users. However, it could also be suggested that this difference was significant later in the simulation because the benefits of learning to use the DSS were emerging.

## Effects on performance homogeneity

Table 2 presents the standard deviations of net worth for the different years and stages of the experiment. Performing the Barlett's test at a 5% level of significance on the variances revealed that @RISK reduced performance variability in the early stages. This is interesting because @RISK use (as measured using an automatic counter of the number of simulations run) steadily declined throughout the experiment. A post hoc analysis using the Scheffé decision rule reveals that the difference between years 1 and 6 was significant at a 5% level.

In the growth phase, control group users performed significantly more homogeneously than those in the Lotus group. Some DSS users started performing very well while others lagged behind, perhaps those who never understood the model or were not suited to DSS use.

## Effects on confidence

Confidence was self-reported on a 7-point Likert-type scale. Both Lotus and @RISK users had significantly higher overall confidence levels than those in the control group (p<0.01). Interestingly, there was little variation in confidence between periods or complexity levels in the task. This suggests that people's feelings of confidence may have been somewhat independent of task feedback and learning. However, it could also be due to a common limitation with repeated measures experiments, where people become desensitized to repeated questions and start responding routinely.

## Effects on alternatives considered

Figure 3 presents the mean number of alternatives considered by the three treatments across the years. Alternatives considered were measured on a self-reported basis on a form handed in each week.

ANOVA results indicate there are significant differences in alternatives considered between the three groups, both overall and for the two phases of complexity $p<0.01$ . On a period-by-period basis, Lotus users considered significantly more alternatives than Control and @RISK users for the first and last periods. But the Lotus and @RISK groups were statistically equivalent for the remaining periods. These results are quite encouraging. They suggest that the DSS users were taking advantage of the much touted characteristics of spreadsheet modelling.

## Effects on prediction

Participants were required to estimate their net profit for the coming year. Mean absolute error was used to operationalize predictive accuracy. DSS users, who had a model which calculated net profit based on their sales estimates, were more accurate with their predictions in general than their unaided counterparts in the start-up phase $p<0.02$ . In the growth phase, DSS users were still superior forecasters, but this was no longer significant. This suggests that, in the growth phase, either the estimates of the control group improved relatively, or the added complexity made it harder for DSS users to predict directly from their model.

## Effects on decision efficiency

Time taken was measured by participants writing down the time they started and finished the experiment. Figure 4 presents a graph of the mean times taken by the three treatments across the periods.

The first fact that can be noted about the times taken is that all three groups spent longer making decisions in the initial period than in later years. Undoubtedly this is because of the time required for task and DSS learning.

@RISK users took consistently longer to make decisions compared with either the Lotus or the control group over the entire business simulation. This statement is also supported by post hoc contrasts, both overall, and in the different decision making phases in the business simulation. It is likely that they took the extra time because of the requirement to run @RISK simulations and interpret them (it took approximately one minute to run a simulation, but some users commented that they thought it took longer, perhaps because all they saw was a number ticking over). Only in year 6 were no significant effects found. This could be because @RISK users were running very few simulations toward the end of the experiment (see Table 2).

In the growth phase, Lotus users took considerably less time than others $p < 0.05$ . This possibly implies that the efficiency advantages of automatic spreadsheet calculation were emerging towards the end of the business simulation (performance also improved, but by no means to the same extent).

In summary, DSS users generally tended to take longer to reach decisions in early periods, whereas later on, the evidence is mixed. Risk analysis in a DSS seems to consume significantly more time in specifying the probabilistic inputs, running simulations, and interpreting the probabilistic outputs. It appears that greater complexity may mean that DSS users take less time relative to decision makers without a DSS.

## Conclusions and implications

The results reported in this paper suggest that DSS in spreadsheet form, with or without risk analysis capabilities, may have some overall effect on decision making performance in the latter phases of a business simulation. This reinforces the conclusions of Sharda et al. (1988) that the benefits of a DSS appear in the long term and implies a need for longer studies of DSS effectiveness. It suggests to practitioners that they should not be discouraged overmuch by early poor results in DSS implementation if the learning curve is steep.

However, the study also found that performance homogeneity worsened for DSS users as the task became more complex. Some DSS users did very well, while others performed poorly. Perhaps the good performers understood the model, while others may have used it blindly. This reaffirms the need for training and user involvement in DSS development. Results also suggested that @RISK users took considerably longer to make their decisions than other groups. In the latter stages of the simulation, Lotus users took the least time. DSS users were found to consider more alternatives than the control group, reinforcing the findings of Eckel (1983).

In summary, this study has observed a long term effect of DSS support on financial performance in a business simulation. It has shown the differential effect of DSS effectiveness over varying complexity in the simulation. However, it does not lend much support to the notion that risk analysis helps users understand uncertainty and make better decisions. Our study suggests strong reasons for further contingency-based research into the effects of problem complexity on the effectiveness of DSS.
