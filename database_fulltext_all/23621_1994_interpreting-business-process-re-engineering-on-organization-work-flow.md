# Interpreting business process re-engineering on organization work flow

J.P. KAWALEK

Department of Business Information Technology, Manchester Metropolitan University, Manchester, UK

The advent and growth of recent discussion on business process re-engineering (BPR) has been accompanied by its increasing application to a myriad of research areas and approaches. This paper examines its tenets using sociological and organizational paradigms and investigates its significance for software geared to enhancing coordinative and cooperative working methods and relationships within organizations. By taking the experience of implementors of such software and using a grounded and interpretive approach, initial conclusions suggest that BPR concepts have an impact for large scale and highly structured working activities but seems to have an interestingly inverse relationship with information-oriented cooperative and user-enhanceable approaches to process coordination and improvement. Taking these themes, this paper outlines some conceptual interpretations and theoretical managerial considerations for cooperative/coordinative and process work flow implementations.

## Introduction

The first section reviews the concept of BPR. It focuses on a number of interrelated themes which pervade the literature. Using these broad characteristics and for the purposes of this paper, a clearer definition of BPR emerges. In order to conceptualize and contextualize the BPR characteristics and ideas, wider frameworks for social and organization analysis are utilized. This kind of analysis is developed in the second section.

The third section introduces some case material from implementors of Lotus Notes - a coordinative and cooperative work flow software product. This section attempts to interpret the significance or, alternatively, the caricature and definition of BPR introduced in the first section in the experience of an expanding range of products such as Lotus Notes. A number of interesting themes emerge, some of which appear incongruous with some of the characteristics of BPR as defined in the first section. The fourth section attempts to interpret some of the emergent themes. The paper concludes that new conceptual models and ideas concerning the areas of end-user empowerment, software enhanceability and informational processes require further investigation for contextual applicability and that these ideas may also enable a better understanding of the significance of the differing elements and characterizations of BPR in organizations.

## Business process re-engineering (BPR): towards a definition

Business process re-engineering has become a very fashionable term in recent years. Although there is no consensus regarding a pure definition of the term, there are a number of themes which pervade the literature. This section details some of the major themes in the literature and characterizes BPR in a number of broad and interrelated categories: the radical approach to change, the orientation towards processes, the top-down method which depicts its implementation, the cross-functional and cross-organizational thinking, the competitive requirement for re-engineering, the role of technology in the re-engineered organization and the implications for coordinating activities.

Interest in the last few years has been aroused and developed by a number of significant works such as Davenport and Short (1990), Hammer (1990), Harrington (1991), Davenport (1993) and others. Most of the BPR literature has focused on the drastic redefinition of the processes within organizations, with IT being a major instigator of change. The make-up of our organizations, it is said, requires radical change. It is the bureaucratic assumptions that lie behind processes, built up over longer periods, that are at the heart of the fundamental re-examination which depicts BPR. Thus Hammer (1990, p. 105) depicts BPR as an 'all-or-nothing proposition' which is unable to be accomplished in small and cautious steps. In fact it is the work of Hammer (1990) which is largely credited with the term 're-engineering' and is developed as a 'radical' rather than 'incremental' change programme, in order that organizations may disregard existing procedures and structures (Hammer and Champy, 1993, p. 33). This is a theme developed in writing by authors such as Ganz (1993) who describes re-engineering as revolution not evolution. However, major change has been recognized as risky with analogies to 'triple heart bypass' (Davenport, 1993, p. 171). Thus, the requirement for the degree of change has been taken and more fully developed and analysed with a cross-reference between the requirement or ambition to change and that of the type of programme required (Heygate, 1993).

The revolutionary or ‘radical change’ theme has been developed to describe BPR as a business or enterprise concept rather than being function oriented (Hammer and Champy, 1993, p. 35). Thus, the organization is not analysed by function but by sets of interlinked processes with a radical effect on the organization as a whole. Numerous writers have built upon and around such themes. Davenport and Short (1990) define a process approach to system requirements as a set of logically related tasks to achieve a business outcome. A process might be, for example, ‘developing a new product’ or ‘process insurance claim’. Similar examples of a process are given by Stewart (1992) in order to specify and define ‘process’ better. Davenport (1993) develops the term ‘process innovation’ and contrasts it to previous ideas of improving processes suggesting that cross-functional improvements require top-down design over long periods. Stewart (1992) develops the idea behind process management as opposed to function management as it focuses on objectives rather than unit costs, the grouping of employee skills to accomplish sets of work and information movement which is blind to existing management hierarchies.

Indeed, there are insights into BPR which can be gained from such literature in terms of the concept of the application portfolio (as described, amongst others, by Peppard (1993)). This classifies system applications into one of (usually) four broad types. Key operational systems are those which support core business activities and are critical in sustaining existing business, support applications improve management effectiveness, but are not necessarily critical to the success of the business, high potential systems describe innovative application areas which might have some effect on future business success and strategic systems are innovative systems which have the potential of levering advantage, such as the exploitation of IT for utilizing data in a cross-organizational manner. Thus, concentration on the process seems to link most closely to the key operational and the strategic categories as defined above. Putting application types into these types of categories is an interesting and useful way of furthering the definition of BPR. Given the levels of investment required for fundamental reappraisal of business processes, then clearly BPR is highly strategic in its orientation and central to improvements in critical business processes. The investment levels in a broadly BPR strategy implies that development initiatives come from the top of the organization structure downwards.

That the process ignores traditional functional boundaries is generally a central theme in BPR literature.

Thus, it conceptualizes the breakdown of processes into subprocesses each having well-defined boundaries, internal interfaces and responsibilities (Harrington, 1991, p. 30). Function and work can be attributed and defined to those processes, procedures formalized and documented, and there is better feedback control enabling well-defined targets and quality measurement within each process (Harrington, 1991, p. 164). It is cross-functional in the sense that it draws traditional subfunctional operations together and transcends the traditional organization boundary. It implies a change in the overall structure and relationship of the industry sector with a more formalized linkage to customers and suppliers, automated and standardized processes it is said lead to overall cost leadership by driving operating costs below those of competitors and analysing processes enables difficulties and exceptions within the process to be better understood, preventing error and increasing efficiency.

BPR writers put together persuasive reasons why dramatic reorganization is a necessary task in many circumstances. Ford Accounts Payable is the most widely quoted example which reported a 75% reduction in staff (Hammer, 1990) by radically challenging assumptions surrounding working practices and processes, enabling significant restructuring, streamlining and involving computing technologies to automate and coordinate process activities. Thus, the concept encompasses challenging the assumptions of outdated methods of working and recommends fundamental process reorganization of a business from a cross-functional business subsystem perspective, with immediate competitive implications. This is endorsed somewhat by organization-oriented research such as Scott Morton (1991). Whilst this is of obvious importance, particularly for the longer term survival of some older and traditional organizations in the private sector in North America and whilst the potential stakes are high, the risks and complexities can also be high. This dramatic solution-oriented approach has been influenced by Japanese working methods and the threats to the traditional markets of North American companies (Davenport, 1993, p. 43; Johansson et al., 1993, p. 10). Indeed, it is the threats in the international market-place which are the main instigator for a BPR type change programme (Katzenbach and Smith, 1991, p. 196).

Furthermore, the exploitation of technologies can deliver more effective working practices particularly in production and coordinative areas of work and it is the analysis of the process which is the key to potential efficiency gain. The argument is that change in the assumptions stem from analysing organizations not from their structure, but from their processes and that the focus is primarily on the customer interface (Harrington, 1991, p. 5). This, it is said, enables cross-functional thinking which information and coordinative technologies are ideally placed to be applied. It facilitates a better and perhaps more scientific identification of the relationships and synergy between processes. This exploitation of information technology results in a powerful set of concepts particularly when considered in conjunction with the traditional competitive analyses and ideas (Porter, 1979, 1985; McFarlan, 1984; Porter and Miller, 1985). There seems to be a powerful synergy between the concepts of the process and that of cross-functional, cross-organizational use of IT and the 'value-chain', a theme developed by Johansson et al. (1993).

Some of the BPR literature focuses on office processes, importing and interlinking industrial engineering to the office (Davenport and Short, 1990). The link is thus strong between BPR and that of coordinating activities within organizations and, hence, the theoretical work done in this area. Thus, a routine set of office processes can be depicted by sets of events using graphical techniques such as data flow diagrams, petrinets, process or role activity charts and event-driven diagramming tools (Mentzas, 1991; Ang, 1993). By doing this, office tasks are depicted as being routine in nature, triggered by event or status change. These trigger mechanisms can result in decision making and cause many other simultaneous or non-simultaneous activities. Each office function may require referencing data repositories, analysing the data and having a destination data flow. Thus, understanding and coordination of such activities is necessary to ensure that the appropriate sequence of activities and optimization of differing sets of activities, can meet prescribed goals (Mentzas, 1993). Thus, coordination is a central implication of the BPR or more generally process-oriented approaches. The coordination activity within the broad concept of BPR has been the focus of increasing debate, heightened by the requirement of computer systems to play an increasingly important role in organizational processes. Thus, writers such as Malone and Crowston (1990) identify a number of constituent elements that characterize coordinate activity: goals are the motivating forces behind an activity, activity results from the goals, activities are performed by one or more actors (humans or machines) and activities can be broken down into one or more interdependent subactivities. Thus, each activity or subactivity can have varied characteristics.

Summarizing and reviewing BPR literature can be bewilderingly complex as it covers a wide range of areas and issues: theoretical foundations, approaches to re-engineering, roles for information technologies, issues for successful implementation and measurable results. The literature generally encapsulates the automation of newly defined, formalized and radically re-engineered processes. Understanding the fundamental or enterprise-wide organization processes enables definition and redefinition of process activities and interfaces over time. Thus, human activities are defined and delineated for continued monitoring, with subsequent improvement for analysis by management in order to enable continued adaptation. The approach tends to ignore existing organization structure. Better understanding of the processes and the synergy between processes enables high levels of coordination and technology can be applied to the automation of processes and to the coordinative activities between processes. The effective implementation for BPR concepts is essentially top-down. BPR is normally implemented by large investment with the aim of reducing bureaucracy. It is conceived from the highest organization level in a top-down manner and it is the fundamental or key operational/strategic areas of organizations which are likely to be subjected to re-engineering. BPR concepts are likely to be most effectively utilized and applied in the key areas of business which are central to the operation of the organization.

## BPR and the sociology of organizations

Equating the concepts of BPR with conceptual sociological and organization models or a set or paradigms is an attempt at a better understanding of its nature. This type of approach is in part a trend that is influenced by modern approaches to managerial and organizational research and is thus also at the heart of research into the contextual or organizational settings in which technology is placed. It might be seen as a response to the need for better understanding and interpretation of the work and characteristics of organizations beyond that which is available from the more functionalist or positivist approaches. Indeed, the debate in many areas of the social sciences has been heavily influenced and fuelled by the conflict in sociological approaches of the interpretivist and functionalist schools.

The development and emergence in management and organizational behaviour of the phenomenological approach along with the exposure of the epistemological assumptions of the functionalist or more positivist approaches has at times polarized debate in management research. A good example of this debate might be within the BPR field at the moment. Indeed, the natural sciences, dominating research into the technological aspects of computer systems, utilize sets of methods which can be largely described as positivistic in nature. However, orientated as it is towards organization processes and being defined in this paper as a contextual, organizational and managerial concept, it will need to be exposed to phenomenological and interpretivist approaches to research. Such analyses may be based around a number of theoretical constructs to depict the requirements and processes of change within organizations. This organizational change which is central to BPR activities must be subject to interpretation within sociologically based theoretical constructs such as the structuration theory of

Giddens (1979) or the 'web models' described by Kling and Scacchi (1982) and Kling (1987). Thus, studying the relevance of BPR will need to be set into organizational theory and the trend towards qualitative, in-depth and longitudinal study for fuller exposition of its central precepts. Indeed, the concept may usefully be set within the frameworks of current management theory detailing the content, context and process of change as described by Pettigrew (1985, 1987, 1990). However, for the purposes of this paper a paradigmatic approach is taken as an initial focus to stimulate debate.

Of the many models which attempt to define paradigms in social and organizational theory, it is the one developed by Burrell and Morgan (1979) which has attracted most attention (White, 1983; Hirschheim and Klein, 1989; Morgan, 1990). This analysis categorizes the nature of social science as 'objective' or 'subjective'. The objective stance implies an ontology which describes an absolute social world which is separate from the observer and approaches to research will be heavily positivistic in nature. On the other hand, the subjective dimension describes an ontology which is derived from the phenomenological school where reality is constructed by the experience, norms and values of the observer (Heidegger, 1962; Gadamer, 1975). At the same time social phenomenon is studied in terms of a further two categories, regulation and radical change. Regulation approaches depict order equilibrium and consensus (Lockwood, 1956; Dahrendorf, 1959). Radical approaches are used by those who explain social phenomenon with reference to conflict, coercion and entropy (Coser, 1956; Cohen, 1968; Van den Berghe, 1969; Silverman, 1970). These order-conflict and the subjective-objective axes can be depicted as shown in Figure 1.

The functionalist paradigm is based around the assumption that society is objective and is systematic in character and is directed towards the production of order and regulation. There is some distance between society and the person studying it.

The radical structuralist paradigm describes a world characterized by intrinsic entropy, tensions and contradictions, which are the forces behind change in society. This paradigm also considers these phenomena a 'fact' which is some distance from the observer who is attempting to 'discover' it.

The radical humanist paradigm is a significant movement away from the above in that it does not describe the world in terms of objective fact. All observations are intrinsically related to the values, beliefs and experiences of the observer. At the same time this paradigm describes the human world as dominated by sets of ideological super-structures and constraints which inhibits or prevents human fulfilment.

The interpretive paradigm considers social reality as subjective and without external concrete form. However, it does consider the social world as possessing some order and regulation and by using shared realities, it is possible to construct discourse. The philosophical approaches within the broadly interpretivist tradition have actually been wide and varied and have been discussed in Hirschheim and Klein (1989), ranging from Checkland (1981) to the social conflict theories of Habermas (1972).

Thus, the differing interpretations of BPR can be placed within the context of these four paradigms. Interpreting BPR concepts against this type of model and within the context of current academic debate within the field of management is quite subjective as there are many writers with differing emphases. Indeed, a paradigm model itself can be questioned for applicability within organizations. Nevertheless, an initial critique of BPR can be developed to stimulate discourse and debate and by examining its central themes within the context of the kinds of ideas presented by the four paradigms presented by Burrell and Morgan (1979), a clearer picture of BPR may follow.

## BPR in the context of management research

Analysing the BPR literature and using the definitions of BPR described above, the concept of 'process' is central. Thus, BPR tends to focus on and explain efficiency gain via the process. The output of the white-collar worker is poor and at the heart of the poor performance is ineffective and inefficient hierarchies of management (Harrington, 1991, p. 5). There are many good examples of over-bureaucratic organizations but the generalizability resulting from the over-simplification of BPR as entirely process driven may result in over-prescription and a shift towards objective-realism or the functional quadrant of the paradigm model. More contingent or interpretive types of analyses and approaches to research in this field might suggest where and in what circumstances analysis of the process is relevant. Indeed, focusing entirely on the process can hide other factors such as the roles that cultural, socioeconomic, political and psychological subsystems play in the development of over-bureaucratic organizations, an approach which may ultimately yield better understanding of how to avoid future trends towards ineffective working practices. Indeed, given the relevance of a BPR type approach to change these 'other' factors will have implications for the change required for effective streamlining. These factors are utilized in BPR literature (e.g. Johansson et al., 1993, p. 196), but are often treated as rather incidental.

Analysis and understanding of the business process has been shown to help in the streamlining of the organization for efficiency. However, the type of language used in the literature is quite functionalist in character. Thus, correctly designed processes, it is said, will have 'the voice and perspective of the customer built in' (Davenport, 1993, p. 15). Conflicts of interest are not described in detail, resulting in a sometimes simplified view of productivity, cost effectiveness and efficiency. These factors are not easily measured in the implied 'objective' manner, particularly in complex organizations with a multiplicity of goals, values and subsystems. This is typically the case in the service and public sectors, a theme exemplified by studies in the utilization of IT in the National Health Service in the UK which highlight the conflict between the motivation for high turnover and reduced costs with that of patient needs (Jones and Worsdale, 1993). Indeed over-simplification and concentration on the process may impede other better alternatives to heighten organization objectives. For example and as already implied, better use of or more intuitive and innovative approaches to information to support effective decision making might be hidden by taking a purely process-oriented approach as depicted by BPR.

Challenging assumptions surrounding outmoded working practices and processes, which pervade the BPR literature, make little reference to the difficulties of identifying which are the outmoded practices. It tends to be seen as an objective exercise, again, as in the right-hand side of Burrell and Morgan's (1979) paradigmatic model. The suggestion at its most simplified level is that consensus regarding which are outmoded practices can be almost simplistically alluded to. The role of the consultant or BPR champion tends towards that of the objective systems expert as described by Hirschheim and Klein (1989) who can lead the way towards more effective working methods (see, for example, Harrington, 1991, pp. 28–30). By concentrating on the processes a better understanding of the process may well ensue, but perhaps there could be more recognition that actors involved in this interpretation process cannot be entirely objective.

The capture and complexity behind requirement analysis is an intricate, political and a chaotic activity whether BPR or another type of label is used to describe it. Thus, replacing outdated processes with new ones cannot be seen as an entirely objective exercise full of logical reasoning and mathematical precision.

The concentration on the processes within the BPR literature which are separated from the complexities of requirement capture and the sociopolitical factors, results in a depiction of BPR which treats change as rather mechanistic and scientifically logical. There are many good and valid examples of a purely process-oriented approach (e.g. Johansson et al., 1993, pp. 60–83), but the generalizability and validity of such approaches requires further contextualization. It seems that applied wrongly or over-simplistically interpreted, BPR results in some form of déjà vu of scientific management (Taylor, 1947). The over-simplification of the change mechanisms in organizations can result in an over-deterministic view of human nature within the functionalist vein of the paradigm model. Humans do not easily see the advantage of new methods of working and consensus is rarely achieved by better and effective working methods.

The examples of the appropriate use of the process approach may stem from the context or business nature of the processes undergoing change. Some organization processes are indeed mechanical in nature, but many business processes are highly complex with many anomalies and exceptions requiring intuitive intervention. Useful grounded examples of the applicability of the process have been provided by the Informatics Process Group at Manchester University and are referred to in a recent paper (Wastell et al., 1994). The cases referred to indicated a certain validity in a process-oriented approach which is somewhat loosely related to some BPR principles. For example, concepts of drastic and radical redefinition of processes seemed less appropriate than more flexible enhancement and piecemeal concepts applied in a process type manner. For example, the billing processes at Telserv were already highly structured and effective; human intervention was required to deal with more intuitive ‘exceptions’. The IT system required adaptation and enhancement in order to handle the billing process more effectively. It was found that the invoice production process required greater discretion by the invoice production staff to respond to customer needs. In a similar way, in the out-patient section of the cardiology unit described in the same paper, departments were given more autonomy within the context of greater decentralization of control. Constraints in existing working methods again were identified as a problem.

The applicability of the drastic process re-engineering concepts of Hammer (1990) seemed inappropriate in these examples. This radical ‘break the china’ (Johansson et al., 1993) characteristic originally developed by Hammer (1990), Hammer and Champy (1993), Gantz (1993) and others should also be criticized for contextual validity. There is no doubt that radical change has proven a useful and important concept in many organizations. However, it could be argued that the experience of the USA is likely to be very different to that of organizations operating from UK markets. Radical shake-up of many UK organizations, it might be argued, started in 1979 and, thus, the radical nature of BPR will be of less relevance perhaps opening an opportunity for a semiotic study of BPR within the context of recent industrial history in the USA. By doing this, better understanding of the relevance of BPR in UK organizations may be achieved. The applicability of a radical approach to re-engineer the organization needs theoretical underpinning by the utilization of grounded examples. Many of the examples given in the literature are not so much radical; rather they are streamlining in approach which hardly requires the adjective of 'radical'.

However, the examples developed by Wastell et al. (1994) do illicit how a process approach can be used successfully, particularly on relatively structured and formalized activities within organizations. Modern approaches which depict managerial processes and activities as complex, unstructured and intuitive and characterized by inconsistency (Peters, 1989; Russell and Muskett, 1993) seem to require increasing levels of employee intuition flexibility, inventiveness, empowerment and cooperation. Thus, in these circumstances the orientation of BPR towards a purer formalization of process is less appropriate than at perhaps lower organizational levels with more predictable and structured working patterns. Particularly useful is the concept that a process approach in some situations seems able to highlight where employees are being constrained by overprescriptive working methods.

Putting BPR into the paradigm model and into the context of current management thinking can be further expanded and developed. This simple analysis of BPR nevertheless gives penetration into some of the potential problems the approach may have regarding its central tenets. It could be argued that BPR concepts can tend towards the over-simplification of change in organizations and potentially has a rather over-deterministic view of human nature. Its epistemology sometimes tends towards the positivist and its ontology towards objective realism or functionalism. However, it seems reasonable that BPR has a certain significance. Concentration on the process is a valid managerial and systemic strategy along with many others. A fuller and more analytical method of approaching interpretation of BPR writers might be achieved with grounded examples which focus on particular characteristics that are implied or emanate from the BPR concept. The case that follows focuses particularly on approaches to coordinating activities in organizations and contrasts the findings with that of process coordination that stems from BPR, as described in the first section of this paper.

## A case study in Lotus Notes implementation: Management Tech Services (MTS) $^{1}$

Thus further grounded insights might be gained by analysing the way in which process, coordinative and cooperative software is currently being applied and utilized within organizations. The case that follows is taken from the experience of Management Tech Services (MTS) who use and implement Lotus Notes. This product is interesting because it is able to provide coordinative functionality which is very much process driven as in the depiction of BPR characterized in the first section. The product, however, is generally used as an enhanced form of electronic mail and a data-sharing mechanism for groups in a cooperative environment. In either case, its concentration on work flow and the automotive/informative application of technology enables insight into BPR perspectives.

MTS are a management service attached to a large British corporate based in the South Yorkshire area of England. Thirty percent of its work deals with internal managerial and technical infrastructural issues of that corporate and the rest is external consultancies for a wide range of organizations nationally. It employs approximately 350 full time technical, business, management consultants and support staff.

In 1992 MTS embarked on a major investment programme to install Lotus Notes company-wide and subsequently became technical and business consultants to others investing in the product and advisers on the business effect of cooperative and coordinative type products. Three interviews of approximately 3 h on separate occasions were held in order to, as far as possible, assess qualitatively the way MTS utilizes the product, as well as learning from their experience of installing Lotus Notes in other organizations. These were unstructured interviews with a broad set of guidelines in order that certain issues could be teased out. Each interview was taped and subsequently analysed. The approach taken was to focus on broad BPR concepts and the use of Notes. The technical issues and implementation considerations were not the main agenda unless the aspects of BPR as defined in the first section of this paper were implicit within this. The idea was to obtain empirical data on cooperative/coordinative implementations in order to investigate the relevance and importance of BPR concepts as depicted in the first section of this paper. The stance is unashamedly subjective and open to interpretation and is presented here to stimulate further analysis and thoughts.

The interviewees were given a general indication of the purpose of the interview, but precise detail was not given in order that interviewees should not preconceive 'correct' answers. The interviewer attempted to extract information on broad BPR, process and coordinative concepts, on the use of Notes both internally and in MTS's client organizations. Some consistent and interesting points emerged and are summarized. However, it was found that some of the defined BPR precepts were quite inversely related to this type of installation. Some degree of this was expected as the choice of Notes was deliberate for its generally loose definition of coordination and cooperation. The extent of the inverse relationship suggested that new concepts for user-oriented software are required which significantly move away or perhaps broaden traditional BPR concepts and other top-down, process-oriented and formalized approaches. Furthermore, it did indicate further areas and/or directions of research.

An interpretive outline of the important themes that resulted in the interviews are summarized below. It is assumed that the reader has some knowledge of the structure of cooperative and coordinative work groups type software. The findings are summarized into five broad areas of interest each having a perspective on BPR: cost–benefit analysis, use of a traditional database, integration with end-user applications, groupware cooperation and coordination perspectives.

## Cost-benefit analysis

When appraising Notes in terms of cost–benefit analysis, the interviewees expressed some ambiguity and uncertainty. This kind of appraisal seemed particularly difficult prior to implementation. The reasons given for this were a little varied. For instance, how the product was to be utilized within the organization determined the actual benefit an organization was to obtain. It seems that evaluation of the product only comes with the assessment of the degree of sharing of information and the degree of accessibility end-users obtain to different sources of information. Furthermore, it seemed that users were in direct control over these two factors. Management attempting to pre-define work activities for users could enable access to certain information and groups, but within that it was in the hands of the end-users what information was to be accessed, what groups and subjects were to be 'kicked off' and, to a large extent, which groups they read and contributed to. Thus only a relatively small proportion could be prescribed or preconceived by managers. The conclusion here is that the effectiveness and competitive or organizational benefit is precisely in the effectiveness of the end-users and in their need for and use and access of information. The valuation of information within this context is obviously a complex and difficult aspect to assess. Effective cost–benefit analysis in these circumstances requires very much a contingent approach to its evaluation both prior to implementation and, because of the fact that users are constantly enhancing the data, evaluation should occur as an ongoing process after implementation.

Thus, to gain advantage, end-users within the organization are required to contribute to, enhance and adapt information. They define their own requirements, who they work with and what information they should extract from available databases. This decentralized, user-oriented and informational set of concepts is very different from the BPR definitions as given above.

## Use of a traditional database

Using Notes, users linked to traditional structured data in standard relational tables. This is defined as 'hard' data for the purposes of this paper to distinguish it with Notes' own 'database' terminology, which is commonly textual or graphical with little or no inherent structure. This type of user-oriented data is defined here as 'soft' data.

Access to 'hard' data in Notes is controlled and defined by the traditional data access controls of the databases concerned. The hard data sources were conceived by management and controlled by traditional database administrators. Most soft data in Notes is not pre-defined, in a top-down fashion, except where access to traditional database management system structures required. In other words the 'soft' data access initiative comes from the bottom of the MTS organization structure. The 'harder', more structured and well-defined data, usually held in centralized databases might be pre-defined and developed at least in part using traditional hard systems analysis methods, engineered originally from major innovations for change arguably perceived and/or with the consent or approval from the higher levels of the MTS hierarchy. This would be quite natural considering the high levels of investment and potential change in working method that might be involved.

A potentially useful parallel can be drawn with the BPR concepts of obliteration and dramatic process re-evaluation which tend to focus on the formalization to assist automation of processes across organizational subfunction in order to further organizational effectiveness. This also is conceived, motivated or at least approved by central management initiatives. The use of the processes and the data is done by means of a top-down initiated mechanism. It is very much an approach which would need to be harnessed by the top levels of the organization, in order to restructure the business. The hard data concepts seem to link quite closely to BPR definitions and yet the soft is very much the opposite, being user driven, unstructured, informational and user enhanceable. The implication of this is the danger of oversimplifying or applying BPR in an incorrect organizational context. Defining sets of process descriptors, states and activities may result in processes that are well defined, delineated and controlled, but ones over which the user has little intuitive control. In these circumstances over-prescription may result in automation which controls and does not enable and harness human abilities as depicted by Zuboff (1988).

## Integration into end-user applications

Users at MTS access and interrogate orders, the accounts payable, the stock files, etc., held on DB2, producing reports direct or enhancing the data by incorporating it into more traditional end-user tools such as spreadsheets for different purposes. Lotus Notes provides check boxes and menu items to generate structural query language commands for accessing, for example, DB2 data. Thus, users are put in control of bringing in harder data from other sources. The kind of hard data accessed by users in essence is the by-product of the partial automation of order processing, invoicing and stock control over the last 20 years in MTS's mother corporation.

The fact that groupware products such as Lotus Notes assist the users to enhance data extracted from the more traditional database-oriented systems highlights further issues. The choice of data used in this way is potentially limited only by the extent of the data in the database and, of course, as already mentioned, the security restrictions imposed on user access. Users of Notes at MTS focused on the informational, whereas traditional BPR often concentrates on the analysis, formalization and automation of aspects of process engineering. The information extracted from hard data sources is really a by-product of the automotive transaction-oriented processes. Further, BPR tends to focus on the key operational/strategic organizational activities. Taking experiences of the MTS staff it would be fair to assume that user-enhanceable systems such as Notes can improve management effectiveness and enable innovative use of data. It would also be logical to suggest that traditional process engineering tends to be most useful on business processes which are well defined, clear and stable, but do not focus directly on this form of innovative use of data which potentially might harness the expertise of users which cannot be pre-specified. Thus, concepts of user enhanceability are more likely to be effective in ad hoc, fuzzy, more complex managerial environments, which depend on experts in fields other than business technology, applying the technology to their own working activities. BPR perspectives are more appropriate in automotive application areas, for processes which are clear and well defined and 'functionalist' in nature.

## Groupware cooperation

Thus, the interviews revealed that interrogation of hard structured data in this way has implications for the way we view BPR and the informational aspects of user-oriented, informative systems. Notes is most commonly used for the more unstructured 'soft' data. Users are able to define subjects, to define groups to contribute to and interrogate data in groups to which that user has access. This obviously requires an understanding of what information is of importance. This question of the value or importance of information at any particular time for the organization is very interpretive and soft in nature: the interpretation of meaning from data requires understanding of the decision-making requirements that it will serve (Checkland and Scholes, 1990). Thus, an information system is not a computer system but a computer system may be one component of an information system (Land, 1992). The traditional view of information as being somewhat a by-product of automation fails somewhat to consider the informational role to which a computer system may be applied as a primary rather than a secondary objective. In these cases it is the context in which the information is required that necessitates understanding (Galliers, 1993). It requires organizational and operational knowledge and, inevitably, by its contextual nature, the process of defining information requirements necessitates a user-driven approach. Whether this implies that an information analyst works with users to understand their information needs and prescribes software functionality in order to meet those needs or whether the user gets involved directly in defining and processing data is of crucial importance. Perhaps the requirement for processes to be adaptable to change determines the degree to which users are required to be involved in process and data definition. To use Notes effectively, end-users are required to enhance and adapt. They define their own requirements, who they work with and what information they should extract from available Notes 'databases'. The data cannot come from the top of the organization hierarchy downward. In the experience of the MTS interviewees, it was generally agreed that it resulted, in part, in the harnessing of knowledge, expertise and experience in a quite informal and on-going manner and having a very different perspective on coordination than the characterization of BPR which is used in the first section of this paper.

## Coordination perspectives

Coordination theory currently does not differentiate between 'hard' and 'soft' coordination of processes. It seems a logical progression from the previous discussions to differentiate between coordinative functions which are 'hard' and 'soft' in nature. The harder coordination is used here to specify a rigorous method to determine what processes are to be transacted at a particular moment in time. Thus, a computer system can model and control which processes need acting upon, dependent on a set of predetermined criteria. This is closer to the BPR concept of formalized and well-defined sets of interactive processes. The soft coordination here describes where users might determine what needs to be done and when and by sharing information and messages, coordinate action in a more human-like intuitive manner. Soft coordinative concepts do not prescribe the work flow necessarily. Notes itself is oriented towards softer coordination via shared information much like an extended form of electronic mail. However, it can use the harder work flow aspect of process architecture. Thus, hard coordination requires a series of stages and steps that are reasonably clearly defined, well structured and determinable. Soft coordination is more reliant on the intuitive nature of human intervention. Accounts payable systems (Hammer, 1990), thus, have a set of clearly defined stages so that a particular order can be processed effectively. Linking processes in this way would be defined here as 'hard' coordination. Human input may be required for exceptions to the sets of processes and the movement of information on an ad hoc and intuitive basis is seen as 'soft' coordination.

Harder coordinative applications were being developed at MTS at the time of the interviews with the formalization of process coordinative activity as an objective but with recognition of the potential problems or liability of disabling intuitive decision making when exceptions were encountered. As already implied, BPR writers tend to focus on the formalization and automation of processes using technology, without necessarily or explicitly differentiating between the automotive and informative concepts (Zuboff, 1988). These transaction-oriented automotive sets of processes have information at their heart, but it is not the information which is the focus of attention. It is the automation and work flow. The applications being developed at MTS were relatively simple automotive processes. Notes, however, was more naturally used as an unstructured information-sharing tool with a much more intuitive method of coordinating the activities of different groups within MTS. Other products exist which are much more oriented to the harder coordinative function and are thus closer to traditional BPR concepts.

The above highlights a number of issues with regard to formalization and automation of processes, information and methods of coordinating activities. The point of the discussion has been to highlight some central issues towards greater understanding of not only BPR but of issues that develop from a process-oriented approach.

Indeed, it is of obvious importance to understand the contextual or organizational setting before making decisions on the flavour of the BPR or process-oriented approach that is required. Reflecting on the wider trends in the utilization of computing technologies may enable insight into future issues which emanate from the discussions so far.

## Interpreting BPR, automation and information

Nowadays types of computer systems running different types of software have often been implemented in different areas of the organization and there is a need to use corporate, departmental and personal computing equipment as a unified set of resources. This has called for new metaphors in systems thinking. In some respects client-server computing or architecture encapsulates this principle. This integration of different sets of services has been enabled by developments in open systems and allows the organization to determine and control information resources in flexible and innovative ways. It allows greater or lesser degrees of central control, adaptability and potential end-user application enhancement of systems. The concept generally recognizes the requirement for processing power and data manipulation tools in the hands of the end-user. The caricature of BPR as depicted in this paper, tends to be oriented towards the centrally developed, controlled as well as the key operational/strategic activities within organizations. These usually larger types of system currently work most effectively on well-structured and automotive applications, which are likely to have high degrees of stability and formalization.

The diagram can be used to map control of processes and of data either centrally or locally. It also highlights a number of issues, for example, the degree to which end-user computing can enable enhancement of data and process in order to heighten organization adaptiveness and flexibility. Using Notes, control is certainly oriented to the users and, thus, the data may be more likely to be adapted and changed according to user requirements (and whims).

The shaded areas are those where CPU power is potentially allocated to the end-user. This it seems is particularly important for both the contextualization of BPR and that of user empowerment and enhanceability. ## Conclusion

The central focus of BPR on the organization processes is useful and interesting and the development of the theory requires assimilation between empowerment concepts with that of the basic BPR definition used in this paper. The Notes interviews were limited in scope and in depth, but highlighted some contrasting issues. Many of the assertions require further investigation. For example,

(1) Do products like Notes provide information overload? The argument here is that users can select and in part navigate through information. How might knowledge navigation or indexing assistance mechanisms be developed for end-users?

(2) Enabling users may result in increasing bureaucratic processes by their movement away from the more automotive and traditional area of BPR. This cannot be evaluated without much greater in-depth study.

(3) This initial study does not answer whether or not MTS and clients really do gain advantage – and in what way. They were all enthusiastic. Enthusiasm is not a bench-mark of advantage.

(4) The contribution and/or potential that end-user empowerment and enhanceability concepts might have for organization learning.

This paper attempts to move BPR towards the science of building flexibility and enhanceability within processes, grounded in further understanding the applicability of appropriate contexts for differing approaches to coordination, information and formalization of organization and work activities. Organization or contextual understanding may have an effect on the increasing number of BPR initiatives that fail (Hammer and Champy, 1993, p. 200; Heygate, 1993, p. 73). The broad BPR concept requires accommodation of alternate ideas such as that of user enhanceability, as well as the related areas of on-going development, evaluation and improvement of information resources which should be defined and redefined according to the context in which it is to be applied. Speculating on the longer term pertinence of BPR, its value seems to be as a set of theories to enhance cross-functional and organizational thinking, particularly in the context of IT utilization. The process approach seems able to identify activity and working constraints and is able to question generally accepted working methods. Focused as it is on organizations, BPR should not be researched as a natural science using a positivist-oriented epistemology. It may be best understood as a behavioural or social science requiring appropriate research methods in keeping with its complexity and importance.
