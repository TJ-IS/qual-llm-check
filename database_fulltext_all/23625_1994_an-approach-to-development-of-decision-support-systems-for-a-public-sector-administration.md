# An approach to development of decision support systems for a public sector administration

MOHAMMED BIN YUSOFF

Jabatan Sains Komputer, University Kebangsaan Malaysia, Malaysia

JOHN JENKINS

Department of Business Computing, City University, London, UK

The decision making process in complex domains involves inconsistent, incomplete and uncertain information and a lack of established models or theories. These characteristics influence the nature of approach to be used in the development of any decision aids to support the process. This paper presents an approach adopted in the development of a DSS to support complex decision making in a Public Sector body. The approach incorporates the use of Soft System Methodology (SSM). The role of SSM in Information Systems in general and in the development of DSS in particular is not well understood. This paper attempts to enhance this understanding by presenting the role of SSM in the development life cycle. SSM was found to be useful especially at the initial stage of DSS development, namely in understanding the problem area, as well as in identifying models and information to be used as inputs to the DSS design.

## Introduction

Malaysia is a federation of thirteen states. She is a multi-racial nation of Malays, Indians, Chinese and several other ethnic groups. The thirteen states in the federation vary in terms of economic and social well being. This is due to various factors, such as their geographical location, availability of natural resources and the policy of the former colonial master which gave more attention to certain parts of the Federation in economic and social development programmes. Given this situation and the major political and social problems that can be created by the imbalance in economic and social well being of the States, the Federal Government has devised various measures and steps to help alleviate the problems. The financial provisions of the Malaysian Constitution include the articles that describe the responsibility of the Federal Government in providing financial assistance to the State Governments in meeting their operating and development expenditures (from now on this assistance will be called Federal State Relation, FSR). Managing and implementing the tasks specified under FSR is the responsibility of a section in the Treasury Department of the Ministry of Finance.

In this paper we will present an approach adopted in the development of Decision Support Systems (DSS) for FSR. DSS is a form of computerized information system which enables decision makers to improve their effectiveness in ill-structured environments. The DSS concept is now widely accepted and has attracted a lot of attention from academic researchers as well as users from the business community. Our attempt to develop a DSS for FSR has highlighted a number of issues. The ill-structuredness of the decision making in FSR renders it difficult for those involved to obtain consistent accurate information and hence established methods and techniques to find solutions to the problems. A feature of ill-structured decision making is the reliance on soft information, namely information that lacks objectivity, and which is generally qualitative (Finlay, 1992). Brookes (1985) has listed a number of problems that arise in efforts to build DSS that can handle soft information, these include:

\- its existence is often not known to those who need it;

\- its owners often place tight constraints on its distribution;

\- its interpretation is intimately linked to the context in which it is received;

\- it tends to have a very short lifespan.

Accordingly the effectiveness of any approach to be used in the development of DSS can be judged in terms of its ability to help in problem understanding and also in establishing potential DSS. However the choice of approach to be used represented one of the major concerns in our attempt to design DSS for FSR. The major focus of DSS, namely providing support for high level decision making in an ill-structured environment, also implies that the DSS development approach should have a number of important characteristics, including the capability for high level understanding and modelling of decision making in such a way that helps lead to appropriate DSS design. In this paper we will describe such an approach.

## An approach to DSS development

The complexity and novelty of the decision making process in FSR makes it difficult to start with a right definition of the problem and solution procedures to solve the problem. Those involved in FSR were more at ease and more confident in describing what they did than in describing what problems they faced and how to go about improving the situation (Yusoff, 1992). Accordingly the nature of the DSS to be developed to support the process is imprecise. One of the approaches that have been developed to help in the DSS development is prototyping. No concrete definition can be attached to prototyping, but it is well described in the literature, for example Riddle (1984) describes prototyping as the building of trial versions of software systems that emphasize the preparation of immature versions that can be used as the basis for assessment of ideas and decision in preparation of a version that is complete and deliverable.

Basically the aim of prototyping is to clarify requirements and identify the desirable features of a system. A number of methods of prototyping have been described in the literature. For example the evolutionary method uses the prototype as the final system after a series of evolutionary changes based on user feedback (Boar, 1984).

Prototyping has been criticized for a number of reasons. It is difficult to manage and control (Dearnley and Mayhew, 1983). This is due to it being an evolving system designed with limited understanding of the user's requirements making planning and budgeting difficult. Furthermore the number of iterations needed before user satisfaction is obtained is unknown. Prototyping also poses a number of risks to a system's performance (Alavi, 1986). This is due to the lack of proper documentation, inadequate data security and inconsistent problem specification. The multiple roles played by the user, namely as analyst, programmer and data entry operator also contribute as does the lack of data management.

Prototyping has its rationale and advantages. Besides serving as the basis for determining the adequacy of a proposed solution before investing in a large-scale implementation of the target system, it can be used to:

\- secure active use of the system by the users;

\- secure active participation by the user in the design;

\- secure system adaptability to changing need;

\- assist user learning.

But it is essential for the development of large-scale

DSS that prototyping should be preceded by comprehensive understanding of the decision making process. This will serve as the basis to guide proper development of a prototype system. A comprehensive understanding of the decision making process helps in the identification of appropriate DSS architecture. The need for comprehensive understanding of the problem domain also implies that any approach to be used should have:

\- the capability for high level understanding and modelling of decision making;

\- the capability for low level modelling of decision making;

\- the capability to monitor the solutions generated, i.e. a capability for learning;

\- be easy to use.

## Understanding decision making

In arriving at a correct identification of FSR's problem and potential solution procedures, efforts were needed to identify the following:

\- purpose or mission of decision-making task;

\- the owner of the system: those who have the power to discourse about the decision making process, e.g. to create problems, control and monitor their performances;

\- the ultimate players, viz those directly responsible for the decision making, together with the nature of their responsibilities and authorities;

\- the environment, political and social, in which the decision making process takes place;

\- characteristics of present data, information, rules and procedures used in making decisions, namely characteristics of individual mode of problem solving.

The goal of FSR is ‘ensure balanced economic and social well being of the thirteen states in the Federation’. FSR is also perceived as an important instrument in achieving the goals of National Economic Policy (NEP) (Malaysia, 1991). NEP serves as a framework and guiding principle for all economic and social development programmes in Malaysia and its goals are:

\- Eradicating poverty by raising income levels and increasing employment opportunities for all Malaysians irrespective of race. This is to be achieved by programmes aimed at raising the productivity and income of those in low productivity occupations, the expansion of opportunities for intersectoral movement away from low productivity occupations and the provision of a wide range of social services especially designed to raise the living standard of low-income groups.

\- Acceleration of the process of restructuring Malaysian society to correct current economic imbalance, so as to reduce and eventually eliminate the identification of race with economic function. Programmes for this purpose include the modernization of rural life, the rapid and balanced development of urban activities, the establishment of new growth centres and the creation of a ‘Malay’ commercial industrial community in all categories and at all levels of operation. The objective is to ensure that Malays and other indigenous people will become full partners in all aspects of economic life of the nation.

The responsibilities for policy making, planning, implementing and controlling development programmes are vested in Central Agencies, Operating Agencies and State Corporations. These Agencies and Corporations are interrelated and by the virtue of the roles they play they both influence and are influenced by others. This interrelationship will determine to a certain extent how these agencies solve problems and make decisions, namely the nature of information and models used. For example in Malaysia planning is done at the Federal level (macro) and in States and operating agencies (micro). States and operating agencies submit their proposals for the approval by the Federal Government. It is not however necessary for States to submit their proposals to the Federal Government for approval when they intend to finance the projects from their own resources. But in the normal course of events, the amount of financial resources involved and the complexity of projects make the States dependent on the Federal Government. (3) Determine changes or modification that need to be made to the rules or models that govern activities (1) and (2) above. This is done in order to improve the performance of the problem solving mechanism in FSR (decision of type-e).

## Understanding the limitations of FSR decision making process

At the macro level the task facing FSR is to produce decisions or actions that satisfy the requirements from the States and also satisfy the stipulated objectives and constraints. The constraints include political, legal, social, governing value systems, norms and ideology. At the micro level the problem faced by FSR is to find information and formulating models or rules to help in generating decisions that satisfy these constraints. Soft System Methodology (SSM) was found to be useful for providing framework to discourse about the limitation of the present decision making in FSR and identifying solution procedures. SSM owes much to the work of Checkland (1981). The approach has been used in many applications (Checkland, 1981; Wilson, 1984). Basically the approach is a learning process which looks at the objective definition of the problem rather than solving the problem. A clear objective of the problem cannot be defined in a complex environment; this makes it necessary to consult everyone who has a direct interest in solving the problem as well as those affected by its solution. In the application of SSM the following entities, known as CATWOE, need be identified:

Customers: persons affected by the solutions to the problems.

Actors: persons involved in problem solving.

Transformation process: conversion of input into output. Weltanschauung: world view that make problem definition and transformation process meaningful.

Owner: those who own the system.

Environmental constraints: environmental aspects that need to be considered.

The use of SSM also requires the need to define Root Definitions (RD): which is basically a verbal statement of the objectives, points of view, environmental constraints and transformation process, which combined make the statement a meaningful system, and also Human Activity System (HAS): which represents a set of purposeful activities to deal with the input to the transformation process, the transformation process itself and the outputs from the transformation process. For example in the context of FSR the CATWOE is presented below:

C: < State Governments >,

A: < NFC, Senior Officials in FSR, Participative Agencies >,

T: Needs of the Effective rules and States---- transform----> procedures for resources allocation

W: “Providing aids to the states in general and poor states in particular is a sensible step toward helping to reduce the imbalance economic and social well being of the thirteen states in the Federation and to alleviate some of the resultant social problem in multi-racial society”

O: < Finance Minister, Parliament, Treasury Director, NFC >,

E: < Federal Constitution, Participative Agencies, Federal Financial Position, Political and social Systems >.

A Root Definition applicable to FSR is given below:

Root Definition: A system for interpreting the financial needs of the States in order to devise effective Strategies/Actions for effective allocation of aid to the States to help toward eradicating the imbalance in their economic and social well-beings subject to the constraints (social, political, legal, financial) imposed

The first level HAS modelled for FSR is shown in Figure 3. The HAS model in Figure 3 serves as a useful basis for diagnosing the limitation of the present decision making in FSR and recommending feasible and desirable changes to improve its effectiveness. Wilson (1984) has suggested four methods one of which involves developing a coherent model which is used as the basis for generating questions about the existence or otherwise of the activities embedded in the model. In the context of FSR comparing the model with reality identifies whether the major activities in Figure 3 exist in FSR or not. For example activity (5) in Figure 3 corresponds to FSR's present decision making activities 1 and 2 described earlier, namely administration of financial aid to the State Government and determining the Federal workforce requirements of the States. Whereas activities (1), (2), (3) and (4) in Figure 3 corresponds to the present decision making activity 3, namely determining the changes or modification that need to be made to the present rules or models used by FSR. Activities in cluster-2 and cluster-3 did not exist in FSR and were introduced into HAS as they were perceived to be important for learning. Effective action requires successive comparison between actions and feedback from the environment, this provides information for the next decision. Cluster-2 activities reflect the need for the organization to have the capability for 'single-loop' and cluster-3 HAS for 'double-loop learning' (Argyris and Schon, 1978). Single-loop learning reflects the intention of the organization to remain stable with the range of objectives or goals set by the organizational norm and value systems, it involves modifying the present strategies and assumptions embedded in the current ways of doing things. Double-loop learning is important when there is an occasion when a decision needs to be made not only to modify the present models or rules but also the norms and values systems behind the philosophy of decision making. Norm and value systems modification occurs as a result of the need to resolve conflicting requirements, changing economic and political conditions and changes in the perception of the measure of performance. In the context of FSR decision making the need for learning is reflected in its present decision making activity 3.

In the context of Figure 3 the decision making in FSR will be effective if all the embedded activities are carried out properly. Providing necessary decision aids to some of the activities in Figure 3 helps toward achieving this aim. Improving decision making in FSR requires enhancement of the creativity and intelligence of the decision maker. Enhancing creativity means reducing bias, improving judgment and reducing ambiguity. DSS for FSR will be useful if they provide support in these respects. Analysis of the HAS identifies activities susceptible to improvement by DSS. What functionality should be provided is the most important issue that needs to be resolved. The need to support some of the activities in cluster-2 and cluster-3 suggests that two forms of DSS are applicable. DSS to support activities in cluster-2 belong to the class of Cybernetic Information Systems (CIS) (Mason, 1981). These help by observing the effect of actions in the light of the targets set by measuring the effects and producing feedback which will show the degree of deviation from the target. The class of DSS to support activities in cluster-3 is termed Systemic Information Systems (SIS) (Churchman, 1981; Mason, 1981). The aim of SIS is to help those concerned keep controversy alive, to use actions as experiments, to create problems whose study will increase understanding. The information and models in these systems have a tentative existence. The concept of SIS is important for the development of DSS in situations such as FSR as there are many ways of using the information and models to describe reality. SIS can be used to provide supportive information to help users re-evaluate the assumptions which underly their decision making.

## DSS design and implementation

It was discovered during the study that the lack of relevant information was prominent in decisions of type-e and heuristic models used in this decision making. It was inferred from the study of the decision making process in FSR that heuristics were justifiable and sometimes unavoidable due to the complexity of the problem. They provide a time-pressured decision maker with a simpler way of dealing with a complex problem. This leads to a saving in time, effort and cost, due to limited information being collected and used. Heuristics may produce a satisfying or partially satisfying solution. But as they rely on the use of limited information, simplifying strategies, judgement and common sense in problem solving, they may also create bias. A biased decision can result in serious and systemic errors (Barzerman, 1986).

FSR relies on information provided by other Agencies for some of the input to its decision making activities (for a more detailed account of these Agencies and the nature of information they provide see Yusoff, 1992). As this information is produced by these Agencies for their specific purposes, its usefulness to FSR is questionable. The timeliness and accuracy of this information represent one of the major constraints to decision making in FSR. The lack of relevant information typifies the general problem faced by public sector organizations in Malaysia. Major policy issues such as the New Economic Policy have been formulated in a situation of uncertainty and lack of information. Decisions are essentially based on ‘common knowledge’ rather than a comprehensive set of data that has been systematically collected. Central Agencies in Malaysia depend on each other for information (Omar, 1980). This implies that there is often controversy among Central Agencies and even among departments within the same Agency regarding the relevance, accuracy and sufficiency of information.

The Treasury Department was among the first Public Sector Organizations to use computers in Malaysia. At the time of writing computers are still not being used in FSR. The potential use of modern methods and techniques in management are thus not being fully exploited. The use of modern methods and techniques such as Operational Research in the Malaysian public sector has been reported to be quite small. According to a sample survey 5 out of a total of 26 public sector organizations which participated in the survey were found to have used operational research techniques (Kwong, 1988). A more recent survey conducted by Yusoff confirmed that there was little use of modern methods and techniques by the public sector organizations in Malaysia (Yusoff and Nalini, 1991).

One is organizational history. An organization has a history which influences decisions made in the organization (Knight and MacDaniel, 1979). Organizational history is shaped not only by the requirements from its task environment but also by the organizational tradition and the consensus of the individuals within it. Organizations capture much of their history in rules and guidelines, statements of philosophy, objectives and records of past decisions. These constitute Organizational Knowledge. Organizational Knowledge is 'shared' knowledge, which acts as a frame of reference that constrains and guides individual decision makers. The importance of understanding organizational knowledge in the development of DSS has been stressed by Ciborra (1988). Understanding organizational knowledge brings to light collective and organizational issues related to the sharing of knowledge and the implications they pose for individual knowledge processing, namely the influence of organizational history and knowledge on individual cognitive process and cognitive style. Observation of the decision making process in FSR generated an issue which is important for the development of DSS. This issue revolves around the role and the relationship between quantitative and qualitative models. In FSR qualitative models were used in conjunction with the quantitative models as in Figure 6. For example in identifying economic status of the States for diagnosing their problems (see Figure 5) FSR use the following 'rules':

For any State s,

Rich(s) If High-Economic-Development(s) And High-Infrastructure-Development(s) And High-Revenue(s)

Poor(s) If Low-Economic-Development(s) And Low-Infrastructure-Development(s) And Low-Revenue(s)

These rules were supported by quantitative models. For example FSR used models to calculate the 'Scores' for economic and social development of the States (the nature of these models is described in Yusoff, 1992). Rules and models bases represent some of the major components of the DSS architecture proposed for FSR and the nature of this architecture was also described in Yusoff (1992).

Prototype DSS were developed for administering grants based on Economic and Social Well Being Indicators and capitation grants. Figure 7 shows example of the flow in administering grant based on Economic and Social Well Being Indicators.

One of the design issues that needs to be resolved in the development of DSS for FSR concerns representing rules and linking them with the quantitative models. Methods and techniques that have been developed for Expert Systems, especially those for knowledge representation, were found to be useful in this respect. There are a number of widely accepted methods for knowledge representation, for example production systems, semantic networks, frames and first-order-logic (Brachman and Leverage, 1985). These methods are well suited to specific types of knowledge. First-order-logic, such as Horn Clause can be used to represent knowledge in a semantic network, as well as providing a framework for analysing, representing and manipulating a relational database (Kowalski, 1979). First-order-logic also has well established procedures for theorem proving which are useful for problem solving and query processing (Kowalski, 1979). Logic-based formalism can also be used to represent models (Bonczek, 1981).

## Conclusion

The literature mostly presents DSS in the context of problem solving, where the problems are always objectively defined. But with decision making in ill-structured environments, the problem is not always clear and much effort is needed to define it. A number of factors contributed to the complexity of the decision making process in FSR: the objective of FSR, its multiplicity of roles, the governing environmental factors, social, economic, political and legal. It is rare, in the field of DSS, to find work which treated the formulation of the problem in depth (Landry et al., 1985). This paper has presented an approach adopted in the development of DSS, especially the use of SSM. SSM can be used as the first stage in system identification; it provides the tool to investigate the context, the system development process, and the people engaged in it (Ormerod, 1992). In this case study SSM was found to be useful in the problem formulation stage of the DSS development. It helps in the problem definition. The advantage of using SSM revolves around its ability to offer an opportunity for multiple views of the problem. Multiple views provide an avenue for discoursing about many important aspects of the organization, such as norm and value systems, that need to be incorporated into the problem definition. This methodology views the problem as a subjective representation of those people involved in the decision making. Getting the views of these people at the initial stage of DSS development has a number of advantages. Organizational norms are important in the development of Information System (Huber, 1983) as is user participation. In this respect it helps toward greater acceptance of the DSS.

SSM has some limitations. In the context of FSR there was no difficulty in identifying the Owners and Actors and determining what is the environment, but identifying the Weltanschuung and Root Definition in FSR was more difficult. According to one senior official, problem solving and decision making in FSR represents the most difficult task in the Treasury Department, hence to capture the 'essence' of the situation into a good Root Definition requires a difficult process of inquiry and exploration. The approach taken was to study the relevant documents, such as management manuals, working papers, meeting agendas, conference papers as well as interviewing those outside FSR who have an indirect involvement in the decision making in FSR. The documents were to supplement information gathered from personal interviews with those directly involved with the decision making in FSR. The documents and personal interview were also found to be useful for inferring and identifying the problems faced by FSR and the limitations of the present way of working. Another limitation of SSM is that HAS itself is non-terminal, it describes what needs to be done but not how to do it. This is agreed by Ormerod (1992) according to whom, SSM provides no special assistance in moving from function to data, and what data could be used and required and whether or not this data be included in databases is a question of design based on facts about the world, not an extension of the logic contained within the conceptual model of SSM. Much of the debate about the potential use of SSM in the IS has been theoretical in nature. This argument results from the lack of understanding of the potential use of SSM in the development of IS in practical situations. This paper attempts to enhance this understanding by describing the role of SSM in the DSS development life cycle.

Mohammed Bin Yusoff obtained his MSc degree in Operational Research at the London School of Economics, and his Doctorate at Imperial College. He is currently a Lecturer at the Department of Computer Science, University Kebangsaan Malaysia. His research interest is in the application of DSS concept in public sector organization. He is currently heading a research on the development of DSS tools. The project is sponsored by Malaysian government under “Intensive Research in Priority Area” (IRPA).

John Jenkins is a Senior Lecturer in the School of Informatics, City University.
