# Introducing IT into a mature production related work environment: the human resource factor

JONATHAN HELLIWELL

Product Manager, Cookson Minerals Ltd, Cookson House, Willington Quay, Wallsend, Tyne & Wear, UK

ALAN FOWLER

School of Business Management, Armstrong Building, University of Newcastle-upon-Tyne, Newcastle NE1 7RU, UK

The paper addresses the human resource issues associated with the implementation of IT into the Production facilities of a company operating in the process industry. The primary focus of the research is a field study based on a questionnaire issued to the process workers who would be most affected by the changes. Management was initially concerned that resistance to change could pose a significant problem. In this respect there was particular concern that the age and background profile of the workforce could exacerbate any underlying tendency to change resistance. This paper evaluates the background to these concerns and illustrates how this particular organization dealt with them. The questionnaire results indicated a very low level of initial familiarity with any form of computer based technology. Nonetheless the results indicated a very positive response to the IT implementation proposals with virtually no evidence of attitudinal rigidity. In this sense, the results broadly support many of the observations revealed in the literature.

## Introduction

The need to establish or sustain competitive advantage inevitably imposes a requirement to introduce new IT systems in virtually all contemporary organizations at some stage in their development. The introduction of such new technology invariably implies changes to the working patterns of those involved.

Evidence that resistance to change is potentially a major problem area emerges in the copious literature of organizational development and change-management, for example Cummings and Worley (1993, 144–53), Robbins (1991, 632–7) and Kanter (1985). Resistance to change is commonly acknowledged within this context leading to the development of classical ‘interventions’ aimed at facilitating change and improving organizational effectiveness. Technology in general and IT in particular are clearly important sources of change which require planning and careful accommodation in what may otherwise, over-simplistically, be perceived as a purely technical domain in which social, organizational and political contexts are too easily overlooked (Willcocks, 1991).

Furthermore there is evidence that, in the context of change, older groups of workers may be subject to prejudice in so far that apparently they are often assumed, by management, to be particularly change resistant (Rosen and Jerdee, 1976, 1977). These prejudices may be compounded in those organizations which have evolved into the mature stage of the business lifecycle because this is often accompanied by relatively stable, long running working practices, procedures and group identities. Indications that employers favour workers under 35 (Gallup, 1990) appear to reflect a popular, although weakly substantiated, belief that older workers exhibit declining productive capacity and are less able to adapt to the new requirements imposed by technological change.

This study examines these related issues, drawing upon published literature and analysing primary data obtained within a process manufacturing organization during the introduction of new IT into its production plant. Initially, managers in this organization were particularly concerned about the possibility of dysfunctional change resistance since their workforce broadly fitted the characteristics presented above. Management was also eager to ensure that its IT implementation plans adequately addressed 'soft' issues as well as technical ones. Awareness of IT and the likely response to its introduction were therefore evaluated, based on the results of a questionnaire.

## Background to the case study

The fieldwork component of the research was conducted in a relatively small operating unit within the mineral processing industry. The IT requirements for such 'process' based production systems are somewhat differentiated from the more familiar 'discrete-item' manufacturers due to fundamental differences existing between these two broad categories of operations management. For example Tinham (1991) has commented that although

Materials Requirements Planning (MRP) software packages have been available for some time they have, until recently, been aimed primarily at the 'discrete' manufacturing environment. After consideration of available systems the decision was taken, in this case, to develop the new IT system in-house using available expertise. A key factor in the specification was the perceived need to avoid the creation of 'information islands'. Any newly introduced production systems would therefore be required to integrate fully with existing inventory, stock control, costing and financial systems. Furthermore it was anticipated that these systems would be developed continually on an incremental basis in order to provide an overall 'business' system: the ultimate goal being the full implementation of Computer Integrated Manufacture (CIM). In this sense the implementation of the new IT system, for the production plant, was seen as but one stage in a continuum of development as characterized in the process described by Brennan (1990).

It was concluded that in this particular case the likelihood of success when introducing IT would be particularly strongly affected by the reaction of shopfloor operatives. This aspect of systems development was therefore afforded special attention as it was suspected that given the nature of the working environment and the profile of the workforce, strong resistance could possibly emerge.

## Human resource factors in IT implementation

In assessing the likely response of the workforce, to the introduction of the proposed IT system, a number of factors were identified as being potentially problematic and therefore deserving of prior consideration. These factors are briefly reviewed below.

## Review of change resistance concepts

Change resistance is a familiar concept to practising managers and researchers in organizational behaviour.

'One of the most well documented findings from studies of individual and organisational behaviour is that organisations and their members resist change' (Robbins 1991, p. 639).

Resistance may be overt, implicit, immediate or deferred. It may arise due to disruption of habit, feelings of insecurity, fear of economic penalties or fear of the unknown. Threats to structural inertia, expertise, established power relationships and established resource allocations can all engender resistance. Recognition of the widespread existence of change resistance has led to the definition of clearly established strategies for overcoming it (Cummings and Worley, 1993, pp. 144–8).

Resistance may also reflect personal perspectives of change which range from unitarist and technocratic to pluralistic, radical and augmentative (Willcocks and Mason, 1987, pp. 21–3). The technocratic view is arguably quite prevalent amongst IT professionals who tend to see the ‘obvious virtues’ of the system and therefore associate resistance with misunderstanding, ignorance or reactionary attitudes. Conversely the radical view (Braverman, 1974) arguably characterizes the stereotype of organized labour which sees IT as a source of managerial control, ultimately designed, to extract additional ‘surplus-value’ from the workforce. Familiarity with these concepts, in terms of understanding the impacts of IT, assessing reactions to it and planning for acceptance, are thereby identified as essential prerequisites for an IT implementation team.

## Technofear

Computerphobia, technophobia, technofear and cyberphobia are alternative terms which have been used to describe the particularly adverse reactions of some individuals to computers and other examples of modern technology, for example Willcocks and Mason (1987), Crimando (1992), Cunningham et al. (1991) and Aeh (1990). Some have suggested that this level of reaction implies illness (Coli, 1986) but others see these symptoms as being essentially fear of failure or the unknown (Galitz, 1980). Bar-Tal (1990), in investigating the perception of control over work and the use of personal computers, suggests that computer novices may be affected strongly by their a-priori beliefs regarding their ability to control these devices, their attitudes to computers and their fear of innovation. This view is supported by Hill et al. (1987), Lee (1970), Simon (1977), Caporael (1984) and Sproull et al. (1984). These concerns do not necessarily imply that there is something about the use of computers which inherently causes loss of control, but they do point to the importance of recognizing user perceptions within the IT implementation remit.

## Age related aspects in change resistance

There is evidence that older workers can suffer discrimination resulting from the belief that they are set in their ways and generally unable to make the necessary adjustments to new work situations. These alleged characteristics were ranked third out of nine as a reason why employers are reluctant to employ older workers (Loether, 1975, pp. 73–5). Although there is some evidence to suggest that learning ability, reaction time and decision making in older groups are noticeably impaired relative to younger groups (Hupert, 1988), the overall findings in the literature of industrial gerontology appears far from conclusive (Chown, 1972; Aldag, 1977; Parker, 1982; Doering, 1983; Rhodes and Schuster, 1983; Laczko and Phillipson, 1991, p. 35). It also appears that with suitable training, specially formulated to their particular needs, an older workforce can compare very favourably with younger workers (Belbin and Belbin, 1972). Notwithstanding, it appears that prejudice still exists (Gallup, 1990).

Rosen and Jerdee (1990), researching the attitudes of a large sample of human resource (HR) managers, detected, amongst respondents, warnings of ‘tunnel vision’ and unwillingness on the part of older employees to stay in touch with changing technologies. These researchers identified the need for more active HR policies to cope with the problems of skills obsolescence and reduced motivation in older employees, stressing that such groups are often found in larger organizations which are also in the decline stage of the business lifecycle. They conclude by suggesting the need for further research to identify organizations and methodologies which have successfully promoted career management of older workers.

Rhodes (1983) conducted an exhaustive study of age related differences in attitude, behaviour and values and concluded that significant trends exist although causality was difficult to establish. Schulz (1990) compares Japanese and American reactions to their ageing workforces concluding that there is good evidence that many older workers would respond to an education and training environment that encouraged mid-life learning.

In summary the literature suggests that largely unsubstantiated prejudice against older workers does exist and that recognition of the needs and potential of older workers, leading to the incorporation of imaginative training programmes, should be more actively targeted by HR management, especially in organizations wishing to introduce new technology to employees falling within this category.

## HR related aspects of IT systems failure

There is increasing recognition of the behavioural as opposed to the technical reasons for new systems failing to meet expectations (Allingham and O'Connor, 1992; Willcocks and Harrow, 1992, p. 190–3). This view presses strongly for the recognition of the HR and organizational dimensions on an equal footing with the technical and environmental issues (Willcocks, 1991). This recognition leads inevitably to implications for training (Rowe, 1984). For example Clegg and Kemp (1986) showed that companies spent millions of pounds on a new Flexible Manufacturing Systems (FMS) plant, only to run into serious HR problems due to an over technocentric total system design. Similarly Kearney (1988) indicated a shortfall of 30% between expectations and actual performance of Computer Aided Manufacture (CAM) systems with the deficit mainly attributable to human resource and organizational factors. Clegg (1989) suggests that the IT industry conveys images of technology being seen as more important than people and an attitude stressing trust in the technology and the technologists while leaving IT to the experts. Consequently users do not develop a significant role in new advanced systems.

## Union and employee reactions to new technology

Schultz-Wild and Kohler (1985) in a study of FMS based in a West German manufacturing company, comment generally on ‘technological progress’ using socio-political discussions to derive two opposing views of the subject. On one hand is the horror vision of the unmanned factory of the future. Here the high productivity and manpower savings resulting from technologically oriented capital investments such as Computer Aided Design (CAD) and Direct Numeric Control (DNC) are generalized into an expectation of high work-place job losses. This gives rise to employee and union fear of increasing unemployment which is perceived to be the inevitable outcome of these technological and economic developments. The authors comment that the negative social effects of technological progress, and its accompanying symptoms, can be directly traced back to this view.

On the other hand (and here the writers are referring to attitudes in Western Europe generally and West Germany in particular) experience shows that when such systems have been introduced into manufacturing plants, no dramatic consequences have resulted. Very little worker resistance to new technology has been encountered and those major lay-offs which have occurred, do not appear to have been linked directly to the introduction of the technology (Schultz, 1990).

Similarly Backstom (1986) comments that many employees fear that computerization will lead to greater unemployment and individual loss of control at work. However, he concedes that most workers accept that technological change is necessary to promote social and economic progress. In contrast, Daniel (1987) comments that in the UK, technological change is generally welcomed by both workers and their representatives. Indeed, workers and trade unions do not appear to present a barrier to technological change. He concludes that, in practice, it appears that they generally welcome it.

Daniel also comments that the impact of new technology in recent years appears largely benign. This view is notably in agreement with the work of Schultz-Wild and Kohler (1985). Whilst new technology has a varied impact, on balance it leads to enhanced skills and responsibilities and better pay. However, it must also be recognized that IT is nonetheless traditionally associated with job losses. This observation is accompanied by the suggestion that there may also have been a decline in workers' control over how they do their jobs and over the pace at which they are expected to work. This view is clearly in agreement with the citations of Bar-Tal (1990) as presented above. Daniel points out that new technology is more common in workplaces where unions are recognized and that, in practice, consultation is relatively common in such settings. This refers not only to discussions with shop stewards, but also extends to more direct but informal consultation with operatives at shop floor level. He also identifies a ‘spontaneous appetite for technical change’ amongst British workers, stating that:

‘Among the attractions of new technology, independently of any more favourable context for jobs that it may have, is the fact that it represents investment, optimism, progress and achievement’.

Daniel also states that workers have been more willing to accept even the threatening aspects of restructuring in those cases where it has been tied to the introduction of 'new technology'. Perhaps this is due to recognition that the final outcome is inevitable, and it is therefore better to try and retrieve some advantage, in so far as this may be possible. Clearly this line of reasoning echoes the comments already made above, and is amply supported by other authors such as Batstone and Gourlay (1986).

## Management responses to new technology

Quite distinct from the stereotypical scenario of union resistance to management's attempts to introduce IT, the comments of Willcocks and Mason (1988) following their examination of management's role in new technology and human resources in the workplace conclude that:

'If the potential of IT was not realized in the 1980s, this was, by and large, not due to trade union resistance or traditional IR problems'.

They emphasize that on the contrary it is recognized that anti-union legislation in the same period, in combination with the effects of recession and high unemployment, found unions largely on the defensive and in no position to seriously hamper managements' attempts to implement new technology. The inference drawn by these authors is that in practice managerial resistance to new technology has been as noteworthy as union and workforce acceptance of it. They attribute this resistance to managements' ignorance of IT, fear of the unknown and a desire to preserve the status quo. This particular line of reasoning leads to the proposition that the rate of adoption of IT and its corresponding level of success is predominantly influenced by managements' handling of the implementation process. In general, trade union influences are seen as minimal. For example, they do not control capital spending or project planning and any impact which they may have is generally not felt until after the system is in place and operational. However, it is argued that careful management of the process, including the provision of adequate training, should preclude the incidence of significant problems.

Willcocks and Mason (1987) develop this argument further, pointing out that although managements have taken different approaches to the introduction of IT they have, in general, introduced it on their own terms: a view supported by Rojot (1986) and endorsed by Lansbury (1987). This, in turn, appears to have led to an unconscious decision by many managements not to adopt a strategic approach to those IR problems which arise from computerization. As a result it has become general practice, in the UK, for managers and technologists to retain a firm hold over the investment and technology dimensions of such projects. This localized ‘ownership’ of IT initiatives may have correspondingly coloured perceptions of the HRM issues associated with IT. Consequently both management and trade unionists may come to see conflict as being synonymous with computerization. In this sense both sides may tend to see computers as a ‘scapegoat’ for other failings.

Finally, in his review of the main issues associated with IT and industrial relations, Elger (1987) notes that the implementation and operation of new technology occurs within a complex matrix of pre-existing occupational groupings, possessing distinctive expectations and forms of occupational organization. Much change is often mediated through this matrix. Hence, although the perception of management is that it has introduced IT ‘on its own terms’, this may not always be completely true. Certainly the introduction of IT has led to a definite but variable shift in bargaining power in favour of management. However, it is argued by Elger (1987) that this has not been translated into efforts to exclude unions or to obtain more managerial control and worker effort.

## Field research at Cookson Minerals Ltd

Primary aims of the field research were first to assist in the implementation of a new IT system within an established organization and secondly to compare and evaluate field observations relative to issues raised in the literature review. The company featured in the field research was the Tyneside operation of Cookson Minerals Limited (CML), a process manufacturer producing milled zirconium silicates for use in the production of ceramics and in the foundry industry. It also produces antimony based flame retardents for use in the plastics industry. The total number of people employed in the two plants at the time of the study was 193 of which 75 were employed in the production department. Using the description given by Porter (1980) the organization in question can be considered to be ‘mature’ with reference to both its processes and the age profile of its staff.

The production function in the company comprises two discrete chains associated respectively with the zirconium and antimony based products. At the outset of the study each production department was generating its own production sheets by hand. In cases where machines had a Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) facility, printouts had to be copied manually onto the appropriate production sheets.

## Existing computing systems

Figure 1 schematically illustrates, in a simplified form, the computing facilities existing at the inception of the study. A number of PCs and terminals were supported in the respective departments by a central mini computer. The company's order-entry, inventory, payroll and costing systems were also held on the mini computer. Links were also available to Cookson's Stoke on Trent site (CML Stoke), the Banks Automated Clearing Service (BACS) and IBM's Electronic Customer Support system (ECS).

Production data input to the system, at this time, was achieved manually; a process clearly incompatible with the other business systems. This subsequently resulted in many problems and inconveniences. For example, the inventory and order entry systems were both real-time but, because of the linkages between them, orders could not be despatched until inventory files had been updated with information stating the amounts of material passed for release by quality control. This information had to be collected manually from the plant and typed into the system by the stock control section. As a result situations could sometimes arise in which the sales department was aware that material required for urgent orders was ready for despatch, but could not be released until the information system had ‘caught up’. The problem was that a standard company-wide decision support system, using a common database, simply did not exist.

Similarly the production management department did not have direct access to a management information system and consequently had to work through third parties in order to acquire its own management information. In addition, the production department was not an internal consumer of any of the computer-based systems existing within the company although it did supply paper-based data to those systems, as described above. The need to update these arrangements was starkly evident but this would clearly involve significant changes to working practices.

## Survey of perceptions on IT

Recognizing from the literature the importance of the human dimension in the design and implementation of IT systems, a questionnaire was developed to examine perceptions of IT issues within CML. The questionnaire was specifically designed to obtain information on the workforces' a-priori attitudes with the objective of easing the implementation of the new IT system into the production facility. The questionnaire was designed in accordance with the principles outlined by Lancaster and Massingham (1988), the approach being tempered by personal experience of the target recipients and preliminary comments received from senior managers.

## Profile of the target respondents

The target respondents consisted of day and shift process workers and maintenance personnel in the two main processing units within the plant. The respondents spanned the C1, C2 and D socio-economic groupings with the majority of respondents being located in group C2. Notably, the majority of the workforce were also trade union members. This was the first time that this section of the workforce had been asked to fill in a questionnaire so to encourage response rate and alleviate inhibitions, the questionnaire was kept anonymous and completely confidential.

## Information required

Screening of the information requirements identified four main areas which were translated into corresponding subdivisions within the questionnaire. The categorization of subject areas was also designed to provide insights into the areas identified by Bar-Tal (1990), namely:

Attitudes towards computers in general.

Fear of innovation (including resistance or reaction to innovation).

Concerns about personal ability to control the devices.

Section One aimed to determine how many process operators had been exposed to computers, or to items of equipment with similar interfaces, such as typewriters and hole-in-the-wall (ATM) bank machines. Typewriters were specifically mentioned as their keyboards are similar to those of computers. ATMs were also considered to be significant since they are similar to the shopfloor data collection devices which the company intended to install as part of its production system IT strategy. This section of the questionnaire also tried to ascertain prevailing personal attitudes towards computers in general.

Section Two was designed to determine the process operators' preconceptions of the effects of computers in the production environment both generally and in comparison with the paper systems which they were destined to replace. The questions did not attempt to obtain objective answers but were more concerned with probing for subjective perceptions such as those described in the literature. These factors were considered to be potentially highly significant with respect to the implementation of the IT strategy in this particular case.

It should be noted that at the time of initiating the research the first steps towards implementing the new IT strategy, in the production department, had already occurred since the mechanical time-clocks had been replaced with electronic clocks linked to computers. Since the new clocks thereby constituted a very simple but fundamental data entry point to the system, an attempt was made to determine the prevailing attitudes to 'clocking in' since this could be regarded as a control variable reflecting the attitude of the workforce to the introduction of IT. For example, a negative reaction to the first stage of the implementation could conceivably have arisen from feelings of hostility towards the reality of clocking-in rather than the fact that computers were being used as part of the clocking-in system. It should be noted that the survey was completed before installation of the system supporting the new clocks was complete.

Section Three comprised a short set of questions designed to determine whether workers felt that they could cope with IT either with training or without it. Again, the answers can only be subjective. However, a negative attitude with respect to the anticipated effects of training would clearly produce implications for the subsequent design and implementation of training courses.

Finally, Section Four explored attitudes to change and the management of change within the company. The recent introduction of the BS5750 quality system was used as an example of a change in the work environment, to which the respondents could relate through previous experience. Implementation of BS5750 had entailed a radical realignment of attitudes towards production practices amongst both management and the workforce. The shift in emphasis had involved moving away from the prevailing perspective of ‘getting the work out of the door at all costs’ (which often implied considerable reworking of ‘out of specification material’) towards a newer philosophy of ‘getting it right first time’ with due emphasis placed on customer satisfaction, traceability, etc.

## Responses

A pilot study was initially used to test the questionnaire. This was conducted on a full shift consisting of eight process operators and one shift controller. A 100% response rate was achieved. From the results there appeared to be no major problems with the construction of the questionnaire or its interpretation. All questions were answered in the desired manner, with two notable exceptions. These were in the sections which asked the respondents for direct feedback in the form of a written answer to an open question. None of the respondents replied to these particular questions on this occasion. No substantial changes were made to the questionnaire as a result of the pilot study, and its results were subsequently amalgamated with those of the main study.

The total target population for the main survey was 75. Fifty replies were obtained (including the pilot study) giving an overall response rate of 67%. No forms were spoiled, although one or two questions were missed out on certain questionnaires. The results were subsequently adjusted to allow for this by calculating percentages on the basis of the number of replies received for each respective question.

## Analysis of the results from the questionnaire

Analysis of the results involved determination of the percentage of the target population responding to each category. The results, which were considered for each section in turn, are documented in the sections below.

## Section One: exposure to computers

The age profile of the workforce is shown in Figure 2. This clearly indicates that the workforce is relatively mature, with a distribution which is skewed to the right giving an average age of 46. Furthermore, since personal computers only appeared in the late 1970s to early 1980s, it is almost certain that the majority of the workforce had not exposed to computers during their education. This appeared to be borne out by the questionnaire results.

The results indicated that 52% of the production workforce had never used a computer and 18% had not used a computer, an ATM or typewriter keyboard. It thereby appeared that approximately 20% of the workforce had never been exposed either to computers or to primary input devices such as keyboards or numeric keypad of the type proposed in the IT strategy.

## Preconceptions of computers

The results indicated that 36% of the workforce described themselves as either very comfortable with computers, or capable of using them for some tasks. Of the 64% who did not fit into this category, 80% (51% of the total sample) thought that they would have difficulty with computers, whilst 20% (13% of the total sample) regarded them with some hostility.

Although over half of the workforce had not been previously exposed to computers, the overall majority (92%) nonetheless regarded computers as useful (with or without reservations). Clearly, many workers felt that, despite their dislike of the machines, computers were still useful. These results were considered to be potentially very encouraging with respect to the implementation of IT strategy, providing that such positive perceptions could be built upon during the process and not dissipated therein.

## Section Two: computers in the production department

The majority of workers (84%) saw the introduction of computers as leading to a reduction in the amount of paperwork in the production department. A small number thought that computers would eliminate paper entirely. The balance of the workforce felt that computers would not change the amount of paperwork to any meaningful degree. These results are in broad accordance with stated aims of the CML strategy, namely the reduction of paperwork.

The results also provided an indication of respondents' attitude towards the fallibility of computer systems. One particular concern held by the strategy team was that people who did not have experience of computers may regard them as an ‘instant’ solution to the existing problem of production information systems as characterized by the view ‘just press the button and out come the answers’. Fortunately, the results indicated that most of the workforce (94%) realized that computer systems are only as good as the information that is fed into them. This fundamental point was strongly emphasized to all members of the production department during subsequent implementation.

Most workers regarded computerization as a positive move (62%) and only 4% regarded it as a ‘bad thing’. These outcomes showed clear agreement with the literature findings (e.g. Daniel, 1987). Ideally, it should be possible to harness such positive attitudes to the advantage of the implementation team, but only if care is taken to ensure that any problems are dealt with quickly and effectively. If these feelings were to change owing to poor implementation, they could be very difficult to rebuild.

In general, computers were regarded as being neither more nor less reliable than paper systems (56%). This perception could also be interpreted as useful, since it implied clearly that computing systems were not regarded as an instant panacea for success. Consequently there should be no false expectations; once the system has been installed and is running properly, it should emerge as being more reliable than the paper system which it replaced. However, if the majority of the workforce had initially regarded computer systems as being more reliable than paper systems and if this perception was proved to be wrong in the initial implementation, subsequent disillusionment could potentially have been very difficult to overcome.

The majority of workers (90%) appeared not to be worried by the introduction of computers. However, it must be recognized that the fears of the residual 10% minority still needed to be addressed carefully as part of the implementation strategy.

Feelings towards electronic clocking-in were split, with 53% approving and 47% disliking it. It should be noted that since the survey was carried out the electronic time-clocks have been installed and have now run for a prolonged period. No major problems have been encountered and the issue of dislike of clocking-in does not seem to have affected the acceptance of the new technology. It is noteworthy that even though attitudes were evenly divided towards what could have been regarded as the first stage of introducing IT, this did not appear to prejudice the overall impression of IT in general (only 4% against as cited above).

## Section Three: training

This section explored perceptions on the likely effectiveness of computer use and training. In essence it assessed the possibilities of ‘teaching old dogs new tricks’. The results generally suggested that from the perceptions of the respondents training would prove effective, giving rise to an increased ability to cope with computers. However it is worth noting that 10% of the workforce felt that they would not be able to cope with computers even after training. It must be emphasized that these were only perceptions and that with the assistance of suitable training these people may, in practice, have been well able to cope. However, the results obtained did offer an indication of the attitudes that would be faced when designing and implementing training schemes.

## Section Four: the management of change

The first question in this section asked people specifically about their personal reaction to change. There were no further qualifications on this question, e.g. change at work, change at home etc. This was done deliberately in order to keep the question simple and explore initial reactions. It was hoped that this approach would both improve the response rate and provide information on attitudes.

From the results, most of the respondents are marginally prepared to accept change (60%). A further 30% indicated that they were comfortable with or welcomed change and 10% were uncomfortable with it, or regarded it with suspicion. These results were reflected in the answers received for Section Three, which specifically asked about new systems in the working environment. Most respondents (51%) just accepted new systems while 27% accepted new systems with interest. The rest (22%) regarded them either with apathy or suspicion.

The results from Section Four were potentially most useful when considered in connection with the question which asked for the workforce's opinions on management communications, in which the options allowed ranged from ‘very good’ to ‘very poor’. In the response 29% of respondents felt that communications were neither good nor bad, whereas 32% felt that they were poor. This certainly indicated that there was ample room for improvement in management's communication processes, particularly as nearly half the workforce appeared to regard new systems with either apathy or suspicion.

## Section Five: communications

The final question in this section referred to the recent introduction of a BS5750 quality system covering every aspect of operations throughout the organization, except the maintenance function. Within the present context this was used as a real example of previous experience in which a new system had been introduced into the operations environment. Again, the question was based on individual perceptions, so no qualifications were given for ‘total’ or ‘partial’ involvement.

After the quality system documentation had been written, a series of presentations had been given to the workforce to inform them of progress. The answers to the questionnaire suggested that these presentations had been relatively unsuccessful. A significant proportion of the workforce (49%) felt uninformed or uninvolved by the process. A few respondents qualified their answers with further comments which indicated that their perception of these sessions was not one of communication and involvement. They saw them as an opportunity, taken by management, to point out the shortcomings in the existing operations, leading to demands for extended targets accompanied by beration for lack of progress. The respondents also indicated a feeling that this process did not ‘involve’ them; they felt that more communications sessions which discussed progress and ideas stemming from the workforce were needed if the system was to be accepted by everyone.

This response came as something of a surprise to management, given the effort which they perceived had been invested into the communication process. However, upon reflection and with the benefit of hindsight, it is perhaps easier to see how such differences in perception arise. Furthermore it is possible that much of the lack of progress actually arose from the history of management's practices and attitudes towards the downward dissemination of information, rather than workforce's actions or inherent attitudes. This point is expanded below.

This company had traditionally experienced a history of management-controlled, formal workforce communication. However, in the last two to three years, with the introduction of the quality programme and the reduction in workforce size, a need for more regular, informal communication had arisen and had been recognized. Nonetheless initial discussions along the lines of improved communication had still consisted of a somewhat polarized dialogue between the two distinct 'sides' – management versus workforce. In this respect it was clearly desirable that the perceptions on both sides should change. For example, many of the issues facing businesses today have less to do with internal operations than they do with external forces (Lansbury, 1987). In reality it must be accepted however that the achievement of such a state of harmonization requires time and dedication on both sides. Effective communication is clearly a key contributor to the achievement of such a position.

## Discussion

The survey clearly illustrated that the introduction of computers into the workplace was welcomed, overall, by most members of the workforce. This supports several of the findings in the literature, for example Daniel (1987) and Schultz et al. (1985). One reason for this response may be that the introduction of computers into a mature production department, such as that existing at CML, shows a management commitment – through capital investment – to the future of the business. It had certainly been the case that in recent years preceding the survey at CML, there had been little capital investment in the production process. This had led to adverse speculation about the future of operations at this site. Against this background, the installation of an IT system offered hope for the future. Daniel (1987) clearly recognized this as a general reason for workplace and trade union acceptance of new technology.

It must be recognized that some negative reaction was also experienced although this was a minority response. Most of this negative reaction appeared to be of the type described by Schultz-Wild and Kohler (1985) and was related to the fear of job losses.

In general, computers were regarded by the majority of the workforce as being potentially useful, although some workers felt that they would have difficulty in using them, even after training. This reflects the point made by Schultz (1990) concerning the special training needs of older workers. This factor was fully recognized at CML, and led directly to the subsequent introduction of a computer awareness course, designed in cooperation with a local training agency. Over half the workforce in the survey had no previous experience of computers whatsoever and it was considered that training would offer the best prospect for breaking down any perceptual barriers towards computerization. The trainers contended that it did not matter too much if the detail of course content was forgotten by the time that the technology actually arrived! The key objective was to remove any basic perceptual barriers. Furthermore, it was argued that, if perceptions could be changed from ‘fear and difficulty’ to ‘acceptance or enthusiasm’, then it should follow that subsequent courses, designed to teach specific skills, would have a correspondingly enhanced probability of success. This reflects the view of Alston (1991) who presents a good example of workforce perceptions on computers becoming distinctly more positive following the actual introduction of new information technology.

Management communications were also identified by the survey as requiring specific action. Communication clearly needs to be given the same priority in a project of this nature as equipment and capital outlay. This need for communication was reinforced during the early stages of the study, when it became clear that the production workforce had no idea that an information technology strategy actually existed, let alone how it would affect them. This view was borne out by the results of the survey which led, in this case, to the subsequent arrangement of a series of informal presentations which were attended by all members of the production and maintenance departments. The issues raised with regard to ‘management ownership of IT’ were also seen to be relevant within the context of communications. It was established that care would be required in order to obtain commitment to the programme without it being seen as a management imposition which had little relevance to the aspirations of the workforce.

The industrial relations aspects of the IT strategy at CML appeared to have followed quite closely those described in the literature. The workforce and their representatives had been involved only after capital and technology had already been allocated. In this respect, CML appeared to have behaved similarly to the companies described earlier by Elger (1987), Daniel (1987) and Rojot (1986) who noted the lack of strategic approaches to human resource issues during IT implementations. This does not appear to have been a conscious decision but it does mirror the observations made earlier by Willcocks and Mason (1987). The questionnaire results also indicated that, owing to the fairly widespread acceptance of computerization, the lack of a truly strategic approach did not on this occasion appear particularly problematic.

With respect to the initial worries about potential change-resistance linked to the workforce age profile the responses to the survey also proved very encouraging. These results arguably answer, in part, the call by Rosen and Jerdee (1990) for further research into mature companies which successfully address the management of an ageing workforce in the face of technological change. In this case the company had made careful provision for appropriate consultation and training and has, apparently, been rewarded by the responses achieved in accordance with the predictions made by Schultz (op. cit.). It appeared that management at CML had, on this occasion, heeded the warnings made against adopting an over technocratic approach to IT implementation at the expense of the HR dimension (Willcocks 1991). This cognisance is evidenced by the emphasis placed on the study currently being reported; this having emerged as a specific but central feature of a wider study into IT implementation. It was concluded that there was virtually no evidence that the relative maturity and established stability of working practice in this workforce would pose any particular impediment to the introduction of new IT systems. On the contrary, the workforce appeared, in general, to welcome the prospect openly.

## Conclusions

In conclusion it was noted that operatives' attitudes towards computing systems in the workplace were generally encouraging. Despite the relatively large number of computer novices employed, the need for accurate data was fully recognized. It was also recognized that computers could not be expected to provide an automatic, plug-in solution to production information problems. This implies that a degree of tolerance towards any teething problems and associated imperfections could be anticipated; an important factor since these are common in most implementations.

When considered overall the survey results indicated that in this particular organization a useful foundation existed upon which to build and implement a successful IT production strategy, subject to some reservations with regard to communications. Of wider interest is the observation that here was a traditional, relatively mature, male, unionized workforce who at first appearance might have been expected to offer fierce resistance to the implementation of new and unfamiliar IT based systems. However, on the basis of the research undertaken, the reverse appears to be true. This could be seen as potentially very encouraging to organizations facing similar circumstances since it reaffirms that with careful attention to communications and training an apparently formidable task may be reduced to manageable proportions.
