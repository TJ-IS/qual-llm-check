# The state-of-the-art of CSCW: CSCW systems, cooperative work and organization

JOHN McCARTHY

Department of Applied Psychology, University College, Cork, Ireland

Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW) is a young research field, no more than ten years old, concerned with the development and use of computer systems to support cooperative working. Although researchers and practitioners in this area have many interests in common, there is a great deal of diversity in definitions of and approaches to CSCW. This diversity has arisen despite the clarity of intention of the early seminal papers that attempted to set the agenda for CSCW research. Ten years into the development of this research area, a number of tensions appear to be diluting this initial clarity. These tensions reveal themselves in apparent disjunctions between theoretical papers and statements of intent on the one hand and CSCW practice (research and design) on the other. In this paper a review of the state-of-the-art of CSCW is offered. Some existing systems, and related design and evaluation research, are described. Then three sources of the aforementioned tension are explored: the role of technology in driving or being derived from the science; conceptions of working and cooperative working in particular; and the relationship between CSCW and organization.

## Introduction

The state-of-the-art of Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW) includes technology, organization, groupware, group work, cooperation, cooperative work and computer supported cooperative work. CSCW is many things to many people. It is a young research field concerned with the development and use of computer systems to support cooperative working. The initial impetus for CSCW development was provided by three factors: the relatively low cost of computers, the widespread availability of networking technologies and telecommunications, and the growing recognition of the role of both formal and informal groups in modern organization. But this does not tell us what CSCW is.

A deep concern with social, political, cultural and organizational issues in the use of computers in work settings and a profound dissatisfaction with the then predominantly context-independent, asocial approach to Human Computer Interaction (HCI) research is apparent in many of the seminal papers which provoked initial interest in CSCW (see, e.g. the collection of seminal papers, many of which are collected from earlier sources, edited by Grief, 1988). These papers were concerned not so much with small design issues as with changing the way in which researchers and practitioners thought about human computer interaction. Their radical agenda was to put HCI in a social and organizational context. For instance, Crowstone et al. (1986) were concerned with the relationship between information technology and organizational structure. Sproull and

Kiesler (1986) explored the social context of electronic mail and Blomberg (1986) explored “the reciprocal relationship that obtains between a technology and the social organization of work” (p. 771). While these early writings may have set a radical agenda for the development of CSCW, it is not at all clear that that agenda is being followed in current CSCW research and practice.

The aim of this paper is to explore current CSCW research and practice to evaluate the extent to which the original CSCW agenda is being followed. First, some systems and relevant research are described as a way of introducing the general IT reader to CSCW. This is not intended to be a comprehensive review of available systems, neither is it intended to be technocentric. It is aimed at introducing the reader to some of the conceptual and evaluative work surrounding these systems rather than describing the systems themselves (for a comprehensive review of CSCW technology see Rodden, 1991, 1993). It is a brief exploration of what I consider to be some of the more accessible systems, with a view to giving the general reader a 'feel' for the kind of CSCW systems that are now emerging. Then three fairly broad questions are addressed: (1) to what extent is CSCW technology-led; (2) what kind of understanding of work is reflected in CSCW systems and research; and (3) to what extent does CSCW have or represent an organizational perspective? Each of these questions reflects a source of tension that marks CSCW today. It is in trying to answer such questions that we really begin to appreciate the state-of-the-art of CSCW.

## CSCW systems

Broadly speaking, and notwithstanding subtle distinctions made by some between CSCW and computer mediated communication, four main classes of systems are currently widely acknowledged in the CSCW literature. They are: messaging systems; conferencing systems; meeting support systems; and collaborative writing or shared editing systems. A commonly used classification of CSCW systems, illustrated in Figure 1, is in terms of the temporal and spatial characteristics of the interaction they support. Some have been designed to support interaction which takes place in the same place but at different times; some in different places but at the same or different times, and so on. This classification system is used here to provide guidance (a basic map) for the novice. However, it is acknowledged that a thoughtful reading of the literature provides examples of systems and research issues and themes that do not easily fit into this classification. To categorize those issues a more sophisticated map would be required. Nonetheless, this a useful place to start.

## Email and other messaging systems

Messaging systems are generally used by people who are in different places and who are communicating with each other asynchronously, that is sending and receiving are separated temporally. Electronic mail or email is a wholly text-based communication system. In its simplest form it involves one person composing a message and sending it in full to another person or group of people. Sender and receiver can be in the same building or on different continents and, although the message can be 'received' within minutes, response time ultimately depends on when the receiver gets around to reading and replying to it.

Attempts have been made to enhance the capabilities of email in a number of directions. Some, for example the Information Lens (Malone et al., 1987), have tried to augment the structure of message organization to increase the amount of information which can be automatically processed by the computer. Others, for example COSMOS (Configurable Structured Message System, described in Rodden, 1991) and Chaos (DeCindio et al., 1986), have also tried to formalize the communication process.

According to Rodden (1991), the Information Lens represents a strand of research which attempts to merge traditional message handling with artificial intelligence techniques to minimize the effects of information overload on the user. The Lens system is a prototype information-sharing system built on top of an existing email system which is already widely used. The Lens systems offers four optional capabilities over and above those offered by the email system: structured message templates for message composition; receivers have the ability to specify rules for filtering and classifying incoming messages; mailbox use can be extended to allow the receiver to broadcast a message; and receivers can specify rules to find particular classes of messages in generally broadcast mail. Malone makes the point that most of these capabilities can be expected to work best when people share goals and generally do not have conflicts of interest about what kinds of information are worthy of attention. One reason for this is that Lens embodies what Rodden calls a pragmatic approach to control of the communication process. In other words the communication process is not formally controlled and aspects of it can be negotiated between the participants.

In contrast with Information Lens, the COSMOS project is representative of a strand of research on message sending which is based on formal models of the communication process. Communication within COSMOS is based on the use of a communication structure, that is a set of rules which characterize the form of a communication activity. Bowers and Churcher (1988) describe the communication structure as involving 'the definition of a set of expectable communicative actions and a set of constraints over their temporal orders and contexts of realization' (p. 132). Rodden (1991) gives the example of a committee structure which includes roles (chairperson, secretary and participant) and rules or procedures that relate them. Thus certain communicative actions would be expected of a chairperson and very often other participants would not be expected to contribute until acknowledged by the chair.

One way in which systems which implement a formal model of the communication process have been construed is as coordination systems. That is to say that some see their primary function as coordinating the flow of communication, or seen more broadly, coordinating group activities. Malone and Crowston (1990) have gone so far as to suggest the emergence of a coordination theory in CSCW. Many coordination systems employ a linguistic approach to CSCW based on speech act theory (Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969). One of the best known speech act systems is the Coordinator (Winograd and Flores, 1986; Winograd, 1987). It is also one of the most highly criticized CSCW systems amongst researchers despite the fact that in modified form it appears to be a commercial success. Coordinator provides facilities for generating, sending, storing and retrieving messages. Each is treated as a move in a conversation and they are all expected to 'string together' in much the same way as they would in a spoken conversation. To compose and send a message in Coordinator, users are expected to specify the type of speech act (e.g. a request, an acknowledgement, a promise and so on) as well as when they expect a response and when they expect the conversation to be completed. The receiver is presented with a menu appropriate to the speech act type specified by the sender. Thus on receipt of a request the user would be presented with a menu which included the options to promise, counter and decline. Finally, Coordinator issues reminders to participants in a 'conversation' concerning reply-by and complete-by deadlines.

From the point of view of evaluation, messaging is the most mature area of CSCW. Not only has there been quite a lot of empirical work on messaging systems, but a variety of evaluation questions have been posed and techniques used. For instance, COM, a messaging system developed by the Swedish National Defence Research Institute, has been evaluated using a questionnaire to explore reasons for use, experiences of use (Adrianson and Hjelmquist, 1988) and using detailed analysis of COM messages to explore dialogue processes (Severinson-Eklundh, 1986). Severinson-Eklundh found that, although the medium is a written one, users employed it primarily as a means of carrying out interactive dialogue. However she found that the dialogue structure in COM was different to spoken dialogue because of the absence of feedback and delay between turns. McCarthy et al. (1992) identified a range of strategies employed by users of Conferencer (McCarthy and Miles, 1990), a text-based electronic conferencing system which supports synchronous interaction, to overcome some of the constraints of text-based interaction. Finally, an interesting body of work is emerging from Manchester and Amsterdam (Spears et al., 1990; Lea, 1991; Lea and Spears, 1991) which is concerned with the way in which an individual construes a messaging system. For instance, using repertory grid techniques, Lea (1991) found that users construed electronic mailing as being similar to an activity like writing notes on a number of dimensions, e.g. asynchrony and emotional quality, and similar to face-to-face interaction on other dimensions such as spontaneity.

## Conferencing

There is a wide variety of conferencing systems available, from traditional, asynchronous systems to real time multimedia systems. The common feature is that users typically interact by accessing a shared information space. They may also use direct user-to-user communication. The advent of high bandwidth local area networks made real time conferencing possible and more recently the integration of different media within workstations has made multimedia conferencing possible.

HICOM, the British HCI community communications and information service is an example of a fairly traditional, text-based conferencing system. It is composed of a number of conferences, each conference normally representing one topic. For instance, it might include conferences addressing the following topics: HCI, organizational perspectives, CSCW, upcoming events, situations vacant and even a conference dedicated to managing the HICOM system itself. Users subscribe to conferences of interest to them, and each user gets information such as how many entries to the conference have been made since the last time he or she logged in. Each conference is organized by an administrator who may make judgements about the relevance of individual contributions, or split some contributions off into a separate conference, and so on. (For a detailed description of HICOM see Newman, 1993).

Real time multimedia conferencing is a more recent development which enables participants to confer with each other in real time using a range of media such as video and audio links to complement the traditional text link. Examples of real time multimedia conferencing include XeroxPARC-Portland (Abel, 1990), Bellcore (Root, 1988), EuroPARC (Buxton and Moran, 1990), and CAVECAT (Mantei et al., 1991). CAVECAT (Computer Audio Video Enhanced Collaboration and Telepresence) is a University of Toronto based real time, multimedia conferencing system which enables a number of individuals or groups in separate offices to engage in cooperative work together without leaving their offices. The system consists of a number of enhanced workstations connected by a digital + audio + video network. Each workstation consists of a personal computer, a TV monitor, a TV camera, a pair of speakers and a microphone. A 4-in-1 video board allows composite images of up to four sites.

Much evaluation of text-based conferencing has focussed on the use of and difficulties with shared information spaces. Whittaker et al. (1991a, b) have examined the use of remote audio communication with and without a shared workspace for different tasks. While the shared workspace was of little benefit for a text-based task (joint production of textual summaries), it was quite beneficial when the task was a graphical one.

McCarthy et al. (1991) found that a shared workspace supported shared recollection of the results of a jointly performed task even when the system and the task were wholly textual and there was no auditory communication.

Some research on real time multimedia conferencing has used video records to try to identify interesting interactional patterns and routines. For example, Heath and Luff (1991a) in an observational case study of the use of the EuroPARC media lab found that technological mediation had introduced interesting asymmetries into the interaction between people. Although users initially treat the situation as if they were co-present, actions such as gesture and gaze appear to lose their communicative impact when performed through video. There appear to be two senses in which participants are not co-present: the recipient treats the video image as a unit and is therefore less sensitive to subtle movements and the sender designs gestures for the video image rather than for the person represented.

The other focus for empirical analysis has been on the value of different media configurations. One of the best examples of work in this area is reported in Sellen (1992). When comparing same room and video-mediated multi-party conversations, Sellen found little difference in terms of how often people spoke, for how long, or in terms of the pattern of distribution of turns among group members. Whereas most video systems would allow only one participant to be visually present at any time, the video systems used in these studies allowed all participants to be visually present all the time. This may account for the similarity with face-to-face.

## Meeting support

Real time multimedia conferencing systems can be, and indeed are, used to support long distance meetings. But, some attention has also been paid to computationally supporting meetings for people who can come together in the same meeting room. According to Mantei (1991), between 50% and 70% of the knowledge worker's time is spent in meetings and many of those meetings are not as productive as they might be. Often these meetings are dominated by one or two individuals, there is pressure to conform, they are not well focussed and there is some degree of misunderstanding among participants.

Mantei argues that computational support can help make face-to-face meetings more productive by enabling people to work and contribute simultaneously, by providing a shared dynamic workspace and an automatic record of the meeting, and by reducing cognitive load. However, computers do not control meeting protocols and do not ensure equal participation. Meeting rooms typically consist of a conference room furnished with a network of computers and a large screen or screens on which images from the computers are projected for general viewing. Examples of meeting rooms include Electronic Data System's CaptureLab in Michigan (Mantei, 1988), University of Arizona's Group Systems Support (Nunamaker et al., 1989), Xerox Parc's Colab (Stefik et al., 1986, 1987) and ICL's Pod (Seward et al., 1993).

The Colab project was an attempt to provide computational support for small teams working together in the same room. Technologically, it involved networked computers, video network facilities and a specially designed room. The Colab room was designed to support the use of computers in meetings of between two and five people. It consisted of three tables in a U-shape facing a large screen. Each table had a display, keyboard and mouse on it. The displays were connected to one another and to the large screen; images on any of the displays could be projected onto the large screen. The Colab project also had a model of the meeting process that it tried to support by developing 'cooperative' software tools. For example, Cognoter was a tool for brainstorming, organizing and evaluating ideas, and Boardnoter was a tool which imitated the informal functionality of a chalkboard. A central idea that guided the design of Colab software was the WYSIWIS (What You See Is What I See) abstraction of multi-user interfaces, which in implementation often needed a relaxed interpretation.

Initially the design of Colab was driven by a set of design principles. However in the last year or two, Colab has been refined using insights generated from empirical analysis of its use (Tatar et al., 1991). In contrast Mantei's work on Capturelab (1988) appears to have been informed by empirical analyses of meeting processes from the outset. Evaluation of the Arizona Group Support System has been carried out in the field, with respect to both outcome or productivity measures and process measures (Nunamaker et al., 1989). It was rated by users as being very effective in terms of helping in the process of generating ideas, achieving goals and solving problems. When compared with projected man-hours for each project it was also found to be extremely efficient.

## Shared editing

Much white collar or knowledge work involves writing reports, minutes, papers, proposals and so on. As time goes on these activities are becoming more and more collaborative, even without appropriate computational support. For example, a research paper with a single author now seems to be the exception; research funding agencies try to promote interdisciplinary research, thereby involving a number of proposers, and most EC research proposals require a minimum of three laboratories. Typically partners email versions to each other or pass around diskettes or hard copy. This may not be the most efficient way to proceed and involves a range of problems not least of which is version control. Shared editing systems have been developed to deal with these problems. They include Shredit (CSMIL, 1989), Mule (Prendergast and Vogel, 1990), and Grove (Ellis et al., 1991).

GROVE (GRoup Outline and Viewing Editor) is a prototype editor designed for use by a group of people working simultaneously on the same text outline. The group could work in a single room equipped with a number of workstations or with individual members in different places using office workstations and an audio link. They might also have a video link. A 'session' is a set of Grove editors involved in editing the same outline. Users can enter or leave a session at any time. When they enter or re-enter they are given an up-to-date version of the work including any changes made in their absence. A session ends when there are no editors running.

One of the main criticisms of work in this area is that it is the writing task and not cooperative writing that is supported. Cooperation is relegated to sharing views of or access to a document. When the cooperative process is considered it is represented in the system almost as a parody of the system in terms of defining roles and access rights. Evaluation of these systems so far has been quite informal. For instance, Grove has been evaluated using 11 groups from within the laboratory where Grove was developed. Nonetheless some of the conclusions are worth citing here. Grove was found to make discussion more difficult, to make group focus more difficult, to increase information access, to encourage parallel working and to cut down on social interaction. (See Sharples (1993) for a review of design and evaluation issues surrounding collaborative writing.)

In passing it is worth noting some developments in the area of shared drawing, a facility being developed to support teams of designers. Groupsketch (Greenberg and Bohnet, 1991) is a workstation based tool designed to allow a small geographically distributed group to list, draw and gesture simultaneously on a communal work surface. Videodraw (Tang and Minneman, 1990) and videwhiteboard (Tang and Minneman, 1991) are two very innovative systems developed by Tang and colleagues at XeroxPARC. As the names suggest these are predominantly video mediated tools to support very high definition mediated interaction. The work domain which motivated the development of these systems was software design but other distributed design tasks might also benefit, for example, distributed design in the automotive industry (Powrie and Siemieniuch, 1990; Joyner et al., 1991).

## To what extent is CSCW technology-led?

There is a disjunction between the emphasis put on 'cooperative work' (CW) in conceptual critiques which attempt to define the research domain that is CSCW on the basis of its title or their own paradigmatic preferences and the emphasis put on 'computer support' (CS) in the many papers which describe systems, their architectures and how to use them. For instance, Robinson (1991a) suggests that the emphasis in CSCW is on 'problems of culture, interpretation, and personal interaction' (p. 36) and that this is what distinguishes it from technology-driven and formalized approaches. Moran and Anderson (1990) argue that the emergence of a research community under the CSCW banner reflects a recognition of the limited asocial stance of traditional HCI. Greenberg (1991) defines CSCW as 'the study and theory of how people work together, and how the computer and related technologies affect group behaviour' (p. 1) and argues that, rather than being technology-led, CSCW is the 'scientific discipline that motivates and validates groupware design'. He goes on to distinguish CSCW from 'groupware', a 'technically-oriented label meant to differentiate 'group-oriented' products, explicitly designed to assist groups of people working together, from 'single-user' products that help people pursue only their isolated taks' (p. 1). In contrast to these more or less aspirational statements, Bannon and Schmidt (1991) capture the current state of the field as follows: 'What unites CSCW is the support requirements of cooperative work. Accordingly a technology-driven approach to CSCW will inevitably dilute the field. To some extent the current lack of unity in the CSCW field bears witness to that' (p. 5).

The introductory paragraph of this paper suggests that the convergence of three factors, (low cost computing, the availability of networking and telecommunication and the growing recognition of the role of groups in modern organization), gave the initial impetus to CSCW research and development. These three factors appear, at least on a global level, to reflect a close coupling of technological development and organizational needs. However, this coupling is not always evident in CSCW research. As I argued earlier, some of the earliest CSCW work promoted the relationship between IT and organization as a key research area. However, in the years that followed a disjunction between technology-driven research and conceptual work often aimed at re-establishing the initial research agenda of CSCW is apparent. Only recently are there signs of some attempts at synthesis.

The early fascination with technology, and the consequent promotion of the CS (Computer Supported) at the expense of the CW (Cooperative Work) is evident in the early CSCW conference proceedings. Most of the early work described particular systems or architectural models, or presented case studies of system development with mainly anecdotal data on evaluation of the system (see, e.g., CSCW '88 proceedings and proceedings of the IFIP conference on multi-user interfaces and applications (Gibbs and Verrijn-Stuart, 1991)). More recently, papers which attempt a conceptual critique of the field (Bannon and Schmidt, 1991) or attempt to specify an appropriate paradigm for CSCW (Moran and Anderson, 1990) have appeared. Although I am convinced that a clear definition of the scope and objectives of CSCW is important I am not so convinced that this form of conceptual critique can ever provide that. It is often obsessed with defining the words and attempting a semantic or conceptual analysis when it is not the words but the work that is important. It is the coherence of the work, not the integrity of the title that is in question. That is why some of the following developments are particularly important: formal experimental evaluation of CSCW systems (McCarthy et al., 1991; Whittaker et al., 1991; Sellen, 1992); ethnomethodological studies of work places before and after the implementation of cooperative technologies (Heath and Luff, 1991b; Nardi et al., 1993); and business case studies of the effects of CSCW systems on productivity, efficiency and effectiveness (Nunamaker et al., 1989; Post, 1992; Kirkwood et al., 1993).

Of these studies the ethnomethodological are particularly salient when considering the question of whether CSCW is technology-led. Whereas experimental and business case studies generally follow the implementation of a system or at least the development of a prototype to which a design team is highly committed, some ethnomethodological studies have preceded the introduction of any technology. Heath and Luff's (1991b) study at London Underground Control Rooms examined informal work practices whereby employees communicate information and coordinate a collection of tasks as the basis for the design of new tools to support collaborative work there. While one may argue with their methodology or the scope of their analysis, at least having some understanding of the human side of the system before introducing the technology is a step forward.

If it is no longer predominantly technology-led, there is still a case to be answered that CSCW has become predominantly problem-centred. With some exceptions, where research is theoretically driven, much published research is concerned with resolving small design issues with little or no link to theory building. CSCW research now needs to develop theories of cooperative working, especially technologically mediated cooperative working, if it is to avoid replacing technocentrism with an obsession with small design problems. CSCW research and practice is beginning to look a little more balanced. Although there is still a preponderance of papers describing systems and architectural models and although there is still a fascination with what the technology can do, researchers are at least beginning to evaluate these systems in a human-centred manner. It is possible to argue that in a period of rapid technological growth and innovation the first priority is to demonstrate that an idea is even feasible. Only then can research on the interaction between people and technology at work follow.

Although empirical research is beginning to emerge and answers to some questions about the usefulness of many of these innovations may be imminent, one still has to be a little dissatisfied. Most immediately this dissatisfaction stems from the anecdotal nature of much of the empirical research. Take Diaper and Sanger's (1993) collection of papers as illustrative of the standard of current work. Although many of the papers in that collection at least nod in the direction of empirical evaluation, for the most part the evaluation consists of an individual's (sometimes the author's) experiences with a system. One could say the same of a collection such as CSCW '92, although there the nod is less evident. However even if we assume that that will eventually come right and that anecdotes will eventually be followed by formal evaluation there are two further sources of dissatisfaction: the fairly limited conception of work in general and cooperative work in particular apparent in many of the system description and empirical evaluation papers; and the lack of a real organizational perspective in this work.

## What kind of understanding of work is reflected in CSCW systems and research?

Definitions of work differ across time and between cultures. Most assert that work is an activity which is directed towards some valued goal (e.g. financial reward) and which is not necessarily enjoyable in itself. More often than not it is a productive process, in the sense that there is some hard or soft product (e.g. an idea) to show for work. But it is also a psychological process in the sense that it helps to structure ones experience, brings about contacts with people outside the family, and connects a person with goals outside ones own (Jahoda, 1982); and an inherently social phenomenon in the senses that people are socialized to work and at work and can play a varied set of roles there (Schmidt, 1990).

Much CSCW design is not, at least explicitly, informed by an understanding of work – much less cooperative work. Tom Moran, chairing a session on CSCW at CHI '91 commented that while all of the papers involved some level of computer support and it was possible to see elements of cooperation in all of them, work was conspicuous by its absence. The question of the relationship between CSCW and concepts of work can be tackled at three levels: the scope, process or skill, and politics.

Looking at the ‘official’ CSCW literature (see e.g. Greenberg’s bibliography, 1991) one would be forgiven for thinking that CSCW relates only to a very limited work domain, office or paper-based work. With one or two exceptions (e.g. some research on cooperative working in a health centre (Engestrom et al., 1988) or cooperation in the control of a helicopter (Linde, 1988)) most CSCW systems and research relate to what might loosely be called office work. The CSCW literature does not appear to recognise manufacturing work – much of which is just as cooperative as office work and more and more of which is computer supported – as a legitimate form of CSCW. For example, Schmidt's (1991) excellent work on CSCW in manufacturing is published in a human factors in manufacturing journal and is therefore lost to many in the CSCW community.

The situation with regard to skill is mixed. By reading some papers, one would be led to conclude that only the effectiveness of task performance or communication varied when people work through computers. There is little recognition in the literature that the whole nature of a task may change and that a new set of skills may need to be learned. This is despite the evidence of a generation of studies of mediated communication reviewed by Williams (1977) which concluded that although mediation may not always affect the outcome of communication there is often some effect on the process. It is also despite the apparently significant impact in CSCW of Suchman's (1986) argument on the situated nature of action. Take a quote from Robinson (1991b) for example: 'Work has to be understood as situated and artful, negotiated and constructed as it goes along' (p. 188). Again a disjunction between theory, which promotes quite a dynamic perspective on the process of work, and research which is often limited to questions concerning the adequacy of a particular channel of communication to carry a particular message. It is worth noting that one does not need to take an ethnomethodological approach in order to take the dynamics of work seriously. McCarthy and Monk (in press) have argued, from a particular tradition in experimental psychology which takes process seriously, that a dynamic experimental approach is possible.

Another domain in which the dynamics of work, and in this case the crucial but sometimes very subtle social skills required for group working, is neglected is in the allocation of rigid work roles to participants in systems such as COSMOS. Role allocation is treated as a relatively formal process rather than a subtle social process requiring a repertoire of social skills. Roles are also treated as relatively fixed even though the trend is towards role flexibility which enables workers to play a number of different roles at the same time or over time. Systems such as COSMOS, because of this limited understanding of work, are likely to limit rather than enhance potential (see Kirkwood et al., 1993, for an evaluation of COSMOS operating in an organizational context).

Finally the political dimension is often ignored. Whereas information exchange may be the appropriate level at which to treat communication issues, it does not seem to be an appropriate level of discourse for cooperative working where interaction is often more concerned with influence and the exercise of power than with informing. An example of this lacuna in CSCW was given by Ken Eason at the DTI workshop on CSCW and Organizational Perspectives, March 1993. He pointed out that CSCW researchers seem reluctant to treat non-peer interaction. They appear to operate under the misapprehension that all cooperative work takes place between workers who have the same status. Of course this is not the case: non-peer interaction is an important aspect of cooperative working. Neither ethnomethodological nor process oriented psychological studies have managed to treat the political dimension because both conceptualize the context of work in a very limited manner. The meaning of a person's contributions to an interaction pattern or the effort a person invests in carrying out a task is often specified by a broad social, cultural and political context, the language of which includes words such as responsibility, accountability, loyalty, authority, power, conflict, and trust. CSCW has not even approached this organizational dimension yet.

## To what extent does CSCW have or represent an organizational perspective?

Following Hosking and Morley (1991), it is argued that organizing is essentially a process of mutual influence with cognitive, social and political dimensions. Evaluation as it is now emerging in CSCW treats cognitive factors such as remembering and understanding, and socio-cognitive factors such as goal sharing and social perception, but pays little attention to the inherently political process of influence. There are a number of reasons for this omission. To date, most CSCW evaluation is laboratory based and therefore ignorant of the socio-political context of organized work. It is also almost entirely preoccupied with ‘usability’ to the exclusion of broader political concerns such as ‘acceptability’.

We know that using CSCW systems promotes a range of interesting social phenomena from ‘flaming’ to status equalization in certain contexts (Kiesler et al., 1984). What is more interesting in the ‘real world’ is how management would be likely to respond to these phenomena if they occurred in their organization. It is clear from the management literature that when middle management in particular feel threatened by developments such as quality circles, semi-autonomous work groups, or the introduction of computer controlled manufacturing, they are prepared to work for its failure even to the extent of sabotage. What we do not know is whether CSCW technologies are likely to pose such threats. In an exceptional piece of research for the CSCW area, Robinson (1991b) suggests from a detailed analysis of a case study that the use of technologies such as email can lead to the dilution of organizational boundaries, causing problems in this case with loyalty and labour turnover. Would email catch on if this story was told widely? Because of the predominant focus on design it could be argued that CSCW has so far ‘missed the boat’ which was launched by people like Blomberg. Research on the acceptability or the implementation of CSCW technologies, with attendant political issues such as trade union acceptance or sabotage by those who feel threatened, is lacking with one or two exceptions (especially Grudin, 1988).

Robinson (1991b) in a conceptual analysis of CSCW systems presents a very interesting critique of the Coordinator on the basis that far from facilitating the organizational process of mutual influence, it probably inhibits it. This case is all the more interesting because Coordinator is not an example of an organizationally blind, technology-driven project. It would appear from the literature that a thorough empirical and conceptual analysis of organizing processes informed its design. Yet the evidence from evaluations of the system support Robinson's argument.

Erickson (1989) points out that a system such as Coordinator, which enforces explicit, rigorous control of commitments made by the users, removes the very flexibility that enables people to make commitments in the first place. Reder and Schwab (1988) concluded that applying principles derived from an oral tradition of cooperation to the design of a system for written co-operation is unwise. Bullen and Bennett (1990) argued that cooperation is a social process which has to be designed by the participants and not by any outside agent, and that in this respect Coordinator users' unwillingness to label their messages as anything other than requests, the first choice on the menu, is revealing. Why did Carasik and Grantham's (1988) users feel that Coordinator was a 'straight-jacket'? I would suggest that this is due to the fact that Coordinator was designed on the basis of a very thorough analysis of one model of human linguistic behaviour; speech act theory. While this model might (or might not) be a very useful model of humans' use of language it is not an adequate model of collaborative working, primarily because it ignores the cognitive, social and political organizational context in which collaborative working takes place.

Rigidity is also evident in projects such as COSMOS where meeting roles are fairly rigidly defined at the start of a meeting. Such systems appear to mimic aspects of the formal organization and formal meeting, apparently (and maybe intentionally) ignoring the importance of informal groups and meetings. Maybe the following quote should be taken quite literally: 'Talking to people is the most important way of getting things done; (Robinson, 1991a, p. 35). It is interesting in this context that even those working on the COSMOS project appeared to find it an inadequate medium for making those final important decisions. It was good for sharing information and ideas but not for 'getting the work done' (Wilbur et al., 1986).

If artifacts do have politics, and if Coordinator and COSMOS represent the politics of bureaucratic control, the laissez faire liberal politics of negotiation are represented by the Amsterdam Conversation Environment (Dykstra and Carasik, 1991). The Amsterdam Conversation Environment is a semi-structured, non-task specific application designed to support interaction or conversation, not the problem solving process or group. Dykstra and Carasik argue that group processes and task performance do not require ‘formalised methods of conversation’ (p. 298) and that the lack of procedural structure may even increase both individual and group competence. This system and the design philosophy underlying it represent a self-organizing approach to social systems – shades of the semi-autonomous work group, an element of particular sociotechnical systems in manufacturing, which is not studied by CSCW researchers.

Finally, there have been one or two recent attempts to tackle the political dimension directly: a panel session at HCI '92 (Sanger, 1992) and a collection of papers from a workshop on conflict and cooperation in CSCW (Easterbrook, 1993). Both are useful attempts to highlight the important points that not all cooperative work is 'co-operative', that conflict is common (and often creative) in work relations, and that organizational structure, including its manifestation in power relationships, is at least a significant constraint. Authors would probably be the first to acknowledge that they have only initiated a debate, the effects of which are likely to take some time to work their way into CSCW research and design.

## Conclusions

So what is the state-of-the-art of CSCW today? CSCW is still a young research area which, after a decade, could be construed as approaching the end of its first iteration. In the early eighties, a clear and quite radical research agenda concerned with the social and organizational context of computer use emerged. However, the bulk of published work in the intervening ten years has been concerned with describing systems and systems architectures, with little reference to social and organizational context. A smaller body of conceptual work concerned with defining and redefining the field, often in terms of the words Computer Supported Cooperative Work rather than in terms of the aforementioned work, has also been published. In the last couple of years there have been some signs of a synthesis. Whether the endeavour has been successful or not depends on the extent to which the theoretical work is embodied in the designs that emerge during the second iteration.

In the first ten years some interesting systems have been produced in the name of CSCW. Many of them raised both technological and social issues which probably had to be dealt with before the discipline could progress. I think the time for interesting systems has now passed and the time has come when the usability of these systems has to be considered more seriously. Anecdotes will have to give way to systematic, longitudinal analyses of these systems being used by 'real' people in 'real' work settings. Only then will we be able to tell whether the endeavour has been more than interesting, and actually useful.

Of course it follows from much of what is said that the traditional HCI view of ‘usability’ will not do here. We are dealing with systems, some of which claim to be organizational. Whereas awareness of social and organizational issues has been raised somewhat in the last ten years, it has not found its way into design and evaluation yet. Perhaps we could start by considering more than design issues in our evaluations and look at the political acceptability of these technologies and the response of middle management and trade unions who might have some reason to feel threatened by these technologies.

Finally, what of the big issue: the relationship between CSCW and organization structure. In time CSCW, alongside developments in the manufacturing area such as Computer Integrated Manufacturing, may present opportunities for reconceptualizing organization. But for the moment I think the main lessons are ones to be learned by CSCW.
