# A methodology for the evaluation of IT for strategic implementation

STEVE ELLIOT

School of Information Systems, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia

PETER MELHUISH

Department of Information Systems, Asia Pacific International Institute, Auckland, New Zealand

The integration of business strategic planning and IT has been raised as one of the most important issues facing management in the 1990s. At the same time, there have been spectacular failures when business ventures have sought to exploit information technology. So how does business management go about applying information technology in order to maximize the possibility of gaining success, while minimizing the possibility of failure? Most literature concerned with information technology and evaluation focuses on the evaluation of benefits from the use of a technology. The limited amount of literature which considers the 'front end' of the process of selection, as well as implementation of information technology, is more concerned with identifying the business problem areas, the actions of competitors or how to set priorities between projects, than looking at the potential utility of a new or emergent technology. This paper presents a practical methodology which can be used by management to evaluate an information technology and to determine its apparent suitability for their business at this particular time. Its strength lies in the capability of IS management and line managers to quickly and cost-effectively identify the major opportunities for the application of technology to business problems.

## Introduction

The assertion that information technology (IT) is critical to the success, if not the very survival, of a business has been made by many commentators (Porter and Millar, 1985; Benjamin and Scott Morton, 1988; Keen, 1988; Earl, 1989; Tomlin, 1991). The integration of IT with business strategic planning as a means of achieving this success has been raised as one of the most important issues facing management in the 1990s (Watson and Brancheau, 1991; Neiderman et al., 1991). Effective management and integration of the capabilities of IT has been considered to be a major difference between successful and unsuccessful firms (Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984; Scott Morton, 1991). At the same time, there have been spectacular failures when business ventures have sought to exploit information technology, for example, the Chemical Bank/AT&T electronic home-banking initiative, Pronto. An estimated \$70 million was written off when that project was abandoned in December 1988 (Clemons and Weber, 1990).

So how does business management go about applying information technology in order to maximize the possibility of gaining success while minimizing the possibility of failure? Strategic information systems planning (SISP) frameworks such as those shown in Table 1, with one notable exception, identify the potential for strategic investment in IT but fail to provide assistance or direction to management in their evaluation of potential information technologies.

Most literature concerned with information technology and evaluation focuses on the evaluation of benefits from the use of a technology. The limited amount of literature which considers the 'front end' of the process of selection, as well as the implementation of information technology, is more concerned with identifying the business problem areas (Clemons and Weber, 1990; Sabherwal and King, 1991), the actions of competitors (Feeny and Ives, 1990) or how to set priorities between projects (Buss, 1983; Parker et al., 1988), than looking at the potential utility of a new or emergent technology. Assistance to management on how to determine the appropriateness of a particular information technology, as a potential solution to an accurately defined business problem, is hard to find. Management attempting to determine which technologies may be the ones to help their firms to succeed have had little alternative but to undertake formal evaluations requiring a substantial commitment of time, expense and effort from key staff.

This paper presents a practical methodology which can be used by IS management to evaluate an information technology and to determine its apparent suitability for their business at this particular time. The methodology goes no further than an initial evaluation of suitability and is not intended to replace a full feasibility study/pilot project. Its strength lies in the provision of a set of formal processes by which IS management can quickly and cost-effectively evaluate current and emerging information technologies in order to identify those most likely to be able to address the specified requirements of their organization.

The paper identifies the need for assistance to assess the business opportunity for application of one or more ITs, current models and frameworks are examined to determine the level of assistance available, the need for additional assistance is considered, a methodology to address this need is proposed and a case study demonstrates the application of this methodology. The conclusion contains general recommendations on the management of IT in organizations.

## A review of current models and frameworks

A large number of models and frameworks are available to provide assistance to organizations conducting SISP activities. Given the complexity of the area and the varying requirements of different organizations this should not be seen as surprising. While many organizations use an internally developed approach to SISP, examples of models and frameworks which are either commonly used (Premkumar and King, 1991; Earl, 1993) or appear of significance are shown in Table 1. These models have been categorized according to focus (on alignment of corporate and IS planning or on strategic impact), objectives, approach, effort required and intended users of the model. The models range from major undertakings requiring involvement of strategic and departmental management as well as specialist planners over extended periods of time in order to produce an IS architecture for the organization (IBM, 1981) to a model which can be used for ad hoc analyses of customer relations requiring little time or effort (Ives and Learmonth, 1984). Some models support application at varying levels of management, such as by strategic management only or incorporating interaction with departmental management. In these cases the amount of effort required will vary according to the number of layers of management involved.

All of the models shown assist in the identification, categorization and prioritization of alternative proposals for the implementation of IT; however, with the exception of Huff and Munro's (1985) technology-driven model of IT assessment and adoption, none address the issue of how to identify and evaluate the specific technology(s) to be proposed. Several models accept the critical importance of establishing IS resource requirements but emphasize, correctly, the organizational rather than technological issues (Parker et al., 1988; Earl, 1989; Galliers and Sutherland, 1991; MacDonald,

1991a, b; Singh, 1993). This emphasis is, perhaps, a result of earlier work which focused exclusively on technological issues and all but ignored the organizational context. While the current emphasis is considerably more appropriate for IS models there remain areas of strategic IT management which should be integrated with the larger SISP activity and which could benefit from the application of new models and methodologies.

Huff and Munro (1985) categorized the IT assessment and adoption practices of large firms into four models according to the relative emphasis given by the firms to technology and to business issues. The emphasis ranges from technology-driven solutions in search of problems to business problems seeking solutions. Firms using the technology-driven approach undertake a thorough scanning of sources of new technology as they consider a key corporate success factor to be the ability to identify and to apply appropriate technology. This approach relies on the thoroughness of the scanning and the ability to obtain high-level support for the selected technologies. Disadvantages of this approach were found to be the time and effort required of key IS staff in the scanning and evaluation processes. Unfortunately for firms considering this approach, no assistance has been provided in either the detailed assessment processes or ways in which the effort required for this assessment could be reduced.

Research into the SISP practices of organizations in both the UK and USA reveal that practitioners find the processes to be expensive and time-consuming and are experiencing problems with obtaining the commitment of top management (Lederer and Sethi, 1988; Galliers, 1991; Earl, 1993). Lederer and Sethi (1988) note that the methodologies current at that time ‘use extensive resources, provide limited results and raise expectations for projects which might never be implemented’ and call for the development of new methodologies. Earl (1993) notes similar concerns in UK organizations. The following proposed methodology does not attempt to consider the wider SISP issues but is seen as a means of addressing the problems experienced by practitioners.

## The value and purpose of methodologies

A methodology may be defined as ‘a coherent set of methods used in carrying out some complex activity’ (Dictionary of Computing, 1990). While this definition could be improved by expansion to incorporate philosophical and conceptual elements it is seen as encompassing the essential principles. Methodologies used in the business world can provide direction and/or sets of procedures that may be followed when performing work which is complex, costly or infrequently attempted in order to increase the level of performance of work and enhance outcomes. Different people trained in a methodology may be expected to reach the same outcomes given the same input data. For practical purposes a methodology should deal in units that can be well understood by the users.

The problem area under review in this paper is the selection of new information technologies so that there is a good match between the capabilities of the technology and the needs of the organization. New IT products, both software and hardware, are constantly being released onto the market. Some are clearly beneficial to many users, others are less so. There are many types of users of computer systems and not all tools will suit all of these users. The problem is made more complex by the reality that the needs, expectations and skills of the organization can and will change. Technologies that were once thought to be of little value may now be regarded as mandatory; other technologies that seemed to be important at the time have now been forgotten.

In applying the methodology described in this paper, two basic assumptions are made. The first is that the decision maker is reasonably aware of the characteristics of the technology(s) being considered. The second is that he or she is well versed in the requirements of the business which the technology is intended to address. These assumptions are not unreasonable: IT professionals should keep up to date and IT managers needing to exploit technology to solve business problems must be aware of the capabilities of current IT and be able to recognize the opportunities that IT developments may bring. The decision to select a given technology should be a business decision based on a comparison of the costs of acquiring and operating that technology weighed against the anticipated returns. All such decisions must relate the technology to one or more well-defined business needs. The careful specification of business needs is a prerequisite for application of this methodology.

## Overview of proposed methodology

The objective of this methodology is to reduce the risk of implementing technology by providing a standardized approach to the process of evaluation. The outcome of the methodology is a recommendation on whether or not a specific technology is potentially suitable for application to resolve a specific business problem. In the process there is an interim step which concludes if one or more technologies are, in fact, appropriate as a solution to given business problems.

The three major processes in this methodology are as follows.

(1) Analyse the business situation.

(i) What are the current underlying business problems?

(ii) What is the likely future situation?

(iii) Determine the current and likely future requirements.

(iv) Weight requirements.

(2) Determine the IT contribution.

(i) Evaluate technologies against each requirement.

(ii) Consider risk factors.

(iii) Conclude if an IT solution is viable or not.

(iv) If more than one technology is suitable, determine and justify sequence of implementation.

(3) Recommendation(s).

(i) Make formal recommendation(s) for further action.

The following sections discuss these three major steps in more detail.

## Detailed methodology

These processes should be completed in the order shown as the outcomes of earlier processes are subsequently required as inputs.

## Step 1. Analysing the business situation

The present business situation must be considered and the main problems identified. It is critically important to identify real problems and not just the symptoms of an underlying problem. As well as the current situation, future requirements should also be considered. If the future looks very uncertain and the business environment is very unstable then this must be reflected in the requirements for a solution, e.g. short implementation time and rapid payback. Figure 1 gives an outline of this step.

The purpose of analysing the business situation is to identify the current and likely future business requirements. The full requirements of a business include the following:

(1) Business objectives which describe the business objectives, tactics and opportunities as seen by the management.

(2) Specific requirements which translate the business needs and user requirements into specific requirements for a solution.

(3) Scope and interfaces which define the limits of the business problem under review and define also the means by which the solution must communicate with existing business systems and procedures.

(4) Constraints which may include existing technology, deadlines reflecting the level of urgency to resolve problems, likely future business environment and cost factors.

(5) User characteristics which describe the users who will need to use this system and whose strengths and weaknesses will determine the appropriate solutions.

The following paragraphs discuss the various processes in this proposed methodology and illustrate their simple application in the context of a small firm specializing in desktop publishing (DTP) which is considering extending their operations to incorporate multimedia services.

Quentym's Publishing Ltd is a small desktop publishing company in London. After an initially successful period in which the company achieved modest growth, QPL is now in trouble. The company is barely profitable at current revenue levels. More and more clients are installing their own DTP software and are building adequate DTP skills in-house. QPL is now considering expanding its services into the multimedia market. The management consider some form of expansion vital to ensure its continued survival.

At present, QPL restricts its activities to the provision of a range of paper-based services, producing high-quality printed documents, either directly from the DTP equipment or indirectly by preparing files for typesetting. It has substantial experience and skills in the DTP area.

Recently, it has been approached by a software vendor who could supply a range of additional, multimedia facilities to run on QPL's existing hardware systems. This would allow QPL to cut, copy and paste multimedia documents such as sound, video and animation into base documents such as word processing and DTP files. There is a possibility of buying a small and struggling multimedia firm whose staff possess the skills that QPL technical staff lack.

## What are the current underlying business problems?

The level of detail which is necessary for the evaluation of business problems will vary according to both the situation and the individuals involved. In determining the range of business problems it is prudent to discuss the issues widely within the organization since a problem for one manager may be simply resolved or indeed may be seen as an opportunity by another manager. A problem which is seen as having strategic impact on the business will require the determination of the full set of requirements for that business. If a problem is considered of limited impact or if the current business objectives have been clearly stated elsewhere, then the requirements need not be stated in as much detail. In every case, however, the users' needs must be clearly identified. This includes determination of which users, what technology(s), in which location(s), how it (they) can be used and the intended purpose(s). In other words, for a specified set of users the questions of what, where, how and why must be addressed since the world's finest technology will be of little practical benefit if applied inappropriately.

Whatever the level of detail appropriate for a situation, a list of all requirements which are to be addressed should be prepared and formally approved by senior management.

In this example, QPL is facing a critical situation. Expansion is vital, for economic survival. After a successful period, when DTP skills were at a premium, QPL finds its core client base eroding and the revenue being generated is insufficient for medium-term survival.

## What is the likely future situation?

Current business problems must be reviewed, in the light of predicted needs in the future. This is frequently a difficult and risk-laden task, but failing to attempt it creates the greater risk of selecting information solutions which impede rather than assist the business developments. A number of procedures, including the Delphi technique, may be used to aggregate the opinions of many and produce a degree of consensus about the future situation.

QPL's future currently appears bleak. Current technology-based services are not sufficiently in demand to support the growth and survival of QPL and the trends that have caused this downturn appear to be continuing. The future market for multimedia tuition appears substantial. Assuming that QPL management have researched the market well, this may provide substantial opportunities.

## Determining the likely current and future business requirements

A clear understanding of the current and future problems that a business faces will allow a fairly confident specification of the actions needed to confront these problems in an effective manner. It should be noted that these requirements are business not information technology requirements. The requirements are a statement of the more focused needs of the business, often at departmental or functional levels, which, when attained, will address (at least to some degree) the problems identified in the earlier two sections.

Assuming that the development of an innovative multimedia training capability is a worthwhile objective, a number of requirements need to be in place for QPL to create and benefit from a business opportunity. A summary of these requirements is listed in Table 2.

## Weighting the requirements

Once the list of requirements has been finalized, decisions need to be made on the relative importance of each requirement. Numeric weights can then be assigned to each requirement, with senior management needing to agree and to approve these weights. It is most important that the weightings be determined and approved before the ratings for each technology are allocated. Otherwise the process will become subjective and subject to political pressures. The weights are used in a weighted score method which is the preferred means of evaluating the potential utility of technologies.

Alternative means of evaluation include the following.

(1) Subjective judgement (i.e. it just seems like a good idea to use this technology now). The problem with this method is that it does not consider your requirements.

(2) Advice from others, including reviewers. Advisers may be professional consultants, product reviewers in magazines or acquaintances who are using an interesting system. The technology may be right for their requirements or it may be used by most companies, but will it do the job you require?

## Evaluating each technology against each requirement

There may or may not be a suitable IT solution for the problems and requirements identified in the first step 'Analysing the business situation'. In the second step, IT solutions are matched to the user requirements. After discussions between relevant staff, a 'score' is agreed, which indicates the 'closeness of fit' of the application of that technology with the stated business requirement. This scoring process is repeated for all the requirements included in the list. An overall result for each technology is obtained by extending the scores by the agreed weights for each requirement and summing these figures. This process provides a means of comparing the effectiveness of alternative technologies, for the same set of business requirements. In order to minimize the potential for subjectivity it may be helpful if the staff scoring each of the technologies are not aware of the relative weighting of each requirement.

The technology which comes closest to the score of the ideal is the most suitable. You may, however, find that none of the technologies evaluated comes sufficiently close to your ideal technology. This can occur where many of the requirements could not be met by the technology(s) under evaluation. In this situation you may decide not to proceed with any of the technologies, but to look for other solutions. In effect there is a 'cut-off' point which is the score below which technologies are not acceptable. A cut-off point may be determined at a level approximately 25 or 30% below the maximum score of the ideal technology. In every evaluation a cut-off point should be determined. In Table 2 the cut-off point has been set at 217.5 (75% of the ideal score of 290).

## Considering the risk factors

The introduction of new technology into a working situation inevitably involves risk. In the minds of many senior management, avoidance of unnecessary or unreasonable risk is a major factor in determining whether or not to invest in a technology. IT specialists should assess and preferably quantify risk when considering and ranking alternative solutions for a given problem.

Some of the major factors which increase risk are as follows.

(1) Newness of the technology being selected.

(2) High initial costs of acquiring the technology.

(3) High costs of training staff to become competent users of the technology.

(4) Relative inexperience of IT staff in the implementation of the technology coupled with relative inexperience of the user groups in the usage of the technology.

(5) Lack of management commitment to the project and the technology.

(6) An inadequate degree of technical support from vendors of the technology.

(7) Vulnerability of manufacturers or vendors of the technology to cease trading.

(8) The critical nature of the selected application (Davis and Olsen, 1985).

The greater the risk associated with the introduction of a new technology, the greater the time and commitment needed to ensure the decision to introduce the technology is properly made. Increased risk also presents the possibility of failure to meet stated goals of performance, budgeted costs and budgeted delivery times. In the most extreme cases, increased risk can cause business failure.

Risks must be minimized. Like any shrewd investor, wise managers of IT will have a portfolio of systems under development. Some will present virtually no risk to the business, while others may carry much greater risk, but also offer greater returns. An effective balance is required.

The following actions are needed to ensure that the risk factor remains under control.

(1) Recognize that in most cases investment in IT should be treated on a similar basis to any other investment since IT investments compete for funds that can be used elsewhere in the business.

(2) Cost-benefit analyses should be carried out. Estimate both set-up and regular costs, write off the set-up costs over a realistic time frame and put the best estimates of benefits to be gained from the investment. Then ask the question . . . does it pay? Where benefits are difficult to quantify, an assessment should still be made, however, in this case the assessment needs to consider the impact on the business and its competitive capability if the technology is not adopted.

(3) Consider the human impact. Make sure that any assessment of a technology considers the impact that this technology will have on the people associated with its introduction and use. Does the work-force have the skills and aptitude to work with these new tools? Will the introduction of new technology mean that valuable skills and experience will be lost to the organization? Many solutions (using excellent technology) have not performed to the expectations of management due to the failure of employees to work easily and effectively with new tools.

(4) Consider long-term goals as well as short-term finances. Frequently, there are strategic as well as operational and tactical factors to be considered when deciding on the introduction of a technology. As well as asking 'What are the costs of introducing the technology?', it is often necessary to ask 'What are the long-term costs if we do not introduce the technology now?'

QPL's proposal faces risk. The market opportunities rely on the willingness of clients to accept multimedia as a training process and there is no firm evidence that they will. Effective production of multimedia materials require skills and techniques that QPL does not have at present, but needs to acquire. There is a risk that the technical solution may be unacceptable to many of the potential users.

Further, some aspects of the technology may well be improved and this always involves risk.

## Reaching a conclusion about whether or not there should be an IT solution

It is not realistic to suggest that the decisions to introduce an information technology are entirely quantitative. The solution that appears optimal from a technical viewpoint may prove to be unworkable due to lack of support from senior management. While this methodology cannot resolve such a problem, its application may help to reassure senior management that the essential issues have been considered. If the requirements and weightings have been properly determined/approved and a technology scores well in the weighted score analysis and the risks associated with it are considered to be acceptable, then a strong case can be made to senior management for the commitment of further resources.

In the case of QPL, the methodology does not demonstrate a clear-cut ‘yes’ or ‘no’ for QPL’s proposal. Further analysis should be carried out before any substantive investment is made. It may be most appropriate to postpone further action until the case becomes more compelling. The fact that a proposed investment in IT can be determined to be marginal in benefits without a major and costly evaluation or, indeed, a costly commitment, is seen to be of significant benefit to management.

## If more than one technology is suitable, decide on the sequence of implementation

Many reasons influence the sequence of introduction of ‘multiple information technologies’. In some cases, technology ‘Y’ will only work when technology ‘X’ is in operation. In other cases, the skills and experience used in the implementation of the (relatively) simple technology ‘X’ will make the more complex task of implementing technology ‘Y’ that much easier. Apart from these cases, the technology with the closest fit to requirements and the highest return on investment should be given priority.

Only multimedia has been considered in QPL's case.

## Step 3. Recommendations

After conclusions have been reached on the likely contribution of the IT under evaluation, the final step is to decide what recommendations are to be made. Typically recommendations will be for no action based on the analysis undertaken or a recommendation of further study. Further study is recommended when a conclusion is made that a technology is of potential benefit to the organization. The further study typically entails a more detailed and costly evaluation, such as a feasibility study, a pilot project, a prototype or the evaluation of products from a variety of vendors for a specific technology. This final step is shown diagrammatically in Figure 3.

The provision of interactive multimedia systems to support training appears to be an excellent example of developing uses for computer systems. As the trainees use a single-user computer system, their training can be self-paced, with frequent tests on knowledge acquisition. The use of multimedia can add variety and interest to the training sessions which should make the training products more effective. Important concepts can be highlighted and emphasized.

Because of its experience in the design of paper documents, QPL is in a good position to develop the skills to use multimedia processing effectively. Knowledge of DTP gives an understanding of good graphics design standards and an awareness of the ways in which documents communicate effectively. Such experience should prevent QPL from producing training courses which use the facilities of multimedia as gimmicks and in annoying and unproductive ways.

The lack of specific multimedia skills, however, is a real problem. QPL should investigate the size and sophistication of the market and be more convinced of the viability of the business opportunity. They should also investigate the possibility of a takeover or merger with a company that specializes in multimedia. Only after such analysis and research should QPL move forward in what does appear potentially to be a valuable business opportunity.

Make formal recommendations on actions, which may or may not include introducing IT.

## Application of the methodology in a case study

The following case study demonstrates application of this methodology in a more complex situation involving multiple technologies.

## Defining the case

The Space Age Control Technology Incorporated (SACTI) company is a multinational manufacturer of mini- and microcomputer-based process control equipment. The head office is in the United States, with the Asian regional headquarters currently in Tokyo. Local area offices are sited in Hong Kong, Taipei, Seoul and Singapore. The company remains profitable, despite the increased competition of recent years. It has consistently earned record profits. It has expanded at a mean of over 12% per year and expects to maintain such growth. The Asian regional operations comprise the fastest growing part of the business.

Confidence in the rapid expansion of trade with China has resulted in a decision to relocate the Asian regional headquarters to Hong Kong. This will require a major expansion of the Hong Kong operation. The new office will provide the centre of all sales and marketing, manufacturing, distribution and servicing within the region.

The staffing implications of this development are substantial. Many additional office staff will be needed to support the different managers, engineers and specialists. SACTI's policy, however, is to minimize the number of support staff through the use of automated support systems. Many senior staff are being redeployed from Tokyo and the United States to Hong Kong. SACTI is prepared to invest substantially to ensure a highly efficient and effective regional office.

Being a supplier of computer equipment, SACTI demands of all its offices a high level of computer usage. In the days when the Hong Kong office was simply an area office concentrating on supporting two small SACTI manufacturing sites the level of computer usage was small and fairly unimportant. With the upgrading of the office to the Asian regional headquarters, information technology must rapidly take on a much more important role.

It would appear that office automation, networking and data communications and desktop publishing could be necessary to support the office; however, are all technologies equally able to provide cost-effective support of SACTI's requirements?

## Applying the methodology

## Step 1. Analysing the business situation

(1) What are the current underlying business problems? The major problem facing the management of the SACTI regional office in Hong Kong was determined to be the development of an effective office environment as rapidly as possible.

(2) What is the future business situation? SACTI are a profitable organization and their sense of urgency in the setting up of the Hong Kong office is based upon a need for an effective and efficient base for future expansion. The future situation is hopefully one of rapid expansion, as the needs of Chinese businesses are translated into orders and revenue for SACTI.

(3) What are the likely current and future requirements? The office must be able to react rapidly and effectively to requests for information from within SACTI and from its customers and suppliers. Costs must be contained. The productivity of the SACTI staff must remain at a high level. These and other requirements are listed in Table 3.

(4) Weight the requirements. The weights are given in Table 3.

## Step 2. Determining the IT contribution to the business

(1) Evaluate each technology against each requirement. A number of technologies are considered here. Obviously, the number of technologies that could be evaluated are considerable, however, the methodology will be demonstrated by consideration of office automation, desktop publishing and networking. We will rate each technology against the complete set of requirements. Tables 4, 5 and 6 show the results of these assessments. Note that the relative scores (in this case, 215, 211 and 156) give an indication of the relative contribution made by each of the technologies to the defined and approved user requirements.

(2) Considering the risk factors. Individually, no technology represents undue risk to the business aims of the company; however, the problems of attempting to integrate all three technologies simultaneously may create a substantial risk to the achievement of business goals.

(3) Reach a conclusion about whether or not there should be an IT solution. SACTI will need to develop skills in a number of information technologies as a means of meeting their business objectives. After studying the goals of the company and the business in which it operates it seems essential that SACTI rapidly develop, in Hong Kong, the skills to manage and use the technologies under study.

(1) Make formal recommendations to management, which may or may not include the introduction of IT.

It is recommended that further study be commissioned on office automation and networking/data communication technologies. Teams should be established to undertake detailed planning for the introduction of both sets of technology into the SACTI Hong Kong regional office. Such teams should determine the detailed requirements and examine and evaluate alternative product-based solutions. A high degree of coordination between the projects is strongly recommended. It is further recommended that no action be taken to advance desktop publishing at this stage.

## Conclusion

This paper is intended to provide practical assistance to managers when considering the potential benefits and limitations of specific information technologies within business. It particularly addresses the critical focus of using IT to enable strategic business innovation, which was identified by MIT's management in the 1990s' research programme as being the major thrust required for business (Scott Morton, 1991). While this emphasis has been confirmed in the UK by IS researchers such as Earl (1992) and Galliers (1992), practitioners in both countries are reported to have experienced difficulties with SISP processes which include the cost, time required and a lack of commitment from top management.

Details have been presented of a methodology to support evaluation of one or more technologies without the cost in time, effort and staff required for full feasibility studies or pilot projects. In addition, application of this methodology may be of assistance in obtaining the support of top management for recommendations to ,undertake major evaluations as it provides a means of culling less promising technology-based proposals while at the same time providing a direct link between business objectives and the recommendation.

As is appropriate for a complex decision, the methodology is not simplistic. Managers have long recognized that there are few simple solutions to their most pressing problems. The methodology provides a structure which incorporates well-known techniques such as risk analysis and weighted scoring and should not be difficult for a reasonably well-informed manager to apply. The methodology goes no further than an initial evaluation of suitability and, while it is not intended to replace a full feasibility study and/or pilot project, it is considered to address the reported difficulties being experienced by managers in this area.
