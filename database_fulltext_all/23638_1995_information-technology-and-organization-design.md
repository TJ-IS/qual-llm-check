# Information technology and organization design

MICHAEL W. STEBBINS

College of Business, Cal Poly State University, San Luis Obispo, CA 93407, USA

JAMES A. SENA

Department of Economics, Norwegian Institute of Technology, University of Trondheim, N7034 Trondheim, Norway and College of Business, Cal Poly State University, San Luis Obispo, CA 93407, USA

A. B. (RAMI) SHANI

College of Business, Cal Poly State University, San Luis Obispo, CA 93407, USA

This paper pursues the growing importance of information technology within the field of organization design. We propose a new macrotheory model, examine the leading approaches to organization design regarding their treatment of IT innovations and propose an eclectic design process that incorporates IT during redesign projects. The implications for emerging organizational forms are also explored.

## Introduction

Executive preoccupation with organization design and restructuring during the 1980s and early 1990s is a documented trend within the management field. A decade of downsizing has continued unabated within Fortune 500 companies. Restructuring has been a recommended strategic solution for industry behemoths such as IBM and small-size firms like UCAR Carbon. The public sector has followed suit, through the US Department of Defense consolidation and base-closing activities, and with the recent Clinton administration call for sweeping consolidation and downsizing within federal government agencies. The lingering economic recession and slow economic growth are driving similar actions within other nations. With academia, organizational scientists have explored issues related to downsizing and organizational decline (Whetton, 1980; Cameron, 1988; Cameron et al., 1991). It is clear that downsizing can be distinguished from organizational decline (Freeman and Cameron, 1993), in that downsizing represents a strategic move on the part of the organization to increase its performance. Downsizing is not a homogeneous phenomenon (McKinley, 1993) and it can be conducted in ways that are creative, proactive and compatible with organization development (OD) theory (Stebbins and Shani, 1989).

A recent comparison of organization design perspectives identified ten design schools or orientations that can be used to guide restructuring programmes (Shani and Stebbins, 1987). Five of the design orientations are comprehensive, that is, they provide guiding organization theory models, they spell out design principles, they provide a design/redesign process, and they have an established empirical record of application within organizations (Stebbins and Shani, 1989). The five approaches are organizational hologram theory, an integration and advanced development of the ABCE and organization audit and analysis (OA&A) design process (Mackenzie, 1986, 1991), five-track theory, an integrated expansion of multivariate analysis, participation and structure (MAPS) design technology (Kilmann, 1989), information processing theory (Galbraith, 1977; Galbraith and Lawler, 1993; Nadler and Tushman, 1988), sociotechnical system theory and its various perspectives (Davis and Engelstad, 1966; Hanna, 1988; Pasmore, 1988; Stebbins and Shani, 1995) and self-design (Weick, 1977; Mohrman and Cummings, 1989). These comprehensive redesign approaches have been applied to diverse organizations during the downsizing era. The Appendix provides a brief synopsis of each approach. Very recently, the authors of some of the perspectives have begun to recognize the importance of information technology in redesigning programmes (Galbraith and Lawler, 1993; Nadler et al., 1992).

Organizational downsizing is in part a response to the bulge in organizational pyramids (Tomaska, 1987). The bulge refers to middle managers and staff members. Executives have come to believe that their organizations have excess people relative to competitors and believe it represents inefficiency and restricted flow of information. Executives seek to downsize and replace people by installing new computer software and other information technologies.

In the present article, we propose a new macrotheory design model, explore information technology innovations and their impact on structure and other formal arrangements, examine the leading design approaches to document how they treat information technology and propose new ways to use information technology during redesign projects. Our purpose is to bring information technology into the core of redesign thinking, through better macrotheory and improved organization redesign processes. Finally, we discuss IT impacts on emerging organizational forms.

## Information technology advances

The growth of information technology systems and their pervasive use in industry is a familiar development in the 1990s. Information technology including executive information systems, groupware, voice messaging, online transaction processing systems, electronic mail, teleconferencing, object linking and embedding and other computer and telecommunications advances are fundamentally changing the nature of the work-place. Although controlled studies of IT impacts have been few, it is widely claimed that IT improvements require changes in organizational structure and managerial processes. Recent studies associate IT innovations and applications with corporate downsizing, elimination of organizational levels, improved cross-department coordination and better interorganizational relationships. Some studies associate information technology advances with greater organizational flexibility, open flow of information, greater participation and voluntary input and decentralization (Huber, 1990). Daft (1992) notes that managers can also use IT for greater formalization, standardization and centralization. Managerial philosophy and corporate culture have a substantial bearing on the relationship between IT and structural characteristics (Shani and Sena, 1993). Certainly, the resources devoted to information technology and overall strategy for IT are sources of continuing debate and struggle in many companies. At AT&T the trend is toward reducing mainframe and centralized information management. Business units are assuming 'responsibility for applications previously run on the mainframe through the use of local area networks and workstation technologies. The central facility is being replaced by a distributed network. At the same time, the corporate office is building a small policy-setting, standard-setting, architecture-setting group at a very senior level. They operate similar to any other business unit employing a local area network system, which in turn is linked via a bridge to other parts of the distributed network. In this way, local or proprietary information is retained within the business unit while other shared information is available along the distributed system chain. It is apparent that IT innovations will increasingly affect organization processes and outcomes and it is in this context that we explore the IT role within the theory and practice of organizational design.

The potential for IT innovations is exemplified by recent Andersen Consulting work with UCAR Carbon, based in Clarksville, Tennessee. Beset by cut-throat pricing and outmoded production methods, the graphite products maker hired Andersen Consulting in 1991 to help it re-engineer. Within Andersen and similar consulting firms, IT is considered the engine that drives re-engineering. With expert advice from Andersen, the company eliminated assembly lines and formed new worker teams in manufacturing, order processing and other mainstream work areas. Now, one employee with a workstation connected to a network can respond to a customer's request for price quotes within minutes, in lieu of time-consuming information gathering from affected departments. Workers on the assembly line can access vital order information on the same network using workstations. UCAR's work processes also automatically set the wheels in motion to replenish customer inventories on demand. All the changes were made possible by new information and communication systems that Andersen designed (Business Week, 1993).

Information technology applications at the tiny ECCO electronics company in San Luis Obispo, California shows the potential for EDI (electronic data interchange). At ECCO, both customers and suppliers use the same network. A customer reorder for 1000 disc-drive components to be assembled by ECCO automatically triggers purchase orders for needed components, corrected against existing inventory. The software covers purchase orders, inventory control, production scheduling, customer billing and other related work processes. Through implementation of compatible systems using common data formats and interchange criteria for the supplier-company-customer interface, ECCO, its suppliers and customers are becoming IT-integrated enterprises.

## Conceptual framework for information technology within the field of organization design

Information technology advances lead us to propose a new macrotheory model of organization design. The model (see Figure 1) builds on sociotechnical systems and information processing approaches to organization design and provides a new perspective on the role of information technology in organization design. Briefly, we begin with the business situation (Hanna, 1988). Perceptions of the environment and business situation help determine the organization's goals and strategies. Goals and strategies influence decisions about tasks and work processes (Galbraith, 1977). Based on these strategic decisions, the organization chooses among grouping (departmentation), linking and managerial process alternatives (Nadler and Tushman, 1988). All design choices are influenced and driven by information technology and cultural variables. Emergent behaviour within the firm and business results (Hanna, 1988) reflect the quality of managerial choices made throughout the design chain. The impact of information technology is further explored below.

The business situation is made up of elements and forces in the organization's environment. It includes expectations of various constituents ranging from customers to stockholders, along with competitive and societal pressures. Goals and strategies are influenced by these forces, but also reflect executive perceptions and interpretations of the business situation. Information technology plays a vital role in supporting sound executive judgements about the external environment and internal organizational capability, experience and competitive advantage. In today's rapidly changing environment, information about market conditions and product acceptance must get to senior managers quickly. Strategic information systems and executive support systems are two of the IT tools that allow rapid movement of information to executive decision makers (see Figure 2). Executives can also enter this information into expert systems to create profiles of the changing business situation and suggest new goals and strategies.

Goals and strategies determine what critical tasks will be accomplished along with new work processes. Procedure-oriented IT innovations allow reconceptualization of basic tasks and work processes. Procedure-oriented systems are used for repetitive tasks such as ordering and billing. They include transaction processing, management information and control, and formalized tactical and operational decision support. As applications are moved from the mainframe to local area networks, newer, more flexible software technologies are utilized. These systems facilitate changes and permit easier access to information stored in distributed databases. In many cases, these systems are fundamental to the core technology or transformation process.

Insurance, banking, investment, travel and related organizations focus on selected transaction processing systems to conduct their external business operations. Within manufacturing firms, computer-aided design, computer-integrated manufacturing and EDI with vendors and customers are being aggressively implemented. In these examples, procedure-oriented systems are mission critical.

Tool-oriented IT systems are less directly related to the production of goods or services. Tool-oriented systems are designed to help people communicate and make decisions at all levels and locations within the firm. Teleconferencing, electronic mail, voice mail and groupware applications as well as the repertoire of end-user software products are usually categorized as tool-oriented systems. An example of the redesign potential from tool-oriented systems is found at the Mrs Fields company. At Mrs Fields, cookie store managers at over 500 locations have access to the owner/founder and her corporate staff through E-mail and phone-mail systems that bypass the geographic district level managers. Store managers can expect answers to their operations inquiries within 48 h, supporting the idea that Mrs Fields is a flat organization with small-company procedures and culture (Richman, 1987). In brief, procedure-oriented and tool-oriented systems produce dramatic changes in the way that ordinary and complicated tasks are carried out within organizations.

The next segment of our model considers organizing and other structural choices. Information processing design theory is the basis for this part of the model. With tasks and work processes established, we consider grouping employees into work units and departments, linking work groups in various parts of the organization using coordinator roles, liaison roles, teams and other horizontal linkage devices. Further coordination and integration is achieved by adding a wide range of managerial and organizational processes such as selection, training, team building and rewards. As depicted in Figure 1, we believe that IT options influence grouping, linking and managerial process decisions.

In the 1960s and 1970s, computers were used primarily for accounting and control systems. Most computing was centrally controlled and administered. In the 1980s, the personal computer was introduced, making local and departmental computer tasking possible. In the 1990s, the introduction of networks has brought together the critical computer facility with the departmental systems. Companies are now positioned to shape future tasks. Design decisions in the early decades emphasized IT for managerial control systems. Managerial decisions to group employees into departments and to link the departments through the hierarchy and linking roles were made first. Information systems and other IT choices were to support the basic managerial structure. The traditional view was that information systems choices followed virtually every other design choices to be made. Accordingly, an information systems plan was unnecessary if an overall strategic plan and an operational plan had not been formulated. New IT capabilities such as the widespread use and acceptance of local area networks has made it possible for management to reverse this sequence and identify situations in which other forms of IT drive consideration of task processes, grouping, linking and managerial process alternatives. At the minimum, IT considerations play an important role in design choices concerning administrative grouping of employees, work location of employees and coordination of employee activities across departments.

The IT connection to grouping decisions is illustrated by the dilemma of the physical location of employees. In matrix organizations within the defence industry, executives must often decide whether to place technical employees such as systems engineers within multispeciality project teams (project matrix form of structure) or to place specialists within technical departments under the direction of functional supervisors (pure matrix form of structure). If this latter structure is chosen, project coordinators face the difficult problem of integrating diverse contributions from people working in diverse settings. The other extreme of grouping employees under project managers has the disadvantage of weakening professional expertise and training. Less experienced specialists are without mentors and employees miss the ongoing support and problem solving from fellow experts in their fields. Recent advances with local area networks and other IT systems allow reconsideration of grouping decisions. Many of the problems associated with matrix organizations can be alleviated through open communications among project coordinators, team members and other specialists via local area networks and groupware. For software development teams the use of computer-aided software engineering tools can enhance productivity even if the team is dispersed throughout the organization.

Linking roles such as that of the project coordinator in a functional matrix organization are greatly supported by other IT innovations. In situations where project members are at remote sites, coordinators can use audio and video conferences to include members in important project meetings. With the use of networks each participant can also function in a peer-to-peer fashion using workstations that facilitate data sharing and interpersonal communications. Coordinators who are skilled at organizing such conferences can eliminate high travel costs and can efficiently conduct business with low disruption to normal work routines of team members.

Numerous managerial processes are now computer-based or benefit from other IT innovations. The incentive reward system at a large health maintenance organization (HMO) is one example. Use of a popular database software programme at the HMO allows entry of cost and productivity data from diverse work settings, formula-based comparisons of work unit performance and accurate group and individual incentive payouts based on global and local outcomes. Incentive programme administrators are now able to provide positive reinforcement of good performance in a timely manner. Earlier complaints about long time lags between performance and payout, the inability to control for different size work units and qualitatively different products and services provided at some locations no longer plague the HMO's programme.

Information technology advances can have strong impacts on emergent behaviour and business results. Electronic mail systems allow communication of problems and solutions directly, bypassing the formal hierarchy and departmental barriers, but still within the confines of business protocols. In situations where reciprocity is possible, as with providing technical advice or solutions, E-mail can increase social support through relayed ideas, opinions and congratulations. Computer-literate employees are highly familiar with the advantages of peer communications about short-cuts, unique capabilities and obstacles associated with popular software programmes and mainstream company database programmes.

The organization's cultural values and assumptions influence design decisions, and, in turn, the design configuration helps shape emergent behaviour and values. We have separated the behavioural or visible aspects of culture from values and assumptions. Design decisions, including IT choices, determine what is required of employees. Actual emergent behaviour may or may not be consistent based on the culture and employee needs. Our model of design closely follows Homans's (1950) widely accepted views about background factors, the required system, the emergent system and the consequences for the organization.

## Organization design schools of thought: recent developments

Over the last few years, several of the leading design approaches cited in the introduction have been further developed, offering better tools and guidelines for design consultants and managers. Table 1 provides brief descriptions of the five approaches. A synopsis of each approach is found in the Appendix.

Kilmann's (1977) MAPS design model has been significantly revised. Kilmann (1989) currently includes MAPS within one track of his comprehensive approach to organizational change. Kilmann's (1989) theory relegates MAPS to a somewhat minor role within the overall change programme and he welcomes the notion that other design approaches may substitute for MAPS in specific work situations. The information processing school has also been significantly altered. Nadler and Tushman (1988) and their associates at the Delta Consulting Group (Nadler et al., 1992) have written two books that build on Galbraith's (1977) work and provide important clarity concerning strategic design, operational design and the role of culture in redesign. Design is now conceived as the search for a fit between the information processing requirements of the task and the information processing capacity of the organization's structure. The authors also provide careful attention to strategic and operational design processes and principles to guide design projects. The new books are a major rework of information processing theory.

The newest orientation is self-design. Mohrman and Cummings's (1989) book explains how organizations can create new strategies, structures and processes on a dynamic basis. This theory has close ties to STS models and principles, but emphasizes internal action learning so that managers have ongoing capability to conduct redesign activities. While the original author of self-design is Weick (1977), Mohrman and Cummings (1989) have extensively enhanced his work by creating a comprehensive design approach.

Table 1 provides other comparative information on the leading design approaches. In contrast to an earlier review (Stebbins and Shani, 1989), all the leading models now contain a well-developed redesign process. Moreover, each design approach has textbook guidelines and design principles to help steer redesign projects. In most cases, there is enough information available to enable experienced organizational designers to selfdesign. It appears that only MAPS and ABCE/OA&A now require the specific consulting services of their authors, although all authors would likely recommend professional assistance. It is also evident that the five leading approaches have been used in diverse organizational settings.

## Treatment of information technology within leading design approaches

In reviewing the literature on design approaches, we are surprised by the limited discussion of IT impacts. Whether the focus is on guiding macrotheory or a way of thinking about design issues, redesign process or design principles, IT innovations are not emphasized. This is especially striking in examination of the information processing school of thought, where IT considerations are predicted to be strong. Instead, the authors describe IT in general ways, with little or no mention of specific IT tools and innovations. The discussion of information processed during task execution in text material on strategic organization design fails to mention a single IT example. The material on operational design is better, where IT is again mentioned as a part of the basic work flow and as a managerial control process or mechanism to facilitate performance of work. Table 2 provides a summary of IT within the leading design approaches.

Traditionally, the sociotechnical systems approach focused on the design of routine work. Focus on non-routine work has been a very recent development. The traditional routine studies treat IT in non-specific ways. For example, the Pasmore (1988) STS approach views IT as one of the tools aiding the basic transformation process. Information technology is part of the existing technical system and additional IT choices are made during STS redesign. Somewhat greater clarity is provided in Pasmore's (1988) description of operational design, where information systems are created to provide feedback to employees concerning variances. Information technology innovations are viewed as part of the new flexible technology within the work-place. Hanna's (1988) STS-based organizational performance model incorporates IT indirectly via the focus on information as one of six design elements that are linked to both business strategy and culture factors. His book does not include examples of IT applications.

Some exceptions within the STS category appear in the literature on high technology redesign and with a few published manufacturing cases. The utilization of the STS approach in the design of advanced manufacturing systems brings IT into the mainstream of both systems design principles and the design process. The literature suggests that the degree of joint optimization of the social and technical subsystems is critical for both system integration and end performance.

Another recent development in STS is the focus on design of non-routine work. While traditionally STS was geared primarily towards linear work systems featuring programmed tasks, new studies have been conducted with knowledge-based work (Taylor and Felten, 1993). When non-linear unprogrammed systems such as hospitals and research and development organizations are examined, IT is usually considered a critical design component. Specific ideas and principles have been advanced about IT within control and support systems, client transaction systems and expert systems designed for non-routine work settings (Shani and Sena, 1993).

The ABCE/OA&A and Kilmann models provide a general perspective on IT and strategic design and include IT within task processes in operational design. The self-design model includes information/decision systems within strategic design and calls for IT support, flexibly engineered to meet local needs during operational design. None of the recent textbooks on these three models provide IT application examples. We conclude by this review that IT developments post-date the creation of most modern design theories and that the connection between IT and design requires new research.

## Information technology as a tool during the design process

While all the leading models are fuzzy on the role of IT and design, two of the management science-oriented approaches use computers extensively during design projects. Kilmann's (1977) MAPS technique is based on software that allows design participants to identify new goals and tasks, to select work activities that they personally want to perform and to select co-workers. MAPS is a matching programme that creates alternative task clusters, people clusters and an assignment of task clusters to people clusters, resulting in alternative subsystem designs for the organization. Each solution includes people who want to work together on specific tasks. After computer matching, the MAPS process provides open debate concerning the merits of alternative designs. The debate stage highlights the probable impacts of new designs and makes it easier for senior executives to choose among design options. The entire MAPS design process is a creative mix of computer and social systems change technologies, including a ‘collateral phasing’ process to deal with transition to the new design.

Recently, other software programmes have been developed to aid managers in strategic redesign. Porras (1990) has created a programme that guides the manager through a systematic organizational diagnosis. The programme is based on a stream analysis framework. It is apparent that software is being created by a number of design experts and that it will be increasingly available to aid managers in the redesign process. Furthermore, the variety of IT design tools is likely to increase as IT is more fully integrated into organizational routines.

## Top-down strategic design versus bottom-up organizational design process

While both the Mackenzie (1986) OA&A process and the Kilmann (1977) MAPS design process rely on management sciences methods, they are radically different in their approaches to design decision making. The MAPS approach asks participants to ignore the current organization completely and begins with new goals and tasks, based on input from employees at all levels. Later in the process, employees openly debate the merits of specific designs. In many ways MAPS begins as a bottom-up process and ends with an executive level decision. In contrast, Mackenzie's (1986) OA&A process starts with a strategic assessment by top executives. The process then explores how work is currently done. Middle and lower level personnel play critical roles in the audit work. Lower level participants help identify problems inherent in the existing design, stimulating exploration of new ways of working. However, lower and middle employees have little say about the final strategic organization design. Strategic redesign is accomplished by the consultants working with one or more senior executives, after the audit is completed.

An examination of the other leading approaches also provides mixed results. Hanna's (1988) STS approach is clearly a top-down, external environment-oriented design process. Middle managers and lower managers are involved in strategic design, but it is clear that an outside-in open systems focus is desired. Pasmore's (1988) STS design process begins with a focus on demands of external stakeholders and development of a new vision statement. Clearly, the outside environment and strategy drives design thinking in both of these STS models.

## Information technology and the organization design consultation process

Information technology provides a unique opportunity to foster organizational learning and ongoing organizational improvement. The development of an organizational learning process during redesign can set the stage for continuous monitoring and improvement. Our guiding model in Figure 1 places IT at the core of design decision making. Information technology alternatives should influence design choices at all stages, whether the concerns are strategic, operational or systems integration. Earlier, we provided examples of IT influence on goals and strategies, selection of tasks and core task processes, grouping, linking and managerial processes along with the effect on employee behaviour. We need to point out where these choices are made during the organization-design consultation process.

Stebbins and Shani (1989) have constructed an eclectic design process to demonstrate special features of the leading models. Table 3 shows how information technology can be incorporated at various design stages. The eclectic process requires a number of decisions at the preliminary project planning stage, including programme scope and depth of investigation. IT experts must be included in project planning, so that initial goals reflect IT considerations. The start-up phase is critical to the establishment of a learning mechanism. Parallel learning structures (PLS) provide powerful leverage for change (Bushe and Shani, 1991). During step 2 of the redesign process, a steering group or PLS is formed to design and implement the overall programme and to increase the organization's learning. When decisions are made on membership of the steering group, IT experts are included along with other operations experts providing the technical focus.

During the project study phase, IT experts can help document current applications and suggest alternative IT innovations (see Figure 2). A top-down perspective is useful in the beginning, since systems integration is always a concern. IT experts or consultants may also be able to apply software tools to document task processes and to generate alternative structural designs. Use of both groupware and computer-aided software engineering tools might also be appropriate. While initial exploration of IT possibilities is top-down, employees at all levels must be active participants in connecting tools and systems to the work-place. ## The challenge: supporting the emerging organizational forms

During the 1980s, various management gurus and executives called for the demise of military-like hierarchical organizations in favour of new team-oriented designs. Massive downsizing efforts have supported this movement and restructuring is in vogue. Galbraith and Lawler (1993) note that information, careers and performance incentives were originally vertical, functional and hierarchical. Currently and in the future, conventional organizations will be flatter, more lateral and less hierarchical. Organizations will be customer driven and will depend on cross-functional teams dedicated to products, projects and customers. With greater exposure to cross-unit problem solving and through tighter linkages with suppliers and customers, employees will become more generalist in orientation rather than specialist. The role of IT, especially the tool-oriented processes, will aid and abet the generalists. New horizontal IT systems will be created to allow changes in product specifications, customer requirements and service needs (Boynton, 1993). The emphasis on horizontal integration will be reinforced by performance incentives. Employees will be rewarded for teamwork, building strong relationships with suppliers, quality achievement and customer satisfaction.

Galbraith and Lawler (1993) emphasizes strategic and technological factors in organization design and identifies structures for large-size organizations that have evolved beyond the single business form. The viable options include traditional structural forms such as the divisional form (related products), the conglomerate (many unrelated product divisions), neo-conglomerates (a few unrelated production divisions), leveraged-buyout partnerships (such as Kohlberg, Kravis and Roberts) and the 'front-end/back-end' structure. The front-end/back-end concept is new. Companies in the computer industry are examples of front-end/back-end structures, building separate structures for product development, purchasing, engineering and manufacturing (back end) and sales, service, application software, industry marketing and customer education within global market sectors (front end). The companies operate with a product-oriented back end and a customer-driven front end.

Non-conventional organizations such as joint ventures and the network form will become increasingly popular. Network organizations (Miles and Snow, 1986; Snow et al., 1992) perform only those functions that they know best and procure other functions from outside organizations. The focal or integrator company builds relationships with suppliers and relationships range from tight to loose linkages. The integrator performs the tasks most demanded in the particular industry. In retail products companies, the integrator organization manages marketing, product development, advertising and other entrepreneurial activities but might contract out all manufacturing. In industrial, technology-oriented industries, the integrator manages basic research, product development, product engineering and manufacturing, but might contract out marketing and distribution to organizations having greater expertise. The network organization continually monitors the health of each component and is prepared to seek out new suppliers or perform functions internally if distinctive competence can be created. Network organizations will evolve with experience and adopt different design configurations. Accordingly, these new forms of organization will be fluid and transitory.

Both conventional and unconventional organizations will increasingly depend on information technology for learning and integration. Our model (see Figure 1) identifies multiple opportunities for strategic, procedure-oriented and tool-oriented applications in the various stages of organization design. In both conventional and non-conventional scenarios, companies want to influence and control the flow of information. In network organizations, design and control of information technology is central. Resources must be devoted to advanced information systems and the full range of information technology options if the organization is to effectively carry out its learning and systems integration role. Similarly, in conventional organizations, integration depends on lateral communications and problem solving. Effective strategic and operational design includes selection of procedure-oriented and tool-oriented IT systems that meet cross-unit and customer needs.

Organization design experts can be expected to facilitate and extend these trends. It is apparent from this article that IT considerations must be at the centre of redesign work. Accordingly, we predict that design consultants, managers, IT experts and IT-literate employees will increasingly collaborate on future redesign studies. In today's world, a significant amount of knowledge and know-how can be found at different organizational levels both about IT and the utilization of IT in the design of effective organizations. As increasingly radical forms of structure are considered, it will be important to combine the strategic top-down viewpoint with high involvement of diverse groups of employees in the redesign process.

## Appendix: abstracts of the five leading design approaches

## Mackenzie's ABCE/OA&A model

Kenneth Mackenzie is a management consultant and academician with a long track record in the field of organization design. His contributions include basic laboratory research on group structures, a model for development of organization design technologies, a congruity theory known as the ABCE model and a detailed design/redesign process known as organization audit and analysis (OA&A). OA&A has been used in banks, supermarkets, health industry organizations and manufacturing companies.

The OA&A process provides ongoing adaptation of the organization to environmental changes. The process begins with a strategic assessment of organizational issues and ends with a comprehensive review of the new design after implementation. The methodology relies on software to document existing work processes and to generate new design alternatives. The process includes extensive management consulting services to support transition to the new design.

Mackenzie's ABCE model postulates that organizations are enmeshed in their environments (E). Goals and strategies (A) must fit the environment. Organization technology (B) translates goals and strategies into practice. Organization technology includes assumptions regarding the feasibility of strategies, logic that describes the hierarchy and flow of task processes, allocation of task processes to positions (the organization chart) and the actual organization. Results and effectiveness (C) depend on consistency or congruity among all elements of the model.

## Kilmann's five-track and MAPS technology

Ralph Kilmann created multivariate analysis, participation and structure (MAPS) as a method of redesigning organizational subunits. During the past two decades his approach has evolved into a comprehensive organization design technology that we have labelled five-track. The five-track design model is viewed as comprehensive organizational change and involves the following steps: initiating the programme, diagnosing the problems, scheduling the five overlapping tracks or phases of the change programme, implementing the tracks and evaluating the results. The tracks include an examination of the organization's culture, managerial skills training, team-building, strategy-structure analysis, and creation of new reward systems.

The MAPS technology is applied in track four, strategy structure. MAPS allows all organization members to define problems and create solutions that enable the organization to function in more purposeful ways. The object is to find an optimal match between people and tasks and among people who wish to work with each other within the organization.

MAPS has a strong appeal to people in professional and technical work settings and has been applied in public schools, universities, community service organizations, US government agencies and private sector companies. The five-track model has recently been applied in several Fortune 500 manufacturing companies.

## The information processing approach

Jay Galbraith states that organization design is a decision-making process involving strategic choices. Design involves finding a coherence among strategy, task, structure, information technology, reward systems and people. The approach focuses on the uncertainty of the organization's task, since uncertainty determines the amount of information processed during task execution.

Galbraith's original theory has been significantly enhanced by David Nadler and Michael Tushman. They have proposed a strategic redesign process that begins with the examination of precipitating need, a preliminary diagnosis of the entire organization and development of criteria to guide the redesign effort. The succeeding stages include examination of alternative grouping alternatives, identification of information processing needs and coordination for each grouping alternative, creation of linking mechanisms for each alternative, impact analyses and refinements for final designs and identification of issues for operational design and implementation. As with Kilmann's approach, there is attention to the organization's culture and comprehensive organizational change.

Both Nadler (1992) and Galbraith (1993) have recently written books that focus on the rapidly changing environment for business and implications for organization design. Information processing design processes have been used within a wide range of public and private organizations.

## The sociotechnical systems school

The most diverse school of thought on design is sociotechnical systems (STS). Eric Trist of the Tavistock Institute coined the term sociotechnical to describe the interrelatedness of social and technological subsystems of organizations. The principle of joint optimization is the backbone of STS theory. An organization will function at its best if the social and technological systems are designed to fit the demands of each other and the environment.

STS is a flexible design theory. It relies on a contingency approach and encourages organizational choice, instead of insisting on the idea that there is one best way to organize. It has strong ties to quality of work life (QWL) experiments and is also associated with autonomous work groups, self-inspection of work quality, job enrichment, team development and other principles.

There are numerous STS design processes. Recent approaches, such as the process proposed by David Hanna, emphasize the environment interface and strategy. STS design processes follow action-research methodology and examine linkages among business-environment analysis, vision and strategy statements, system diagnosis, alternatives for joint optimization, experimental implementation and system-wide redesign. Steering, study and action groups plan and carry out the work.

STS programmes have been implemented in many countries, notably in British coal mines, Indian textile mills, diverse Norwegian, Dutch and Swedish companies and in American manufacturing companies. Recent attention has been directed to design programmes in high-technology businesses and to knowledge workers.

## Self-design

Self-design is an outgrowth of STS theory. Thomas Cummings, a leading self-design author, has also contributed to the STS school of thought in earlier decades. Self-design approaches encourage managers to plan and implement their own strategy/structure change programmes. However, there is a clear educational and guidance role for consultants to help with first-time design projects.

Self-design is neither simple nor quick. It involves an investment in training organizational members in diagnostic, process and design skills needed to create high-performance organizations. It emphasizes the need for practice in design implementation, assessment and continuous modification. The process of designing organizations is dynamic and iterative and facilitates organizational learning.

Although new, self-design programmes have been conducted within companies in telecommunications, aerospace, electronics, software and pharmaceutical industries.
