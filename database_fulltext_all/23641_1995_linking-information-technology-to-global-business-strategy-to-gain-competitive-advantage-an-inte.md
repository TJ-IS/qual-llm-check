# Linking information technology to global business strategy to gain competitive advantage: an integrative model

NARENDER K. RAMARAPU

Department of Management, College of Business Administration, The University of Tennessee at Martin, Martin, TN 38238, USA

AUGUSTINE A. LADO

Department of Management and Labor Relations, College of Business Administration, Cleveland State University, Cleveland, OH 44115, USA

While information technology has been recognized as critically important for supporting global business strategy, the contingent relationships between salient dimensions of an information technology and key sources of competitive advantage have not been systematically explicated and discussed in the literature. Instead, a universal view of the role of information technology in developing competitive advantage for a firm is often adopted. Taking a contingency perspective, this paper more systematically examines the links among global business strategies, global information technologies (GIT) and competitive advantage within an integrative framework. Additionally, the issue of sustainability of GIT-based competitive advantages is addressed using the conceptual tool kits of the I/O (industrial organization)-based and resource-based theories of strategic management. The proposed conceptual model provides an encompassing framework for future research on the increasingly important topic of global information technology and its potential to generate competitive advantage for firms.

## Introduction

The globalization of markets and increasing competitiveness have forced multinational companies (MNCs) to seek global business strategies for effectively achieving cross-border integration, coordination and control of activities to generate a sustainable competitive advantage. Of critical importance is the requirement that firms should effectively align their information technology with the strategic requirements at each stage in the evolution of their global strategies. If managers do not understand and are unable to control their worldwide operations firmly and manage them in a globally integrated manner, their companies may be at a strategic disadvantage vis-à-vis competitors (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1989). To attain a sustainable competitive advantage managers need to understand the underlying dynamics of global business strategy. Such an understanding can be fostered through effective configuration of global information technology with the global business strategy.

The purpose of this paper is to propose a conceptual model, integrating global business strategy, information technology (IT) and competitive advantage. The rationale for the conceptual integration is rooted in the contingency-based concept of 'fit', which suggests that a global strategy that is appropriately linked or 'co-aligned' with information technology should lead to competitive advantage for a firm. From this theoretical perspective, the paper seeks a broader understanding of the linkages among global business strategy, IT and competitive advantage by addressing the following questions.

(1) In what ways and to what extent can the dimensions of IT in a global environment be effectively linked with the dimensions of global business strategy?

(2) In what ways and to what extent can the dimensions of IT in a global environment generate competitive advantage for firms?

First, a literature review and synthesis on global business strategies, global information technology and competitive advantage are presented. The literature review provides the theoretical foundation on which the integrative conceptual model is based. Second, from the conceptual model the linkages between the salient dimensions of global strategy, information technology and competitive advantage are explicated and discussed.

## Literature review and synthesis

While the importance of the multinational environment has been recognized in the strategic management literature, the issue of what constitutes the environmental pressures to which MNCs should adapt has been conceptualized from divergent perspectives. One view maintains that MNCs are increasingly faced with the imperatives of a monolithic, global environment. Accordingly, the ‘globalization of markets’ (Levitt, 1983) has led to the convergence of consumer needs and preferences and to the uniformity and standardization of operations, equipment, methods and techniques of production and distribution. Proponents of the globalization perspective have cited the emergence of such global industries as automobile, aerospace, computers and electronics and telecommunications as evidence of a rapidly integrated ‘global village’ (Hout et al., 1982; Porter, 1986b; Ohmae, 1989).

Scholars subscribing to another viewpoint see the ‘globalization of markets’ perspective as a myth (Douglas and Wind, 1987). Rather, these scholars argue that national boundaries do indeed cirumscribe unique patterns of techno-economic, political, social and cultural forces to which MNCs and their subsidiaries must adapt with unique organizational structures and strategies. At the limit, these unique ‘pressures for national responsiveness’ (Prahalad and Doz, 1987) may be so intense as to inhibit flows of resources, knowledge, skills and products between the headquarters and subsidiaries of global business firms.

Scholars subscribing to a third viewpoint adopt an interaction-based contingency approach to conceptualizing the global business context (Prahalad and Doz, 1987; Rosenzweig and Singh, 1991). These scholars recognize that both sets of pressures for global integration and national responsiveness exist simultaneously in a given national environment. Differences in business contexts arise from the interactions of the imperatives of global integration with the forces for national responsiveness. Thus, it is argued, global business firms must simultaneously confront the conflicting pressures for global integration and national responsiveness if they are to compete effectively in the global environment. This view provides a more encompassing description of the global business context to which global business firms and their subsidiaries must adapt.

A detailed synthesis of the literature on global business strategy, global information technology and competitive advantage follows.

## Global business strategies

The strategy literature most often explains global strategies with respect to the type and extent of headquarter (HQ)-subsidiary relationships, product/market breadth and geographic scope of global businesses (Porter, 1986a; Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1989; Gupta and Govindarajan, 1991). A review of the global strategy literature is undertaken here in order to highlight implications for the design and implementation of global information technology that can yield competitive advantage for the global business firms. The strategy literature has generally settled on three types of global business strategies: global, multinational and transnational.

## Global strategy

Firms pursuing global strategies are primarily driven by the logic of economic efficiency. Such firms place emphasis on configuring their value chain of activities on a world-wide basis to take advantage of low-cost inputs such as labour, raw materials and components. In response to the economic imperative of globalization of markets, the firms pursue globally integrated strategies to achieve optimum production efficiencies through producing large volumes of standardized products that are sold across national borders at below-average industry prices (Levitt, 1983; Porter, 1986a; Ghoshal, 1987). This type of strategy is characterized by heavy investments in large-scale production plants and globally integrated distribution and marketing systems. Typically, companies pursuing a global business strategy emphasize centralized control and tight integration of operations to achieve economies of scale and scope. Thus, intraorganizational linkages tend to be unidirectional, permitting the flow of knowledge (technical know-how, skills and expertise, information technology, etc.), capital and products (inputs and final products) from the headquarters to subsidiaries.

## Multinational strategy

Firms pursuing multinational strategies focus on meeting the unique needs and preferences of customers in different national environments. Such firms perceive the imperative of national responsiveness as critical to their operations success and, thus, emphasize differentiation and customization of products and operations to conform to host country mandates (Hout et al., 1982; Porter, 1986a; Prahalad and Doz, 1987). MNCs pursuing multinational or multidomestic strategies maintain broad portfolios of products or businesses, emphasize investments in production/information technologies that are context-specific (i.e. specific to their countries of domicile) and focus on marketing research to identify unfilled customer needs. Unlike the global strategy, in the multinational strategy, HQ–subsidiary linkages are rather weak, subsidiaries enjoy a relatively high degree of autonomy in designing and implementing strategies that are appropriate to their distinct contexts and the HQ only demands financial accountability on the part of the subsidiaries (Karimi and Konsynski, 1991).

## Transnational strategy

This strategy is designed to cope effectively with the imperatives of global integration and national responsiveness simultaneously (Ghoshal, 1987; Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1989). It reflects a strategic orientation toward enhancing flexibility and innovation on the part of the subsidiaries, on the one hand, and centralized (HQ) control and coordination of activities world-wide, on the other. Firms pursuing transnational business strategies seek dynamic interdependence that enables them to 'think globally and act locally' (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1989, p. 69). Thus, a transnational strategy maintains bidirectional linkages that permit flows of resources, information, skills and products among subsidiaries and between HQ and subsidiaries (Karimi and Konsynski, 1991). Such reciprocal linkages are essential to creating and sustaining global competitive advantage (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1988, 1989; Ghoshal and Bartlett, 1990).

## Competitive advantage

The concept of competitive advantage underlies the logic of business strategy and has been conceptualized from two broad theoretical perspectives within the strategic management literature. One approach, rooted in industrial organization (I/O) economics (Caves and Porter, 1977; Porter, 1980), views competitive advantage as the position of superior performance that a firm achieves through creating and delivering value to customers in the form of standardized, low-cost, low-priced products (overall cost leadership) or offering differentiated, high-quality products for which customers are willing to pay price premiums (differentiation) (e.g. Porter, 1980). The other approach, which is rooted in the resource-based logic of business strategy, views competitive advantage with respect to the stock of ‘distinctive competencies’ (i.e. skills, capabilities, information, specialized equipment and technical know-how) that are not easily subject to imitation by competitors (e.g. Winter, 1987; Barney, 1991; Rumelt, 1991) and that may generate superior business performance.

From the I/O perspective, competitive advantage is viewed as an end towards which any business strategy must strive (Reed and DeFillippi, 1990). In this framework, competitive advantage may be attained fundamentally through three sources: scale economies, scope economies and learning-curve (or experience) effects (Porter, 1980, 1985; Kogut, 1985). Scale economies pertain to the low per-unit cost (or low per-unit price) of products produced (or sold), arising from producing and distributing products in large volumes. Scale economies are ascribed to expansion of plant capacity and investments in specialized equipment, production processes and personnel (Oster, 1990). These economies refer to the cost savings arising from sharing of resources, skills, knowledge and technology among organizational subunits. Alternatively, scope economies may be viewed in terms of the synergistic benefits (low per-unit price for a given level of product quality) gained through multilateral exchange of resources, competencies and know-how among organizational units or divisions (Teece, 1980). Finally, learning-curve effects (or experience economies) describe the operations economies gained through the repetition of activities, leading to enhanced learning and proficiency in performing those activities. Thus, learning-curve effects reflect enhancements in information processing and retention capacities. Put simply, learning improves with doing an activity over and over again.

From the resource-based perspective, competitive advantage is viewed with respect to the idiosyncratic, firm-specific competencies that are not easily discernible and, therefore, are not easily competed away through competitor imitation (Lado et al., 1992). Reed and DeFillippi (1990) have recently extended the conceptualization of competitive advantage (based on firm-specific competencies) by linking it to the concept of 'causal ambiguity' (Lippman and Rumelt, 1982). Causal ambiguity refers to the difficulty of establishing causal connections between organizational resources (and actions) and outcomes (or performance) (Lippman and Rumelt, 1982; Reed and DeFillippi, 1990). Reed and DeFillippi (1990) have argued that in order to achieve a sustainable competitive advantage, a firm must constantly invest in causally ambiguous competencies, technical know-how and skills that are not easily subject to imitation by competitors.

To the extent that competitors are unable to establish causal connections between a firm's resource profile and overall performance, the firm may enjoy a sustained competitive advantage. Causally ambiguous resources that may generate sustainable competitive advantage are characterized by tacitness, complexity and specificity (Reed and DeFillippi, 1990). Tacitness refers to information and competencies that cannot easily be articulated and codified into standard operating procedures and, consequently, are not easily replicable (Polanyi, 1967). Tacit knowledge is embedded in organizational histories, routines and cultures. Complexity refers to the type and extent of the interface between human skills, talents and competencies and machinery. For example, an information technology that is characterized by high levels of human-machine interface may be a source of sustainable competitive advantage, leading to superior firm performance (Barney, 1991). On the other hand, a fully automated or manual information technology may lack the requisite complexity arising from a human-machine interface and may not yield sustainable competitive advantage. Resource specificity characterizes the degree to which skills, information and technology are not easily transferable to alternative uses without incurring substantial costs (Williamson, 1985). That is, specific resources have zero opportunity cost and, if advantageously channelled to selected customers and clients, may yield superior benefits to the firm (Reed and DeFillippi, 1990).

The resource-based conceptualization of competitive advantage in terms of tacit, complex and firm-specific competencies that heighten causal ambiguity complements the I/O-based view and, together, these approaches provide an encompassing framework for competitive advantage. The I/O-based, techno-economic variables (scale, scope and experience economies) may be necessary conditions for attaining competitive advantage for firms in a given strategic group, but are not sufficient to generate sustained competitive advantage for any one firm in that strategic group or any other group. On the other hand, the competency characteristics of tacitness, complexity and specificity, may be sufficient factors to generate a sustained competitive advantage through their link to causal ambiguity (Reed and DeFillippi, 1990). However, they must necessarily require a critical mass of resource investments (scale economies), synergistic combinations and interlinkages among different types of resources, knowledge, skills and capabilities (scope economies) to enhance organizational learning (learning-curve effects) and innovation.

## Global information technology

Given the rapid globalization of markets and the increased role of information technology (IT) in the formulation and implementation of corporate strategies, global information technology (GIT) has emerged as a topic of considerable importance to IS researchers and managers. In the context of this paper, GIT refers to a data processing system that receives input data from subsidiaries in several countries and transmits the information to several other subsidiaries in other countries. Unlike a domestic data processing system, a GIT transmits information (data, voice, message, etc.) across national boundaries. Thus, GIT systems are exposed to a wide variety of environmental and organizational requirements. In the following paragraphs, elaborations on the role of information technology in global competition are made with respect to information technology architecture.

An information technology (IT) architecture defines major categories of technology, information and information subsystems or applications. The relationship between HQ and subsidiaries of a global firm can be modelled as a function of technology architecture, data architecture and communication architecture, as well as organizational structure (Mandell and Grub, 1979). The three-way categorization of IT architectures and their links to the structure and strategy of a multinational/global company has been proposed by a number of researchers (Keen, 1987; Reck, 1989; Alavi and Young, 1992; King and Sethi, 1992) and is adopted in this paper.

Data architecture defines the format of the database content or the arrangement of an organizations' database(s). For global firms, management of data pertaining to cross-border operations with diverse product families is highly prone to problems and challenges. The problems entail getting the right data in the right amount to the right person at the right time. The implementation and management of a particular form of data structure allows for the timely, accurate and complete provision of the required amount of data. This in turn may yield a competitive advantage to a firm.

Communication architecture relates to the theme of integrating communication facilities between the HQ and subsidiaries of global companies to permit proper flow of data. King and Premkumar (1989) have outlined key business, technological, organizational and environmental factors relating to telecommunication planning. These include compatibility of equipment, differences in operating standards across national borders, communication infrastructure of the host country (whether voice, data lines or leased lines) and the telecommunication regulatory regime (whether privately controlled or regulated by a post, telephone and telegraph (PTT) agency) (King and Sethi, 1992). A company's communication infrastructure makes available the information that is needed for the standardization and rationalization of cross-border operations to achieve production and distribution efficiencies at the lowest possible cost. Moreover, communication infrastructure enables firms to enter new markets by expanding the time window for doing business in the international context. For instance, a stock brokerage firm can operate in multiple trading markets effectively on a 24 h basis, thereby permitting transactions with its customers and clients around the clock.

Technology architecture addresses the configuration of a firm's hardware devices and software systems (King and Sethi, 1992). The choice of hardware and the need for standardization across units are critical issues in technology architecture. Incompatibility of equipment can cause communication barriers, rendering the sharing of data and resources between organizational units particularly problematic. The selection and coordination of hardware and software appropriate to firms for sharing data or processing requirements has to be based on cost efficiencies and economic considerations. Hardware and software compatibility permits efficient and effective transfer of information, knowledge and skills across subsidiaries and between HQ and subsidiaries of global companies.

## A conceptual model

Figure 1 depicts a conceptual model that integrally links global business strategy, information technology and competitive advantage. The linkages between global strategy and global information technology structure are based on the premise of contingency perspective that there exist systematic and substantial differences in information technology infrastructural requirements across global strategies. That is, the different global strategies (multinational, global and transnational) are associated with different types of information technology infrastructure that most effectively meet the information requirements of the strategy.

Firms pursuing multinational strategies have decentralized linkages with their subsidiaries. Because these firms' subsidiaries have high autonomy in designing their structures and strategies, HQ–subsidiary linkages are generally weak and limited in scope. The transactions that support these linkages are also limited, so a low level of data integration and low capacity of communication link is expected. The technology architecture of a firm with a multinational strategy takes the form of a loose federation of the technology architectures of the HQ unit and those of the different subsidiaries. Both the HQ and its subsidiaries retain control of their respective IT subarchitectures (Ives and Jarvenappa, 1991; Alavi and Young, 1992). This permits flexibility and promotes local initiatives on the part of the subsidiaries. Therefore, a decentralized GIT infrastructure is expected to provide a better fit with the multinational strategy.

Firms with a global business strategy are centrally coordinated from the HQ to achieve economies of scale and efficiency. Because of the requirements for high centralization, coordination, formal communication and tight control, a centralized GIT infrastructure is expected to provide a more effective fit with the global strategy. With respect to data architecture, the centralization will enable a firm to maintain a large number of enterprise-wide databases. Only specialized databases (i.e. data suited to the local environment) will be maintained at the subsidiary level. A centralized communication infrastructure greatly enhances communication and sharing of databases and application systems between HQ and subsidiaries. The centralized data, communication and technology infrastructure also permits realization of economies of scale in information gathering and processing and standardization and control of subsidiary operations.

Firms with transnational strategies maintain bidirectional linkages among subsidiaries and between the HQ and subsidiaries. In this context, distributed architecture allows a high degree of interdependence. The multilateral linkages support managerial input from the subsidiaries, which is essential for strategic responses to local opportunities as well as to the global distribution of resources among the organization's subsidiaries (Ives and Jarvenappa, 1991; Alavi and Young, 1992).

To achieve a focused treatment, the three components of IT architecture – data, communication and technology – are adopted and linked to several sources of competitive advantage. A broader conceptualization of competitive advantage, incorporating I/O-based and resource-based concepts, is adopted. Specifically, from the I/O perspective, IT architectures are analysed with respect to their potential to generate scale, scope and learning-curve economies (e.g. Porter, 1985). From a resource-based perspective, an IT architecture is analysed with respect to its potential to generate causal ambiguity via tacitness, complexity and specificity, leading to sustained competitive advantage (Lippman and Rumelt, 1982; Reed and DeFillippi, 1990).

## Data architecture and competitive advantage

The configuration of an MNC's databases is posited to yield different types of competitive advantage. Firms with centralized databases and applications seek competitive advantage by capitalizing on the economies associated with standardized product design and operations and centralized control of world-wide operations. Thus, where the need for global integration of value activities is deemed imperative to attaining global competitiveness (Porter, 1986a), firms with centralized data architectures can economize on the bureaucratic costs of information gathering, processing and exchange between HQ and subsidiaries better than their counterparts with decentralized or distributed data architectures. The centralized databases enable firms to achieve economies of scale and cost efficiencies in data gathering and processing. The centralized data architecture also permits realization of economies through exchange of information (scope economies) and learning between subsidiaries and HQ (learning-curve effects), albeit to a lesser extent than scale economies. However, the advantage of high-scale economies and moderate scope and learning-curve economies may not be sustainable over a long period of time. Because centralized data architectures must, of necessity, deal with information that is easily codifiable and articulable (i.e. less tacit) and non-specific to a particular context, they may not generate a sustainable competitive advantage. Put differently, any competitive advantages (scale and, to some extent, scope and learning-curve economies) associated with a centralized data architecture may be competed away through competitor imitation (cf. Lippman and Rumelt, 1982; Barney, 1991).

A decentralized data architecture enhances subsidiary autonomy in collecting and processing information pertaining to its local environment. Where responsiveness to unique host country requirements is viewed as the critical success factor (Prahalad and Doz, 1987), a decentralized data architecture may foster learning of the local environmental and strategic requirements of a particular subsidiary. However, it is unlikely to generate competitive advantage for the entire organization through scale or scope economies. Thus, as shown in Table 2, decentralized data architectures are posited to yield low competitive advantages through scale and scope economies and high competitive advantages through learning-curve effects, relative to centralized or distributed data architectures. However, since this form of information technology configuration does permit accumulation of specialized, context-specific knowledge that is not codifiable and articulable for the benefit of other subsidiaries, it has the potential to generate a sustainable competitive advantage through causal ambiguity.

A distributed data architecture offers the greatest advantage by permitting multilateral flow of information/knowledge between HQ and subsidiaries and among subsidiaries. The distributed IT configuration enables an MNC to realize economic benefits and cost efficiencies simultaneously through scale economies and to adapt to local conditions through learning and innovation effectively. Furthermore, the complex pool of skills and capabilities that enables firms with distributed data architectures to ‘think globally and act locally’ simultaneously also becomes a source of sustained competitive advantage insofar as competitors are unable to understand and replicate those competencies.

In sum, centralized data architectures have the potential to generate high economies of scale, relative to other IT configurations, but such a competitive advantage is unlikely to be sustained, since it is low in tacitness, complexity and specificity. Decentralized data architectures permit development of competitive advantage through learning-curve effects; the knowledge so acquired is high in tacitness and specificity and, thus, can give rise to a sustainable competitive advantage. However, such an IT configuration may put firms at a competitive disadvantage when the competitive imperative is on world-wide integration of activities. It appears that the distributed data architecture has the potential to both develop and sustain competitive advantage by enabling cross-border transfer of information, knowledge and skills among subsidiaries, while providing sufficient flexibility and autonomy in gathering and processing of information that is suited to particular local needs of the subsidiaries.

## Communication architecture and competitive advantage

The value of a communication architecture lies in its ability to transfer information/knowledge between various business entities as effectively and efficiently as possible. A company's communication infrastructure makes available the information that is needed for the standardization and rationalization of cross-border operations to achieve production and distribution efficiencies at the lowest possible cost. Companies such as IBM and AT&T have set up purchasing units in the Far East to buy inexpensive and high-quality electrical components for their world-wide operations. Their well-designed communication infrastructures have enabled them to meet effectively the inventory requirements of their plants world-wide. These companies can easily make price comparisons and assure component availability before committing to purchase. Thus, the cost savings obtained outweigh the additional administrative and transportation costs entailed (Neo, 1991). The communication infrastructure also permits cost reductions by capturing and reducing cost at its source, thereby minimizing the need for re-entry of data upon receipt and reducing costly errors.

A communication architecture also enables a company to deliver superior value to its customers and stakeholders relative to its competitors. The communication architecture not only links the various business units or subsidiaries of a company, but also links the company to its various stakeholders (customers, dealers, suppliers, etc.) in such a way as to facilitate the flow of information/knowledge that is essential to delivering and producing high-quality products and services to clients. The delivery of superior product/service quality to clients may enhance the reputation of the company, which in turn leads to sustained competitive advantage and superior performance (Lado et al., 1992). Global companies such as Sony, Toyota and NV Philips have developed sophisticated communication architectures that permit knowledge transfer across their various subsidiaries for the design, production and distribution of top-of-the-line, innovative products to customers world-wide (Ghoshal and Bartlett, 1990).

Consistent with the contingency-based thrust of this paper, it is submitted that competitive advantage accruing from a communication architecture depends on the type of configuration of the communication system. Specifically, multiple nodal networks, connecting HQ and subsidiaries for transfer of information are suited to accumulation and exchange of information/knowledge pertaining to a unique MNC context. Such a communication architecture draws together information systems, marketing, production, R&D and other experts that have the unique skills, experience and knowledge of a particular local environment of an MNC subsidiary, thereby enabling the company to respond/adapt effectively to its context with product/service offerings that are uniquely tailored to the needs and preferences of customers. However, because of cultural variations and political/legal differences among nations, it may be difficult, if not impossible, to transfer idiosyncratic knowledge pertaining to a particular subsidiary's product offerings for the enhancement of other subsidiaries' products. Thus, multiple-node network communication architectures may be a source of sustained competitive advantage to the extent that they permit accumulation, processing and use of context-specific, specialized knowledge that is difficult to codify and articulate in other contexts. However, multiple-node network communication infrastructures may lead to a low competitive advantage through scale and scope economies given the difficulty of transferring data/information between subsidiaries and across national borders.

On the other hand, direct (that is, simple communication between HQ and individual subsidiaries) communication architectures permit transfer of standardized information/knowledge from a central processing facility at the headquarters to subsidiaries domiciled in various countries, thus permitting realization of economies of scale and scope. However, the sustainability of competitive advantage accruing from direct communication architecture may be open to question since it entails transfer of knowledge that is low in tacitness, specificity and complexity.

An integrative (network that provides a high degree of linkages between HQ and subsidiaries and among subsidiaries) communication architecture enables information to be effectively transferred between HQ and subsidiaries, while providing individual subsidiaries with the flexibility to collect and process information that is uniquely suited to their local circumstances. Thus, an integrative communication architecture would generate a competitive advantage through scale, scope and learning-curve economies. Furthermore, this type of communication architecture enables complex interactions between global technology (hardware and software) and local skills, expertise and knowledge to exploit global opportunities with local talents and initiatives. Hence, an integrative communication architecture can potentially generate a sustainable competitive advantage, through generating causally ambiguous, knowledge-based competencies.

## Technology architecture and competitive advantage

The transferability of knowledge across borders also depends on the extent to which a company's technology is compatible. A technology architecture refers to the type of configuration of IT hardware and software systems to permit transfer of information, skills, knowledge and capabilities between business units effectively and efficiently. An interdependent (that is, HQ and subsidiaries are interdependent) technology architecture provides the highest flexibility to a global business firm because it uses multilateral linkages among subsidiaries and between subsidiaries and headquarters. The multilateral linkages permit efficient and effective transfer of information, knowledge, skills and hardware systems across subsidiaries. The sharing of technology can enhance competitive advantage through realization of scope economies. For example, some countries may be rich in highly skilled IS professionals or may have a well-developed infrastructure (telecommunication capability, supply of technology equipment and services). Through its technology infrastructure, a company operating in different countries can effectively exploit these resources and channel them to its subsidiaries. Thus, an interdependent technology architecture can be a source of sustained competitive advantage by permitting realization of scale, scope and learning-curve economies in accumulation and processing of difficult-to-imitate information that is transmitted to subsidiaries through complex channels.

A central (that is, centrally shared IT facilities at HQ) technology architecture can create competitive advantage through promoting scale-based efficiencies in information processing and transmission. However, it should again be emphasized that competitive advantage flowing from centralized IT architecture, though necessary, may not be sufficient for sustained superior performance since they are less causally ambiguous (i.e. low in tacitness, complexity and specificity).

On the other hand, an independent/local (that is, facilities independent at HQ and subsidiaries) technology architecture is designed to adapt to local requirements effectively; thus, it is more effective in processing causally ambiguous information/knowledge that is difficult to replicate by competitors.

## Conclusion

In an intensely competitive global market-place, the importance of global information technology for developing competitive advantage for MNCs cannot be overemphasized. Although the topic of information technology and its link to global strategy has received a good deal of attention and treatment in the literature, much of the literature remains fragmentary, offering only partial and potentially contradictory normative implications for managers. This paper sought to systematically discuss the linkages among global strategy, information technology and competitive advantage within a global business environment. A contingency-based perspective was adopted to analyse the fit between global strategy and global information technology, leading to competitive advantage. Additionally, the paper more closely examined the sustainability of GIT-based competitive advantage using the conceptual tool kits of the I/O-based and resource-based strategic management theories. It is argued that information technology will have a differential impact on the development and sustainability of competitive advantage, depending on the type of information system architecture that is congruent with the strategic requirements of a multinational/global company. Thus, rather than taking a universal view of the role of global information technology in developing competitive advantage for a firm, this paper underscores the need for researchers and managers to critically examine key strategic contingencies in evaluating the sustainability of IT-based competitive advantage.
