# Participation in the development of inter-organizational systems involving users outside the organization

ANGÈLE L.M. CAVAYE

School of Systems Engineering, Policy Analysis and Management, Delft University of Technology. Jaffalaan 5, PO Box 5015, 2600 GA DELFT, The Netherlands

User participation is usually discussed in the context of internal systems. This study places participation in an inter-organizational context where the intended users are outside the sponsoring organization. This paper uses case evidence to investigate whether factors identified in the user participation literature as contingency factors for participation are also associated with participation in the development of inter-organizational systems. It was found that previously identified factors are not predictors of participation or non-participation in the inter-organizational context. Instead, the case evidence suggests that the underlying reason for development of such systems was associated with participation or non-participation by intended users from outside the sponsoring organization.

## Introduction

This study looks at participation in the specific context of inter-organizational systems. A theoretical paper (Johnston and Vitale, 1988) and two empirical studies (Runge, 1985; Reich and Benbasat, 1990) have mentioned the importance of participation of intended users in the development of inter-organizational systems. All three studies focused on inter-organizational systems and the participation issue received only peripheral attention. No previous study has specifically focused on user participation in the context of inter-organizational systems.

Inter-organizational systems are a specific type of system: they are systems that extend beyond the boundaries of one organization. User participation in the development of inter-organizational systems differs from that in the development of systems which are internal to the organization. In inter-organizational systems, intended users are outside the organization which is developing the system and this may have implications for the ability and willingness of users to participate. Also, the development of an inter-organizational system is likely to have a strategic impact and, for reasons of confidentiality, the sponsoring organization may want to limit or avoid participation by users external to the organization (Reich and Huff, 1991).

There are different kinds of inter-organizational systems: those that link organizations with their customers, suppliers, distributors or other business partners. Use of some systems is compulsory (like some EDI applications in the automobile industry); adoption of others is voluntary. This study concentrates on customer-oriented systems (COS). These systems are defined as systems that link organizations with their customers, and their adoption is voluntary on the part of the intended users.

Focusing on the participation of users in the development of COS, this study responds to calls for participation research in the context of a specific type of system. By extending the participation concept to systems where intended users are outside the organization, this study adds a new dimension to user participation research.

The paper is structured as follows. First, previous research concerning participation in system development is summarized; next, the research question is highlighted and the research method is described. The major part of the paper presents results of the fieldwork which was designed to find out what underlying factor(s) lead organizations to seek out participation of customers during the design of inter-organizational systems. This is done, firstly, by testing for factors previously identified in the user participation literature as facilitating or inhibiting participation. Then, pattern-matching techniques are used to find major factors which distinguish between the cases that did and those that did not involve customers during system development.

## Previous research

Barki and Hartwick (1989) define user participation as ‘a set of operations and activities performed by users’ during system development (p.86). Using this definition it is possible to identify an area in the IS literature which focuses on the participation of users during various stages in system development. This literature addresses a number of issues concerning user participation; these issues include reasons for participation, the process of participation, outcomes of participation and participation and its link to system success. Very few studies (Runge, 1985; Johnston and Vitale, 1988; Reich and Benbasat, 1990) mention participation in the setting of inter-organizational systems.

Two empirical studies (Runge, 1985; Reich and Banbasat, 1990), focusing on systems linking organizations with their customers, report some findings concerning user participation during development. Both studies report a correlation between participation and subsequent success of the systems. Their research findings also suggest that customer participation is not a feature of all development projects; customers participate in the minority of cases only (Runge, 1985; Reich and Benbasat, 1990). Since these studies do not focus on the participation variable, no explanation is given for the reasons for participation or the contingencies which are associated with participation of customers during development.

Participation may be more appropriate in some conditions and irrelevant in others; for that reason it is important to investigate contingencies. A number of factors impact on a sponsoring organization's decision regarding customer participation during development. Such factors may be derived from literature on user participation in development and from literature on strategic information systems and inter-organizational systems. Previous research distinguishes between the circumstances which make user participation desirable or possible and, on the other hand, the circumstances which make user participation unlikely or unnecessary.

The contingency factors can be categorized as organizational factors (which can be controlled by the organization), project-related variables (which are project-specific and cannot be controlled) and customer-related variables (which are customer-dependent and cannot be controlled by the organization).

## Organizational factors

## Time constraint

Projects which have to be completed quickly tend to discourage or preclude user participation in development (Hirschheim, 1985; Tait and Vessey, 1988). Projects which require a longer time to develop, or which are developed without a time constraint, tend to encourage user participation (Hirschheim, 1985).

## Financial constraint

When there are plenty of resources, user participation is facilitated (Doll, 1987). Constraint on resources discourages user participation in development (Tait and Vessey, 1988).

## Top management commitment

Commitment of top management ensures resource provision and encourages user participation (Ginzberg, 1981; Doll, 1987). Lack of top management commitment inhibits user participation (Ginzberg, 1981).

## Project-related factors

## Structuredness of task

Tasks which are relatively structured or which are at the operational control level in an organization do not require user participation in development (Schonberger, 1980). Unstructured tasks do require user participation (Ives and Olson, 1984; Tait and Vessey, 1988).

## Complexity of task

Tasks which are technically complex require user participation (Tait and Vessey, 1988); projects which are cross-functional benefit from user participation (Beath, 1987); large projects benefit from user participation (Hirschheim, 1985).

## Initiator of project

If the idea for the system does not originate from users, user participation is desirable (Kim and Lee, 1986).

## Expected change

A system which is likely to bring about significant change for the user benefits from user participation (Markus, 1983).

## Confidentiality

This issue is not discussed in the user participation literature and may be peculiar to the development of inter-organizational systems. Strategic considerations may inhibit or preclude customer participation during the COS development process. Organizations seeking to obtain a strategic advantage over competitors with a COS may be concerned about security leaks (Reich and Huff, 1991). In order to preserve and ensure confidentiality, organizations may not wish to involve customers in the COS development process.

## Customer-related factors

## Willingness to participate

Potential users are motivated to participate in development if the system and output are perceived to be important to the user (Anderson, 1985).

## Ability to participate

Intended users may not be able to participate if they are geographically distant from the project or when their own jobs take priority (Beath, 1987). Users are unlikely to be able to participate in a meaningful way if they do not understand the technology (Anderson, 1985).

## Method

The objective of this study was to investigate what factors encourage or discourage participation of customers in the development of customer-oriented systems. Because of the exploratory nature of this study, in-depth data were required for a broad range of variables. For this reason a case research method was adopted. Use of case research enabled the collection of full, rich data and facilitated the capture of contextual information relating to each individual development project.

Data was collected concerning the development of nine systems linking organizations with their customers in New Zealand. The cases included: two from the banking industry (cash management systems enabling customers to access accounts and carry out bank transactions); one clothing manufacturer (an order entry system); one credit reporting company (a system enabling customers to carry out independent credit reporting searches); one office products wholesaler (an order entry system); two pharmaceutical wholesalers (order entry and stock control systems for pharmacists); and two transport companies (enabling preparation of consignment notes, transmission of freight information and freight tracking).

The criteria for selection of individual cases were similar to those used by Runge (1985) and Reich and Benbasat (1990) for their empirical studies. Thus the firms had to be profit-oriented and had to sell a product or service. The systems had to be fully implemented systems with facilities which enabled the sponsoring firm to enhance their core business. Adoption and use of the systems by customers had to be voluntary. Each of the case-firms allowed customers the choice of using the COS or obtaining products or services in an alternative manner (using telephone or fax or relying on a salesperson to call). Customers were not coerced into COS use, something which was verified by random telephone checks with customers of the various systems.

The specific case methodology followed was the structured methodology advocated by Yin (1989). Data were collected through lengthy personal interviews with at least two members of each organization; documentation in the form of annual reports, promotional literature and newspaper clippings was also collected. The interview and documentary data were analysed using techniques described by Miles and Huberman (1984).

After data collection, the cases were categorized according to customer participation: some organizations did and others did not involve customers during development. Since customer participation is defined as activities performed by customers during system development, various customer participation activities were identified. Although authors disagree on the number and names of stages and activities in the development process, the IS community agrees on the general process that takes place: a project moves progressively from the initial project definition stage, through analysis of requirements and design of the system to programming, testing and installation.

## Results - testing of contingency variables

This section describes the case findings concerning the independent variables which may be expected to influence participation. The ten previously identified customer participation contingencies were investigated to see if these variables correlated with the dependent variable customer participation.

The user participation literature suggests a number of underlying factors which might contribute to involvement or non-involvement of users in the system development process. This study set out to test the appropriateness of these factors for predicting participation of potential users in a different context: the development of systems linking organizations with customers. Data about each of the previously identified, potentially contributing factors were obtained for all cases. It is thus possible to list the factors, to place a value on each factor for every case and thus to ascertain whether these factors are valid predictors of customer participation.

Aggregate data for each factor are presented but for the majority of factors such data are not very illuminating. Therefore finer-ground analysis of the various factors is also presented. The extent to which individual factors support the research model is evaluated on the basis of the case data. If distinct values of factors do not separate the organizations according to the dependent variable, then it can be argued that the factor is not important as a contributing factor to customer participation in COS design.

Organizational factors, project-related factors and customer-related factors are now discussed in turn.

## Organizational factors

Table 3 summarizes the case findings concerning organizational factors which may encourage or discourage customer participation in the development of COS. Potential organizational factors impacting on customer participation include the extent to which the system is developed under time and financial constraints, and commitment and support from top management.

This aggregate presentation of findings shows unexpected results. The systems developed with a time constraint appear to involve customers during development which is contrary to expected findings. None of the systems were developed under financial constraints and all but one of the systems enjoyed normal levels of top management commitment. This suggests that neither financial constraint nor the top management commitment variable are factors distinguishing between systems developed with or without customer participation.

## Time constraint

The user participation literature suggests that systems which need to be developed in a limited time frame tend to be developed without user input, while user participation is more likely for those systems where there is no development time constraint.

The organizations which involved customers during development were also the ones which were under external pressure to complete the COS. Conversely, the organizations which did not involve customers tended to have no pressure to complete. This finding is contrary to the established research findings on user participation in the development of internal systems.

One organization was under pressure from customers to complete EDI development; one of its customers has a policy that it will not trade with major suppliers unless they are EDI capable. Another organization needed to replace an outdated product in order to compete in the market and was pressurized by some keen customers to complete development as soon as possible. In each of these cases the organizations had customers literally waiting for the launch of their systems.

Two further cases were under both external and substantial internal pressure to complete system development. One of these cases, a bank, had to develop a cash management system in order to be able to compete seriously in the corporate and retail market since other banks already offered such systems. Internal pressure was put on the development team so that the bank would be the first in New Zealand to offer a cash management system with a Windows version. The second case developed EDI capability as a strategic tool in the highly competitive transport industry. In order to be able to gain a competitive edge with the system top management felt the company had to be a clear first-mover.

It appears that time pressure itself is not associated with customer participation, but that the source of that time pressure is correlated with customer participation. External pressure to develop a system puts a time-constraint on development and, at the same time, is correlated with participation. External pressure may make an organization anxious to develop a good product and may thus encourage it to involve customers during development in spite of pressure on time.

It is interesting to note that three of the four organizations which were under external pressure to complete system development launched their COS prematurely.

In each of the three cases, pressure to bring their products on the market was such that the COS were launched even though the development teams were not totally satisfied with their systems' quality.

## Availability of financial resources

The user participation literature suggests that a constraint on financial resources inhibits participation during system development. When the system's budget is not of paramount importance, systems are more likely to be developed with user input during development.

In all cases financial resources for development of the COS were potentially available within the organization. In eight out of the nine cases the project was not cost justified. In four of those eight organizations such projects were never cost justified either because there was plenty of money around at the time or because of the entrepreneurial nature of the organization. In the other four cases the project was not cost justified because the system was considered to be of strategic importance; the project was to be completed with no specific regard to cost.

In only one case had development of the system to be cost justified. Unfortunately, it was not possible to cost justify the project. In spite of this, top management approved the project – on strategic grounds rather than on the basis of cost savings.

The sponsoring organization of each system considered the development costs low and/or unimportant. The finding that all of the cases studied in effect disregarded cost as a significant factor in developing systems may indicate that this is a factor that is present for any COS development to take place at all. Such a finding corresponds to earlier research findings concerning inter-organizational and strategic systems (King et al., 1989; Reich and Benbasat, 1990).

Willingness on behalf of the organizations to make all necessary financial resources available would facilitate customer participation. In spite of this facilitating factor only two organizations did involve customers during design of their systems (the two organizations with a high participation rating).

The case evidence does not support the financial resources factor as a contingency variable which distinguishes between systems that are developed with customer participation during design and those that are not.

## Management commitment

Management commitment is important in order to provide the funding, resources and clear support for IS which then enables development of systems. User participation literature has shown that user participation in IS development projects is more likely in organizations where such commitment from top management is high. When top management are less committed, user participation is less likely. Management participation is not necessarily required for management commitment, but is a strong indicator of management commitment.

In all cases studied for this research, top management was aware of, and gave the go-ahead for, development of the system. As was explained earlier, in all cases management made available the financial resources to enable development. Top management commitment beyond the early stages of the projects varies among the cases. Informants of the cases where management did not get involved beyond the early stages of the project all mentioned that top management was interested in having the project carried out and completed, but otherwise concentrated on the core business. Those organizations were also the larger, more established organizations, while the organizations where management remained involved all the way through were the more entrepreneurial companies.

The case evidence indicates that the organizations with the highest management commitment and involvement tended not to involve customers during development of the system; customers did participate in development of systems where top management commitment was less obvious. This finding is contrary to the suggested and expected relationship between top management commitment and user participation.

In the cases where top management fully participated in the development project, managerial enthusiasm for and commitment to the project seemed unlimited. In all three cases the managers were convinced of the worth and value of the project. Such enthusiasm and conviction that the system they were developing was right precluded rather than encouraged customer development. Fine-grained analysis of the commitment variable has presented an interesting finding: top management hands-on participation appears to correlate negatively with customer participation.

In only one case was top management commitment for the COS project considered to be greater than commitment to other projects in the sponsoring organization. Management was making a substantial investment in a strategic system, the benefits of which were mostly intangible and as such hard to quantify at the outset.

Making a substantial strategic investment was unusual for the parent company and hence their wish for weekly meetings to be kept informed of progress was understandable.

In eight out of the nine cases the extent of top management commitment was normal. This suggests that top management did not consider the development of the COS to be particularly special. This case evidence therefore suggests that top management commitment is not a contingency particularly associated with customer participation in COS development.

## Project-related factors

Table 4 presents a summary of the case findings concerning project-related factors which impact on customer participation during the development of COS.

The combined findings for project-related factors show mixed results. Moderate and high structuredness of task correlates with lower levels of participation. There appears to be some support for low complexity of task as a factor associated with customer participation. Confidentiality seems not to have been an issue for any of the case organizations. Aggregate results of the other two factors are inconclusive or contrary to expected results. Each of the project-related factors is now discussed in turn.

## Structuredness of task

Projects are considered structured if requirements are well-known or are standard. Transaction processing systems address relatively structured tasks and are not likely to be developed with user input; relatively unstructured tasks, like the development of decision-making systems, benefit from user participation.

In all COS cases the project objectives and their high-level requirements were clear-cut. Structuredness of specific project tasks varied according to functionality of each system. Using Johnston and Vitale's (1988) categorization of inter-organizational systems according to system function, it is possible to assign a structuredness rating to the various systems. Systems projects which merely automate boundary transactions are more structured than systems which move away from the sponsoring organization's main business.

Eight of the nine cases concerned the development of systems automating relatively structured tasks (having a high or moderate structuredness rating). The finding that the vast majority of the COS were relatively structured is consistent with previous findings in the inter-organizational literature (Johnston and Carrico, 1987; Copeland and McKenney, 1988; Reich and Benbasat, 1990).

When examining the expected pattern of participation according to structuredness of task, it is clear that this case evidence does not conform to the expected pattern. Case findings therefore show no support for a potential correlation between structuredness of task and customer participation during COS design.

## Complexity of system

Complexity of the project is increased by the task being technically complex, by increasing the size of the project and by the project crossing functional boundaries. Greater complexity is associated with user participation in development.

Operationalization of the complexity construct was based on the definitions of complexity by several previous studies: McFarlan (1981) associates complexity with size, newness of the system and experience of the development team; Beath (1987) suggests that cross-functional projects increase project complexity; Hirschheim (1985) reports on size as an indicator of complexity. Previous research has not operationalized the construct.

Contrary to expectation, the most complex of the nine cases did not involve customers in system design at all. The reason for involving users during the development of complex systems is to obtain their help in defining requirements (Markus, 1983). The reason given by informants for not involving customers is also related to complexity of the system. Because the system was new and complex, it was felt that customers would not be able to contribute to the development process in any meaningful way. This one case clearly shows that, contrary to recommendations in the user participation literature, complexity of the project can have an inhibiting effect on participation.

The other cases which did not involve customers tended to be the systems of lower complexity. Case evidence therefore provides some support for the proposition that lower project complexity correlates with the absence of participation of intended customers. However, there is no evidence that higher complexity correlates with increased participation.

## Initiator of project

A system project can be initiated by the users or by non-users. Projects initiated by non-users benefit from user participation as it is prudent to ensure that user requirements are met to enhance acceptability. Conversely, systems which are initiated by users do not require participation.

In only one case did the idea for the system originate from intended users. This organization was approached by two separate customers to become EDI trading partners. Each of those customers has an EDI strategy which aims to link them with all major suppliers; one of the two now demands that major suppliers are EDI capable if those suppliers want to be considered as serious trading partners. Each of these two customers approached the sponsoring organization with plans for electronic transmission of purchase orders and, in one case, invoices.

In all eight other cases the systems were initiated by top management of the sponsoring organization. In spite of these systems being initiated by non-users (the sponsoring organization) instead of the intended users (the customer organizations), only one was developed with use of customers during the design stages of the project. This one organization involved customers to elicit requirements for the finished system; such participation was considered essential to the project. This organization's reasons for customer participation in design are exactly those put forward by the user participation literature for involving users during development (Markus, 1983).

Since seven out of the eight systems initiated by the sponsoring organization did not involve customers in design, this case evidence does not support the initiator of project variable as a factor which distinguishes between systems developed with and without customer participation. Only one out of the eight who were expected to involve customers actually did use customer input during design.

## Expected change

When the system is expected to bring about significant change for the user it is desirable to seek user input during development. The users can then help ascertain the type and the extent of impact which the system will have. This information can be used by the system developers during design of the system and, later, at the implementation stage by modifying design and by adequately preparing users for change. Systems which are not expected to affect user operations in a significant way do not require the same kind of user participation.

Several organizations did not even consider the potential impact of the system on the customer's business. Of those who did consider potential impact, most foresaw that the customers' businesses would change significantly with the system.

The case evidence shows no correlation between expected change and participation and therefore does not support expected change as a factor distinguishing between participation and non-participation cases.

## Confidentiality required

A need to keep the development of a system confidential is expected to discourage participation in development. If there is no need for strict confidentiality, participation is more likely.

None of the informants mentioned the need for confidentiality. When prompted about the possible need to keep the system confidential during the development stages, informants' reactions showed that confidentiality had not even been considered. Usually competitors were aware that the sponsoring organization was developing a system, but did not know any details of the development project.

The sponsoring organizations were quite happy for the market to know that they were developing a system. Such knowledge among customers and would-be customers prepared the market for the launch of the product.

Since confidentiality had not been considered by any of the sponsoring organizations, it was not possible to test the Reich and Huff (1991) suggestion that the need for confidentiality may play a role in inhibiting customer participation in the development of COS.

## Customer-related factors

The two final factors which potentially impact on user participation are related to the potential users: customers' willingness and ability to participate in COS development.

It is hard to assign values to customers' willingness and ability to participate in the development of the systems. Asking customers retrospectively whether they would have been willing and able to participate in development is not likely to give reliable data. Similarly, asking sponsoring organizations about customers' willingness and ability to participate is likely to provide biased data: system development teams usually underestimate the users' abilities and, secondly, organizations may wish to justify their involvement or non-involvement of customers by now presenting biased views.

Customers and sponsoring organizations were not asked directly about customer-related factors. Instead, interview data about industrial conditions and organization at the time of system initiation and development were used to assign values to the two customer-related factors. A summary of the data obtained is presented in Table 5.

These aggregate data suggest that there is little evidence to indicate that the factors (customer willingness and ability to participate) are correlated with customer participation. Each of these two factors is now discussed in turn.

## Willingness to participate

If a potential system is considered important by the user, the user is likely to be willing to participate in the system (Anderson, 1985).

Interview data for the nine cases were searched for evidence to indicate whether customers regarded the proposed systems as important or not. Four of the nine cases could be expected to encounter little customer enthusiasm to be involved in system development. The reasons for this lack of interest in COS are two-fold: the function to be supported by COS was considered as a low priority by the customers and, in one case, customers were averse to any change or use of technology.

## Ability to participate

Users' ability to participate in development is dependent on their understanding of the system to be developed (Anderson, 1985), the geographical distance between the user and the system development team (Beath, 1987) and the extent to which their job takes priority over system development (Beath, 1987).

In COS the intended users are customers who, by definition, are outside the organization. Because customers are not members of the sponsoring organization there is a definite geographical distance between customers and the team developing each COS. Secondly, because customers are outside the sponsoring organization their commitment lies with their own organizations rather than that which is developing the system. This means that individual jobs in the customer organizations take priority over any involvement with COS development tasks. In both these respects all cases are similar in terms of customer ability to participate.

Customers in the five no participation cases were considered to have either low or moderate understanding of the system being developed. In only one case had the customer obvious technical understanding of the system to be developed – in the case where the customers asked the company to become EDI partners, the customers were familiar with the application and experienced with the technology. Their customers could be expected to be able to participate in COS development and, in fact, did participate during design of their EDI system.

This case evidence provides no clear support for a correlation between customer ability to participate and actual customer participation during development of a COS.

## Summarizing the testing of the contingency model

The previous section tested propositions in order to determine if certain factors known to impact on user participation in the development of internal systems are also valid in the development of inter-organizational systems. In order to do so the authors started off with factors in mind and then searched for specific data on those factors for each case.

A number of factors have previously been shown to correlate with user participation in the development of organizations' internal systems. However, on testing these factors in the context of external systems, little support was found. This was an exploratory study which examined the context of nine inter-organizational systems. A sample of nine cases can hardly be called representative of inter-organizational systems in general. However, on the basis of this case evidence preliminary conclusions can be drawn; it is possible to generalize the results to theory. Table 6 summarizes the conclusions, that, based on this case evidence, must be drawn concerning the support for the various potential contingency variables impacting on customer participation.

None of the factors potentially affecting customer participation were strongly supported by case evidence. Some support was found for one of the ten factors (complexity of task). The results for two of the factors were exactly opposite to expected findings (time constraint, initiator of system). The remaining seven factors presented inconclusive findings.

One could argue that the lack of positive findings may be due to methodological or other limitations of this study. The specific research method (multiple case research) and the small sample size may have made it difficult to obtain conclusive results. Also, the specific environment from which the cases were drawn (New Zealand) may have introduced a regional bias which might account for disparate results.

However, there is also a further possible explanation for the lack of consistency between the findings of this study and the findings of previous ones: in this study the contingencies were being investigated in a new context. Previous researchers (Ives and Olson, 1984; Doll and Torkzadeh, 1989; King and Lee, 1991) have suggested that different types of systems create different contexts for user participation and that different contexts may present different contingencies for participation. This study investigated participation of potential users who were outside the organization. The inter-organizational context may be so different from internal system development conditions that traditional user participation contingencies may not be appropriate for explaining customer participation in the development of COS.

## Customer participation – pattern matching

Accepting that there is a real possibility of the previously identified factors not being applicable in the context of systems linking organizations with customers, it is prudent to investigate the data further. The analysis method of testing for previously identified factors did not provide conclusive findings. Therefore an additional, different data analysis approach was adopted in order to try and find out what factors encourage or discourage customer participation in the development of customer-oriented systems.

Rather than starting off with the factors and searching for evidence among the data, data analysis in this section starts off with the data, looking for patterns, and then generates a hypothesis concerning the factors encouraging or discouraging customer participation in COS development. Using the principles of grounded theory (Glaser and Strauss, 1967), factors were identified which distinguish between cases which did and those which did not involve customers during development. With this in mind the data were examined for a second time and in a different way: the interview transcripts were searched for reasons, volunteered by informants, as to why or why not customers had participated in development.

The analysis method used was one of pattern-matching. The cases were divided into two groups: those with high and moderate participation and those without customer participation. Analysing the high and moderate participation cases revealed a common thread among these cases; there were identical conditions present in each case. When comparing with the group of low participation cases, it was found that this second group displayed a different pattern which was opposite to the moderate and high cases. Using analytical logic (finding literal replication among each group and theoretical replication between groups) it was possible to find a causal pattern which explains why some organizations did and others did not involve customers during development.

Having briefly explained the method used for the analysis presented in this section, details of the findings are now presented.

## The group of cases developed with customer participation

An in-depth look at the comments made by informants of the four organizations which had high and moderate participation revealed four separate reasons for seeking customer participation. Examining these reasons one common thread could be found. First, the four separate reasons and then the common pattern will be described.

There were two cases with high and two cases with moderate participation in system development. In each of these cases the development team of the sponsoring organization felt it was necessary to seek customer involvement either in defining and justifying the project definition and/or for obtaining help in defining system requirements.

## Need to adopt customers' system design

In one case customers approached the company with the idea for the system and with the specific technology to be used. The sponsoring organization thus had to adopt a system which was already basically designed by the customer.

## Need to develop system with superior functionality

One case organization decided to develop their COS because they wanted to keep up with their major competitor. In order to ensure that the system would meet market needs, the sponsoring organization sought customer input when determining specific requirements for the system.

## Need to change top management view of system

One bank had taken the decision to develop a cash management system for its customers in order to match the competition: banks have to be able to offer an electronic business package in order to be taken seriously when tendering for corporate accounts. In order for the product to be competitive it was imperative that the new COS was the right product. Therefore the project was defined with the help of and according to customer input.

## Need to make system fit into customers' operations

One EDI project was undertaken in order to gain a competitive edge over the competition. The intention was to make the system as attractive as possible to customers by making the system fit easily into clients' operations. It was therefore essential before starting design and building of the system to find out about general customer requirements and the computer equipment used on customers' premises. In this way customers influenced the general direction of the COS development project.

## Common pattern

The development teams at each of these four organizations were under pressure to develop the right system. Each of the sponsoring organizations had external reasons (relating to competitive pressure and/or customer demands) for developing their COS. This is a factor common to all systems which used customer participation in the development of their COS.

In order to confirm this as a distinguishing factor, the reasons for development amongst this group of cases must be compared to those of the second group of cases. If the pattern of the first group is significantly different from the pattern relating to the second group, then confidence in the finding is enhanced (Yin, 1989). Therefore, if the reasons for system development among the cases which did not involve customers are different from those of the first group, then a strong conclusion can be drawn concerning the contingency of customer participation in COS development.

## The cases developed without customer participation

There were five cases where customers did not participate in development. The cases in this group mentioned two main reasons for developing their systems: to improve internal efficiency and/or to enhance customer service. None of the cases reflect urgency, particular importance or need for the development of the system.

## Improve internal efficiency

One organization (a pharmaceutical wholesaler) introduced electronic transmission of orders to pharmacies in order to increase their own efficiency and, hence, profitability. At the time the New Zealand pharmaceutical wholesaling industry took all orders from pharmacies over the telephone. No market research was done to investigate if the market would accept electronic transmission of orders. The managing director had seen similar systems work in the US and decided that electronic ordering by pharmacists would enhance operations of the sponsoring organization.

## Enhance service

Four organizations all mentioned they built their systems in order to enhance service to their customers.

## Common pattern

The systems developed by this group of organizations are all internally initiated. None of the cases expressed a competitive reason for developing their system. Although enhancing customer service is likely to have a competitive effect, none of the informants mentioned a desire for strategic impact as a reason for developing their COS. If mentioned at all, a competitive effect was seen as a potential fortunate outcome of or corollary to offering the service to customers; it was not given as a reason for developing the service. This group of cases lacked external pressure, a push or a real need to develop a system. Instead, the systems were developed because it seemed an interesting and innovative thing to do.

## Comparing the two groups of cases

On comparing the group of cases which did involve customers with the group of cases which did not seek customer participation during development, two distinct differences between the groups can be reported. There are clear differences in the reasons for COS development and in the attitude of the sponsoring organizations towards customer participation.

## Reasons for COS development

Development of systems among the cases which did involve customers was clearly externally motivated. These systems were developed as a result of customers initiating development or because competitive pressure pushed the sponsoring organizations to develop the COS.

In contrast, development of systems which did not involve customers was internally motivated. Although the completed systems were inter-organizational and linked with customers, the reasons for developing the systems were internal: systems were not only internally initiated but were also developed for internal reasons (Bakos and Treacy, 1986). The sponsoring organizations found out about a new technology and its application and then wanted to use the technology to enhance internal operations or to offer additional services to customers. Among this group of cases there is evidence of enthusiasm for technology and its application, but no evidence of a competitive motive (Bakos and Treacy, 1986) for system development.

The $2 \times 2$ matrix in Figure 1 shows how the two groups of cases fall in two opposite quadrants when categorized according to their reasons for COS development, indicating whether this development was internally or externally motivated.

This case evidence suggests that the absence of external pressure and absence of a real need to develop a system is correlated with a lack of customer participation. Conversely, when there is strong pressure from outside the firm almost forcing the organization to develop a COS, moderate or high customer participation takes place. External pressure corresponds to a pressing need to develop a ‘good’ and ‘right’ system. Sponsoring organizations that experience such pressure may therefore be more likely to involve customers during system development.

## Attitude towards customer participation

The organizations which did not involve customers during COS development advanced several reasons for not seeking customer participation. Those reasons concern the perceived lack of ability of customers to contribute to the COS development process. The group of organizations which did involve customers were aware of the difficulties associated with customer participation.

The difference between the two groups of cases is not related to the ability of various customer groups. Instead, the major difference between the two groups lies in the attitudes of the sponsoring organizations towards these difficulties. The non-participation group considered the difficulties sufficient reason not even to attempt to involve customers. In contrast, the group which did seek participation was wanting input even from those customers who might appear unable to participate.

Attitude towards participation is correlated with customer participation and with a strong external pressure to develop the COS. It is difficult to determine whether the favourable attitude (which enabled participation) was created as a result of external pressure, and hence was a result of the need to develop a good COS system, or whether a favourable attitude towards participation existed in the sponsoring organizations anyway. Similarly, it is hard to determine whether an unfavourable attitude towards participation is retrospectively used as a justification for not having involved customers during COS development or whether such unfavourable attitudes prevailed in the non-participation organizations anyway.

## Conclusions

This study investigated the participation of intended users in the development of inter-organizational systems where users are by definition outside the sponsoring organization. To date the user participation literature has not addressed participation in the context of inter-organizational systems. This study therefore extends the literature beyond discussion for user participation in the context of internal systems and as such adds a new dimension to the user participation debate.

The results of this exploratory study suggest that contingencies for participation in the development of inter-organizational systems are different from those in internal system development. Case evidence showed that participation of users outside the organization (in this case, customers) was not automatic. Organizations do not explicitly consider the involvement of customers, though some organizations do use customer input during development while others do not. Customer participation appears to be linked to the extent to which the COS is developed for strongly externally-oriented, strategic purposes. According to the study findings, the inter-organizational context has a stronger impact on the participation decision than traditional user participation contingencies.

User participation has traditionally been linked with the success of a system (see review by Ives and Olson, 1984). However, extending the user participation debate to participation in the development of inter-organizational systems means that the participation–success link has to be seen in a new light. Since the potential users are outside the organization, users cannot be coerced into adopting and using the system. Although there are examples of COS where customers have been forced or coerced into adoption, customers are usually offered the option to adopt or not. Success of the COS then depends on its adoption which is not solely or directly influenced by participation.

Customer participation during the early stages of COS development enhances the potential of the system to reflect accurately customer requirements. When the COS fulfils customer needs and fits into the customer's business operations easily, then COS is more likely to be adopted. Customer participation does not necessarily guarantee fast adoption: half of the customer participation cases in this study enjoyed fast adoption while the other half of the systems developed with customer input was adopted slowly. This indicates that customer participation is only one of a number of factors influencing the adoption of COS by customers (Runge, 1985; Reich and Benbasat, 1990).

Further research could test the findings of this study by investigating another set of cases or by using quantitative research methods. Further research could also investigate whether the findings of this study extend to inter-organizational systems at large or are idiosyncratic to systems linking organizations with customers.
