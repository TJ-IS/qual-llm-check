# Learning from abandoned information systems development projects

K. EWUSI-MENSAH and Z.H. PRZASNYSKI
Loyola Marymount University, Los Angeles, USA

Survey research is used to determine what kind of post-mortem appraisals companies undertake concerning their abandoned IS development projects. The data suggest that most organizations do not keep records of their failed projects and do not make any formal effort to understand what went wrong or attempt to learn from their failed projects. A learning paradigm is suggested which companies could use as a guide to undertake post-mortem analysis of their abandoned IS development projects.

## Introduction

Individuals as well as organizations learn by examining their past mistakes, errors or failures and by taking the necessary corrective actions to forestall future recurrence of those activities under the same or similar conditions or prevent the occurrence of the same or similar failed outcomes in the future. In the area of IS development, failure tends to plague the efforts of a number of companies. Such failures result in substantial loss of capital, resources and at times may even have a negative effect on some of those associated with the project(s) (see for example, Brooks, 1975; Comptroller General, 1979; McFarlan, 1981; Gladden, 1982; Glaser, 1984; Boddie, 1987). What companies do in handling such failures and how such failures should be treated in the future are the focus of this article.

The problem of IS development failure is generally recognized as a major issue in the literature (see McFarlan, 1981; Glaser, 1984; Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987; Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski, 1991). Glaser (1984, p. 45), for example, reports that 'in the computer industry . . . [project] objectives are missed and schedule and cost targets overrun with distressing regularity and, at times with equally distressing results' Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) confirm that IS 'failure' continues in organizations despite the enormous progress and strides made in the development, implementation and usage of IS. Similarly, Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski (1991) argue that abandoned IS development projects should be subsumed under the general umbrella of IS 'failure'. Projects are invariably abandoned because they failed to meet some expected standard.

The abandonment of IS development project occurs whenever management decides, for whatever reasons, to discontinue temporarily or retire permanently a project under development. In this paper, we concentrate on what organizations learn, if anything from an analysis of the abandoned project(s). Boddie (1987) and Abdel-Hamid and Madnick (1990) have both suggested and recommended the need for companies to undertake some analysis of failed or abandoned projects routinely in order to extract the important lessons of the failure 'from deep within the project experience'. Boddie (1987, p. 77), for instance, argues that 'failed projects need more than explanation or rationalization if they are to teach any lessons. Projects that fail need an organized effort to uncover what really caused the failure. What these projects need is a post-mortem.' He cites experiences from the field of engineering where 'obviation of failure' is considered a foremost objective in any engineering design. Boddie concludes that 'failures need to be seen as opportunities to learn something rather than as embarrassing moments to be quickly forgotten' (emphasis added).

In this study, we attempt to shed light on what sort of post-mortem appraisals organizations routinely undertake on their failed or abandoned projects, why they undertake such examination, what they understand and learn about the processes leading to the project failure or abandonment and, finally, what companies do with the records of such failure and whether or not such records are even kept. We conclude with a learning paradigm which companies could use as a guide to undertake post-mortem analysis of their failed or abandoned projects.

## Research design

A survey questionnaire was used to investigate the general issue of abandoned IS development projects. Organizations reporting abandonment were then asked to report in a separate section of the questionnaire on their efforts to identify and learn from the failed projects, that is, on postmortems performed on the failed or abandoned projects.

## Reasons for the abandoned IS projects

In our earlier work, we discussed some of the underlying reasons for the abandonment of IS development projects as multidimensional with several interacting components. In Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski (1991, 1994) we provided detailed discussions of the factors contributing to abandoned IS development projects in organizations. A summary of the main conclusions from the earlier work are as follows.

(1) Economic factors, that is, project cost and time schedule for completion were not significant contributors to the abandonment of IS development projects.

(2) Technological factors were also found not to contribute substantially to IS project abandonment decisions.

(3) Organizational factors, for example, organizational behavioural and political issues as well as end-user-related issues all might contribute to project abandonment if corporate and project management fail to deal satisfactorily with the varied problems stemming from these aspects of IS project development.

## Post-mortems of abandoned IS projects

The central issues raised in this article are whether organizations keep records of abandoned IS projects and what, if anything, do they do with those records. In other words, what do organizations learn from the records of those failed or abandoned projects? The questionnaire attempted to address these issues through a series of statements for which the respondents were asked to shed light on their organizational practices with respect to the abandoned IS projects. ## Discussion of results

What do organizations collectively learn about the causes, circumstances and reasons for projects failure? How is such information and knowledge captured and communicated within the organization? In the preceding paragraph we have presented data based on the perception of a number of top computer executives and systems managers groups in Fortune 500 companies. A review of the data suggests that though most respondents indicated that post-mortems are undertaken within their organizations to presumably understand what went wrong and why (see Table 8) a disturbing half of all the TCE respondents indicated that the circumstances of the abandoned projects are ‘never’ documented (see Table 9). It is, therefore, not surprising that more than 60% of the same respondents indicated that more than one project has been abandoned for more or less the same reasons (see Tables 10 and 11). Perhaps, even more disturbing is the fact that more than 70% of the TCEs indicated their organizations do not keep records of abandoned projects (see Table 10).

The picture that emerges from the data is one of organizations which are ‘cursed to repeat past mistakes’ because no effort is made to understand what went wrong nor are attempts made to learn from the past mistakes. The failure of organizations to document their projects failures and use that information to avoid a repetition of similar problems, perhaps, more than any single fact attests to the continuing problem of IS development projects abandonment in organizations. As Hedberg (1981) points out ‘. . . organizations increase their understanding of reality [e.g. IS development projects] by observing the results of their acts’ which are often experimental. When such organizational learning is lacking then the consequences are that there will be no viable formal mechanisms for ‘detection and correction of errors’ nor will there be attempts at ‘repeated testing, construction and reconstruction of knowledge’ within those organizations (Hedberg, 1981). It is, therefore, not surprising as Lyytinen (1987) claims that ‘IS development is fraught with recurrent problems caused by poor, undisciplined and incomplete development practices'.

In short, organizations which operate under such circumstances are, indeed, more likely to repeat past mistakes especially as members come and go and leadership changes take place. There will be no organizational memories to warn against those behaviours, procedures and operating practices and standards which may be at the root of those project failures. Thus, the likelihood of repeating those same problems looms large especially if new personnel are assigned to new projects. The data clearly support Boddie's (1987) assertion that 'We talk about software engineering but reject one of the most basic engineering practices: identifying and learning from our mistakes.' It is thus not surprising that 'errors made while building one system appear in the next one'. Failure to keep records of the abandoned projects robs the organization and its future leadership from learning about the mistakes of their past acts and, thus, creates organizational memory loss for the long-term. It is apparent that in most of these organizations the management treat those project failures as 'embarrassing moments to be quickly forgotten' and not as opportunities for a real concerted effort to uncover what caused the failure nor as opportunities to acquire new knowledge and discard obsolete and misleading knowledge or practices (Hedberg, 1981; Abdel-Hamid and Madnick, 1990).

For those respondents who indicated that records of abandoned projects are kept by their organizations, the picture that emerges is neither encouraging nor hopeful for the future of IS project development practices. For example, even though Table 13 indicates that quite a wide range of individuals have access to the abandoned project records only the system developers and IS management generally consult those records. New project leaders (only 30%), senior management, management in other departments and programmers (10% each) do not make as much use of the records as may be expected (based on TCEs responses). Thus, although the records are kept in some instances for as much as 6 years or more not much usage or value is obtained from them as would be expected.

As pointed out earlier, the data also indicate that organizations were experiencing project abandonment for more or less the same reasons (see Tables 10 and 11). Therefore, it was prudent that some organizations in an effort to avoid these pitfalls in project development chose to evaluate the abandoned projects for a variety of reasons. We suspect the organizations undertook the evaluations primarily to ensure that the underlying factors or reasons for the abandonment of the project were clearly understood and communicated with project developers. We also believe other reasons why some organizations chose to evaluate their abandoned projects were to account fully for the expenditure of resources on the project and to learn from the variety of interacting factors. For example, factors such as well-defined project goals, project team composition, availability of adequate technical infrastructure, technical know-how and competence of the project team, project management and control, active end-users participation, senior management support and others all have the potential to affect the project's outcome negatively if they are not properly understood and dealt with in the project development process.

When the respondents were asked to indicate the benefits derived from the stored records, the majority of both TCE and systems managers groups cited the desire to minimize repeating past problems or errors and improve performance on future projects through better planning and management.

While these objectives are commendable, they are unlikely to be realized if a majority of the responding organizations do not even routinely keep those project records much less consult them on a regular basis. This singular ‘failure to learn from mistakes has been a major obstacle to improving software project management’ as Abdel-Hamid and Madnick (1990) charge. We are, therefore, doomed to ‘continue to produce too many project failures, marked by cost overruns, late deliveries, poor reliability and user dissatisfaction’ (Abdel-Hamid and Madnick, 1990).

In contrast, those respondents who indicated using the abandoned project records overwhelmingly presented the view that the records were beneficial in establishing management procedures in new projects as well as contributing to the success of new projects (see Tables 18 and 19). We are, therefore, led to surmise that the problem of software projects failure is not an intractable technical issue but rather one of failure on the part of both senior and IS management in instituting enforceable guidelines and procedures for undertaking post-mortems of failed or abandoned projects and making proper use of the lessons learned from those projects in subsequent projects.

## Learning paradigm

It is apparent that improvements in IS project developments can be made if organizations show a willingness to learn from past project failures. Consequently, a study of how and why a particular project failed will, in essence, be the best prescription against a repetition of the same or similar problems. Thus, organizations should allow the problems underlying the causes of the failed project to serve as a trigger for a deeper learning and understanding of the entire systems development process. Learning takes place when the results of the analysis of the failure, uncovered largely through post-mortems, lead to changes and modifications of organizational practices and procedures.

The type of learning referred to above is similar to that described by Lehman (1980) in discussing the laws of software evolution, with specific reference to program types 'P' or 'E', that is, programs which attempt to solve real world problems or programs which 'mechanize a human or societal activity', respectively. For the 'P programs' the solution's value and validity as compared to the real-world environment or context from which the problem emanated is the main concern. Whenever differences are detected in the course of the comparison, 'it causes the program, its documentation or both to be changed'. Thus, 'P-programs are very likely to undergo never ending change or to become steadily less and less effective and cost effective'. However, for the class of 'E programs' change is intrinsic to its very nature. Lehman (1980) describes the changes involved as 'once the program is completed and begins to be used, questions of correctness, and appropriateness and satisfaction arise . . . and inevitably lead to additional pressure for change'. In other words, as organizations begin to learn more about the strengths and weaknesses of the implemented systems, change becomes a necessary consequence of that knowledge and experience which leads to a better or improved system. The costs incurred in effecting the changes are generally accepted as essential to the organization in the long run because the changes help to prolong the useful life of the system.

In Figure 2, we present an organizational learning cycle appropriate to IS development projects. The figure is an adaptation of the organizational learning cycle of March and Olsen (1976) applied within the context of IS projects. There are essentially two parts to the paradigm.

(1) The first part deals with successfully completed and implemented IS projects

(2) The second part deals with abandoned IS development projects.

For completed and implemented projects, learning takes place in the form of maintenance and enhancements to the system (Swanson 1976; Blum 1987). In both instances, positive feedback is generated as a result of using the implemented system and learning about its deficiencies and shortcomings. Thus, through maintenance and enhancements organizations have come to accept as normal or standard practice post-mortem appraisals of even successfully implemented systems as the appropriate means for improving the performance and operation of such systems. In other words, organizations accept as normal the roles individuals and collective learning can play in ‘perfecting’ the IS development process. Lyytinen (1987, p. 15) puts it succinctly when he writes ‘A successful IS development process is more a matter of social learning. The information system is an incremental outgrowth of this learning, and it continues to evolve over time owing to new learning’ (emphasis added).

It is this type of evolutionary learning which takes place as successfully implemented systems are subjected to maintenance and enhancement changes as a consequence of users' increased learning and experiences with the capabilities of the implemented system.

The model presented in Figure 2 seeks to make such organizational learning standard practice for failed or abandoned IS development projects through formal post-mortems performed on those projects. The abandoned project(s) is analysed at the project team level for the causes or reasons underlying the failure. The results of such an analysis should be widely communicated to all individuals associated with the project including those who managed the project. In addition, documentation of the analysis of the failure must be stored and made readily accessible to future project team members for regular consultation. The consequence of the post-mortem should lead to the formulation of guidelines in the form of changes and modifications to some organizational practices and procedures in project development in order to avoid the recurrence of the problems and/or to improve the overall performance of future project teams on subsequent projects. Such new guidelines should also be widely communicated to project team members and other individuals with responsibilities for future projects. We believe that individual as well as organizational learning will be achieved under these circumstances when post-mortem analysis is routinely performed on each failed or abandoned IS development project attempted and the lessons learned are widely communicated to all concerned within the organization.

However, any discontinuity in any leg of the cycle will have the same consequence as the organization not undertaking any post-mortem appraisal of the abandoned project. It is, therefore, critical that each leg of the learning cycle be maintained if the learning process is to result in the necessary changes or modifications on how the organization or individuals should approach subsequent project development and, if the organization is to realize fully the benefits from the lessons of the post-mortems in subsequent projects.

## Conclusion

The need for more organizations to undertake formal post-mortems of their failed or abandoned projects is quite apparent from the evidence presented above. Though the data is somewhat limited, we feel the compelling facts of the problem suggest that organizations need to do more to learn from the errors of their past mistakes in systems development efforts. We side with Boddie (1987) that IS professionals need to pay close heed to the practices and experience of the more established engineering professions which have as their ‘foremost objective the obviation of failure’. The frequent practice of viewing failures ‘as embarrassing moments to be quickly forgotten’ and not as opportunities to learn from must be discarded (Abdel-Hamid and Madnick 1990).

Instead, organizations should, as a matter of policy, institute formal mechanisms for uncovering the underlying causes and reasons for failed or abandoned projects on a regular basis.

Nowadays maintenance and enhancement of successfully implemented systems are routinely undertaken because it is considered standard practice that any new system will have to undergo some ‘corrective, adaptive and perfective’ maintenance (Swanson, 1976) in the course of its life, if it is to continue to be useful or beneficial to the organization. By the same token, such formal practice of learning from the experiences of past failed projects, through post-mortems, will produce a wealth of ideas and information which can be viewed as returns on the organizational resources invested in those failed projects. We believe that if more organizations adopt the practices of the engineering professions in dealing with systems failures we will see a marked decline in the number of failed projects in IS. Even as each new project attempts to stretch the limits of the technology we may have a better sense of what is possible as opposed to what is infeasible in new systems development efforts. Still, we think more studies are rightly called for to enable us to understand better the whole spectrum of issues surrounding the failure of IS development projects which will help management to better understand how to plan and manage new projects to minimize the chances of failure and/or to improve organizational performance in the delivery of new systems.
