# An information system profile of the UK manufacturing sector

DAVID WASTELL and ANNECHRIS SEWARDS

Department of Computer Science, Manchester University, Oxford Road, Manchester M13 9PL, UK

A survey of the information systems (IS) sophistication of small to medium sized UK manufacturing firms is reported. The survey revealed that manufacturing firms exhibited an intermediate degree of IS sophistication. Typically they were situated in the ‘contagion’ and ‘control’ stages of Nolan’s stages of growth model, although a minority of firms were significantly more advanced. Organizational size was found to influence the degree of IS sophistication with larger firms manifesting a more highly developed information processing capacity. The influence of a number of factors on project success was investigated. Companies using their own formal methodologies for software development achieved higher than average rates of success. The degree of awareness of the strategic potential of IT was also positively correlated with success.

## Introduction

The term ‘information systems (IS) profile’ has been coined by Li and Rogers (1991) to designate ‘the specific characteristics of the IS organization and its functions’ (p. 20). The IS profile of a company is, in essence, an attempt to delineate the sophistication of its IS function in terms of a broad range of technical and organizational dimensions. As the first phase of a study of the use of information technology in the manufacturing sector, we have carried out a large-scale postal survey of IS sophistication in small and medium sized manufacturing enterprises in the North West of England. The aim of the second phase, now in progress, is to follow up key issues identified in the survey through a series of detailed case studies.

The results of the first phase are described and discussed in this paper. The study focused on the manufacturing sector as manufacturing is widely seen as an index of national economic virility. In many advanced economies, this sector has declined relative to other sectors over recent years. In the UK this decline has been the subject of much poignant comment by economic pundits and by party politicians. Evidence from US studies indicates that IT investment runs at half the rate for manufacturing industries compared to service industries and the public sector (Hodges, 1989). This suggests that a lower level of IS sophistication may be typical within this sector.

In their study, Li and Rogers (1991) developed a broad-ranging instrument for assessing a company's IS profile based on Nolan's 'stages of growth' framework (Nolan, 1979). Their questionnaire involved a wide range of questions relating to Nolan's benchmarks (IS expenditure, technological infrastructure, applications portfolio, IS organization, user awareness, IS planning and control). This nucleus was supplemented by an ad hoc suite of items covering organizational and policy issues (for example, the existence of a separate IT department, the existence of an IT plan), software development approaches (for example, the degree of end-user computing) and IT infrastructure (for example, the presence of networks and office automation).

In the present study, IS profile covered a similarly broad range of issues. The aspects of IS sophistication which were addressed included the degree of centralization of the IS function, the areas of the business supported by IT, the breadth of the ‘applications portfolio’, the configuration of the IT infrastructure and the degree of systems integration. Relationships between IS sophistication and features of ‘organizational context’ (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978) were also of interest, especially firm size. It has been argued that larger organizations, because they face more demanding problems of internal coordination, will require a more sophisticated information processing capacity. The weight of evidence favours such a correlation (Moch and Morse, 1977; Harris and Katz, 1991).

The systems development process was given special emphasis in the survey. Two intersecting issues were investigated: the use of methodologies and tools, and the degree to which companies involved users in systems development. Concerning methods and tools, the relative degree of internal versus external development and the use of software tools were examined. The survey also attempted to gauge the intensity of end-user computing (Rockart and Flannery, 1983). The prevalence of structured methodologies, both for project management and systems development, was also addressed as advocates of formal project management techniques have made strong claims for the value of their methodologies (Bentley, 1992) and similar claims have been made for software development methodologies (Dekleva, 1992), such as Information Engineering and SSADM (Avison and Fitzgerald, 1988). Save for a sprinkling of isolated studies, which have yielded conflicting results (Cerveny and Joseph, 1988; Dekleva, 1992), very little is known empirically about the efficacy of these methods, or indeed of the degree to which they are used.

The second issue relating to system development was user involvement. User participation was of interest as this factor is widely regarded as having a strong influence on project success; user involvement is generally held to engender better quality design decisions and to lead to more positive user attitudes towards new systems (Tait and Vessey, 1988; Kaye, 1990; Allingham and O'Connor, 1992; Amoako-Gyampah and White, 1993). Because of its broad range, the survey was not able to treat user involvement in any depth. However, a limited attempt was made to assess the degree of user involvement practised by companies and then relate this to a crude measure of success. On a similar theme, the survey also attempted to elicit the principal reasons why respondents felt information systems projects ran into difficulties and to enumerate the factors that they thought to be crucial to success.

## Method

Initially, a small number of companies in the Manchester locale were approached to obtain some general information regarding their IT policies and practices. A pilot questionnaire was drawn up which was pre-tested on these companies. The final questionnaire was then produced, taking into account the results of the pre-testing.

The target group for the study were small to medium sized manufacturing enterprises, although some larger companies were also included (1500 employees was set as the upper limit). A decision was made to focus on the Greater Manchester area as a local study would be of more interest to respondents and hence more likely to yield a good response. A local study would also have logistical advantages in the second phase. Regarding generalizability, there is no reason to suspect that the Manchester region is unrepresentative of the general situation in the UK.

The sample was drawn up by extracting company names from various business directories. A total of 207 companies were found and the name of the IT director or equivalent person was determined. The questionnaire was mailed to this named individual. In order to encourage companies to complete and return the questionnaires, the local nature of the study was emphasized in the covering letter. Given its size (it ran to six pages), care was taken to make the questionnaire as quick and easy to complete as possible and management jargon was rigorously avoided.

The survey took place in the summer of 1993. Of the 207 questionnaires sent out, a total of 92 usable responses were returned. This response rate of $44\%$ was very pleasing especially given the length of the questionnaire. This rate compares favourably with that found in other survey studies. Li and Roger's (1991) response rate, for instance, was only $13.3\%$ .

## The questionnaire

The questionnaire comprised a number of questions grouped in four main areas.

## Company attributes and IS organization

The first question asked respondents for basic background information regarding the company, namely company size (number of employees), company turnover, number of directors and levels of management, and product type (standard versus bespoke). Respondents were also asked to indicate the degree to which the IS/IT organization was centralized (software and hardware owned and managed by a central department) or decentralized (hardware, software and personnel located and managed within functional units). They were also asked to indicate if there was a separate IT department in the company or not.

## IT budgets, applications and infrastructure

The first of the questions in this category tapped how the IT budget was allocated across different generic areas (office systems, shopfloor systems, financial systems and management information systems). Respondents were also asked how much of the IT budget they considered to be ‘strategic’ (supporting the development of systems giving the company a competitive advantage) and the degree to which they considered the company regarded information systems as a crucial part of business strategy. Further questions under this general heading attempted to tease out the shape of the organization’s ‘applications profile’. One question asked respondents to tick off specific application areas in which they had IT systems (production planning, marketing, stock control, wages, sales, personnel and purchasing, and so on); a second asked about the use of specific tools (simulation tools,

CAD, expert systems, statistical modelling). A further question asked about the IT environment in the company (wide area networks, local area networks, mainframe versus minicomputer facilities). Respondents were also asked to indicate the degree to which different systems were currently inter-linked and the desired degree of future systems integration.

## Systems development, end-user computing and user involvement

This heading subsumed four questions. The first asked respondents what proportion of systems were developed in their company using: off-the-shelf packages; by external contractors; internally using 4GLs and/or CASE tools; internally using traditional programming languages. The second question asked respondents to indicate whether their dominant approach to software development involved either: the rigorous use of structured methods; the relaxed use of structured methods; the company's own formal method; no explicit methodology.

Regarding end-user computing (EUC), respondents were asked to indicate how much EUC currently occurred in the company (none, limited, equal to or more than central development) and how much they foresaw occurring in five years' time. The final question addressed the issue of user involvement. Respondents were asked how much users were involved (using a four-point scale: 'not at all' through to 'doing the bulk of the work') in a typical project at different life-cycle stages (requirements definition, systems analysis and design, design of screen and report layouts, and so on).

## Project motivation and success

A final set of questions asked respondents to indicate the degree to which software projects were motivated in their company by: long-term business strategy; short-term business goals; tactical efficiency savings; without any clear justification. They were also asked to give an overall assessment of how successful their company was at developing systems by indicating the proportion of projects that: produced measurable benefits; appeared to be beneficial although the gains could not be quantified; led to no obvious benefits.

A final question presented respondents with a list of potential reasons why projects fail to deliver hoped-for benefits (company politics, lack of user consultation, changing requirements, and so on) and asked them to check off those that they felt were the most significant. They were also asked about the measures that they felt could be taken to improve the prospects of success (more user involvement, closer alignment of IT and business strategy, and so on).

## Results

The results of the study are described below. Statistical significance was assessed using either chi-squared tests or Student's t-tests, as appropriate. The purpose of the statistical testing was to screen out spurious results.

## Company attributes and IS organization

Average company size was around 350 employees (range from 100 to 1500). In order to assess the impact of company size on other variables, the sample was divided into two groups: a ‘small firm’ subgroup (<250 employees) and a subgroup of larger firms (>250 employees). There were 41 companies in the first category and 51 in the second. As would be expected, turnover was significantly higher in the second group ( $\chi^{2}=39.3, p<0.001$ ) and there was also a tendency for more levels of management ( $\chi^{2}=4.3, p<0.05$ ) and more directors in larger firms ( $\chi^{2}=10.0, p<0.01$ ). See Table 1 for details.

In larger firms there was more emphasis on standard rather than bespoke products although this difference was not statistically significant ( $\chi^{2}=1.6$ ). Larger firms were also more likely to have a separate IT department ( $\chi^{2}=24.8, p<0.001$ ) and budget ( $\chi^{2}=10.2, p<0.01$ ), and to have a medium-term (3–5 years) IT plan ( $\chi^{2}=4.2, p<0.05$ ). When asked about the degree of centralization of IT resources, 86% of respondents in large firms indicated that their resources were over 75% centralized. This level was significantly higher ( $\chi^{2}=13.5, p<0.01$ ) than the corresponding figure for small firms (51%).

## IT budgets, applications and infrastructure

Table 2 shows the relative proportion of the IT budget given over to different generic areas (financial control, management information systems, and so on). Broadly speaking, the figures are comparable for large and small companies except in the area of shopfloor systems where small firms appear to allocate proportionately more resources, although this difference is not statistically significant. Table 2 also indicates that, although large and small firms estimated that they spent an equivalent amount on strategic systems, there is some evidence that larger firms judged IT as of greater strategic importance. However, this difference did not attain statistical significance (t = 0.78).

Considering specific applications of IT, almost all companies (98%) reported that IT was used for word processing and accounting. Over 90% of companies used IT in their sales order, wages and stock control processes and over 80% used IT for production planning and purchasing. IT was used for personnel in 70% of companies. Less popular applications were sales forecasting (56%), marketing (50%), clocking (37%) and strategic planning (17%). Of these applications, personnel, sales forecasting, marketing, clocking and strategic planning were more common in larger organizations; for other applications there was no effect of company size. Regarding specific tools, database management (78%) and CAD systems (61%) were the most commonplace;

tools for statistical analysis (29%) and simulation (11%) were relatively rare.

Regarding IT infrastructure, 75% of enterprises had minicomputers and 27% had a mainframe arrangement. Whereas mainframes were significantly more common in larger companies (37% vs 15%; $\chi^{2}=5.9$ , p<0.05), the prevalence of minicomputers was broadly the same. Local area networks were present in 64% of companies; the incidence was higher in larger companies (73% vs 54%; $\chi^{2}=3.8$ , p<0.05). Overall, wide area networks were less common than local networks (30%); their incidence was significantly higher in the larger companies (43% vs 14%; $\chi^{2}=8.7$ , p<0.01).

Finally in this section, companies were asked about current and desired levels of integration, on a scale from 0% (no linkage between systems) to 100% (total integration). The degree of integration of systems was found to be significantly lower (t = 4.1, p < 0.001) in smaller companies (39% on average) than larger companies (63%). All companies expressed a strong aspiration for more integration. The average degree of desired integration for smaller firms was 67%; the average for larger enterprises was 85%.

## Project motivation and project success

Regarding project motivation, companies were asked to indicate the importance of various factors on a five-point Likert scale running from 1 (not important) to 5 (very important). The tactical need to improve efficiency emerged as the most important factor (the average weighting was 3.7). Long-term business goals was the next most important factor (an average rating of 3.3) followed closely by short-term business goals (3.1). The idea that projects arise spontaneously, without clear justification, received little favour with an average rating of only 1.3. There were no effects of size here.

Regarding project outcomes, respondents indicated that over 52% of projects produced measurable benefits. A further 37% of projects were claimed to be beneficial although the gains could not be quantified. Overall, very few projects (13%) were rated as leading to no benefits at all. A surprising finding was that the larger companies claimed a higher success rate for their projects; 59% of large firms claimed that over 50% of their projects produced measurable benefits compared to the much lower figure of 34% for small firms ( $\chi^{2}=5.2, p<0.05$ ).

Five main factors were cited as reasons why some projects fail to deliver hoped-for benefits. ‘Users changing specifications’ was cited by 58% of companies as an important factor. Insufficient user training and consultation were cited by 48% and 43% of companies, respectively. Weak project management and company politics were also often cited (41% and 40% of companies, respectively). Interestingly, technical complexity and insufficient budget were mentioned by only 23% of companies.

When asked about measures that would improve the success rate for projects, the need for linking IT and business strategies was the most popular choice – it was cited by 65% of respondents. More training of users emerged as the next most important factor, being cited by 55%. Although more user involvement was also popular (44%), there was significantly less interest in user-led development (25%). Improved project management was deemed to be crucial by 42% of companies; however, very few respondents felt that a more formal software development approach would help (18%).

## Relationships between the software process and project success

Of interest is the pattern of relationships between software development approaches, user involvement and project success. Those companies employing their own formal methods for software development claimed that, on average, 65% of their IT projects produced measurable benefits to the company. This figure was significantly higher ( $\chi^{2}=4.2, p<0.05$ ) than the corresponding success rate for structured methods and ad hoc approaches (40% in both cases).

In order to examine the relationship between user involvement and project success, Kendall rank correlation tests were carried out. The data were examined for further relationships between IS success and potentially relevant factors, such as: the presence of an IT plan; the use of consultants; the level of EUC; or the degree of system integration. The bulk of these factors showed no relationship. However, a significant pattern of effects was found for a constellation of variables that variously reflected the degree to which IT was seen as a strategic resource. Significant correlations were observed between success rates (as above, the proportion of projects estimated to produce measurable benefits) and (a) the proportion of IT expenditure that was judged as strategic (correlation = 0.23), (b) the degree to which IT was seen as strategically vital to the business (0.24) and (c) the degree to which IT projects were judged to be motivated by long-term business goals (0.26).

A supplementary question on the questionnaire asked respondents about the composition of development teams. It was found that the most common working relationship was for the project team to include one or more members of the user department (49%), followed by the user department appointing one person to liaise with the IT team (38%). There was no evidence that these approaches led to a higher than average success rate. The third most common relationship (29%) was for the company to enforce joint user and IT staff accountability for the design and development of applications. Those companies following this approach appeared to achieve a significantly improved success rate; 67% claimed that over half of their projects produced measurable benefits, compared to the average figure of 48% ( $\chi^{2}=5.4, p<0.05$ ).

## Discussion

In this paper, we have described the results of a survey of IS sophistication in small to medium sized firms in the UK manufacturing sector. We are aware that such a survey has its limitations. Any factor study of information systems inevitably paints a static, cross-sectional view of what are complex social and technical processes (Newman and Robey, 1992; Wastell, 1993; Wastell and Newman, 1993). Moreover, the survey is broad ranging and many of the issues covered have perforce been explored at a surface level. We are well aware that issues such as user involvement have been investigated in a much more sophisticated way by other researchers (Kappelman and McLean, 1991; King and Lee, 1991). However, this breadth was a matter of deliberate strategy. Our aim was to take a wide-angle view of the IS landscape in order to gather a baseline dataset as a prelude to carrying out further in-depth case studies. Despite these limitations, the survey achieved an impressive response rate and yielded some findings which we believe to be of significant interest.

## The IS sophistication of UK manufacturing

The concept of IS sophistication is closely linked with the ‘stages of growth’ hypothesis of IS evolution, namely the idea that organizations pass through a series of ‘developmental stages’ in their deployment and management of information systems. Although several growth models have been elaborated (Earl, 1989; Galliers and Sutherland, 1991) the most widely known is Nolan’s (Nolan, 1979). Originally formulated as a four-stage model, Nolan’s model now comprises the following six ‘ontogenetic’ stages of IS growth: initiation, contagion, control, integration, data administration and maturity. Movement through these ‘rites of passage’ is argued to reflect a pattern of organizational learning in which the organization shifts from a reactive, technology-focused orientation to a mature, business-oriented philosophy.

Although intuitively appealing, the Nolan framework has been the centre of some controversy. In a much-quoted review, Benbasat et al. (1984) concluded that there was little empirical evidence for many of the key elements of the model. There is scant evidence, for example, that computer budgets grow in the S-shaped fashion that the model predicts. The absence of a strong pattern of correlations between the different benchmarks also casts doubt on the construct validity of Nolan's stages. Despite these criticisms, there is general agreement that growth models have heuristic and analytical value. Nolan's seminal model remains the most widely known. It formed the basis for Li and Rogers's (1991) study which was an important source of inspiration for the present work. Although our questionnaire was by no means rigorously derived from Nolan's model, many of the questions were loosely based on Nolan's benchmarks. The instrument can thus be used to give a rough indication of an organization's level of maturity within Nolan's taxonomy.

The general picture that emerges from the survey is of a generally low level of maturity. The majority of firms were in stages 2 and 3 of Nolan's model (contagion and integration). Typical of such companies was Bellflower Ltd (a pseudonym!), a manufacturer of industrial pumps, employing 220 staff. Bellflower possessed no IS plan. Although major expenditure was foreseen on new applications (such as salesforce management) this was not part of a fully thought-out strategy. Very little integration was apparent: the company operated two main isolated systems, an MRP (materials requirements planning) system and a separate sales order processing system. There were plans to link the systems together, although there appeared to be no technical strategy for realizing this goal. A small IT department existed, but it was essentially concerned with maintaining the day-to-day running of the MRP system. The department played a minor part in IT policy making which was dealt with at board level by the marketing director. A LAN had recently been installed and end-user applications were burgeoning in an uncontrolled way in the administrative areas. The picture of Bellflower which emerges contains elements of both stages 2 and 3 of Nolan's model and could be said to be in a state of transition between contagion and control.

Bellflower is typical of the majority of the companies in this survey. The average company is characterized by a proliferation of systems which have grown out of an original core of financial applications but which now cover the majority of business areas (Table 2). An intermediate degree of system integration exists, and there are strong aspirations for more integration, often based around the concept of a database management system. IT resources are strongly centralized, and the majority of larger companies have a central IT department. End-user development is not centrally encouraged, but it is steadily increasing. Although there appears to be a degree of awareness of the strategic relevance of IT to business, very few companies have a formal medium-term plan.

The typical UK manufacturing company thus sits somewhere between stages 2 and 3 in Nolan's model (contagion and control). Although Li and Rogers's (1991) survey suggests that US firms are significantly more sophisticated, their study covered all sectors and dealt with companies that were typically much larger than those in the present survey. Some fragments of evidence suggest that the situation in the UK is not that much different from that in the US. Hagman and McCahon (1993), for instance, have recently reported that small to medium sized US firms evidence little in the way of IS planning and have a low level of awareness of the strategic potential of IT.

Although the typical company exhibited a moderate degree of sophistication, a much higher level of maturity was observed in some cases. Foxglove plc, a manufacturer of gearboxes and transmission chains, is an instructive example. Foxglove is a long-established enterprise with considerable IT experience. The company has recently undergone major restructuring after a period of diversification, and now employs less than 1000 staff compared to a figure many times as high a few years ago. A clear IT plan existed which has been largely executed, and which involved the replacement of IBM mainframes by midrange Unix boxes, networked together throughout the company. Strategic EDI links were in place between Foxglove and its suppliers, partners and customers. Only three staff were in the central IT department, compared with over 20 before restructuring. The department operated as a small ‘information centre’, helping business units to buy software, defining standards and providing specialist support (for example, project management). The business strategy of the company was to seek out new niche markets and to exploit such opportunities vigorously. The role of information systems was dedicated to support this end (for instance, by EDI). Although Foxglove does not fit neatly into one particular growth stage, it appears to have attained a high degree of IS maturity; we would give it a modal position at stage 5 in Nolan’s hierarchy.

## Growth models: some critical observations

Earl (1989) describes IS stage models, like Nolan's, as 'positioning frameworks'. They have educational value in raising awareness of the weaknesses of current IS/IT strategies and in helping management to anticipate future challenges. By mapping out the way ahead, the 'zone of proximal development' to borrow an apt expression from developmental psychology (Vygotsky, 1978, p. 86), they provide a systematic framework for IS/IT planning. Whilst the stage models are undoubtedly of educational value, the present results underline their empirical limitations. Common to all such models is the idea that organizations progress through a series of clearly demarcated developmental stages and that this progression reflects an orderly process of organizational learning. Our results provide further evidence that, in practice, stages are not clearly defined; that organizations often possess elements from several stages, and that organizational and technological benchmarks are commonly out of line.

Maturational models inherently put a strong emphasis on endogenous learning processes. Our findings indicate that external factors can often play the decisive role. Foxglove plc is a good example of this. Here the rapid movement to an advanced level did not reflect a progressive process of organizational learning; it was a dramatic reflex reaction precipitated by a major crisis in the business. This example also shows that the incremental progression from stage to stage implied by evolutionary models is not obligatory. Galliers and Sutherland (1991) also argue that ‘skipping’ of stages can happen, although they warn of the dangers as crucial learning experiences may be missed.

Regarding Nolan's model, Galliers and Sutherland (1991) and Earl (1989) have commented that the model pays too much attention to database technology which is only one information technology amongst many (examples include office automation and networks). Earl (1989) argues that each of these technologies will follow a separate developmental trajectory; the hallmark of IT maturity is the presence of an integrated IT framework (an ‘architecture’) embracing all technologies, rather than the developmental state of any single technology. We would concur that an emphasis on integration and strategy rather than individual technologies provides a more useful diagnostic approach. Certainly we would agree with the criticism that Nolan's model is showing its age. More recent formulations, such as Galliers and Sutherland's six-stage model (Galliers, 1991; Galliers and Sutherland, 1991), would appear to provide a more up-to-date and comprehensive framework for addressing contemporary IS/IT issues.

## IS profile and firm size

As well as providing basic information about IS sophistication, the present study also allowed a number of more complex questions to be examined, in particular regarding the relationship between organizational context (organizational size, structure, and so on) and IS profile.

The relationship between organizational context and the characteristics of a company's IS organization and infrastructure has been vigorously researched. The organizational fit hypothesis (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978) encapsulates the idea that different IT/IS structures are appropriate to different organizational forms and managerial philosophies. Tavakolian (1989), for instance, has shown that organizations espousing conservative competitive philosophies are more likely to possess a centralized IT structure than more aggressive and entrepreneurial enterprises. In a similar vein, Weill and Broadbent (1991) have argued that small entrepreneurial companies are more likely to use IT in an innovative, strategic way. In their study of manufacturing firms, they provide some weak evidence for this view.

In the present study, there was some evidence of an increase in IS sophistication as a function of firm size. Larger enterprises had a more strongly centralized IS function (the existence of a separate IT department, less decentralization of resources) and were more likely to have developed a medium-term IS plan. Larger organizations also tended to have a more integrated IT infrastructure involving a broad mix of platforms and local/wide area networks. They were also more likely to take a methodological approach to software development and to make use of formal project management procedures. Smaller companies often had no central IT function or explicit IS/IT strategy. They evidenced a less systematic approach to software development and project management, and typically they had no internal development capability. On the other hand, there appeared to be no difference between large and small firms in their strategic appreciation of IT nor in the broad functional areas covered by IT.

## Systems development approaches

The phenomenon of end-user computing (EUC) was first identified by researchers in the early 1980s. The sort of burgeoning growth rates recorded in early studies led pundits such as Benjamin (1982) to predict that by 1990, EUC would ‘consume 75% of the corporate computing resource’. In the present survey, the magnitude of EUC was not of this order; however, respondents predicted that within five years, the amount of EUC would increase threefold to 40% of all IS development. Although the expected prevalence of EUC was not confirmed, the study did reveal a strong shift to the use of packaged software. In 1988, Cerveny and Joseph found that for US banks, packages were the preferred method of development in smaller institutions. In the present survey, 56% of software development in small firms was carried out in this manner, and overall the figure was close to 50%.

Companies were also asked about their use of external assistance for systems development. Rather surprisingly, less use of consultants and contractors was observed than might have been expected. Several researchers (Lees, 1987; Soh et al., 1992) have argued that small businesses in particular will tend to make use of outside resources. However, the firms in our study were generally sparing in their use of external resources and there was no evidence that smaller enterprises made more use of consultants than larger concerns.

One aim of the study was to provide baseline information on the prevalence of methodologies and tools as our knowledge in this area is patchy. Overall, 65% of firms made use of a formal methodology which implies a ratio of two-to-one in favour of structured versus informal approaches, a ratio similar to that reported by Cerveny and Joseph (1988) in their study of US banks. It is significant that the use of formal methods was considerably higher in larger firms. Regarding CASE tools, apparently very little use of these was made. The figure of 17% is comparable with Li and Rogers's (1991) figure of 24% and is consistent with the finding of recent UK studies which have suggested that many organizations make little use of such facilities (for example, Millington et al., 1991).

## IS success: user involvement and other factors

It is striking how many projects were judged by our respondents to be successful. It has become fashionable in recent years to speak of the phenomenon of IS failure and to take a generally pessimistic view of the prognosis for IT projects (Lyytinen, 1988a, 1988b). In the present study, only 13% of projects were judged to be totally unsuccessful; nearly 90% of projects were estimated to have produced benefits of some kind. It must be said that our measurement of success was crude by comparison with studies that focus directly on this issue and apply sophisticated instruments, such as the widely used ‘user information satisfaction’ measure (Ives et al., 1983). Moreover, it is likely that a less sanguine answer would have been forthcoming had the CEO or end users been consulted, rather than the IT director who might be suspected of a partisan view.

Nonetheless the finding that nearly 90% of projects produce benefits is encouraging. Presumably, the Darwinian economic environment in which manufacturing firms have had to operate in recent times has inculcated an ascetic discipline in which all investment has to be very carefully scrutinized. Comments made in interviews and on the questionnaires lend support to the view that the surveyed companies took a very hard-headed view of IT investment. A number of respondents observed that projects were not given the go-ahead unless the financial benefits were deemed adequate. Respondents claimed that this increased the success rate of IT projects, since projects that were not anticipated to yield benefits were not approved.

User involvement is a key issue in information systems research. Although the pattern of findings is mixed (King and Lee, 1991), the general picture that has emerged from survey studies is of a positive link between user involvement and IS success (Tait and Vessey, 1988; Kaye, 1990; Allingham and O'Connor, 1992). Our findings showed that user involvement varied by lifecycle stage, with the maximum involvement occurring during requirements definition, report design and user interface design. Although many firms did claim to involve users in these phases, it is salutary that in a significant minority of firms, users were largely excluded from these activities. In the more technical phases of analysis and design, there was apparently very little user participation. This was despite the views of the respondents that user involvement was an important ingredient of system success.

Regarding the effectiveness of involving users, the survey provided no direct evidence that higher levels of user involvement were positively correlated with the achievement of tangible benefits. However, given the crudeness of our measures this null result cannot be considered conclusive. In-depth case studies have shown that the dynamics of user involvement in systems development are complex and unpredictable; there is no certain link between participation and success (Newman and Robey, 1988). Recent factor studies have shown that sophistication modelling is required to bring out the relationship between user involvement and project outcome from a complex matrix of confounding and moderating variables (for instance, Kappelman and McLean, 1991; King and Lee, 1991).

The concept of IS success is a complex matter (Wastell, 1993). There are many dimensions to success and the evaluation of any project will ineluctably depend on the point of view of the observer. Moreover, system development is a dynamic, changing process in which many factors other than user involvement will play a part (including system complexity). One factor which did seem to bear on success in the present study was ‘strategic sophistication’. Those companies who, through their answers to a series of questions, evidenced a deeper appreciation of the strategic business potential of IT appeared to achieve a higher success rate for their IS developments.

A second factor which showed a relation to success was the use of methodologies. It is intriguing that this advantage only applied to internally developed methodologies, that is, methodologies that had grown up organically over a period of time rather than standard methodologies imported from outside. The result is of considerable potential significance and is being followed up in our case studies. A final point is that those companies enforcing joint user and IT staff accountability for system development appeared to achieve a significantly higher success rate than the average. Of course these findings are subject to potential biases and halo effects. The survey, as we have said, is heuristic and exploratory; it was not designed for the rigorous evaluation of specific hypotheses.

The factors that our respondents judged as bearing on project outcome were of interest. Essentially these were human and organizational (poor training, low involvement, politics, and so on) rather than technical. This contrasts with the rather technical stereotype of IS professionals that is often painted by IS researchers (such as Hirschheim et al., 1987). The need to link IT and business strategies was the most popularly cited success factor, which is consistent with our earlier discussion of the relationship between strategic sophistication and IS success. This finding is in line with the results of other surveys which highlight the importance given by IT managers to the integration of IS and business planning (Earl, 1989).

The main reason cited for the failure of projects was the difficulty involved in producing definitive specifications. This is consistent with the findings of several previous studies (such as Curtis et al., 1988; Lyytinen,

1988b) which have identified requirements definition as the key problem in IS development. The recent interest in soft systems methodology (Checkland and Scholes, 1990) has been inspired by the potential of this technique to help elucidate IS requirements (Lyytinen, 1988a; Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990; Wastell et al., 1994).
