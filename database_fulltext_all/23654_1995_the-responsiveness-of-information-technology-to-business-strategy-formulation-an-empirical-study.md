# The responsiveness of information technology to business strategy formulation: an empirical study

FELIX B. TAN

Department of Management Science and Information Systems, The University of Auckland, Tamaki Campus, Private Bag 92019, Auckland, New Zealand

In a study involving 88 companies, the nature of the linkages between business strategy and information technology (IT) responsiveness was explored. The results indicate that variations in IT–strategy responsiveness, measured by the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in business strategy formulation, are linked to the type of business strategy being pursued by the organization. For example, IT is more responsive to business strategy in organizations which place heavy emphasis on innovation in their product and market strategies than in those which operate in a relatively stable product/market area. This implies that organizations with an aggressive business strategy are more likely than those with a conservative business strategy to explicitly consider IT as a strategic resource in formulating business strategy. This paper suggests that the extent to which IT is used to shape business strategy differs among organizations, and that business strategy can help us understand these differences.

## Introduction

Recent studies have revealed that there are significant differences in the extent to which IT is responsive to business strategy (Lucas and Turner, 1982; Johnston and Carrico, 1988; Venkatraman, 1991). Several authors have attempted to explain these variations. McFarlan et al. (1983) argue via their strategic grid framework that IT is more strategic in some industries than in others, hence the variation. Porter and Millar (1985) propose that industries characterized by high information intensity or content in their products and processes, will have greater opportunities to exploit IT than those which do not. The aim of the investigation described in this paper is to establish if variations in the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in formulating business strategy can be explained by the type of business strategy adopted by the firm. Can an organization's strategic orientation explain the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in shaping business strategy? Is the role that IT plays in shaping an organization's business strategy influenced by the organization's product/market orientation?

## The IT-strategy connection

The traditional view of the role of IT in organizations has been that it is largely to support the functions of the organization. Pyburn (1991) and Venkatraman (1991) argue that this approach is largely reactive – a response to existing business strategy and processes, and not considered critical to either strategy formulation or implementation. As the pace of competition intensifies, senior managers must become aware of the growing need to be more responsive with IT, especially in business strategy formulation. Kantrow (1980) maintains that a critical link between IT and business strategy exists, and the only real choice is whether managers want to see it. In the organizations that seek to apply IT to strategy, the role of IT has evolved from the traditional to the strategic.

Since the early 1980s, the terms ‘strategy’ and ‘technology’ have been appearing with recurring regularity in the information systems literature. Numerous business publications have indicated that IT can provide new forms of customer service, new distribution channels, new information-based products and can even rearrange industry boundaries (Benjamin et al., 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Porter and Millar, 1985). Examples of successful applications of IT into business strategy abound (Benjamin et al., 1984; Wiseman, 1985) and frameworks have been proposed to assist senior managers in identifying and categorizing similar opportunities (Porter, 1980; Parsons, 1983; Porter and Millar, 1985; Wiseman, 1985). However, strategy and technology have been criticized as an area of academic research. The major problem with research in this area is that proliferation of frameworks has been at the expense of explanatory models and that research has been anecdotal or conceptual rather than empirical or theoretical (Bakos and Treacy, 1986). The study reported in this paper is a step towards empiricism. The emphasis is on business strategy types and the extent to which IT is responsive to business strategy (IT–strategy responsiveness).

## Basis of the study

This study attempts to explore whether business strategy can help us further understand the differences in IT-strategy responsiveness in different organizations.

## Business strategy

The development of typologies has permitted the study of various aspects of business strategy (Hofer and Schendel, 1978; Miles and Snow, 1978; Porter, 1980; Hambrock, 1984). Several better known business strategy types are identified in Chan and Huff (1992). These include Porter's (1980) three generic competitive strategies of cost leadership, differentiation and focus; Miles and Snow's (1978) four strategic types of defenders, prospectors, analysers and reactors; and Herbert and Deresky's (1987) classifications of developing, stabilizing, turnaround and harvesting strategies.

The first step in studying business strategy is therefore to select a business strategy construct which incorporates critical dimensions and has strong theoretical underpinning and empirical support. Miles and Snow's (1978) strategic typology meets these conditions. Snow and Hrebiniak (1980) observe that the Miles and Snow typology characterizes the organization as a complete system, especially its strategic orientation, in dynamic interaction with its environment. They state that it focuses on the strategy itself, especially the organization's orientation toward product-market developments. Hambrick (1983) contends that Miles and Snow's strategic typology has important strengths. It appears to account for significant variations across organizations. It views the organization as an integrated system. It allows the business strategy construct to be operationalized in non-industry specific terms. Shortell and Zajac's (1990) findings lend support to Miles and Snow's measure of organizations' strategy. They establish that the Chief Executive Officers' self-typing approach is valid and reliable and that researchers can use Miles and Snow's typology with increased confidence.

Miles and Snow's (1978) typology encompasses four strategic types: defender, prospector, analyser and reactor. A number of authors agree that the first three are recurring viable business strategies which enjoy similar degrees of success, while the last is a strategic failure (Miles and Snow, 1978; Snow and Hrebiniak, 1980; Hambrick, 1983; Smith et al., 1986; Segev, 1987; Conant et al., 1990). These authors argue that, on the average and for any given industry, the level of performance of defenders, prospectors and analysers is similar in terms of profitability. A reactor, however, is an organization with no distinctive product–market strategy. That is, decisions are made in a random manner in response to environmental pressures. The focus in this paper is purely on the three successful strategy types of prospector, analyser and defender (they represent the three points along a continuum of strategy typology) and the differences between them in terms of IT–strategy responsiveness. Hence, organizations that claimed to be reactors were excluded from the study.

The key dimension underlying the Miles and Snow typology is the rate at which an organization changes its products or markets. It is in relation to this key dimension that the following descriptions of viable strategic types are made.

A prospector is an organization which values being ‘first in’ with new product and market areas, even if some of these efforts prove not to be highly profitable. The organization frequently operates within a broad product-market domain that undergoes periodic redefinition. Often this type of organization responds rapidly to early signals concerning areas of opportunity, and these responses often lead to a new round of competitive actions. A prospector will therefore place heavy emphasis on innovation in their product/market strategies.

An analyser is an organization which is seldom ‘first in’ with new products or services. However, by carefully monitoring the actions of major competitors in areas compatible with its product–market base, the organization can frequently be ‘second-in’ with a more cost-efficient product or service. The organization often maintains a stable, limited line of products or services, while at the same time moving out quickly to follow a carefully selected set of the more promising new developments in the industry.

A defender is an organization which attempts to locate and maintain a secure niche in a relatively stable product or service area. The organization tends to offer a more limited range of products and services than its competitors, and it tries to protect its domain by offering higher quality, superior service, lower prices and so forth. Often this type of organization tends to ignore industry changes that have no direct impact on current areas of operation and instead devotes primary attention to improving the efficiency of its existing operations. A defender will therefore place little emphasis on innovation in their product/market strategies.

Miles and Snow's (1978) typology generated a comparatively large amount of interest, investigation and empirical support. Some have applied the typology in their PhD research confirming their support for the theoretical framework (Hambrick, 1979; Glenn, 1985). Other studies have operationalized the typology and used it to categorize strategy content (Hambrick, 1979); to examine the relationships between strategy, distinctive competence and organizational performance (Snow and Hrebiniak, 1980); to establish the link between organizational competitive strategy and IT structure (Tavakolian, 1989); and to investigate differences in strategic planning system characteristics (Veliyath and Shortell, 1993).

## IT-strategy responsiveness

This refers to the degree to which IT is explicitly considered in business strategy formulation. Lucas and Turner (1982), Johnston and Carrico (1988) and Venkatraman (1991) suggest that there are differences in the extent to which IT is responsive to business strategy. They suggest that on one extreme, IT is least responsive to strategy, where developments in IT are independent of the strategies pursued. On the other extreme, IT is most responsive to strategy by expanding the strategic alternatives considered. Then there are the intermediate types, where IT serves as a strategy support function and is considered moderately responsive to strategy. These authors agree on a typology to describe these differences. The key dimension underlying the typology is the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in the formulation of the organization's business strategy. The typology is described below.

Type I: IT's role focuses primarily on creating operational efficiency through improving administrative and management information systems. IT strategy in this category is not directly linked to business strategy formulation or integrated with the organization's strategic plan. Thus developments in IT are independent of the strategies pursued, implying a minimal role in shaping strategy. In this context, IT is least responsive to business strategy.

Type II: IT supports existing business strategy and is not explicitly considered in business strategy formulation. Organizations in this category define and develop business strategy without regard to the strategic potential of IT. Once business strategy is defined, the IT function will actively seek out opportunities to use IT to support the strategy. IT's role in this instance is reactive in the sense that the defined strategy directs the shape of the IT architecture and not vice versa. IT, here, is considered moderately responsive to business strategy.

Type III: IT becomes integrated into business strategy formulation and is in constant coalignment with strategy. Thus, developments in IT signal implications for possible changes and improvements to business strategy, while modifications to business strategy trigger appropriate changes in the use of IT. In this category, IT expands the range of strategic alternatives considered by the organization – enhancing the linkages with customers and suppliers, eroding and fusing organizational boundaries and introducing new products and services. In this case, IT is explicitly considered in shaping business strategy and is therefore most responsive to it.

Venkatraman's (1991) research concluded that it is not effective to be a type I, with IT not explicitly considered in business strategy formulation. Type II is adequate as an intermediate solution but carries the danger that competitive benefits may be eroded as other firms imitate and adopt similar approaches. Type III aims at a dynamic integration between IT and business strategy with the common goal of exploiting IT for competitive advantage. He suggests that it is logical to move from type I to type III via type II.

## Hypotheses

A review of the literature pertaining to IT and business strategy highlights that there are variations in IT–strategy responsiveness. This raises the question: can business strategy help us to understand these variations?

Based on earlier discussions, it is logical to expect IT to be more responsive to business strategy in a prospector than in a defender. A defender tends to ignore industry trends and instead devote primary attention to improving the efficiency of existing operations. It is therefore reasonable to expect that a defender will adopt a type I approach to IT, which focuses primarily on creating operational efficiency through improving administrative and management information systems. On the other hand, a prospector tends to respond rapidly to early signals concerning areas of opportunity, and values being ‘first in’ in new product and market areas. It is reasonable to expect that a prospector will pursue a type III approach to IT, which will expand the range of strategic alternatives considered by the organization. It is also logical to expect the IT of an analyser to be more responsive to its business strategy than a defender’s and less responsive than a prospector’s. The following research hypotheses have been constructed to test these arguments.

H: The degree of IT-strategy responsiveness differs between the three business strategy types (prospector, analyser and defender).

Ha: IT is more responsive to business strategy in prospectors than in analysers.

Hb: IT is more responsive to business strategy in prospectors than in defenders.

Hc: IT is more responsive to business strategy in analysers than in defenders.

In the above hypotheses, the degree of IT-strategy responsiveness refers to the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in formulating business strategy. The higher the degree of responsiveness, the more IT is explicitly considered in shaping business strategy.

## Research methodology

A questionnaire survey was conducted to test the hypotheses. The study adopted the instrument used in previous works (Snow and Hrebiniak, 1980; Glenn, 1985; Tavakolian, 1989) to measure the business strategy of participating organizations. To measure the extent to which IT is responsive to the business strategy of these organizations, another instrument was developed.

A convenience sampling technique drew 392 companies. The survey instruments were sent to the general managers/managing directors (or their equivalent) of these companies. The most senior executive in the organization was selected primarily because s/he would be the best person to identify the business strategy pursued and to identify the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in formulating business strategy. The survey instruments were pre-tested on ten companies, with modifications and refinements made thereafter.

## Operational measures

The instrument used to identify the business strategy of the participating organizations was originally developed by Miles and Snow (1978) and has since been used in other studies (Snow and Hrebiniak, 1980; Glenn, 1985; Tavakolian, 1989). The four business strategy types were described in separate paragraphs. The most senior executive in each participating organization was asked to tick the description which most closely fit their companies compared to other firms in the industry. This paragraph approach to measuring business strategy has a number of merits. It can be completed quickly by respondents, categorization decisions can be made easily by researchers, and it is also the most widely used measure proposed to date. It must be noted that any attempt to categorize the complex notion of business strategy using the paragraph approach will necessarily involve simplification (Smith et al., 1986; Segev, 1987; Conant et al., 1990). For research purposes, the aspect being studied is the organization's orientation toward product-market developments and the paragraph approach is suited to measure this.

A second instrument was used to measure the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in formulating business strategies (Appendix A). The degree to which IT is responsive in shaping business strategy was determined by responses to six Likert-type items. Possible responses range from 5 (great extent = most responsive) to 3 (some extent = moderately responsive) to 1 (no extent = least responsive). The measure for this variable was the mean of the responses to six questions. The items measuring IT-strategy responsiveness have been developed from works which describe the impact of IT on the organization's value chain, competitive forces, business strategy and processes (Porter, 1980; Parsons, 1983; Benjamin et al., 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Porter and Millar, 1985) – for instance, the use of IT to act as a lever to change business strategy, processes, infrastructure and industry rules; to enhance supplier-customer relationships; to create new products and/or services; and to develop advantage through lowering costs and enhancing differentiation.

## Testing of hypotheses

Scheffe's multiple pair-wise comparison test was used to compare the mean score of the three business strategy types (prospector, analyser and defender). If the Scheffe comparison indicates that there is a significant difference between the mean scores of any two business strategy types, then it can be concluded that the two business strategy types differ in the degree to which they explicitly consider IT in business strategy formulation.

Table 5 shows Scheffe's multiple pair-wise comparison of the mean scores for each of the business strategy types. The F-ratio obtained from the analysis of variance is significant at 0.05 (this implies that the mean scores of the business strategy types are significantly different). Therefore, it can be concluded that, in this study, the business strategy types differ in the degree to which IT is responsive to formulating business strategy. According to Scheffe's test, the mean score of prospectors was significantly different from that of defenders at p < 0.05. However, the mean score for analysers was not significantly different from that of either prospectors or defenders. It can be concluded that, in this study, prospectors and defenders differ in the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in shaping business strategy. Therefore, Hb is the only hypothesis that is supported by these findings. Ha and Hc cannot be accepted as the results are inconclusive.

## Discussion

The use of the paragraph approach to measure business strategy enabled the identification of relatively pure business strategy types, as articulated by Miles and Snow (1978). This corroborates previous findings and highlights the different strategic orientations of the participating organizations. The findings of this study further indicate that variations in IT–strategy responsiveness (types I, II and III) can be explained by Miles and Snow's strategic typology. Because the mean score of prospectors was significantly greater than that of defenders, we can expect IT to be more responsive to business strategy in prospectors than in defenders. That is, prospectors are more likely than defenders to adopt a type III approach to IT. To be more specific, prospectors more than defenders are predisposed to consider IT explicitly:

(1) as a lever to change business strategy;

(2) as a lever to change the underlying processes and infrastructure;

(3) in creating new products and services;

(4) in enhancing the relationship with suppliers and customers;

(5) in establishing new standards of performance by changing the rules in the industry; and

(6) in creating advantage through lowering costs and enhancing differentiation.

This penchant to consider IT explicitly in the above processes can likely be explained by the nature of the business strategy adopted. These organizations value being ‘first in’ with new product and market areas. They frequently operate within a broad product–market domain that undergoes periodic redefinition. They often respond rapidly to early signals concerning areas of opportunity and these responses often lead to a new round of competitive actions. Prospectors will therefore place heavy emphasis on innovation in their product/market strategies. It can therefore be speculated that IT in these organizations is explicitly considered to expand the range of strategic alternatives – enhancing the linkages with customers and suppliers, eroding and fusing organizational boundaries and introducing new products and services. IT developed in these organizations tends to be very responsive to business strategy formulation. IT tends to play a type III role in these aggressive organizations.

On the other hand, defenders attempt to locate and maintain a secure niche in a relatively stable product or service area. They tend to offer a more limited range of product and services than competitors, and try to protect the domain by offering higher quality, superior service and lower prices. Defenders tend to ignore industry changes that have no direct impact on their current operations and often devote their primary attention to improving the efficiency of their existing operations. It can be speculated that since defenders are likely to place little emphasis on innovation in their product/market strategies, that IT is less likely to be explicitly considered in shaping these strategies. Consequently, IT is least responsive, or in some cases may possibly be totally independent of business strategy formulation. A type I approach to the use of IT is typical in such conservative organizations.

There are certain aspects of this study that deserve greater attention in future research. While the findings attribute variations in IT–strategy responsiveness to the type of business strategy pursued, they cannot confirm that the variations are linked to firm size or industry types. In their study, Miles and Snow (1978) found linkages between their strategy typology and Chandler's (1962) and Wrigley's (1970) work on organizational developmental stages. Chandler (1962) and Wrigley (1970) suggest a size element as a corollary to organizational developmental stages. This motion is supported in a study examining Miles and Snow's (1978) typology of strategy and firm size which confirmed that firm size can explain differences in strategy (Smith et al., 1986). Organizational size differences may therefore also explain the variations in IT–strategy responsiveness and therefore merit exploration. McFarlan et al. (1983), Porter and Millar (1985), Johnston and Carrico (1988), Dos Santos (1989) and Cash et al. (1992) contend that the role and impact of IT on organizations varies across industry types. Differences in industry conditions, such as increased competitive pressures and significant information content in key relationships, are factors which act as catalysts to set off attempts to find ways to gain strategic advantage through IT. For instance, organizations in the airline and financial services industries have been forced by industry factors to use IT as a strategic resource, whereas the conditions in oil and cement manufacturing do not compel organizations to seek strategic opportunities through IT. The prospect of industry differences influencing IT–strategy responsiveness warrants further investigation.

A number of authors have supported the view that researchers should include organizational performance as a key variable in IT–strategy studies (Ginsberg and Venkatraman, 1985; Chan and Huff, 1992). Research examining the relationship between organizational performance and business strategy using Miles and Snow's typology has revealed that, on average and for any given industry, prospectors and defenders are equally successful in terms of performance (Snow and Hrebiniak, 1980; Segev, 1987; Conant et al., 1990). These findings are in line with the concept of equifinality, which suggests that the same outcomes can be achieved in multiple ways with different resources, diverse transformation processes, and various methods or means (Hrebiniak and Joyce, 1985). Smith et al. (1986) support this view, but also submit that organizational size can be an important performance factor. They found that small defenders generally outperform small analysers and prospectors, prospectors perform better as medium to large firms, and analysers perform better as large firms. Future studies should direct attention to the interactions between strategy, IT responsiveness, firm size and performance. This may also throw some light on Venkatraman's (1991) proposal that a dynamic integration between IT and business strategy is more effective than one in which IT is not explicitly considered in business strategy formulation. Investigations exploring the way IT is exploited in small and large firms and its link with business strategy and performance is important to the understanding of IT–strategy responsiveness.

## Conclusion

This study represents an attempt to examine the influence of a firm's strategic orientation on the extent to which IT is explicitly considered in shaping its business strategy. Consistent with earlier predictions, IT-strategy responsiveness differs among organizations. This study indicates that the degree to which an organization's IT is explicitly considered in shaping business strategy is linked to the type of business strategy pursued by the firm. This implies that organizations with an aggressive business strategy are more likely than those with a conservative business strategy to explicitly consider IT as a strategic resource in business strategy formulation. This paper suggests that the extent to which IT is used to shape business strategy varies among organizations, and that business strategy can help us understand these variations.

Finally, it is important to note that the relationship between business strategy and IT is a complicated phenomenon, only partially dealt with in this study. IT–strategy responsiveness may be moderated by other factors in the organizational context. Researchers (McFarlan et al., 1983; Pyburn, 1983; Doll, 1985; Wyman, 1985; Gerstein, 1987; Rockart, 1988; Venkatraman, 1991; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993) have argued that in order to understand the link between IT and business strategy, focus must be directed to the important organizational domains that moderate the relationship (management infrastructure and processes, and IT infrastructure and processes). Future research exploring these dimensions is clearly warranted.
