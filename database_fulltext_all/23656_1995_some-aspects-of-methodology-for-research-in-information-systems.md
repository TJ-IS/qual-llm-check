# Some aspects of methodology for research in information systems

DAN REMENYI

University of the Witwatersrand, Johannesburg, South Africa

BRIAN WILLIAMS

Epidemiology Research Unit of South Africa, South Africa

A prerequisite for conducting sound academic research in information systems is to understand the research process – how to identify a suitable research problem, how to create a theoretical conjecture and hypotheses, how to collect data and how to test and analyse them. An appreciation of these issues is essential before any professional research may be conducted either for general academic publication or for a higher degree. This paper discusses, in general terms, the nature of academic research and how the scientific method is used in practice. It identifies three major categories of research and explains the steps which the research needs to follow if a claim is to be made that a significant addition has been made to the collection of knowledge. Easy to follow flow charts showing the necessary stages in the research process are supplied.

## Introduction

In all disciplines the principal purpose of research is to add to the body of established knowledge by addressing some of the myriad unanswered questions. To do this satisfactorily, and to claim that a significant addition has been made to the collection of knowledge, the researcher should comply with the ‘scientific method’, that is, an informal but strict set of rules that have evolved to ensure the integrity, reliability and reproducibility of the work.

This paper addresses some of the issues of research methodology for the acquisition of knowledge in the study of information systems in particular and the social sciences in general. It offers some guidelines to information systems researchers for selecting a methodology to use in their research.

In order to understand the scientific method it is first necessary to establish a definition of science. There are almost as many definitions of science as there are scientists, and even

Scientists have not yet settled among themselves on a single model of what science is. (Lee, 1989).

But perhaps the most succinct and useful definition is the following offered by Albert Einstein (1950):

Science is the attempt to make the chaotic diversity of our sense-experience correspond to a logically uniform system of thought. In this system single experiences must be correlated with the theoretical structure in such a way that the resulting co-ordination is unique and convincing.

Einstein does not tell us how this order in our understanding of the world is to be brought about, and it is as well to begin with a caution from Max Born (1950):

There is no philosophical high-road in science with epistemological sign-posts. No, we are in a jungle and find our way by trial and error, building our road behind us as we proceed. We do not find sign-posts at cross-roads, but our own scouts erect them to help the rest.

To complicate matters further the process of carrying out research is itself highly subjective, depending on the intuition, the instinct and the inspiration of the researcher. In the words of Stephen Gould (1980):

Science is not an objective, truth-directed machine, but a quintessentially human activity, affected by passion, hopes, and cultural biases. Cultural traditions of thought strongly influence scientific theories.

Bearing in mind these cautions and warnings, it is nevertheless possible to develop some guidelines which those engaged in research may find useful and may wish to follow.

## The central role of observation

The essence of scientific knowledge is that it is derived from observations made on the world. All our research derives from and ultimately refers back directly or indirectly to our observations, our experiences and our measurements, which are the cornerstone of all research. Things that cannot be observed either directly or indirectly through their effects or consequences, are outside the domain of science.

There are several ways in which we may make observations on the world around us: we may make passive observations, observe the consequences of uncontrolled interventions, or observe the results of deliberate interventions. These three types of observation are not mutually exclusive and a single research project could include any or all of these approaches.

Passive observation\* is the method most frequently used in information systems research, when the researcher is unable to conduct an experiment and has to rely on evidence that already exists. The researcher collects evidence in the form of interviews, written reports, questionnaires, artefacts, and so on. The researcher may be investigating one or many phenomena, but in all cases it is essential to realize that many different influences or variables in the environment will be active and will be reflected in the evidence being collected. Using passive observation it is sometimes difficult to determine which variables or influences are causes, and which are effects of the factors being observed. In the study of information systems, research into the reasons why computer-aided systems engineering (CASE) tools do not always produce the anticipated benefits would be conducted, at least in part, by passive observation. The archetypal example of passive observation is astronomy from which all of modern, natural philosophy is ultimately derived.

Uncontrolled intervention involves observing the effect of a major change in a driving variable on one or more dependent variables. The change in the driving variable will have occurred when something in the environment has changed, entirely beyond the control of the researcher. In this type of research the relationship between cause and effect is usually much clearer than in the case of passive observation, although noise in the environment may obscure the effects of the primary cause and there may be secondary driving variables which complicate the interpretation of the data. In the study of information systems, research into whether an organization purchases more computers after a major devaluation of the pound would be typical of research based on observation of an uncontrolled intervention. A modern archetype in natural or physical science for this kind of research might be the observations of the consequences of increased carbon dioxide emission over this century on global temperature.

Observation of deliberate intervention also involves observing the effect of a major change in a driving variable on one or more dependent variables, but in this case the researcher deliberately brings about the change in the driving variable. Here the researcher has more control over what is being changed and what is being observed and the relationships between cause and effect are therefore easier to interpret. It is not easy to find circumstances in information systems research, or for that matter in most aspects of social science research, where it is possible to conduct such controlled experimental research. In fact it may be argued that because each situation in information systems is so different from any other, controlled research in this field would often not be appropriate, even if it were possible. Nonetheless an example of the use deliberate intervention in information systems research might be if a firm decided to introduce a new system, such as a graphical user interface, into a department in order to determine the costs and increase in productivity before deciding to expand the use of the new system across the whole firm. The archetype for this approach in the physical sciences was Galileo's observations of the effects of changing weights on the speed at which bodies roll down inclined planes.

## Research methodology

Research methodology refers to the procedural framework within which research is conducted. A methodology therefore describes an approach to a problem which may be operationalized into a research programme, formally defined by Leedy (1989) as:

an operational framework within which the facts are placed so that their meaning may be seen more clearly.

Research methodologies are important, but it must always be remembered that they provide guidelines rather than firm prescriptions as to how research should be conducted. Indeed

one must regard all analytical methodologies or structures ... as mere intellectual frameworks and be very cautious about their overuse in detail. (Quinn, 1988)

## Literature review

An extensive literature review is an essential prerequisite for research in all three categories listed above. The literature review will reveal the established facts of the situation and these must be fully understood by the researcher. The second objective of the review is to establish the current theories or models which have been used by previous researchers in the field. Finally the literature review should assist the researcher to identify an unsolved problem (Leedy, 1989) in the field being studied that will become the focus of the research projects.

To perform the literature review adequately the researcher should consider the published work critically. Not all that is published in the field should be taken at face value. This notion was well expressed by von Clausewitz (1832) when he said

it is a maxim in all books that we should trust only certain information and that we be always suspicious ...

Once the literature has been fully reviewed the researcher should be able to provide a narrative description of the current understanding in the field of study, including at least one area where there is incomplete knowledge which could be further investigated.

## Assessment of the established theory

It is important to decide early on if the problem identified in the literature is sufficiently explicit and generally accepted by people working in the field as a relevant problem for the researcher to be able to develop a theoretical framework and to derive workable and testable hypotheses. In a relatively mature field such as psychology, sociology or economics there are agreed facts and established (if sometimes conflicting) theories and the researcher may have deduced a new theory by analysing and then synthesizing ideas and concepts already present in the literature of the discipline. The emphasis in this type of research will be deduction of facts from the new theory in the hope that it provides a better or more coherent framework than the theories that preceded it. Darwin's theory of natural selection, arising in the context of detailed and extensive biological observations, and many competing theories concerning the process of evolution, illustrate this process well.

But in the study of information systems this is not likely to be the case. There is little established theory and few, if any, authors who have developed seminal theories of the kind supplied by Freud, Marx or Keynes. As a result an information systems researcher may attempt to generate a grounded theory, a concept developed by Glassier and Strauss (1967), which they define as

an inductive, theory discovery methodology that allows the researcher to develop a theoretical account of the general features of a topic while simultaneously grounding the account in empirical observations or data.

In attempting to develop a grounded theory the researcher will approach the enquiry with a reasonably open mind as to the kind of theory that will emerge from the study. Of course, preconceptions derived from the literature survey and owing much to the researcher's own knowledge, experience and intellectual background cannot be avoided, but where possible they should be identified and recognized. The grounded theory methodology normally relies heavily on the use of in-depth interviews for the collection of evidence. Where interviews are used to collect data an interview schedule may be prepared to remind the interviewer of the topics being investigated.

## Theoretical conjecture

The primary aim of the researcher developing a grounded theory is accurately to describe the phenomena of interest. Once this is achieved the researcher will begin to develop theoretical conjectures and hypotheses about the nature of the relationships between the observed variables. The grounded theory emerges through the process of concept discovery, within which the researcher develops abstract concepts and categories from the data. This is achieved by describing, defining and specifying relationships. The result is that the researcher moves to a level of abstraction in which the concepts generated by this process become a theoretical framework (Martin and Turner, 1986). It is most important to note that in this approach to research, concepts are regarded simply as more or less useful and not more or less true or valid (Cohen and Nagel, 1984).

Thus once the grounded theory has been developed the researcher in information systems is in a position to make a theoretical conjecture, but there is no structured methodology for doing this. Rather, this aspect of the scientific method may be regarded as an art which relies almost entirely on the imagination of the researcher. It is precisely here that science becomes creative.

The following is an example of a theoretical conjecture developed through the use of the grounded theory approach for a recent doctoral dissertation (Remenyi, 1990) in information systems management:

Strategic Information Systems (SIS) occur as a result of pressure or opportunities directly related to industry drivers. The firm's response to this pressure or opportunity is influenced by its strategy and by its critical success factors (CSF), and these issues determine the formulation of the SIS. The decision to attempt to take advantage of SIS is made with little attention to detail concerning cost-justification and vendor selection, but with more attention to communicating with the staff, training appropriate people and setting up support facilities.

An important change occurs at this stage of the research. Whereas the formulation of the research problem began as a description of the known facts from which a narrative theory was developed, this narrative will now be used paradigmatically. That is to say, it will provide a set of logical conjectures as the basis on which to predict and explain our observations.

## Hypotheses or empirical generalizations

When the new paradigm has been developed the next step is to use it to derive hypotheses or empirical generalizations. If the theory or thesis has been derived from a review of the literature without recourse to grounded theory then the term hypothesis is more generally used, while if the grounded theory approach was employed then the term empirical generalization is usually more appropriate. In either event a series of clear statements will be produced which will be testable against further evidence.

The following set of empirical generalizations has been developed from the above example of a theoretical conjecture from the doctoral dissertation in information systems management (Remenyi, 1990):

Strategic Information Systems occur as a result of pressure or opportunities directly related to industry drivers.

The firm's response to this pressure or opportunity is influenced by its strategy and by its CSFs and these issues determine the formulation of the SIS.

The decision to attempt to take advantage of SIS is made with little attention to detail concerning cost-justification and supplier selection.

More attention is given to communicating with the staff, training appropriate people and setting up support facilities.

Note that from the theoretical conjecture provided on the previous page four hypotheses or empirical generalizations were developed.

Some research studies stop at the stage of theoretical conjecture, perhaps having developed some hypotheses or empirical generalizations. It is argued that at this stage a contribution has already been made to knowledge and this may well be so. However, if the newly discovered knowledge is to be more widely useful then it is usually necessary to progress to a further stage in the research in which the new thesis is tested against a larger sample population.

## Data collection strategies

In the social sciences the collection of data for the purposes of testing a theory can be carried out in many ways. Different approaches include case studies, structured or semi-structured interviews, participant-observer studies as well as questionnaires.

Case studies involve the collection of evidence in relation to a particular set of circumstances from multiple sources (Yin, 1989) which may be quantitative or qualitative in nature.

Structured or semi-structured interviews allow detailed evidence to be elicited from individual informants who are encouraged to raise and suggest issues and problems which they regard as important to the issue being researched.

Participant–observer studies involve the researcher assuming a role in the situation being investigated. Here the researcher actually becomes one of the participants in the phenomenon being studied.

The questionnaire, which is sometimes referred to as a measuring instrument (Oppenheim, 1966), is a highly structured method of collecting specific information as a response to highly directed questions.

Case studies, structured and semi-structured interviews and participant–observer studies allow complex and rich evidence to be gathered while questionnaires usually elicit more specific replies which by their nature may not be as revealing but which are normally amenable to quantitative analysis.

## Measuring instruments

The preparation of questionnaires is an art in its own right and the researcher must be familiar with the guidelines for good practice in this field (Remenyi et al., 1993). However it is absolutely essential that a pilot study be conducted to establish that the proposed questionnaire is intelligible and clear to members of the target population. The researcher must ensure that the questionnaire is unambiguous, reliable and valid for the purpose for which it is to be used.

Where possible a previously validated questionnaire should be used bearing in mind that validation is normally only relevant within similar cultural, social and economic contexts. If a questionnaire is to be constructed ab initio then the following guidelines may be found useful:

(1) Economy – only questions which will be used in answering the research problem should be included.

(2) Completeness – all issues which may have a bearing on the research should be addressed.

(3) Comprehensibility - as the questions should be succinct it is important that they should be fully understood and therefore they need to be unambiguous.

(4) Consistency – questions should include checks to ensure that respondents are being consistent.

(5) Quantification – questions should be set in such a way that the answers will be quantifiable in terms of an acceptable scale.

(6) Balance – leading questions should be avoided in order to attempt to minimize the danger of unintended bias.

Questionnaires are often considered to be a relatively inexpensive method of collecting evidence but by their very nature they are limited in the kind of information which can be obtained. For this reason a research project will often involve using more than one of the above-mentioned evidence collection strategies.

## Sampling

As it is nearly always impossible to test the theory against all members of the target population it is necessary to select a sample of the overall population on which to conduct the test. Selecting a sample to ensure that it is both representative and unbiased is very important. The sample should be sufficiently large to be able to detect the real effects which are often hidden within the intrinsic variability of the evidence. Standard statistical methods are available that allow the determination of the sample size needed given the magnitude of the effect to be detected (Harnett and Soni, 1991). This requires considerable attention and is beyond the scope of this paper. Suffice it to say in the current context that if statistical inference is to be used in order to generalize the research findings, then it is necessary to ensure that the sample has been randomly selected from the whole population or from stratified subsamples of the population (Remenyi et al., 1993). In any event the evidence collected in this way will be used to test the thesis.

In many research studies generalizability is not an issue, rather the concern is to understand a particular, usually successful operational capability. In these circumstances a random sample is not appropriate as specific instances of excellence are what the researcher wishes to examine. The techniques used here to identify suitable informants is often referred to as theoretical or snowball sampling. In this case the researcher identifies one suitable informant and this informant is asked to nominate another who is likely also to be suitable. In this way the researcher collects evidence from a group of qualified respondents.

## Testing and analysis

The evidence collected may be either qualitative or quantitative and different methods of analysis are available in each of these categories. For qualitative evidence, which will usually have been gathered by means of case studies, structured or semi-structured interviews or participant–observer studies, techniques such as content analysis (Berelson, 1980) or correspondence analysis (Greenacre, 1984) will be used. These techniques are particularly useful in the application of inductive reasoning to the development of theoretical conjectures and empirical generalizations.

For quantitative evidence a variety of statistical techniques may be used to test the hypotheses or the empirical generalizations. Univariate methods of analysis\*, such as regression or analysis of variance may be used to investigate the effects of the independent variables on the variable of interest (Moroney, 1963). These statistical techniques are especially appropriate to the process of hypothesis testing where the researcher is validating or refuting deductions derived from an established theory. Various sorts of multivariate analysis may also be used to look for relationships between variables which are not otherwise apparent, or to establish supervariables which simplify the interpretation of the data (Churchill, 1987). Multivariate techniques may be used to produce perceptual maps which are valuable for the understanding of relationships between variables (Harnett and Soni, 1991).

## Confirmation of theoretical conjecture or fuller and refined theory

Testing and analysing the data may lead the researcher to confirm or reject the theoretical conjectures or to develop a fuller or more refined theory. This expanding and refining of the theory should help to bring the original theoretical conjecture into line with the evidence of the findings of the empirical research. In addition, provided that the sample was representative of the broader population and the measuring instrument was valid and reliable, it will now be possible to generalize the theory.

## Uncontrolled interventions

The second research category referred to above as uncontrolled intervention arises as a result of a change that has been brought about by an agency external to and independent of the researcher. This approach to research comes closer to what is normally regarded as experimental research.

## Literature review

Again one must start by reviewing the literature carefully and in detail. In this case, however, one might use the literature review to identify and consider likely events which could be regarded as uncontrolled interventions and which thus could provide the focus for the research.

It is unusual, although sometimes necessary, for the researcher to need to develop a grounded theory in this second category of research and the methodology usually passes on to the next stage of theoretical conjecture. In this category of research the problems being studied will probably be well defined and will relate to some aspect of previously established theory which is now being tested.

## Theoretical conjecture and hypotheses formulation

Making theoretical conjectures and formulating hypotheses are central aspects of this approach to research and require close attention. The way the theory is stated and the way the hypotheses are formulated will be important in determining how and why the data are collected.

Issues such as what data is relevant and available, and which data should not be collected may be raised at this stage. The question of the cost of data collection is always relevant and frequently has a major impact on the research process.

## Data collection design

Data must be collected relating to the situation both before and after the uncontrolled intervention and, where possible, for a control group that is unaffected by the intervention. For example, if the research problem was to determine how the purchase of computer equipment in the UK was affected by the major devaluation of the pound sterling in October 1992, then it would be necessary to collect data on computer purchases both before and after that date.

## Primary and control data

The data collected reflecting the UK purchases of computer equipment might be considered to be the primary source of evidence and the findings of the research would be based on tests and analyses conducted on these data. At the same time, wherever it is possible, data representing a control group should be collected. In the case of the devaluation of the pound sterling described above, a control source of data might be the purchases of computer equipment in France before and after October 1992 as there was no devaluation in the French franc. However for the data from France to be useful for control purposes it would have to be believed that the French economy was similar to the economy of the UK and that French organizations would react to a devaluation in a similar manner. The use of control data is typical of an experimental design which will be discussed later under category 3 research.

## Testing and analysis

As mentioned above for research based on passive observations, a variety of statistical techniques may be used to test the hypotheses or the empirical generalizations and the various techniques of univariate and multivariate analysis are equally applicable here. However univariate statistical analysis is more likely to be the central analytical tool in the case of this category of research.

## Confirmation of theoretical conjecture or fuller and refined theory

In the first category of research, in which passive observations were made, many uncontrolled events will bring about changes in a variety of possible response variables. Multivariate statistical techniques will play a central role in the analysis and deciding on the critical inter-relationships will be difficult. In the second category, the consequences of a major change in a single driving variable are studied in the hope that this will enable many other extraneous factors to be excluded and thus gain a deeper understanding of a more specific aspect of a problem.

## Deliberate intervention

The most powerful experimental methodology involves imposing a controlled and deliberate change on a system and observing the effects that it has on other variables. In this case the researcher has much more control over what is being observed. It is important to emphasize that it is not easy to find circumstances in information systems research, or for that matter in most aspects of social science research, where it is possible to conduct such controlled experiments. People do not generally like to participate in experiments except of the most bland type and for this reason psychologists are frequently reduced to observing rats in a maze. Commercial and even public sector organizations rarely choose to be involved with such experiments. Commercial firms believe that experiments can adversely affect their profits and public sector organizations are concerned that experiments could disturb their routine. Nonetheless there are circumstances where some degree of experimentation in an organization is possible and the controlled introduction of a computer system to a part of the firm on an experimental basis might be just such an example.

## Literature review

Once again a thorough review of the literature is of paramount importance. As before, at the conclusion of the literature review the researcher should be able to create a narrative model of the current state of knowledge in the field of study, including at least one area where the knowledge is incomplete, and suggest a problem that should be researched. Research problems in the natural or physical sciences are often well defined and researchers may actually begin their experiments before the literature review has been completed. This of course carries a degree of risk, as the experiment may have already been conducted by someone else in another part of the world, and this risk becomes unacceptably high in the social sciences.

## Theoretical conjecture

The development of theoretical conjectures together with formulation of hypotheses is again a central aspect of this research and requires close attention. A theoretical conjecture under this category will generally arise out of an established theory which one intends to explore and test. The theoretical conjecture and the hypothesis will usually be deduced from a more general theory and the way the thesis is stated and the way the hypotheses are formulated will be important in determining the next stage which is experimental design.

## Experimental design

The design of experiments is an extensive and involved topic of great importance. In this paper it will only be possible to give a brief outline. The key issues involve the form that the intervention will take, what data will be collected before, during and after the intervention, what sort of control group/s are required, how a sample may be chosen to represent the whole population, who will make up the experimental team and who in the organization will provide the interface for the purposes of data collection.

The essence of a good experimental design is that it will minimize the effect of extraneous variables on the evidence being collected, and elicit the effects arising from the intervention. Noise from the economy or from other aspects of the organization can seriously affect the data and where this cannot be altogether avoided by the design of the experiment then these influences must be investigated in the testing phase.

The timing of the intervention is a control aspect of the experimental design. It is important to decide when the data collection will begin, when the intervention will be initiated, when the ex-post data collection will commence and for how long the data will be recorded. The answers to these questions will in turn depend on whether there are any cycles in the operations of the target organization which could substantially affect the data.

## Measuring the variables before the deliberate intervention - ex-ante

The first stage in the experiment is to collect data concerning the performance of the organization before the deliberate intervention occurs. This data might be accounting or statistical numbers or it may be opinion surveys. This is the base data which will be compared to the corresponding data after the intervention has been completed. If, as is desirable, there is a separate control group to which the intervention is not applied, the data for the control group must clearly be collected at the same time as the data for the intervention group.

## Deliberate intervention

At the agreed time the intervention is initiated. This could be the introduction of a new system in the southern regional office of an organization, for example. The intervention period could take place in only hours or it could take days, weeks or months. The deliberate intervention must be controlled as carefully as possible in order to ensure that it complies with the plan laid down in the experimental design. Where the intervention deviates from the plan a detailed note must be made of how the experiment differed from the original plan.

## Measuring the variables after the deliberate intervention - ex-post

It is usually necessary to allow a period to elapse between the deliberate intervention and the collection of the ex-post data. This is because it often takes a while for the effect of the intervention to produce a material effect on the observable data. This period of absorption can take from days to months. However the situation in the organization participating in the experiment does not affect the collection of data in the control organization which will continue uninterrupted. An important issue here is when to stop the collection of the data as it is possible to continue to observe beyond a useful period.

## Testing and analysis

Once the data have been collected they must be processed to see if they support or contradict the hypotheses. However in addition to hypotheses testing there is frequently scope for modelling of the data to see if new patterns emerge. The subject of modelling is of course beyond the scope of this paper. However it is worth pointing out that in this context modelling refers to the creation of simplified abstractions of reality which capture the critical or key features of the situation. The use of models to consider various scenarios is a very powerful approach and is perhaps the most creative aspect of this category of research.

## Confirmation of theoretical conjecture or fuller and refined theory

As in categories 1 and 2 this testing and analysis allows for the theory to be confirmed or rejected, or a fuller or more refined theory may be postulated offering a greater degree of generalizability.

## The relationship between the three categories of research

Although in this paper research methodologies have been divided into three distinct categories these different approaches to research clearly overlap and have many points in common. It may therefore be instructive to consider their inter-relationships as demonstrated in Figure 5.

Passive observation, the first category, is the most general. Here there may not be a prior theory and therefore the researcher may have to develop a grounded theory by induction from a priori observations. Research of this kind is often undertaken in the newer aspects of social science such as information systems.

Studying the consequences of uncontrolled interventions provides more detailed information about a smaller part of the system under investigation. Normally the second category does not start from an attempt to derive a new theoretical framework inductively but rather begins with an already established theory which will be tested by observing the effects of the uncontrolled intervention.

The third category, in which one intervenes deliberately, focuses even more closely on a particular aspect of the problem and should provide the most detailed information. This research relies on having an established theory as the basis from which the hypotheses may be deduced and which will determine the nature of the intervention. Category 3 research is the most specific and the most directed level of research with the most structured methodology.

## Summary

There is no uniquely best approach to research, either in the natural or in the social sciences, and the best that can be done is to describe the way in which research is carried out in a variety of situations. Nevertheless, there are general lessons that can be learned and some of these are discussed in this paper.

The central issue in modern science concerns the relationship between that part of the world which is the object of a particular study and the theoretical framework which is constructed in order to explain the observations that are made on the world. However, experiments and observations made on the world do not immediately suggest the nature of the theory that will explain them and the particular observations that are made will be constrained by the currently held theory. The best research will often owe as much to the intuition and inspiration associated with art as to the logic and reason normally attributed to science.

The way in which information about the object of study is gathered may usefully be divided into three categories and most research projects can be fitted into one of these. Sometimes, as in astronomy, one is unable to intervene directly or indirectly and this has been called passive observation (category 1). In information systems studies this may also be the case and the best that can be done is observe the evolution of the system under investigation and then construct theories to explain the observations. It is then essential to have a firm grasp of the currently accepted theories in order to decide if they provide an adequate account of the observed phenomena.

It is generally true in science that one builds a broad understanding of the nature of a system by first investigating small parts of the overall system and trying to understand these. For this reason important insights can be gained by studying the response of the system of interest to an abrupt change in a single factor. This has been called uncontrolled intervention (category 2). The hope is that other things being constant, one can relate the effect of this single change to the effects that it produces.

An even more powerful approach involves perturbing the system directly in a controlled manner. This had been called controlled intervention (category 3). By choosing the nature of the intervention it is hoped that specific aspects of the system can be explored but this cannot always be done for ethical or economic reasons. When such interventions can be made the design of the experimental intervention is crucial in order to ensure that the observed changes in the system are indeed the result of the intervention and are not due to other, uncontrolled factors. Clearly, the interventions one chooses to make will depend on the theoretical framework which leads one to expect certain factors to be important.

Clearly there is overlap between these three categories of research and thus one research project may involve elements from all three approaches.

Scientific theories may be regarded as the free invention of the human mind with the constraint that good or powerful theories are those that give us insight into the nature of the system under investigation. Paradigmatic theories of great power nearly always begin as narrative descriptions that allow us to grasp our way closer to an understanding of the essence of the world around us. Success depends not on the discovery of final truth but on the discovery of useful knowledge that leads to a better understanding of the environment which may be the people and their ways, organisms and their interactions or physical objects and the forces between them.

## Conclusions

This paper reviews various approaches to empirically based research in the social sciences. It is not a definitive discussion of research paradigms in this field. Purely theoretical research has not been specifically addressed because empirically based research is the dominant approach in social science today.

This paper may be seen as a manifesto for empiricism. Although non-empirical research is relevant in the social sciences and important contributions may be made by scientists who construct general theories, such theories should ultimately be grounded in empirical observations. The complexity and diversity of the social sciences means that theories of the generality and power such as those that exist in the natural sciences are generally not available and the best research is likely to be that which is firmly based on empirical studies.
