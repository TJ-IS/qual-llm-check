# Analyst and programmer stereotypes: a self-fulfilling prophecy?

CAROLE BROOKE

Durham University Business School, Durham, UK

Previous research into the profiles of analysts and programmers has tended to present their attributes as virtually mutually exclusive and, in particular, has established a stereotype which favours the analyst for development as a manager. This view, historically reified through the division of labour between analysts and programmers and reinforced by the dual-track career paths of many organizations, intensifies the problems which technical staff experience when attempting to develop their potential. Using information from a case study conducted during 1989–1990 this paper proposes that the research profiles have become legitimized and reproduced at organizational level despite evidence of their inappropriateness. The relationship between management practice and documented theory has conspired to produce a self-fulfilling prophecy. However, the methodological basis of previous research is questionable and by recognizing these short-comings (compounded over a period of over 20 years) it becomes possible to critically re-evaluate beliefs about analysts and programmers. The paper argues for a more positive view of programmers and a more appropriate career structure for technical staff.

## Introduction

The objective of this paper is two-fold: firstly, to alert readers to the damaging effects of analyst and programmer stereotypes and inappropriate career structures and secondly and, perhaps most significantly, to challenge the assumptions of over 20 years' research by identifying weaknesses in methodology, concluding that what has become viewed as established 'fact' is the result of a powerful self-fulfilling prophecy.

This fieldwork was conducted between Autumn 1989 and Spring 1990 in one of the UK's largest service sector companies, hereafter referred to as Colour Co. The study focused on a software development group within the IT department where work undertaken was critical to the organization's core business. The group consisted of 50 staff, including four section heads, one manager and one assistant manager. Colour Co's software development staff were divided into ‘analysts’ $^{1}$ and ‘programmers’. Broadly speaking, the role of the analyst was to liaise with the user to produce a software specification and the role of the programmer was to translate this into computer code (see also Mumford, 1972). The management believed that analysts and programmers required different sets of skills, especially with respect to interpersonal communications. This belief was intensified by the existence of an analyst versus programmer rivalry within the organization typical of the IT business world as a whole (Pettigrew, 1973). It was against this backdrop that the research was carried out.

## The research approach

A qualitative case study approach was adopted which included the detailed study of company documentation and recorded interviews with management and staff, both on the project and in other interfacing departments, including Training and Personnel. A wide range of management levels were involved, from members of the executive board down to project managers.

The author occupied a desk in the offices of the software development group and attended every day during office hours. Although conducting research rather than creating software, this arrangement seemed to encourage the group to accept the author as another member of their team. This informal relationship may have helped in providing the in-depth insight into the organization which was required. Interviews were more like informal discussions and informants were encouraged to discuss the issues which they felt to be most important rather than being guided by a pre-structured list of questions. Significantly, the same issues were continually raised. This probably reflected the heightened concerns and insecurities of staff in the light of the changes which were taking place at the time of the research. In addition to the staff working on the software development project, another 17 members of staff from the IT department and Training and Personnel were interviewed.

Assessment of Colour Co's recruitment and development practices involved analysis of many different strands of information. Some of this information was freely available but much of the personnel information (especially individual staff records) was highly confidential and was only obtained on the basis of special confidentiality agreements. Details were gathered on the organization's official selection, recruitment, training and appraisal procedures. The career structure of the company and its various job families were outlined. For technical staff including managerial grades, job grade/salary structures and detailed job descriptions were studied, together with the material rewards associated with particular positions. Reporting lines and responsibilities of individual technical staff were compared. Promotion procedures were looked at in depth since this was to form a central part of the comparison between analyst and programmer career progression. The latter was explored in conjunction with staff comments concerning the status of individuals. Special attention was paid to the selection and initial training of new staff since it was at this point when decisions were made concerning appropriate job roles and career routes.

The consolidation of all this information enabled an in-depth assessment to be made of the individual profiles which Colour Co favoured in relation to particular career routes and how this related to personal development. Once the analysis and findings of the work were complete, a presentation was made to the software development group that had formed the focus of the research. Their feedback was obtained and incorporated in the final formal report which was presented to their senior management a few months later.

It is important to note that no personality tests or skills models were utilized during the research. Instead, by being based on observation and experience in the workplace, this study provides a contrast with previous work and enables the construction of an alternative perspective (Vitalari, 1985).

## The stereotypes

Previous research into the profiles (i.e. skills and related personal characteristics) of analysts and programmers spanned a period of at least 20 years. Many of these dealt specifically with American populations. The validity of extrapolating these findings to other populations needs to be borne in mind, therefore (see Buie, 1988). There were three main categories of study: those that treated analysts and programmers as a mixed group, those that looked at one or other in isolation (fewer focused on programmers) and those that made explicit comparisons. Papers that dealt with mixed groups (e.g. Kaiser and Bostrom, 1982; White, 1984) had not differentiated between them in terms of traits. Some also gave no account of how the groups had been selected nor described their membership. Thus, it was not possible to extract much information from these.

Whilst conducting the literature review it quickly became apparent that the characteristics of programmers were seen as being in virtual opposition to those of analysts. At the crudest level, the analyst was seen as a 'people person' (user-oriented) and the programmer as a 'machine person' (system-oriented). The following condensed profiles could be said to represent a general consensus of the published research (NCC, 1970, p. 37; Morris and Martin, 1972; Szafraniec, 1975; Cheney and Lyons, 1980; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Vitalari, 1985; Soloway, 1986; Cheney, 1988; Dos Santos and Hawk, 1988; Goldstein, 1988; McCubbrey and Scudder, 1988; Green, 1989; Joshi, 1990; Watson et al., 1990). The traits listed in Table 1 have been chosen to highlight the contrasts.

Cheney refers to studies conducted by the ACM (Association for Communication Management) and their recognition of the educational requirements of two types of graduate: technically trained systems designers and management-oriented information analysts (Cheney, 1988). Cheney's (1988) own study was one of few which directly compared analysts with programmers. Between surveys conducted in 1980 and 1987 there was little change. The only significant shift related to the importance of human relations. In 1987 this was ranked higher on the analyst list, whereas it was not only lower for programmers but much lower than it had been in the 1980 study. Cheney (1988) found this puzzling in the context of a move towards more user involvement in IT but it was explained by virtue of the perceived shortage of technical skills. As Cheney (1988, p. 6) said

Evidently, the hiring practices of IS managers are more concerned about the immediate positions to be filled not the existence of skills for future possible management positions.

He also detected a somewhat better skills correlation between managers and systems analysts than between managers and programmers. It seemed well accepted, then, that analysts and programmers were set in contrast to each other in terms of skill requirements and personal profiles.

This contrast appeared to be reflected at Colour Co. Analysts were described by management as possessing good interpersonal skills, an extrovert personality, a keen interest in the business and a broad skills base and were perceived as 'good management material'. In contrast, programmers were described as being bad communicators, often introverted, having an interest primarily in specialist technical areas and possessing a narrow base of skills and were consequently labelled as 'poor management material'. This view has a general historical explanation. The earliest professional computer technologists came from areas such as engineering or mathematics, disciplines which (certainly at that time) were seen to be lacking in human relations skills. The expectation was, therefore, that these technologists would be poor at dealing with people. In addition, some accused these specialists of wishing to maintain a professional mystique whilst at the same time failing to get to grips with the basics of business practice (Mumford and Ward, 1968).

Today groups associated with technical work still tend to be viewed poorly in relation to management potential (e.g. Mackay et al., 1989). The term ‘insubordinate minority’ neatly encapsulates this attitude (Mumford and Ward, 1968, p. 114). The division of labour has resulted in the analyst breaking free of this mould but the programmer has not been so successful (Kuhn, 1989; City University Business School, 1991; Gallie, 1991). Krasner et al. (1987, p. 59) concluded that

Although the model of organizations held hostage by brilliant, unkempt, and socially deviant programmers is popular, it seemed an anachronism from our observations.

Negative programmer stereotypes typical of the 1960s and 1970s have apparently survived beyond their appropriateness (see Friedman and Cornford, 1989, pp.113–15). Yet it is a stereotype which also extends to the standard occupational classification (SOC). Despite the fact that the SOC does not officially allow for it, there is pressure from users of occupational statistics to distinguish between ‘routine computer programming’ and ‘higher level skills of systems analysis’ (Employment Gazette, 1988). Thus, the format of census forms and of informant surveys presume the nature of the distinction and steer respondents in that direction. In this way negative analyst versus programmer stereotypes become reified by the social bureaucrats.

In order to establish if and how such stereotypes were being reproduced within Colour Co, it was necessary to identify the personal qualities which they looked for in staff and the career routes which were offered. A study of recruitment and training procedures and personnel records revealed that extrovert personalities with good communication skills were usually encouraged to train as analysts, even if this was not their original preference. Mature recruits tended to be more confident by virtue of their previous working experience and this may explain why they were also targeted for analysis. Conversely, those perceived to be introverts with less confident personalities but good technical abilities were generally advised to train as programmers. It was thought by management that these traits were what the jobs required and this was reflected in the job descriptions. The situation was reinforced during annual appraisals, too. The managers concerned (and some staff) believed that extroverts with good communication skills would make good managers, whereas introverts with non-assertive personalities would not. By virtue of the selection process at the point of recruitment, it followed that analysts were more often targeted for management positions than programmers. The potential development of individuals was fundamentally influenced, reducing their chances to expand into different skills areas.

Although Colour Co had adopted a view of analysts and programmers similar to that of the published literature, this view was not confirmed when dealing with the individuals themselves. The interviews and field observations suggested that there was a gap between Colour Co's beliefs concerning programmers and the character and needs of the individuals themselves. The information gathered during the case study suggested that the situation was far more complex than simplistic stereotypes allowed. Returning to the literature there not only appeared to be grounds for an alternative view but also evidence that the methodology of much of the research could be challenged.

## Alternative views

In his paper Green (1989) raised a critical issue. He uncovered a potential source of conflict between users and systems analysts relating to the differences in how they perceived skills and roles (Green, 1989). Users placed high importance on analysts' technical abilities, whereas analysts regarded interpersonal communications as their most valuable trait. It appeared that analysts believed they had to rely on behavioural skills in order to communicate with users, whereas possession of a certain level of technical skill (such as programming) was assumed, though not regarded as the most important part of application programme development. If Colour Co's management were assessing individuals on the basis of what they thought users expected from IT staff, then this conflict might be reproduced, particularly where managers had a technical rather than a user background. In Colour Co most of the IT management had come from technical backgrounds. If individuals were being categorized by their superiors, this would have implications for staff development, especially in respect of those who saw themselves as being judged negatively (Ahn and Lee, 1988). Programmers would, therefore, be adversely affected. One programmer interviewed commented that 'managers like people who are like themselves'. The IT managers, who had themselves been seen to fulfil the 'manager' stereotype, reproduced the stereotypes for their successors.

In contrast to the ‘established’ view (e.g. Couger, 1988) a survey of new MIS recruits revealed that individuals had a high need for social interaction (McLean et al., 1991). It may be that certain traits are present at the time of recruitment but become eroded by management expectations, time and lack of appropriate training. Unwillingness to venture into this territory is reflected by management practice. In discussing the importance of careful career planning, McLean et al. (1991) observed that managers of MIS personnel do not feel comfortable with the role of counselling and coaching staff. It seems likely that some technical staff within Colour Co were subjected to this double-bind. They had come from technical training into a project where the potential for personal development existed but with management who were unlikely to provide them with the necessary support. Instead, the managers focused managerial development on those who already appeared to ‘fit the mould’ as they perceived it. In so doing, they perpetuated the management/non-management (technical) divide.

Mumford (1972, p. 94) suggested that a desire for responsibility comes mainly as a result of being given it and liking it, but that individuals will cease to express such needs if experience in the work-place consistently denies their fulfilment. Goldstein and Rockart's (1984, pp. 112–13) recommendations are especially valid here. They propose that leadership training of both analysts and programmers is needed to improve overall effectiveness. If opportunities are limited then development of these skills is unlikely. In this situation 'reality' will be seen to mirror the stereotypes. Ironically, Mumford (1972, p. 206) indicated that when such training does take place the situation is not as clear-cut as the stereotypes might suggest:

But we found the necessary skills had not always been easy to acquire. Programmers told us that they had experienced difficulty in developing the temperamental qualities of patience and thoroughness while systems analysts said that they had been slow to acquire the skills associated with human relations and management.

If the divide runs contrary to the experience of the individuals themselves, it also conflicts with the notion that more ‘rounded individuals’ are essential to the successful operation of organizations. Whilst discussing the influence of information technology upon organizational structures and strategies, Zuboff (1988, p. 399) notes that:

The shape of skill distribution thus is more likely to represent a curve than the discontinuous step function that characterises the traditional hierarchy, with its more rigid distinction between managers and the managed.

There is an essential link here between what Zuboff is suggesting and the scenario portrayed by Barron and Curnow (1979) almost 10 years earlier. A move is identified from pyramid-shaped organizations to flatter but more 'top-heavy' structures, where boundaries between staff and management are increasingly blurred and levels of middle management are eroded (see also Zawacki, 1992). These writers discuss the need for 'all-rounders', especially in terms of training potential managers (Mumford and Ward, 1968; Drucker, 1988; Handy, 1988; Hammond and Holton, 1991 (introversion/extroversion traits); Scott Morton, 1991). The skill implications are clearly expressed by Zuboff (1988). Organizations will have to make long-term commitments to broad-based and on-going training (Willcocks and Mason, 1989). The suggestion is that the organizational all-rounder will be more in demand than the narrow specialist but this demand cannot be met if stereotypic development of technical staff continues. There is little discussion in the analyst and programmer literature of the variety of management styles in existence and the range of skills and motivators which these involve (see, in particular, Ferratt and Starke, 1989). As devil's advocate and particularly in the light of the 'flat organization', it is possible to argue that even some of the negatively stereotyped attributes have validity in recent visions of the post-modern organization, with its shift away from managerial hierarchies to decentralized and networked groups.

So far this paper has argued that stereotypes of analysts and programmers expounded in the literature have been reproduced by way of recruitment and training practices. The next section brings together observations from the literature and fieldwork to argue that dual-track career structures reinforce the stereotypes and are inappropriate human resource development strategies.

## Career structures for technical staff

Kraft's (1979) prediction that there would be career development problems for analysts and programmers appears to have been confirmed. At the time of the fieldwork the importance of improved career structures for technical staff was emphasized by both business experts and researchers (e.g. Causer and Jones, 1990; Ruhl, 1990). Businesses recognized that not all technical specialists would want to progress through traditional management routes and, therefore, required special attention. The importance of providing the right environment within which to develop and retain them was reinforced within Colour Co by two other important factors discussed in the business press.

(1) The dearth of technical specialists reported towards the end of the 1980s (the so-called ‘skills crisis’).

(2) The tendency for high staff turnover in response to competition for labour (reflected in inflated salaries).

In response to the perceived need for an improved career structure Colour Co introduced a consultancy grade in March 1989. The objective, according to management, was to provide specialist technical experts with the opportunity to retain their technical skills whilst progressing through the organization. Initially the consultancy grade was heralded by many staff as a solution to promotional ceilings. This optimism did not endure. The comments received from staff revealed problems typically associated with providing such infrastructures (Causer and Jones, 1990; B. Seward-Thompson, interview conducted at DECollege, Reading, 1990).

There were three problem areas: skill gaps, perceived status and reporting lines. Firstly, providing separate career paths for management and technical staff resulted in a divergence of skills which was difficult to bridge. Seward-Thompson (from an interview conducted in 1990)

has referred to this situation as a skills gap produced by Y career paths (the Y representing the bifurcating of the two tracks). The problem then shifted from being one of providing an upward route for technical staff to one of ensuring that managers and technical experts did not become so differentiated that it was impossible for them to communicate. The idea of having a role which combined technical and management skills was commonly prescribed as a solution to the 'skills shortage' perceived at that time. This seemed especially pertinent with the increasing trend towards user-friendly software. The term used to describe this role was 'hybrid'. Much was written about how hybrids should be developed and the British Computer Society set up a task group to investigate the hybrid phenomenon (for a summary, see Palmer (1990); British Computer Society (1990)). However, in order to provide individuals with the necessary range of skills it was also necessary to provide an appropriate environment in which to learn and apply them. The large, bureaucratic nature of Colour Co had given rise to rigid, formalized career paths. An individual was perceived in terms of their status and skill or profession and this determined their future prospects and the range of jobs available to them in future (Kellner and Crowther-Hunt, 1980, p. 43). The rigid career structures did not provide for much flexibility of personal development. As Smith (1989, p. 390) noted

Staff must be permitted to float up to their own levels of ability, rather than be rigidly pre-categorised according to formal, educational qualifications and recruitment tiers.

Here, the words of Mumford and Ward (1968) ring true. Frequently the analyst or programmer finds that they have ‘entered a bureaucratic, tightly structured company in which change is frustrated because many people have a vested interest in preserving the status quo’ (Mumford and Ward, 1968, p. 65). It was significant that a senior member of Personnel, when challenged on this point, commented that the organization was not in business to provide career structures. In the light of this it is worrying to note that managerially-based career structures can have a damaging effect on the ability of technical staff to influence collectively recruitment and development practices in their favour (Winstanley, 1988). The company training policy itself was also rigidly systematic. A member of the Training department explained that this ruled out the possibility of training staff in the ‘nice-to-know’ skills. There was a tendency for staff to receive a training overload at the time of recruitment. A combination of systematic training procedures and rigid career paths meant that individuals were often unable to put their newly acquired skills into practice leading to a need for refresher courses.

The second problem area concerned the promotion of senior technical experts. If they were promoted to the position of consultant, it would be likely that they would advise and report to a project headed up by a project manager, a manager in a less senior position to themselves. This, together with the fact that the consultant would not necessarily have any people reporting to them, created a problem of perceived status. At Colour Co informants explained that status depended upon five characteristics: salary (linked to grade), position on the vertical hierarchy, financial responsibility, the number of people for whom they were responsible and the seniority of the person to whom they reported. Since career paths were vertical and truncated towards the top of the hierarchy, it was assumed that the higher up the ladder an individual progressed the more these five characteristics came into play. For example, a senior manager would be expected to command a high salary, hundreds of staff and report to an executive member of the organization. This perception of seniority was not necessarily compatible with a technical career path, however. Yet because of the characteristics which were used to determine status, non-people management roles were viewed as less important by their peers. Brooks (1978, pp. 119–20) identified this problem in relation to dual-track career structures:

It is easy to establish corresponding salary scales for rungs. It is much harder to give them corresponding prestige.... A reassignment from the technical ladder to a corresponding level on the managerial one should never be accompanied by a raise, and it should be announced always as a ‘reassignment’, never as a ‘promotion’. The reverse reassignment should always carry a raise; overcompensating for the cultural forces is necessary (author’s emphasis).

If the culture had changed to recognize status in terms of value to the organization rather than just the criteria outlined earlier, then non-people management roles would have been accorded similar status to traditional management roles. Likewise, it was accepted in other organizations (e.g. Digital Equipment Company) that 'reporting' lines only affected the status of a consultant to the extent that the reportee dictated how to do the job. If Colour Co had accepted this, then consultants would have been able to increase their mobility and bring their expertise to bear at all levels of the organization without compromising their own positions.

Finally, the reporting line remained an unresolved issue. There was the question of who could oversee the work of technical experts especially if they had acquired organizational ‘guru’ status. Once again this pointed up the gap that could occur as a result of divergent skills development. Consultants were reporting to seniors who had business rather than technical backgrounds. This was symptomatic of a much more common problem noted in the literature: the lack of integration between IT and the business (see also Zawacki and Zawacki, 1989; Gingras and McLean, 1982).

Introduction of the consultancy role did very little to alleviate the problems experienced by technical staff, but management had also failed to make clear two very important points. Firstly, consultancy was a grade and not a job family. As such, it would have a limited effect upon promotional bottle-necks. It was intended that no more than 12 employees should be promoted to this grade from within the IT department. Causer and Jones (1990, p. 20) described such a situation:

The point here is that the project-team based nature of technical work makes it difficult to establish organisational niches outside that structure, and that the provision of technical leadership within project teams normally carries with it a measure of managerial responsibility, even where that position is not formally designated as a managerial one. This is not to say that some organisations do not have scope for individuals who can operate as expert consultants to a range of project teams. However, whether or not this happens is to some extent independent of the existence or non-existence of a formal dual track structure, and even where such posts do exist they tend to be numerically limited.

Secondly, the job description and requirements for the grade were described in terms very similar to those of people-management roles, in particular putting a heavy emphasis on communication skills. Most career progression involved promotion upwards through the organization. It was generally recognized that promotion meant moving into management grades and that part of the management function was to manage people. As a result of promotion, therefore, there was a tendency for technical experts to become involved in people management to the detriment of their grasp of technical knowledge and, thus, to lose valuable skills (Brooks, 1978). According to the experts themselves, material rewards (such as company cars, higher salaries) did not compensate for this. It certainly did not compensate the company for the loss of their skills. Some technical staff did not want to move into roles which were seen to be primarily concerned with the management of people (Morris and Martin, 1972, p. 37). Whilst Mumford (1972) noted that analysts and programmers were often reluctant to take advice on how to acquire management responsibility even when it was offered to them, this would be expected if their conception of ‘management responsibility’ conflicted with their own view of personal development.

Both analysts and programmers need self-development and consider this more important than ‘promotion’ as an end in itself (e.g. Mumford, 1972). Indeed, upward mobility was not the prime target in Colour Co. One senior individual expressed his resentment at being forced into a management grade against his wishes. All analysts and programmers in Colour Co had to pass an exam in order to receive promotion but these were only held biannually.

One member of the project explained his dissatisfaction with this situation: 'you can't grow into a job here now, you have to pass an exam first'. His comment underlines that whilst individuals needed a sense of achievement and the ability to develop to their full potential, this was often poorly catered for within organizations (Mumford, 1972). This, together with a desire to acquire a broader base of skills, led many to seek a job in another company. Technical staff have needs no less demanding in some respects than those of aspiring managers. Rosenbaum (1991) outlined these needs as autonomy, achievement, missions and goal-setting, support, stimulation, sharing and professional standing. The latter point was intended to highlight that whilst both analysts and programmers may be highly motivated to work, allegiance to their profession tended to be stronger than to a particular company (see also Mumford and Ward, 1968; Mumford, 1972, p. 207; Howard and Lebell, 1989). Colour Co realized that if they did not provide a suitable environment for their technical staff they would lose them.

Towards the end of 1990, Colour Co's management were exploring the possibility of introducing a new job family ‘analyst programmer’. The notion of ‘analyst programmer’ was not itself new to the IT world but differed from earlier manifestations (Pettigrew, 1973; Kraft, 1979). Colour Co's explanation for doing this was to introduce more flexibility into the career structure and to encourage a wider skills base. In other words, it was triggered by both the existing problems and warnings in the literature of impending skills shortages. The ‘new’ analyst programmer role reflected an established and widespread business trend linked to the changing nature of the relationship between users and development staff (Friedman and Cornford, 1989). One aspect of this changing relationship was the appearance of automated systems development aids, known as CASE (computer aided software engineering) tools.

At a superficial level this new job family might have been expected to address some of the problems experienced with technical career development. However, many programmers were suspicious, seeing the merging of roles as a possible precursor to and/or deskilling (see Council for Science and Society, 1981; Eason, 1988; Corbi, 1989; Kuhn, 1989; Gallie, 1991 for alternative perspectives). To an extent this concern was not unfounded. Management did believe that the new CASE tools would result in less dependency upon technically skilled staff, a trend noted elsewhere (Orlikowski, 1988; Incomes Data Services, 1989, p. 6). Thus, the role of the programmer as perceived in Colour Co appeared to be under threat. The trend towards 'front end development' had been predicted as early as 1979 (Barron and Curnow, 1979). The skills displacement which they identified then, is now epitomized by the modern-day software development life cycle itself with a shift from ‘technical’ to ‘managerial’ skills (see for example Blackler, 1988; Bilska, 1989; Whiting, 1990; Farwell et al., 1992). There are now many references to ‘knowledge workers’, even to a ‘knowledge class’, which reflect this idea of the changing nature of work (see for example Rajan, 1984; Lyon, 1988; City University Business School, 1991; Reed et al., 1993).

There was confusion as well as suspicion. As one individual put it: 'you won't know if you are a programmer or an analyst!' Role ambiguity has been shown to be a major cause of high turnover in technical staff (Baroudi, 1985) and highlighted by others as a point of concern (Bostrom, 1981; Goldstein and Rockart, 1984). Of particular relevance are the comments of Goldstein and Rockart (1984) who suggest that combining previously discrete tasks of programming and analysis with the objective of improving skill variety and, thereby, job satisfaction might lead instead to an increase in role ambiguity and conflict (cf. Strassman, 1985, pp. 74–75). Significantly, one senior manager warned that Colour Co were particularly bad at dealing with 'grey areas'.

Against the backdrop of analyst versus programmer rivalry and the general feeling of insecurity amongst programmers, it was not surprising to find that programmers were unenthusiastic about the new title, despite the suggestion that the ‘analyst’ label might help them ‘break the mould’. Analysts, of course, were even less enthusiastic to adopt a ‘programmer’ label. The feelings of staff were summed up in the following statement:

Although Colour Co acknowledged the importance of providing satisfying career paths for technical staff they were discovering that there was a challenging difference between recognizing this need and actually providing an effective solution. In one sense, it appeared that the stereotypes they had perpetuated within their own organization were now beginning to backfire. Colour Co had produced a rod for its own back. However, no changes in practice were likely to happen swiftly even if Colour Co perceived the need. It has been noted of technological development itself that practice lags behind theory (Friedman and Cornford, 1989, p. 89). In this instance, the ‘theory’ as presented in the literature also required substantial rethinking and the next section addresses these issues.

## Methodological problems

In discussing research into the skills of systems analysts, Vitalari (1985, p. 223) made the following comment:

The skill studies are based upon a series of skill lists generated by introspection and expert opinion. The skill lists have not been based upon observed behaviour. As a result, we are unable to ascertain whether the skill lists are based upon the beliefs of the managers and systems analysts or the actual performance requirements of the job.

This statement summarizes well the situation relating to both programmer and analyst profile research. Most studies relied upon the questionnaire method of data collection. Questionnaires were usually directed at managers and sometimes also at staff and consisted of a list of attributes which the recipient had to rank in terms of importance to the job being investigated (this also applied to studies where structured interviews had been conducted). The fact that the recipient was provided with a ready-made selection of attributes, rather than being asked to outline a profile from scratch, meant that the range and content of responses were fundamentally predetermined. It has been suggested that management employing IT staff assign relatively little importance to training and personal development (Cheney, 1988; McLean et al., 1991). The preferential targeting of management groups with questionnaires may also, therefore, have had a significant impact on the outcome of such studies. For example, Goldstein (1988) noticed during a pilot study that 'ability to train others' was removed from a skills list on the basis of feedback from supervisors.

The few studies that have adopted a different methodology have produced results that do not conform to the stereotypes. Although McCubbrey and Scudder (1988) gave respondents the opportunity to add their own ideas to questionnaires, they basically followed the methods of others. Vitalari's (1985) work, however, was unusual in that it used the target population as the starting point, i.e. it was based on discussions with managers and technical staff. It was, therefore, particularly interesting that his findings contrasted with almost all the other studies, with the highest number of references concerning functional requirements of computer systems rather than specific behavioural skills. Another collaborative approach was adopted by Arvey and Hoyle (1974). One of the main strengths of their methodology was that it was based on a series of workshops at which individuals worked together to produce agreed outlines of job dimensions. Their research showed that whilst it was possible to assess the relative importance of job characteristics (based on the agreed understanding of what the jobs required), it was not possible to make assertions concerning issues of job performance and even less sensible to make generalizations across groups of workers. Job performance varies radically with each individual according to where they are located in the organization, a point since reinforced by others (e.g. Ferratt and Starke, 1989). Thus, focusing on particular personality traits would be unhelpful in understanding their overall effectiveness.

In some respects it seems that little advance has been made in analyst and programmer research since Arvey and Hoyle's (1974) work. Twelve years later, in Empirical Studies of Programmers, Curtis (1986, p. 257) identified the danger that

...empirical research will continue to focus on demonstrating already established cognitive phenomena and on refining research methodology. This focus will produce elegant research that misses important questions.

The danger he refers to is compounded by the fact that the methodologies do not appear to have received much attention either and instead the focus has been on reproducing the cognitive phenomena. The outcome is even more likely, therefore, to be as he indicated: a form of self-referential confirmation and not an exploration of new terrain.

In summary, the established research shared fundamental similarities in approach and in the attributes which were submitted to management for comment, some actually basing their skill lists on previous surveys. Thus, any methodological weaknesses were retained and compounded over a temporal span of 20 years. No wonder, as Vitalari (1985) had realized, their outcomes were so similar. The process had become a self-fulfilling prophecy. By failing to mount a critical self-analysis of the research methodology, each author had effectively validated their work on an historical basis.

## Conclusions and recommendations

The case study suggested that certain expectations of teams consisting of analysts and programmers were uncritically fed into and reproduced by the cultural set-up. Ineffective attempts to provide a technical career path reinforced these values. The effect was to consign technical experts, especially programmers, to an isolated route, with little room for flexibility or self-development. The analyst and programmer stereotypes served the same function within Colour Co as in the published literature: a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Management are urged to recognize that successful systems development is dependent upon both behavioural and technical skills (Green, 1989, p. 128). Indeed, a whole range of skills is necessary to form an effective team. Despite this, the fieldwork and the literature indicate that stereotypic profiles of analysts and programmers lead to uneven and pre-determined human resource development. In this context, organizations cannot expect to get the best from their technical staff. This situation calls for careful consideration, especially in the UK where a poor training reputation has been acquired (Leadbeater, 1991, p. 10). Recognition of the technical-management divide has given rise to the creation of bridging roles, albeit with very limited success (Cane, 1990, p. 14). This paper argues that hybrid roles are not the issue: change is required at a more fundamental level. For many companies this will mean a radical overhaul of their existing career structures and training practices. It seems that many career development initiatives are aimed at managers rather than at technical line staff (Sweet, 1991). There is good evidence to suggest that this is a result of stereotypes presented in the literature and reproduced within organizations. Now is the time to focus on broader-based and continuous training for technical staff. In order for change to be implemented in a meaningful way, some well-entrenched beliefs and attitudes concerning technical versus managerial staff and analysts versus programmers will have to be revised.

## Biographical notes

Carole Brooke is a lecturer in Information Technology at Durham University Business School, UK. Her background is interdisciplinary including a PhD in IT and Business Studies from City University Business School and an MA (Archaeology/Anthropology) from Cambridge. She has ten years' commercial experience spanning insurance, R & D and recruitment consultancy. Recent published work includes IT case studies (concerning women, HRM and TQM) and papers on the relationship between archaeology and the management discipline. Her current research interests include development of IT staff, women in management, philosophy of TQM, qualitative research methodologies and post-modern organizations. She has reviewed books for New Technology, Work and Employment and for Long Range Planning.
