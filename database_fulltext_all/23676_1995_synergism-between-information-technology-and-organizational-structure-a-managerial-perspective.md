# Synergism between information technology and organizational structure: a managerial perspective

ANITA LEE

University of Kentucky, Lexington, USA

CHUN HUNG CHENG

The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

GURMEET S. CHADHA

Environmental Chemical Corporation, Pel Norte, USA

This paper presents a hybrid organizational structure chosen by a corporation as a means to combine the benefits of centralization and decentralization. The hybrid structure is the result of a restructuring effort by a coal company in Kentucky under the directive of its parent corporation to ensure maximum resource utilization within a more streamlined organizational structure. Through an observation of the restructuring process, we are able to demonstrate how and why it is important for top-level managers to understand and make the best use of the intricate synergism between information technology and organizational structure. The strategic implications of a hybrid structure and the roles of information technology in organizational changes are explored. We argue that a hybrid structure is a viable and strategically valuable structural alternative, especially for highly diversified conglomerates that grow through mergers and acquisitions of other companies.

## Introduction

Senior managers are confronted with more challenges than ever before in managing organizations in today's highly competitive and rapidly changing information society where vast amounts of information are easily accessible and virtually unlimited sources of computing power are readily available. With the recognition that information is a powerful resource, senior managers need to use and manage information effectively in order to reduce uncertainty in the environment, to continue the well-being of the organization and to stay competitive in business. Information technology (IT), a tool designed to collect, store, process, disseminate and use information, becomes an instrument for managers seeking organizational survival and prosperity.

Successful applications of IT for strategic purposes have been reported (Clemons and Row, 1988; Leonard-Barton and Sviokla, 1988; Wiseman, 1988; Hopper, 1990). Numerous models and frameworks have been proposed to help understand where, when and how IT will impact strategically on a firm. An excellent review on this subject can be found in Ives and Learmonth (1984). As technological advancement continues, computers of tomorrow will not just be faster, smaller, easier to use and more intelligent. They will be able to store and process data, texts, voices, videos and images, as well as graphics.

All these technological developments will provide managers with new ways to compete.

Equally important in facilitating the achievement of strategic goals is an organization's formal structure, which is the arrangement of organization subunits and the accompanying division of labour and hierarchy of authority relations (Hall, 1982). Two forms of organizational structure are prevalent, as described by Francis and Wilman (1980):

At one extreme there will be the possibility of very close monitoring of all individual operations and operating a central system of co-ordination to control the entire network of activities throughout the company. At the other extreme the availability of a great deal of information and the ability to handle it at each location could allow very considerable decentralization of decision making.

The question of what direction organizational structure should take has been a topic of debate since 1958. In this paper, we present a hybrid structure chosen by a corporation as a means to reap the best of both worlds of centralization and decentralization. The hybrid structure is the result of a restructuring effort by a coal company in Kentucky under a directive from its parent corporation in California. Through an observation of the restructuring process, we are able to demonstrate how and why it is important for senior managers to understand and make the best use of the intricate synergism between IT and organizational structure. We urge senior managers to comprehend the strategic implications of different organizational structures and the role of IT in organizational changes. In fact, our paper is written precisely to address these needs.

## Strategy, IT and organizational structure

The strategic importance of IT to an organization is apparent from the success of many strategic information systems. Best known examples are Citybanks' ATM, a network of automatic teller machines (Wiseman, 1988), American Airline's SABRE, a computerized airline reservation system (Copeland and McKenney, 1988), McKeeson's ECONOMOST, a direct-order entry system for retail pharmacies (Clemons and Row, 1988) and American Express's AUTHORIZER, a computerized credit agent (Leonard-Barton and Sviokla, 1988). Telecommunication and database technologies are common among all these systems. Specific hardware such as barcode readers and hand-held terminals are used in ECONOMOST. Expert system technology is employed in AUTHORIZER. Numerous ways to apply IT as a 'competitive weapon' have been suggested (Ives and Learmonth, 1984; McFarlan, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Cole, 1985; Applegate et al., 1988).

The strategic role of organizational structure is equally significant. Chandler (1962) was the first to identify the close connection between the strategy of an organization and its structure. Since then, a large volume of literature has been written on the impact of organizational structure on strategy. Readers can refer to Fredrickson (1986) for a review on this subject. In general, a centralized organizational structure capitalizes on efficiency, improved coordination, economy of scale, clear responsibility, standardization and reduced managerial overhead. On the other hand, a decentralized organizational structure has such benefits as effectiveness, autonomy, flexibility, responsiveness, specialization and attention to local needs.

However, there is no congenial agreement on the relationship between IT and organizational structure. In fact, the question of why and how IT affects organizational structure has been hotly contested and debated since 1958. Past research has presented many different and some contradictory views on the issue.

Markus and Robey (1988) examined three theoretical models found in the literature to explain the causal relationship of IT and organizational structure. The first model is called the technological imperative, which suggests that technology is a exogenous force that determines organizational structures. The second model is called the organizational imperative, which regards technology as a matter of managerial choice to satisfy an organization's information needs. The third model is called the emergent perspective, which argues that there is no inherent relationship between the two because of the presence of complex social interactions rendering the relationship unpredictable. Later, George and King (1991) extended these three models into four positions. They reconciled the four positions under a common assumption that computerization is a means to bring about the desired ends of management and concluded that 'there is a strong and observable tendency toward the use of information technology to reinforce the decision authority status quo'. Table 1 summarizes the essence and supporting references of these models and positions.

The importance of understanding the relationship between IT and organizational structure is indisputable judging from the volume of literature written on the subject. However, as pointed out by George and King (1991), the relationship is not a simple causal one, meaning that there are no uniformly clear or consistent structural outcomes among organizations using similar technology. Hence, we believe that it is no longer useful to debate the causal relationship between the two. Therefore, the purpose of our paper is not to argue which form of causal relationship between IT and organizational structure should prevail, but to acknowledge the strategic significance of both IT and organizational structure and explore the synergism between them.

## Synergism between IT and organizational structure

The synergism between IT and organizational structure requires the alignment of IT strategy and organizational strategy (Figure 1). The synergism manifests the achievement of strategic advantages that are not attainable through IT or organizational structure alone.

On one hand, advancement of technology nurtures organizational changes. For instance, organizations can have improved centralized control without the expense of decentralized decision making. With the widespread availability of computing power in the form of networked personal computers, top managers have instant access to information about any part of the company at their fingertips. All activities can be tightly observed and individual operations can be closely monitored. Control and decision-making authority can hence reside among a few executives at the top of the organization despite spatial or functional dispersion.

Likewise, organizations can have improved decentralized decision making without the expense of centralized control. This is made possible through desktop computing technology. Managers at all levels can have personal computers at their desk top to directly generate, own and access a great volume of information. They can make individual decisions about their jobs faster and more effectively than before. In this case, decision-making authority can be dispersed between many individuals at the bottom of the organization with centralized coordination. Hence, by applying the appropriate technology, organization can be restructured in ways that were not feasible before.

On the other hand, organizational changes foster the advancement of technology. The most noticeable development in this respect is the evolution of desktop computing from stand-alone devices to terminal emulators linked by a local area network to integral parts of a fully integrated corporate network (Huff et al., 1988). The evolution of microcomputers can be viewed as a 'by-product' of managers' need to introduce state-of-the-art technologies to fit an organization's structure as it changed from centralization to decentralization to recentralization (Kathawala and Lingaraj, 1990).

By exploiting the synergism between IT and organizational structure, senior managers can better manage their own information assets and gain access to other sources of shared data and resources. One such undertaking is observed in the restructuring of a coal company in Kentucky under a directive from its parent corporation in California.

## A case study

The company we studied is one of the major coal companies in the United States and a leading coal exporter. Its annual revenues are near \$750 million and it has more than 400 employees. It is a holding company of a multibusiness corporation whose headquarters is in Los Angeles, California. The parent corporation has grown substantially through the acquisition of other companies, resulting in a highly diversified conglomerate concentrating on the petroleum, oil, coal and chemical businesses. A geographical distribution of the corporation and its major subsidiaries is shown in Figure 2.

Buchanan and Linowes (1980) constructed a framework to analyse the influence of corporate structures and product diversification on the choice of centralization or decentralization of information resources. According to this framework, as shown in Figure 3, this corporation will be positioned in the upper right corner, suggesting that decentralized structure is a good fit. This is indeed the corporate structure in which each holding company has its own data centre, hardware, software and application systems.

In August 1990, this corporation launched an extensive 5-year organizational change plan under the advice of an outside consultant. The objective is to ensure maximum resource use within a more streamlined organizational structure. A new centralized data centre was established in Tulsa, Oklahoma to consolidate all databases and supporting software as well as to convert all application systems. A number of new positions were created: a technical services manager to serve as a technical liaison between the centralized data centre and individual holding companies, a database administrator to enhance the coordination between application database support and the application developer, a data centre operations manager to provide network, printing and processing services. Positions such as system engineer and manager of computing services were eliminated.

The consolidation and decentralization effort concentrates only on information resources that are of strategic value. Individual holding companies still have independent resources and freedom to make decisions regarding daily operations at the local level. They function as self-managing units under amiable rules and procedures described from the top. Each holding company has a management information systems department to develop all end-user applications and to provide daily managerial decision support.

The move towards a hybrid organizational structure described above is made possible by advancements in IT. Technological trends that support a centralized structure include the increasing storage and power of hardware; improving software and database capabilities and the advent of personal computer-based networks. The storage and computational requirement of a centralized data centre is easily met by a main frame. Off-the-shelf software tools are available to convert application systems to run on a common platform without reprogramming. Data conversion features presented in database management systems allow the consolidation of different databases. Sophisticated computer networks can be set up to provide direct links from computer to computer. Top managers can observe and control all aspects of company operations whenever and wherever desired.

Technological factors that enable decentralization include advances in telecommunications, diffusion of personal computers and improvements in end-user computer literacy. Decreasing telecommunication costs and fibre optic technology provide reliable and cost-efficient networks to support distributed processing. The increasing power to price ratio of personal computers provides managers at all levels with desktop computational power to satisfy specific end-user applicational needs. The improving computer literacy among end-users leads them to become more involved in making better use of IT to deliver their own services. Frequently these end-users demand increased direct access to their own data and application systems.

The hybrid organizational structure is strategically important to this corporation. The corporation gains cost advantage by eliminating the site-licensing fees for software and avoiding duplicated processing facilities and efforts. A clear career path in a centralized data centre enables the corporation to attract better information professionals, which in turn lowers the overhead of operating the centre. A centralized data centre enables senior managers to control and coordinate activities not by direct supervision, but by centrally designed guidelines and standards. A centralized data centre allows the corporation to take advantage of volume discounts on procurement. It also enhances the negotiating power of the corporation. Integrated data and application systems enable the sharing of company-wide data, whereby consistent company and divisional strategies can be enforced.

On the other hand, the decentralized element of the hybrid structure enables individual holding companies to function autonomously. They can be more responsive and adaptive to demands from customers as well as to the corporate headquarters. End-users are empowered with tools to access all the information they require at the microlevel. By allowing its holding companies to make operational decisions, the corporation has eliminated the time lag associated with centralized decision making. The corporation under this study surely has the best of both worlds of centralization and decentralization in its hand.

As noted in its 1993 annual report, the restructuring has resulted in a streamlined and sharply focused company. Its financial and operational flexibility are strengthened. In just 1 year after its restructuring, the company reportedly has improved its net income and cash flow by \$350 million per year and \$800 million per year, respectively.

## Managerial implications

The restructuring experience of the above corporation clearly demonstrates the viability of combining centralization and decentralization into one organizational structure, thereby attaining the efficiency of centralized control without losing the flexibility and focus of decentralization. Organizations of today no longer need to swing back and forth between centralization and decentralization, a cyclical pattern that occurs every 5–8 years as observed by Davis (1980). A hybrid organizational structure between the two extremes can be a better alternative.

A hybrid structure is especially attractive to organizations that are diversified conglomerates. These organizations are inherently complex, composed of many, some unrelated, business units with multiple geographical locations. Such a high level of complexity makes it difficult to coordinate and control (Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967). In order to allow for flexibility and responsiveness, a decentralized structure is perceived as a natural fit, but too often at the expense of poor coordination, lack of standardization and increased costs. As demonstrated in our paper, a hybrid structure will be a better choice.

The move towards a hybrid structure is made possible because of the synergistic relationship between IT and organizational structure. In other words, such a hybrid structure cannot be implemented without the advancements of IT. On the other hand, future advances in IT are shaped by the organizational structure developed to support its use. The implication is that senior managers must be creative in bringing together the right forms of IT in an organization structured strategically around clearly stated goals.

The process of restructuring was not without resistance, in particular, from the information managers of the holding companies. Many of them were worried about the change because they feared that the move towards centralization from its current decentralized structure would eventually take over all their information management functions. The change threatened their sense of full information control because the new arrangement requires them to share their information resources with other units and to cooperate or even integrate with one another in information resource management, support and advice. They saw the new arrangement as losing control over resources that might have an impact on their bottom-line results although they admitted that it might reduce their units' information resource expenditure.

To what extent then should the holding companies' decisions on information resource strategies and polices and IT operation and management be centralized? The primary objective of the restructuring is to promote centralized strategic control while allowing the holding companies flexibility in deciding operational issues. It is an attempt to balance a centralized and decentralized organization and to reap benefits of both. It is not intended to strip the holding companies of their power. With a centralized strategic management, the corporation's information resource strategies can be formulated at the top and be consistent with its overall corporate mission. Managers at the holding companies can retain control over their local organization but operate with an understanding of the overall corporate objective. This message has to penetrate throughout the whole organization. After all, a change in organizational structure can only be made possible with the cooperation and support from all its constituents.

## Conclusion

We have reported a hybrid structure which is a combination of centralization of strategic control and decentralization of operational decision making. Top managers no longer have to settle for a centralized or decentralized structure. The hybrid structure has the potential to deliver the best of both worlds of centralization and decentralization. However, the strategical success is contingent on the synergistic deployment of IT within a matching organizational structure.

Additional research is needed to explore other structural options that fall between centralization and decentralization to widen the structural spectrum for future organization. The strategic as well as social implications of these options have to be examined. A clear evaluation scheme should be developed in order to help top managers assess different structural alternatives.
