Journal of Information Technology (1996) 11, 373–378

# The mechanics of managing IT risk $^{*}$

ROBERT N. CHARETTE

ITABHI Corporation, PO Box 1929, Springfield, Virginia 22151, USA

The application of formal risk management to IT projects is rapidly growing in popularity. Numerous books and articles on the subject of IT-oriented risk management have appeared, where a decade ago there were virtually none. In this paper, a short tutorial on the general processes and activities involved in performing IT risk management is presented. Some of the practical issues that require attention when putting IT risk management into practice are also discussed. Effectively applied, risk management is a powerful tool for making an IT project proactive. However, poorly applied, risk management can just as easily lead an IT project into ruin.

## The IT risk management process

When speaking of the systematic process or mechanics involved in managing IT risk (and its cousin opportunity), it should be clear that two distinct processes are being implied. The first is what I term as the management of risk process, while the second I call the risk management process. Although they are closely related, and in practice often are used interchangeably (especially in their description in the literature), they really do convey two distinct ideas.

By the term management of risk, I mean the general process used for assessing and managing the spectrum of risks that span an organization's entire business operations such as finance, marketing, operations, etc. in other words, the management of risk implies a systemic, holistic, and/or multi-disciplinary approach to risk and its management, one which weaves elements of cost management, project management, security management, quality management, etc., into a unified whole. The term risk management, on the other hand, is concerned with a phased, systematic approach (possibly implemented through a unique tool or technique) to the analysis and control of the risks occurring within a specific context.

To contrast the two terms, a software project manager may need to take a proactive management of risk approach to his or her project, and in so doing, may apply one or more specific risk management processes to help identify, estimate, evaluate, plan for, control, and monitor the different types of risks present. The use of decision matrices, Monte Carlo simulation or fault tree analysis represent specific risk management processes, but each is only a small element in an organization's overall management of risk approach. Thus, the management of risk process focuses on making the critical judgement calls, i.e., the ‘go/no-go’ decisions, while risk management process focuses on continuously mitigating the risks that exist once the ‘go’ decision has been made.

## Risk management process: overview

The general process involved in risk management follows a classical closely-coupled, two-phase decompositional approach. The first phase concentrates on activities involved in the assessment of the risks, whereas the second involves the management planning and control activities that implement organizational actions to mitigate or eliminate the risks discovered. Taken together, they form an integrated and seamless process as shown in Figure 1. This two-phase, interlocked process is a common trait shared by all of the risk management processes currently in existence, although the exact terminology and steps involved to describe the process may differ from that used here.

The risk management process is inextricably linked with our process of making decisions. The reason for this is when we perform a risk analysis, our typical input is going to be an existing plan of action of some sort, or a desire to formulate such a plan. Either way, it represents something we want to do to change the present situation, for instance create something new, improve the current situation, accomplish something different, etc. Since making changes involves making choices, a plan is therefore nothing more than a postulated set of decisions that occur over time. Through the analysis process, we aim to discover what may ‘go wrong’ (i.e., what are the potential problems?) with that plan at different points in time.

Risks are generated by one or more of three events: (1) actions we have or plan to take; (2) actions others have or plan to take, and; (3) external events outside anyone's control. A very common risk often generated by the actions we take in conjunction with others, is an over-ambitious schedule. For example, our plan calls for a software product to be completed in six months time, when experience shows that it has never been done in less than 12 months. This decision may be caused by a competitor announcing that a similar product will be released in nine months time, which in turn encourages us to try and beat our competitor to market.

In general, risk can be defined as (Rowe, 1988: p.24): 'The potential for the realization of unwanted, negative consequences of an event.' From this definition, we can see that risks are distinguished by two primary characteristics: their likelihood of occurrence and their potential negative consequences. Without one or the other of these present, then there is no risk. For example, if the likelihood is certain, then the event isn't a risk, but a problem (i.e., a gap between what was expected, and what exists). And, if the consequences are all positive, then who cares? Which leads us back to the notion that the risk management process is inextricably linked with our process of making decisions, and primarily exists to help us make better decisions. ## Risk management process: the analysis phase

The risk analysis process itself is typically made up of at least three overlapping activities consisting of:

(1) risk identification;

(2) risk estimation, and;

(3) risk evaluation.

## Identification activities

Risk identification could easily be called risk discovery. Its goal is to seek out and identify any and all risks that may exist. A crucial part of this discovery process is to determine what really are the risks, and what are really the opportunities.

In the identification step, one proactively seeks out risks and their sources. The risk discovery process may use a ‘risk questionnaire’, a specialized tool, a combination of tool and experience, or, as is often the case in developing unprecedented software systems, experience alone. The identification activities are critical, for any risks that are not discovered are risks tacitly accepted. This is also true for all assumptions made. Examining assumptions, and by implication, organizational expectations, are effective means for uncovering risks that are not obvious.

The risk identification activities are usually broad in scope, involving top-down as well as bottom-up search techniques. Quality management-related techniques, such as root cause analysis, quality function deployment matrices, etc., can also be used to help identify areas of risk.

## Estimation activities

After the risks are identified, their likelihood, consequences, and timing are estimated. This usually involves creating a profile of the risk, as most risks change over time and circumstance. A consistent scale, qualitative or quantitative in nature, is used in the creation of this profile.

Relationships between, across and among risks are also investigated in the estimation activity. How risks relate greatly influence their profile, i.e., if they are independent of one another, dependent, coupled, etc.

For example, the decision to use immature hardware or software typically poses a greater risk of unreliable system operations than mature hardware or software. Now, suppose the likelihood of unreliable systems operation is 0.7 when caused by immature software, and 0.5 by immature hardware. The overall likelihood of unreliable systems operations when both immature hardware and software are present if they are independent of one another is 0.35, while if they are dependent upon one another occurring it may range from as low as 0.2 to as high as 0.5. Further, if they couple, then lowering one may in fact raise the other.

The estimation activities also try to determine the uncertainty associated with the estimates of likelihood, consequences and timing, and to see if it is worth the effort to improve them. A key element to the accuracy (and precision) of the estimates will be whether there exists historical data to draw upon – the quality of the whole analysis process depends upon the quality of the data.

The last estimation activity is the initial prioritization of the risks. Prioritization criteria vary, but usually the expected value of the risk is used. This is the product of the likelihood and consequence of risk, e.g., if the likelihood of unreliable systems operations is 0.1 and could cost £500k if it were to occur, then the expected value would be £50k.

Differentiating between ‘important’ and ‘unimportant’ risks is critical, since an organization’s resources are finite, and some judgement has to be made on where resources will eventually be spent on the evaluation and management of the risks identified.

## Evaluation activities

With the risks identified and estimated to their likelihood, impacts, and timing, and relationships, one can start evaluating the risks. This evaluation process means evaluating the risks against a referent or level of acceptability – i.e., is the risk acceptable?

Acceptability implies that the risk, if it should occur, will not change the current direction of the plans. If, on the other hand, it appears that it may, we need to generate alternatives. These alternatives might be to change the likelihood of the risks occurrence, or the potential impact of the risk (before or after the risk occurs) or both.

These alternatives themselves need to be analysed and evaluated as to whether they are acceptable, which is why Figure 2 shows the process linking back onto itself in an iterative fashion. The process of analysing each alternative may mean the discovery of new risks, which may in turn mean the generation of new alternatives, etc. Different techniques are available to help evaluate the risks and alternative courses of action. These include the use of decision trees and tables, linear programming, simulations, models, etc., most of which have commercially available tools to support them.

The critical element in the evaluation process is the level of acceptability set. Set too low, the organization may become immobilized by fear. Set too high, and undue risk may be taken on.

The final evaluation activity is to prioritize the risks (and the alternative courses of action that might be taken) in order that decisions can be made on whether to accept the risks, or to do something about them.

## Risk management process: the action implementation phase

After the risks have been evaluated, the second major phase of the process begins, namely their management. Management consists of at least four closely coupled activities:

(1) planning;

(2) resourcing;

(3) controlling, and;

(4) monitoring.

As with the risk analysis phase activities, these phases are not necessarily purely sequential in nature.

## Planning activities

After the analysis is as complete as is possible given the time and circumstance, and the risks have been placed into a prioritized order of importance, a decision maker is faced with a number of choices on how to deal with, i.e., avert, the risks identified.

The decision maker can either:

(1) assume the risks posed, and do nothing, preferring to deal with the impacts if the risks do occur at that time;

(2) avoid the risks altogether by attempting to eliminate the risks in some fashion;

(3) reduce the risks' likelihood and/or potential consequences;

(4) assume the risks, but plan ahead to mitigate their consequences;

(5) investigate the situation further before any decision is taken, or;

(6) stop the effort from proceeding further.

In practice, a combination of these alternatives is taken, with some risks being assumed, others being eliminated, while still others are only mitigated. Whichever actions are decided upon, they must be planned for.

It is important that when thinking of risk, to think of the risk not only associated with alternative courses of action, but with the risks that exist after the alternative has been chosen. Decisions rarely, if ever, exist in a vacuum, and thus a decision maker must be concerned with both the spectrum and continuum of choices available.

An element of the planning process is, of course, to ensure that whatever actions that are proposed do not make the situation worse by creating unintended consequences. Recall that in situations where risks couple, this is a distinct possibility. Further, one has to be careful that fundamental assumptions are not violated in the planning process, and that whatever actions are to be taken are in harmony with other organizational actions as well.

‘Risk management action plans’ are typically formed as a result of the planning activities to carry out the risk aversion strategies decided upon. Risk management action plans summarize the risk analysis performed thus far, as well as recommend courses of action. The recommendations made within the action plan are commonly incorporated into a revised overall project or program plan.

## Resourcing activities

As part of the planning process, the allocation of resources occurs. This activity is critical to the success of the proposed aversion strategies. Resources are allocated to the highest priorities first, and then re-allocated based upon the successful (or not) execution of the risk management action plan.

The elimination of risk is the optimal choice, of course, in practice, this is not often possible or practical due to the scarcity of resources available for risk aversion. Resourcing activities will be involved in deciding the trade-offs between how much risk aversion is required, and how much risk can be accepted.

Responsibility for risk aversion actions, and the authority to take action, is also determined during the resourcing activity. Each risk, and its aversion approach, is assigned to a responsible party to ensure that it does not turn into a problem by neglect. Once the risk management action plan is resourced, and approved, it can be placed into execution.

## Controlling activities

Controlling activities focus on ensuring that the risk management action plan actually accomplishes its objectives. Objectives that are not being met are examined as to the reasons why, and actions to rectify the situation are taken. Further risk analysis might be necessary if the situation is deemed sufficiently critical.

Communication is a vital aspect of control activities. The activities defined within the risk management action plan need to be co-ordinated with other organizational activities, especially if conflicts arise, as will always be the case.

## Monitoring activities

Affiliated with the control activities are the monitoring activities. Risks are tracked to determine if the aversion approaches are working, if they need to be changed, or initiated due to changing circumstances. Appropriate metrics are required to be defined as part of this activity.

A major role of monitoring is to collect information on risks, especially the effectiveness of the aversion measures taken (or conversely, not taken). This information provides the basis for lessons learned that can be drawn upon in the future, especially in making the identification and estimation of risks more robust.

## Not as easy as it seems

The risk management process described above, of course, is an abbreviated version of the full process. However, you should be aware that although the process appears straightforward, almost cookbook in nature, it is not nearly as easy as it appears in practice. Each choice you make as an analyst can highly influence the quality of the eventual output.

For instance, the analysis process assumes that the underlying decision processes used, say in determining a plan, are rational. However, this is almost never the case – meaning there exists a gap between our process and reality. Perception, biases, politics, and many other things come into play that make the true decision process seemingly irrational – or at least rational in a strange sort of way.

Then there is the decision as to which analysis approach to use. A top-down approach will take a deductive perspective of the situation, uncovering risks usually from a manager's perspective. A bottom-up approach takes an inductive perspective, uncovering risks usually seen from a worker's perspective. Both may use the same underlying risk management process, but the perspective, importance, and the actions required to avert the risks may vary tremendously.

Next, you have a choice concerning how much examination is enough? The top-down approach often sees the forest but not the trees, while the bottom-up sees the trees, but not the forest. Another way to view it is that top-down risk analyses tend to concentrate on the risks involved in how resources are allocated (i.e., capitalization issues), while bottom-up on how those resources are being utilized (i.e., performance issues).

And how are you going to get co-operation from everyone involved, who view you alternately or simultaneously as a management spy, a general pain, one who is going to blame them for any future failures, selling common sense, and so forth? No co-operation means no risks identified. It's not the risks that you see that hurt you, but the ones you didn't see but should have that will cripple you.

And, oh yes, there is the slight issue of getting everyone to understand that just because you have identified a risk and plan to address it, doesn't mean you can now ignore it.

This leads on to the age old debate as to what qualifies as a risk, and what qualifies as a problem? Is a technical design issue, say one that is expected, a risk or a problem? And does it matter? The debate on risk vs. problem is also usually tied to what is decided to be an acceptable level of risk. Few organizations spell these out in detail – especially the stop criteria for a project. And even if spelled out, these may be misinterpreted or forgotten.

The O-rings on the NASA Space Shuttle is a good case in point. Before the Challenger disaster, engineers had found an O-ring that had burned through approximately 1/3 its depth. This fact was seen as neither a risk or a problem, but instead an indication of a safety margin of factor three. The specifications, however, called for no burn-through to occur at all.

Then there is the issue of estimation error. How does one go about putting likelihood and consequences on risks that are unprecedented? And what are the implications of how risks combine? For example, should you choose to view individual risks as independent (and make the mathematics easy), or dependent instead? Choose the incorrect approach, and the project may fail because the risks are underestimated, or fail, but this time because the project was seen as too risky, and it wasn't approved.

Analysis tools also can cause all sorts of trouble as well. They can seduce you into thinking that you are getting accurate numbers when in fact you are generating very precise, but wrong ones. They are also good at helping you analyse situations before you fully understand them, give you confidence where it isn't deserved, and make you think you are modelling reality, when in fact you are modelling a model of reality.

And, by the way, how do you choose to combine dissimilar risks – the ones that pose apple and orange issues? In my experience, individual software projects are unique, not so much in the individual types of risk that occur (these re-occur with numbing regularity), but in the number, severity, and combination in which they occur.

And then you have to choose what to do based upon your choice of risk prioritization. Mess that up, and see how far your career goes.

And finally, do not ever forget you are in a competitive situation, not a passive one. Others are constantly trying to create risks for your organization to overcome, just as you are for your competition. This makes the situation fluid, and time critical. Take too long to perform the analysis, and the choices for changing the situation may be passed by.

## First, do no harm

These, and all sorts of other analysis choices, make the risk management process far less cookbookish than it seems on the surface, and separates the good analysts and analyses from the mediocre and dangerous. For the unwary, risk management is little more than a nuclear hand-grenade waiting to blow up the project.

The following are a few suggestions to help you reduce the blast effects:

Suggestion No. 1 All projects of any size have some level of risk attached to them. The only time they don't is when they are completed, or cancelled. You need to get this fact across to everyone as soon as possible, if you want to make any progress.

Suggestion No. 2 Concentrate on capabilities that exist, not on people's good intentions. People will assume that because they have a plan in mind to deal with a risk, it no longer exists. This belief leads to big trouble, and the rational behind the saying, 'The road to ruin is paved with good intentions.' Go back and review Suggestion No. 1 as to why.

Suggestion No. 3 Take a top-down and bottom-up approach to the analysis – this is called a ‘joint analysis’. You are likely to miss fewer risks, will be able to support multiple perspectives (e.g., customer, suppliers, manager, worker, etc.), multiple contexts (e.g., financial, technical, marketing, etc.) and get a better understanding of their root causes, which are likely to be either a lack of time, lack of control, and/or a lack of information.

Suggestion No. 4 Use a recursive risk management process. This allows you to support the joint approach described in Suggestion No. 3.

Suggestion No. 5 Use automated tools sparingly. People tend to substitute tools for analysis, especially the thinking part. Tools can help answer your questions, but only after you have figured out what the question is. Double your blast effect for not taking this hint.

Suggestion No. 6 Call your risk management process opportunity management instead. It sounds more positive, and keeps it from looking like blame analysis.

Suggestion No. 7 Communicate, communicate, communicate. People appear sceptical of risk management – they are not. They are scared to death of it. You need to constantly inform people what you are doing, and why you are doing it. Give them positive feedback, as well as the bad news. Remember, you are there to help.

Suggestion No. 8 Develop a thick skin. No matter what you do, you will be wrong in someone's eyes. So document your assumptions, and how you reached your conclusions.

Finally, remember to follow the first rule of medicine: do no harm. If it looks like risk management will cause more difficulties than it solves, do not use it.

## Summary

In this paper, I have presented a short tutorial on the processes and activities involved in performing risk management, as well as some of the issues that you need to be aware of before putting risk management into practice. Effectively applied, risk management is a powerful tool to making an organization proactive, notably in the pursuit of opportunities. Poorly applied, risk management can be more dangerous than beneficial. Your choices will determine which it will be.
