# Journal of Information Technology (1996) 11, 321–331 Risky ideas in the risk society ENID MUMFORD Manchester Business School, UK.

This paper describes a new kind of risk with which managers are now confronted. This is the risk that arises from their rapid response to new, often untested, ideas that are offered to the management community and eagerly accepted by it.

I am aware that humanity

Is approaching a crisis

In which its residual ignorance, shortsightedness

And circumstance-biased viewpoints

May dominate

Thus carrying humanity

Beyond the point of no return.

R. Buckminster Fuller

## What are regarded as risks?

Ulrich Beck, the German sociologist, tells us that we are now in the ‘risk society’. (Beck, 1992). Our problem is no longer how to distribute fairly the good aspects of society but how to distribute the bad. We have become focused on ‘problems’. These affect everyone and there is no escape. Yet we do not fully understand what is happening and, according to Beck, we are using blind social processes to address an unknown and unknowable future. (Lash et al., 1996) We not only do not understand, we cannot control much of the change that is taking place in the world today. Policy makers, politicians, managers and other influential groups who have responsibility and make choices all have a desperate need for knowledge to enable them to handle new economic, organizational, technical and social risks. There are also competing concepts of what is risky. Different groups, with different kinds of knowledge, focus on social, genetic, ecological and other kinds of risk.

Risks are becoming greater because the rate of technical innovation and industrial development is still accelerating throughout most of the world. (Giddens, 1990). But our perception of where the most serious, high consequence, risks are located changes as society moves on. In the 1960s Marshall McLuhan warned us that in the mechanical age many actions could be taken without too much concern as slow movement ensured that reactions were delayed for considerable periods of time. But in the new electronic age, action and reaction occur at almost the same time (McLuhan, 1964). Yet Darwinian theory suggests that if individuals do not react quickly to dangers and opportunities they may not survive (Goffman, 1972).

In the 1970s a major risk area was thought to be population growth. The rapid growth of cities was seen as producing an overload of environmental stress caused by the numbers of people living together and their proximity to each other (NAS, 1971). In the 1980s, after Chernobyl, the nuclear threat loomed large and was regarded by many non-scientists as uncontrollable, although the scientists took a different position, suggesting that without nuclear power alternative sources of energy would soon be used up (Wynne, 1992). In the 1990s this fear of environmental disaster has spread to many other issues such as global warming and the irreparable damage of the ecostructure. The problem here, as the psychologist R.D. Laing has pointed out, is that our world is essentially the world of our perceptions. We see some risks but not others (Laing, 1969).

Not only have our perceptions of what is a serious risk altered over the years, there has been an explosive growth in our ability to interfere on a large scale with the environmental processes (McHale, 1973). Unfortunately, this has not been accompanied by a parallel growth in our knowledge of how to manage change effectively, yet this knowledge may be essential for our future survival. Jay Forester has argued that unless we can make a transition from growth to equilibrium we may be overwhelmed by a social and economic system which we have created but cannot control (Forester, 1972). There are now many factors which could interact together to throw us into a downward spiral. Anthony Giddens has identified four of the most threatening of these as the growth of totalitarian power, the collapse of economic growth mechanisms, nuclear conflict or large scale warfare and ecological decay or disaster (Giddens, 1990). He argues that globalization leads to a process of uneven development that fragments as it coordinates and continually introduces new forms of world interdependencies. The new technologies of communication have greatly facilitated this process.

## Managerial risk in today's environment

Managers are not exempt from these new kinds of risk although they may be more concerned with their immediate environments than with global problems. Nevertheless they are constantly being told that they are now moving into global markets and must be prepared to compete with companies in many different countries. They are also told that to compete successfully they must improve their products and reduce their costs (Hammer and Champey, 1993). This is leading them to believe that to survive in the new business environment they must review and drastically change many of their established policies and practices. In their search for competitive improvement many of them then become willing receptors of the latest management fad that promises success. These are frequently a product of academics in business schools who have a talent for communicating, although not always a great deal of practical experience in running a company.

Managers are well used to handling certain kinds of risk, and it is financial risk with which they are usually most familiar. They understand the concepts of operational risk, product innovation risk and regulatory risk and they have tools, concepts and strategies for managing these kinds of risk. (Beaver and Parker, 1995). Operational risk management covers technology, product design, the transaction and settlement process, human error and the legal and regulatory environments. Most of these risks are measurable and there is a belief that you cannot manage risk unless you can measure it.

Unfortunately these managers have now been presented with risks which are new, difficult and poorly understood. Some of these risks are associated with surviving in the new global market. They are told by ‘experts’ that there are strategies, tools and techniques that will enable them to do this and that if they do not use these their company is likely to go out of business. They are therefore provided with a problem, a solution and a threat of disaster if the solution is not used. This is an entirely new kind of risk as it is a risk associated with an idea, not a practical situation such as the breakdown of a plant. This kind of risk is less about solving a problem than in choosing an appropriate problem solving method with which to address it (Kuhn, 1970). To make matters worse there will be little available evidence on what kind of approach is likely to succeed and what is likely to fail. This then, is a very new and unfamiliar kind of risk for managers and it is the one we shall discuss in this paper.

## Business process re-engineering as an example of a new form of management risk

The long term risk issue selected for examination is the belief that industry must change to survive and meet the threat of a new global market. It was suggested by influential consultants and academics in the United States that this threat could be met by introducing a technique called business process re-engineering. It was argued that this risk management technique was so powerful that it could enable a company to increase efficiency, reduce costs and make more effective use of new technology. It was also claimed that companies that did not introduce business process re-engineering might not survive (Hammer and Champy, 1993). Re-engineering is a good example of an innovative idea in today's world because it incorporates both work restructuring and advanced information technology. One of the reasons for its development was the belief that many companies were not making good use of available technology. Hammer and Champy (1993) believed that process reorganization could provide new opportunities for this to take place.

It must be emphasized here that information technology experts can no longer avoid bringing organizational factors into their design responsibilities. Most complex new systems will have organizational implications. Similarly most major organizational change will encompass advanced information technology.

This then is the story of a long term risk management process that generated new short term risks. It began with over optimistic expectations, experienced a change of goals and eventually was abandoned by many disappointed users. In this paper we will examine the reasons for its rapid acceptance and spread and the circumstances, attitudes and techniques that assisted this. This paper will describe the problems and risks that were incurred at different stages of the change process and it will try to identify the reasons for this move from rapid success to eventual failure. The paper will focus on the American experience with business process re-engineering.

Risky ideas in the risk society

The documents used to trace its rise and fall are articles in management journals and consultant surveys published between 1990 and 1996. It is worth pointing out to academics who disparage these references that when current practice is being investigated they are the only ones available. Given the time lag between the submission and publication of papers in learned journals, academic publications will always be out of date.

## How ideas spread

One of the most interesting aspects of business process re-engineering in both the United States and Europe was its rapid and wide scale adoption by senior managers in industry and commerce. It provides a good example of how rapidly ideas can be translated into action in the modern world. This is true whether the medium used is printing or electronic mail. The original message was provided by a group of influential academics and consultants in Boston, Mass. who spread it through books, articles in prestigious management journals such as the Harvard Business Review and public lectures. Propagation through books was particularly effective. Tom Davenport's book Process Innovation came first in 1992, followed in 1993 by Michael Hammer and James Champy's book Re-engineering the Corporation (Davenport, 1992) (Hammer & Champy, 1993). This was in the windows of major book shops throughout Europe and the United States almost as soon as it appeared. Sales were assisted by a quote from Peter Drucker on the front cover. This claimed 'reengineering is new and it has to be done.'

Before describing the progress of business process re-engineering over time let us examine some of the theories of how ideas are transferred from one person and group to another and how this transference leads to action. Unfortunately, we know very little about the kinds of judgements that lead to a new idea being used. We know what people do but not why they do it (Barnes, 1982). Knowledge and contingent behaviour often comes from experience but in this area of new ideas managers have very little experience.

Richard Dawkins, the biologist, sees ideas behaving like genes. They are capable of replicating themselves in order to maintain their own survival (Dawkins, 1989). He argues that ideas, fashions, rumours etc. propagate themselves by leaping from brain to brain via a process which can be called copying or imitation. If scientists or managers hear of a good idea they pass this on, the scientist to his academic associates and students through lectures, discussions and published papers. The manager to his business colleagues and subordinates by arguing, ordering, explaining and cajoling. Dawkins suggests that, like genes, ideas have a capacity to endure but, unlike genes, their survival can be halted by man's capacity for foresight. Because man can simulate the future in his imagination, he has the capability to frustrate or bring to a halt the progress of an idea which threatens security or well being.

Dawkins says that the survival of an idea or a fashion is related to its psychological appeal. This can be either positive or negative. The promise of financial success would attract a manager to use a new technique or approach, but so would the fear of failure if he or she did not do so. Ideas can also propagate on the basis of faith or trust. They are believed because someone of status says that they are true. Behavioural scientists believe that in order to understand why ideas are translated into action we must examine the goals and interests of those taking the action. These, in turn, have to be related to concepts and beliefs and to culture and personality (Barnes, 1982). Goals and interests are fundamental to understanding why a certain decision is taken and it is these that explain institutional dynamics. These have both a history and a future. History and context will often explain why one manager's successful problem solving approach leads another to disaster.

Psychologists interested in communication and persuasion have argued that the rapid spread of powerful ideas and their subsequent application is assisted by persuasive communication and by the arguments used in support of the idea (Hovland et al., 1953). Communications which involve threats are particularly powerful. The theory here is that any disturbing emotion – fear, shame, guilt for example, acts as a drive to trial and error behaviour. The individual becomes highly motivated to try out various responses in the hope of removing the unpleasant emotional state. This suggests that the most effective communications are those which arouse expectations of avoiding or escaping from a particular threat. This is especially true if the communicator goes on to describe how the threat can be avoided. The listener's tension is then reduced and this acts as a reinforcer for the recommended action.

The effectiveness of pervasive communication also depends on who the listeners are and the organization of the arguments in support of the advocated position. Because of their ability to draw valid references, persons of high intelligence tend to be more influenced by communications which rely on logical argument then persons of low intellectual ability. But, because of their superior critical ability, they will also tend to be less influenced by communications that rely on unsupported generalities. Communication credibility is also a critical factor (Goffman, 1969). This requires a speaker who is respected and who uses a language and words which the listeners understand and respond to. Ideas can be distorted by the way in which they are transmitted. Each communicator may pass the idea on in a slightly different form, in this way changing the nature of the message.

## Environmental pressures that made managers receptive to the message

Very effective communication that leads to action also requires a receptive audience. It would seem that US managers' perception of the business and economic situation in the early 1990s made them extremely interested in the re-engineering message. The management literature was full of predictions about the risks and consequences of the new global market. It claimed that for US companies to survive in this they must quickly adapt to intensifying global competitive pressures. This required making their territory attractive to internationally mobile capital (Hirst and Thompson, 1995). Many companies, despite excellent profits, now started cutting costs by making large numbers of employees redundant. Although staff reductions may be essential when firms are overmanned and business is in decline most companies did this to show a reduction in costs and increase their share price. AT&T was an example of one US company that used this strategy. In order to achieve these staff reductions while still producing their basic product or service, many of these companies had to reorganize. This reorganization fitted the new re-engineering message. The response of Wall Street and the financial sector was that this was the right strategy for business and for shareholders.

Because so many US companies were becoming apprehensive about the threat of the global market and competition from firms in the Far East, the re-engineering message that they could improve efficiency through reorganization and more effective use of technology proved very attractive. Michael Hammer suggested that this reorganization should be dramatic and ruthless so that they could restructure from a clean slate (Hammer and Champy, 1995). As high profile firms introduced change programmes, so others followed and Dawkins ‘imitation’ behaviour took place (Dawkin, 1989). Almost all of these companies associated a reduction in the size of the labour force with the changes they were making. The belief was that business process re-engineering would assist them in reducing a major external risk. They were not aware of the serious internal risks that they might be going to encounter.

These firms were responding to hypotheses rather than to proven facts and some commentators are now questioning this global scenario and the need to conform to its demands (Hirst and Thompson, 1995). A number are suggesting that today's predictions may be incorrect and that if these are not countered they could become self fulfilling prophecies. The alternative view is that the evidence for a global market is unconvincing and that what we are moving towards is an open international economy. This is an economy in which trade and investment take place between the distinct national economies of the advanced countries. Companies remain committed to national bases and conduct the bulk of their business within their home region. These commentators believe that a global economy would not be advantageous. It would not be stable, but highly vulnerable to the unintended effects of markets. In such a volatile system the lives of all but the super rich would be very insecure. We would revert to Darwin's survival of the fittest. It remains to be seen which view of the future is correct.

## The influence of vested interests

Davenport now argues that the fast acceptance and spread of re-engineering in the US was due to three ideas coming together that were attractive to powerful vested interests. The ideas were technology, new work structures and a clean sheet approach to change. The vested interests were senior managers seeking to reduce costs, powerful consultants seeking to expand business and hardware and software vendors seeking to sell more products (Davenport, 1996). These three influences were reinforced by management's belief in the future tough market situation and the need to cut costs to compete in this. An alternative approach to organizational design, socio-technical systems, had been offering much the same prescription for change since the 1940s. But this incorporated a concern for people, and a design approach which both increased efficiency and the quality of working life. Socio-technical design, perhaps because it was not associated with an external threat and because it did not have the same communication effort and skills associated with it as re-engineering, was almost totally ignored by US industry.

This emphasis on the ability of re-engineering to reduce labour, and through this to reduce the risk of uncompetitive costs, is an example of Dawkin's (1989) point that messages get distorted as they are copied and transmitted. Davenport (1996), Hammer and Champy (1995) now all point out that they never encouraged the use of re-engineering merely to save labour. The benefits were offered as a means of streamlining or removing bureaucratic procedures and improving efficiency through better work processes. There were also suggestions that this could be done in a way that would improve job satisfaction through the creating of multi-skilled teams who accepted considerable responsibility.

Unfortunately the re-engineering message was misinterpreted. The message wanted and received by most US industry was not improvement through reorganization but reduced costs through cutting labour. The re-engineering concept also helped managers to avoid taking direct responsibility for layoffs. They could argue that the logic of re-engineering required these. Re-engineering was an example of very successful marketing.

## Early reactions

Companies made intensive efforts to become part of the new game. They believed that re-engineering was the route to future commercial success in a new, highly competitive market environment in which product cost and high quality would be a major factor in success or failure. So powerful was this message that re-engineering was not confined to the United States but taken up and introduced throughout Europe. Most companies were claiming that they had one or more re-engineering projects in progress. Management journals everywhere were reporting successful re-engineering projects. Consultants were making vast sums of money by offering expertise in this area.

This early enthusiasm was documented in a 71-page report published by the Boston Consulting Group in 1993. This provided a senior management perspective on re-engineering. (Boston Consulting Group, 1993) The report started with some good advice on why companies should reorganize. But it advised

don't re-engineer simply to reduce costs: solutions that only restructure, consolidate or obliterate tend to optimize processes from an internal perspective. Excessive cost cutting can destroy value. Even when re-engineering is primarily aimed at major cost reduction, it should be made to work in the service of customer.

The report described three of the key components of business process re-engineering as:

(1) A shift to competing on processes: a reorientation from functions to flows and from vertical to horizontal in the ways companies do business.

(2) A rethinking of strategy: the opening of new avenues to growth by layering strategies of process performance and continuous change over strategies of position.

(3) Redefinition of what it means to be a manager: a transformation from command and control, top down management, to leadership based on building internal capabilities and linking them to customer needs.

The Report stressed that technology can open up new possibilities for increasing value through better communication and information. However new technology should not be the driving force for change – this must be the opportunity to create new value. Leadership requires managers to become motivators and skill builders first and results generators second. Leaders must harness the learning power of the organization and direct it towards the greatest possible strategic improvement.

The report ended with the warning that

‘there is no alternative’. Companies that are business process re-engineering are growing and increasing in profitability. Companies that re-engineer later than their competitors must find better ways to compete and create unique value. What is required is ‘aggressive process management’, companies must constantly evolve to stay ahead.

Other articles appearing in 1993 and 1994 were more dramatic and less considered than the Boston Consulting booklet. In June, 1994 the journal Information Week produced a Re-engineering Report in its Issues section. This was based on a survey of how the American business community viewed re-engineering. Its main findings were that manufacturing had launched more re-engineering projects than any other industry, with health care not far behind. Eighty-one per cent of the 400 companies that took part in the survey had at least one project underway with most IS managers pleased with their organization's re-engineering efforts. Accounting and finance had the highest percentage of re-engineering projects. Chief executives expected big improvements in service and quality as a result of their company's change efforts and, so far, were not disappointed with the results that they were getting.

A CSC Index Survey published in September 1994, discussed critical factors for success in business process re-engineering. This survey covered 600 senior managers in large US and European Corporations. Most reported that re-engineering was pervasive in their organizations with 69% of North American companies and 75% of European companies boasting of at least one project of this type. Half of the respondents who had not yet undertaken a project planned to initiate one before the end of the year. But satisfaction with re-engineering results was mixed, with 16% claiming excellent results and 17% good results, more than 40% reported mediocre to marginal results and a quarter said that their project had failed.

## The first internal risks appear

In 1993 the Harvard Business Review produced an article on 'how to make re-engineering really work' (Hall et al., 1993). This article was based on an examination of re-engineering projects in over 100 companies. Its conclusion was that in many, re-engineering had been simultaneously a great success and a great failure. Dramatic results in individual processes were accompanied by a decline in overall results. A major conclusion was that too many companies squander attention and other resources on projects that look like winners but fail.

The article raised the challenging question that, given the potential of business process re-engineering to provide ‘revolutionary process improvements’ why don’t these result in improvements to the bottom line? This survey included a detailed analysis of 20 projects and these demonstrated how difficult redesign was to plan and implement and how often it failed to produce real business results. Hall and his colleagues (1993) concluded that, to be successful, redesign must penetrate to a company’s core and focus on key business activities.

And so, in this early period, the risk generators were seen as poor definition of redesign needs and poor implementation. The approach itself was not yet questioned by either consultants or managers although academics had been recommending caution for some time (Burke and Peppard, 1995). For managers the great benefit was still the possibility of reducing competitive risk by improving efficiency and lowering costs through staff reductions.

## Continuing downhill

Despite good advice from academics and some major consultants, risks and problems continued to increase. Fewer and fewer projects produced desired results and many pioneering companies found that they were left with processes that were more difficult to manage than the old structures, with increased not reduced costs and with an alienated and demoralized work force who saw the results of change as possible loss of job and security (Goss et al., 1993).

In November 1994 the US Economist Journal asked Michael Hammer what had gone wrong. He replied that many firms had equated re-engineering only with downsizing and other ‘slash and burn’ exercises in cost reduction. Re-engineering had then become associated with the shortcomings of downsizing. He reiterated a point he had made much earlier; that ‘re-engineering is the radical redesign of a company’s business processes, reinventing the way the business operates in order to meet the demands of a modern economy. It is about rethinking work, not eliminating jobs'.

In January 1995 the American Quality Digest published an article by a Total Quality Management consultant, Ray Katz, called 'Is re-engineering already dead?' (Katz, 1995) He made the challenging suggesting that re-engineering might already be on its way out. He said that re-engineering was now being used as a euphemism for downsizing and that this was neither creative nor dynamic. It is what business leaders did when they were out of ideas. Katz concluded his article with an appeal to Hammer and Champy to

'save your reputations and your ideas. Speak out now. Its time to restate what re-engineering really means. If you don't defend your term re-engineering, downsizing will take it from you'.

In January 1995, the Wall Street Journal organized a series of conversations with Michael Hammer and James Champy to try and clarify what was happening with business process re-engineering (Lancaster, 1995). The first conversation, reported on 17th January, focused on the impact of re-engineering on careers. This dialogue seemed to show a degree of confusion in the thinking of Hammer and Champy. They argued that re-engineering was not about downsizing. But downsizing as a result was to be welcomed as it ensured the future of the company.

The following week the Wall Street Journal arranged a further conversation around what it called ‘a look at the future’. Hammer and Champy now stressed that there would be five to ten years of very difficult dislocation, and that there would be a lot of people who would never find a job again.

A third article in the Wall Street Journal on 1 March 1995 seemed to recognize that these answers posed some serious risks for individuals and for society. This discussed re-engineering and work-family conflicts. It pointed out that in many firms flexible work and personal leave were being reduced. Workers were now stretched so thin that no one was asking for anything, they were just hoping to keep what they had already got.

In January, 1995, the CFO Journal, a publication for senior finance officers, quoted yet another survey of business process re-engineering, this time carried out by Arthur D. Little in Cambridge, Mass (Barr, 1995). This found only 16% of senior executives fully satisfied with their re-engineering programmes. About 68% were experiencing unanticipated problems. The journal concluded that much of this disappointment came from unrealistic targets. But it also pointed out that re-engineering was a risk activity. No one knew whether re-engineering programmes did grow revenues and profits in the long term. Available evidence was suggesting that re-engineering always took longer than was expected, always involved more resources than were available and always presented problems no one anticipated.

Evidence had also appeared that another hypothesis had proved incorrect and the reduction in the size of labour forces had not necessarily improved profits. (De Meuse et al., 1995) Over a three year period firms making redundancies had ended up with lower profit margins, a poorer return on assets and on equity after downsizing than the equivalent firms that did not downsize. It seemed that newly lean companies had had to replace lost staff functions with expensive consultants and excised headquarters functions had become subsidiary businesses. In May 1996 Stephen S. Roach, chief economist at Morgan Stanley, appeared on British television to announce that he no longer supported the concept of downsizing. He had changed his long expressed view that tightening wages and cutting labour costs was the route to economic success. He now recognized that these strategies could lead to reduced quality and a demoralized labour force. This change of view was greeted with astonishment by much of the British press who pointed out that many British firms had reduced their labour forces because of his earlier advice. These, possibly misguided, change strategies had created a demoralized work force (Hamel and Prahalad, 1994).

## Conflicting philosophies

Popular journals were now entering the debate. A challenging report appeared in the August 1995 issue of Wired. This compared the theories and ideas of W. Edwards Deming with those of Hammer. The article was called the 'Battle For The Soul Of Corporate America' (Kleiner, 1995). It described this battle as between the quality movement of the eighties and business process re-engineering. The fundamental principle of Deming's (1982) quality movement, was that everyone in an organization should be trained to optimize their contribution. Everyone from the shop floor upwards was expected to experiment with how their work was organized, to meet their colleagues regularly to discuss changes and to monitor the effects of these changes. Deming wanted work to be exciting, challenging and enjoyable with management offering both trust and job security.

Deming's ideas fitted closed with those of the socio-technical school of thought of which the author is a member. The socio-technical approach, developed in the UK in the 1950's, and applied in the rest of Europe and the United States until the eighties, believed that companies should aim to provide employees with an efficient and stimulating work environment and a good quality of working life. In this way production effectiveness would be increased, employee morale would be high and they would be motivated to work hard and creatively in the firms' interests. (Mumford, 1996; Trist & Murray, 1993).

A cultural change then took place. During the 80s the Deming and the socio-technical arguments were influential in many American companies. Their kind of idealism was seen as both possible and necessary if relationships in a company were to be positive and creative. However, during the Bush recession the quality and STS movements lost influence. Quality became control and managers fired employees who had poor quality measurements.

## The risks become clearer

On 24 October 1995, the Boston Globe published an article by consultant Edward Deevy (Deevy, 1995). In it he argued that American business was paying out \$20 billion a year to consulting firms for poor advice. He pointed out that major consulting firms had taken a very mundane technique, re-engineering, and converted it into a gold rush. The fact that re-engineering was having a bad effect on the morale and productivity of workers seemed to matter very little to them. US managers were only just beginning to understand the irreparable damage that could be caused to healthy organizations in the name of re-engineering.

## Deevy claimed that

re-engineers can be viewed as the undertakers of corporate America. Organizations that have spent decades building employee commitment, ownership and team work have seen these gains disappear almost overnight with ill conceived re-engineering projects.

## He added:

'business leaders need to wake up to the fact that what is needed is increased levels of commitment at every level in the organization. Re-engineering is a fad that needs to be abandoned. What is needed is commitment to the workers who hold the key to success in the future.' But re-engineers will not tell you this. There is still a great deal of money to be made before corporate America discovers that with re-engineering it is 'sowing the seeds of its own destruction.

In December 1995 the coup de grace was provided by Tom Davenport himself. It was he who, in the late 1980s in Boston together with Hammer and Champy, had been responsible for the creation and first marketing of re-engineering. He now wrote an article in a journal called Fast Company called ‘why re-engineering failed: the fad that forgot people’ (Davenport, 1996). He began the article in the following way

're-engineering didn't start out as a code for mindless bloodshed. It wasn't supposed to be the last gasp of Industrial Age Management. I know because I was there from the beginning. I was one of the creators'.

Criticizing the association of re-engineering with cost reduction through layoffs he described how the re-engineering genie quickly turned ugly. He said it had become:

'So ugly that today, to most business people in the United States, re-engineering has become a word that stands for restructuring, layoffs, and too often failed change programmes. . . . The rock that re-engineering foundered on is simple: people. Re-engineering treated the people inside companies as if they were just so many bits and bytes, interchangeable parts to be re-engineered'.

Towards the end of the article he claimed:

‘In late 1995, re-engineering isn’t dead; it is effectively over. Growth markets for re-engineering today are outside the United States, in Latin America, southern Europe and Australia – where they haven’t “caught up” to US management yet.’

## He concluded:

'As is always the case with any fad, there was a kernel of truth to re-engineering. Over time that truth got lost. But that doesn't make it any less true.

The most profound lesson of business process re-engineering was never re-engineering, but business processes. Processes are how we work. Any company that ignores its business processes or fails to improve them risks its future. That said, companies can use many different approaches to process improvement without ever embarking on a high risk re-engineering project . . . When the Next Big Thing in management hits, try to remember the lessons of re-engineering. Don't drop all your ongoing approaches in favour of the handsome newcomer.'

## What can we learn about risk from business process re-engineering?

Business process re-engineering is an example of a risk situation entered by managers in an attempt to avoid the much larger and more threatening risk of an inability to compete in a new market situation. Business process re-engineering was offered by its developers as a solution rather than a risk and managers only became aware of the risks when they tried to introduce it into their companies. The global market situation that they feared was perceived as a serious risk although there was little knowledge of what entering it would entail. Today, politicians and economists are engaged in a debate as to whether it will happen at all (Hirst and Thompson, 1995).

Managers are now faced with the problem of assessing ‘alternative’ risks, many of which are imprecise and unclear and some of which will be short term while others are long term. These kinds of risk are a new challenge for managers. In times of stability and secure markets they will be familiar with the kinds of risks they are likely to experience and will have monitoring procedures in place and well tested risk management strategies available. But there is no way of avoiding these new risks, by trying to do this more serious risks may be incurred. Every manager must therefore think hard and clearly about risk management. Ulrich Beck has defined this as ‘a systematic way of dealing with hazards and insecurities induced and introduced by modernization’. (Beck, 1992)

Let us now examine the history and development of business process re-engineering described in this paper and see what we can learn from it to assist a manager faced with the risk of implementing, not a new machine or a new plant, but a new idea.

## Clarity of objectives and vision

An important aid to judgment will be a clear knowledge of the situation the company is in at present and what it is trying to achieve in the future. Is it trying to achieve stability with established customers and markets or is it on a route to rapid growth and innovation? Many pundits argue that survival in today's world requires the second, but this is debatable. It can be argued that in many companies social change follows the same pattern as biological evolution with periods of stability followed by episodes of rapid change (Gould and Eldridge, 1972). The important thing here is for the manager to know what phase the company is in and where the greater risks are located. Introducing major change when the company requires stability can be a high risk strategy while the reverse is also true.

The manager also needs to have self knowledge and to recognize whether he or she is predisposed to seek and enjoy change or whether there is a tendency to stay with the status quo. The business process re-engineering saga shows that many managers and firms have a lemming quality and a tendency to follow and copy what others are doing. One of the most striking features of business process re-engineering was the speed with which it was introduced into large sections of American and British industry.

## Understanding what is being offered and the possible risks

In its early days many companies had an imprecise understanding of what business process re-engineering had to offer and what was involved in introducing it. They knew that it was an important concept which involved restructuring work processes and introducing new technology. They also knew that some prestigious individuals and groups were suggesting that they would incur serious risks if they did not adopt it. This may have led to a condition that has been described as 'Risk Fright' (Bauman, 1993). This has commercial value to groups that are interested in selling something and with business process re-engineering these were consultants and information technology vendors.

Companies planning to introduce new computer systems do not always check how other companies have fared and are faring with the systems they are proposing to introduce. Nor are they encouraged to do this by their competitors, by vendors or by consultants looking for clients. This was true of business process re-engineering and the early marketing was so effective that most companies believed that they were on to a risk free winner. Often being ‘first in the field’ requires taking a risk to achieve a major competitive advantage but when others have gone before it is good policy to seek out their experiences (Mumford, 1995).

A major problem with business process re-engineering was that the original objectives of its developers, Michael Hammer, James Champy and Tom Davenport, became distorted and corrupted at an early stage. Their focus on process improvement to improve efficiency was soon replaced by a management recognition that business process re-engineering could be used to reduce costs and push the share price up. This had great appeal to both American and British managers who are frequently judged on their short term results. Many companies now began major employee reduction programmes assisted by the reorganization that was a product of business process re-engineering.

This was seen as a way of reducing the short term risks of share holder criticism but it did lead to the longer term, and much more serious, risk of loss of commercial confidence by the business world. A 1996 report by Mercer Management Consulting, of 1000 US firms, was reviewed in the Observer on 16 June

1996. This found that from 1988 to 1993 companies that improved their revenues through growth and commercial success improved their stock market position by 19% per year. In contrast, firms that increased profitability through cost cutting such as downsizing only had a profitability of 12%. Mercer argues that downsizers get caught in a spiral of decline. By concentrating on internal structures and processes they take their eye off the customer and this leads to disaster.

This tendency to change the original objective of a new idea to fit a perceived short term gain has affected many recent innovative ideas. It can be a high risk strategy. Total Quality Management, for example, was developed by Deming to provide a better customer service by ensuring staff were motivated to achieve this (Deming, 1982). In many companies it has become the latest tight and unpopular control system.

## Who will bear the risks?

Some risks affect everyone more or less equally, for example, global warming. Others affect different groups in different ways. This was true of business re-engineering. In firms that used it to downsize, senior managers frequently secured salary increases as a reward for reducing costs. Their employees were not so lucky. Many found that they had lost their jobs altogether. Others found that because of the reduced labour force their personal work had become more stressful and demanding. Employees with skills that were in demand found that they could find alternative employment. Those with fewer skills lost their incomes and their security.

This introduces another important risk factor, that of ‘trust’. Because innovation is frequently a journey into the unknown, trust is a major factor in its successful assimilation. An ethical approach to business process re-engineering required that employees could trust employers to look after their interests. This could be done either by guaranteeing no redundancy, although a move to a different internal job might be required, or by offering help in finding new external employment. Unfortunately few companies did this with the result that in many firms the aftermath of business process re-engineering were demoralized and alienated labour forces.

Trust has been defined as ‘confidence in the reliability of a person, or system, regarding a set of outcomes or events’ (Giddens, 1990). Risk and trust are inextricably intertwined. Trust can minimize danger. It is necessary when others are involved in a new activity and using skills which a manger does not have. Confidence is part of trust, although blind faith should not be. Trust involves knowledge, integrity, capability and unflappability. All necessary components of successful innovation. It is the responsibility of the manager and the expert to ensure that knowledge that reduces risk is passed on.

## The role of the expert

Business process re-engineering was almost always associated with the use of experts. Many firms and managers did not feel that they had the time or knowledge to handle the change process themselves. They therefore hired consultants who would do this for them and they trusted these to do a good job.

Sometimes this trust was well placed and many of the major consultant firms produced documents full of good advice on how business process re-engineering could be successfully introduced. The Boston Consulting Group was one of these, publishing a 71 page report in 1993. This placed great importance on good human relations and communication. Other consultants had less relevant knowledge. Some had made their reputations as financial or IT consultants and major, comprehensive change of the kind involved in business process re-engineering was new to them. Firms who hired these were then dependent on assistance which could not be trusted because it did not have the requisite knowledge. In particular there was a neglect of morale and job satisfaction factors.

Ulrich Beck makes the point that some experts can increase risks because their reputation comes from being able to solve problems (Beck, 1992). Just as some police forces have a vested interest in crime, so the expert may have a similar interest in an increase in risks. This provides him or her with an opportunity to demonstrate expertise.

## Conclusions

This paper has discussed a new kind of modern risk. That stemming from ideas of how to increase business success in a competitive world. Business process re-engineering was selected as an example of this kind of risk. It came in quickly as a result of excellent marketing and support from influential individuals and groups. It was quickly and widely accepted by industry who saw it as a means for protecting itself from the risks of a future global market. We have made the point that this notion of a global market is a hypothesis rather than a fact and that there is some doubt as to whether it will ever materialize.

This early objective of addressing a long term risk was soon superseded by the desire to keep share prices up by reducing costs. Business process re-engineering then became associated with downsizing, many people lost their jobs and there was a major drop in morale as personal risk through insecurity increased dramatically. Poorly introduced business process re-engineering brought with it financial as well as human risks. Firms could lose money as unsatisfactory new procedures replaced old and well tested ones. The trauma of change itself had both risks and costs. Many firms paid a high price for few benefits.

Risks concern future happenings which are related to present practices. The monitoring of risks and risk assessment is not too difficult when risks are known. The problem with many of today's risks, and this is especially true of risks stemming from new ideas, is that they are not known. In this case they have to be hypothesized, a practice called ‘risk profiling’. (Giddens, 1990). This is a necessary exercise, but of course it may be inaccurate.

We have now passed from the ‘industrial society’ to the ‘risk society’. Ulrich Beck argues that the logic of wealth production is being gradually displaced by the logic of ‘risk avoidance’ and ‘risk management’ (Beck, 1992). In this paper we have focused on institutional risks incurred in an attempt to reduce a global risk. But we are continually threatened by global risks. How risks can be prevented, minimized or challenged has now become a major problem for us all.

## Bibliographical notes

Professor Enid Mumford first took a degree in social science at Liverpool University. She then spent some time in industry before joining the Faculty of Social Science at Liverpool University where she carried out research in industrial relations. After a year at the University of Michigan she joined the newly formed Manchester Business School, where she studied the human and organizational impact of computer-based systems. In 1983 she won the American Warnier Prize for her contributions to information science, becoming the only non-American to win this award.

In 1996 she was awarded an Honorary Doctorate by the University of Jyväskylä in Finland.

She is now an Emeritus Professor of Manchester University, a Visiting Senior Fellow at the Manchester Business School and a visiting Professor at the University of Central Lancashire.

She is the author of twenty-six books and many papers. Her current interest is developing tools and methods to assist the management of technical and organizational change. In 1994 she published a book called Tools For Change and Progress: A socio-technical approach to business process re-engineering. Her most recent books are Effective Systems Design and Requirements Analysis: the ETHICS method and Systems Design: Ethical Tools For Ethical Change. These were published in 1995 and 1996 by Macmillan.
