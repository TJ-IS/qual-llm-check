# The politics of risk: trials and tribulations of the Taurus project

HELGA DRUMMOND

The University of Liverpool, Liverpool, UK

The term ‘risk management’ implies that risk is something which can be quantified, predicted and controlled. This paper seeks to demonstrate the limits of this assumption where complex projects are involved. The argument is based upon a case study of a failed £80 million IT venture known as Taurus. Analysis focuses upon the relationship between politics and the assumption of risk. Acceptance of risk, it is argued, is ultimately determined by the balance of power between decision makers. Moreover, risk analysis and other techniques of management may actually compound the difficulties by fostering an illusion of control and escalation. The implications for project management are discussed.

## Introduction

Science re-assures, art disturbs.

(Proverb)

On Wednesday 11 March 1993, the City of London was shocked by the sudden cancellation of project Taurus. The Stock Exchange had spent over three years and £80 million in the venture. The securities industry had invested over £400 million developing their own systems in preparation for Taurus. On Wednesday 11 March 1993 the Stock Exchange publicly admitted that it had all been for nothing.

This paper assumes that if the Stock Exchange had foreseen the collapse of Taurus, they would not have authorized the venture. In retrospect the risks were obvious. Taurus was a highly complex and controversial project, involving novel technology and an ambitious timescale. It was set to overturn the trading and regulatory framework of the entire securities industry. The present study is concerned with two questions. First, why did the Stock Exchange become involved in such a risky endeavour? Second, why did it persist long after it had become clear that expectations would not be met?

Risk may be defined as the chance or probability of failure (e.g., Jurison, 1995; Willcocks and Griffiths, nd). The literature upon risk management is rooted in mathematics and statistical probability (e.g., Lopes,

1981; Funder, 1987). For example, Pauchant and Mitroff (1992:p.93) state, 'Risk management involves evaluating the cost of a risk after multiplying by its probability of occurrence.' Quantification implies that risk can be predicted and controlled. This paper seeks to demonstrate, by means of a case study of project Taurus, the limits of this assumption where complex ventures are involved. Analysis highlights the impact of politics upon the assumption of risk, and shows how power rather than technical rationality may be the ultimate arbiter in decision making. Moreover, risk analysis and other managerial techniques, instead of reducing uncertainty, may actually facilitate escalation by fostering an illusion of control.

## Theoretical context

The theoretical context of the present study derives from a corpus of literature concerned with a phenomenon known as escalation in decision making. Escalation means persistence with a venture beyond an economically defensible point (e.g., Staw, 1981; Staw and Ross, 1987; Ross and Staw, 1991; Drummond, 1994 a, b, 1995).

The precise reasons for escalation are unclear. Decision dilemma theorists argue that the prime cause is uncertainty. It can be very difficult or even impossible to know how a project will turn out. Moreover, initially feedback may be sparse and unreliable. When a venture appears to be failing, it may be sensible to persist in order to give a strategy a chance to work. Eventually, however, it may become clear that expectations cannot be met. Such information will destroy the decision maker's commitment to the venture (Bowen, 1987).

There is experimental evidence to support the idea of a concomitant relationship between information and commitment. Experiments have shown that when projects fail to turn out as expected, decision makers persist for a while, then, if no improvement occurs, give up (Singer and Singer, 1986; Goltz, 1992; Hanatula and Crowell, 1994). The limitation of experiments, however, is that they can only approximate field conditions. According to some sources Taurus was fundamentally flawed from the start (Currie, 1994; Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994). Moreover, by mid-1991 it was abundantly clear that the decision makers' expectations would not be met. Yet construction continued for almost another two years before the project was finally cancelled. The present study explores the relationship between information and commitment over time in a field setting. Analysis seeks to elucidate what factors obscured the risks involved.

## Methodology

The data presented in this paper were gathered as part of a larger project (Drummond, 1996). The project comprised a case study of the London Stock Exchange's decision to commission project Taurus in 1988 and the organization's subsequent decision to cancel the venture five years later, in March 1993, whilst it was still under construction.

The value of an in-depth case study is that the methodology is highly suited to capturing the contextual richness and complexity of decision scenarios. Whereas a statistical survey seeks representativeness, a case study is intended to illuminate theoretically significant possibilities which may guide experiments and other forms of research (e.g., Mitchell, 1983; Dyer and Wilkins, 1991).

An important rule in conducting case study research is that the work should be replicable in principle (Yin, 1989). Further, since the researcher cannot achieve the same control that experiments afford, the use of multiple data sources is desirable in order to reduce the risk of bias (Becker, 1958). The strategy in gathering data was to achieve triangulation of sources. The argument is that if three independent sources confirm the same point then the researcher can be fairly sure that the findings are reliable (Yin, 1993).

Three primary data sources were utilized to research Taurus. The first comprised media accounts. Taurus was well documented by the quality press and in specialist journals covering the financial services industry. The second comprised documentation including letters, memoranda, consultants' reports, project managers' work books, and market briefings. The third source consisted of interviews with participants including the executive of the Stock Exchange, members of the technical team, members of the committee which designed Taurus, members of the various Taurus monitoring groups and senior representatives of the securities industry. Interviewing ceased when accounts became heavily repetitive – a clear sign of saturation.

Instead of utilizing a pre-determined coding frame, the evidence was analysed line by line after the manner advocated by Turner (1983). Although this method is highly time consuming, it forces the researcher to consider material which might otherwise be dismissed as theoretically insignificant. The field work yielded a mass of paper. In order to cope with the volume every item of data was assigned one or more key words. For instance, the following excerpt was assigned the key words, ‘Taurus monitoring group’, ‘progress’ and ‘1991’:

There was lots of progress. Every meeting lots of progress from where you were in the last meeting. However, when you sat back and said, 'Are we any closer to the end?'

'Well, not really.'

(Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, June 1994.)

Computer search facilities were utilized to retrieve and order the data.

Data gathering and analysis proceeded in a cyclical fashion. Information was read, re-read, played, replayed, analysed and re-analysed many times as preliminary ideas and patterns suggested themselves. In turn, emergent possibilities formed the basis for further enquiry and analysis (Mills, 1959; Glaser and Strauss, 1967). The over-arching question for analysis was, 'why persist with the project?'

The ultimate decision maker was the board of the Stock Exchange advised by the chief executive. However, in order to understand the events surrounding Taurus it is necessary to consider the actions of the range of players identified earlier. All are referred to in the text as decision makers.

## The case study

## The origins of escalation: 'sheer blind panic'

In theory organizational decision making proceeds in an orderly fashion. The decision maker is presented with a problem. The decision maker then generates all possible alternative solutions. Each alternative is carefully analysed. The option chosen is the one most consistent with the decision maker's goals (e.g., Simon, 1957). In practice, the process is rarely so clinical. Action is often stimulated by a crisis (Mintzberg et al., 1976). Although the precise effects of perceived emergency are unclear, there is ample anecdotal evidence to suggest crisis hardly facilitates reasoned analysis (e.g., Neustadt and May, 1986; Janis, 1989).

Taurus is a case in point. Taurus was intended to replace the UK's antiquated paper driven procedures for settling share transactions (securities) with a sophisticated system of electronic transfer. The need for de-materialization was recognized in 1967 (Stock Exchange, 1970). Settlement, however, was perceived as an unremunerative chore. Consequently, twenty years later in 1987 a system designed when stockbrokers wrote their orders with quill pens by the lanterns of Garraway's coffee house (Kynaston, 1994), was handling over a £1 billion a day.

Two critical events provided the impetus to reform. De-regulation in 1986 (known as 'Big Bang'), rendered the once powerful Stock Exchange partially redundant. The Exchange decided that its survival depended upon offering valued services to the securities industry. Settlement was potentially one such service (Stock Exchange, 1989). Coincidentally, the stock market crash of 1987 exposed the weaknesses of the existing settlement system. Under existing arrangements two to three weeks elapsed between contracting to buy or sell shares, and the actual exchange of money and certificates. Two weeks is a long time in the capricious world of high finance. Meanwhile, if one major player defaulted, the entire market could fail (City Research Project, 1993).

A decision is usually defined as ‘to cut’, that is, to make a clear and definite choice between alternatives (Drummond, 1991). Hedberg and Jonsonn (1977), however, have argued that it is more appropriate to view decisions as the emergence of myths. A myth is defined as a partial mapping of reality which gains or loses credence overtime. A myth represents the dominant viewpoint about what should be done. Taurus was founded on the myth that eliminating paper (de-materialization) was the key to streamlining settlement. As all myths are partially true it follows that they are partially false. Paper was only one factor in the settlements crisis. Peter Rawlins the chief executive of the Stock Exchange said: ‘The primary cause of the ’87 fiasco was . . . simply blind panic. The newly established managements of these big organizations . . . didn’t understand the business they were in. . . . It . . . scared . . . them. (Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.) Indeed, the investment houses recognized that another factor in the crisis was their own poorly organized offices. Accordingly they began improving their own systems. Consequently when construction of Taurus commenced two years later, in early 1990, a large segment of the problem had already self-corrected.

## Politics versus technical rationality: 'Is this really the right way to go?'

Crises are analytically significant because they reveal the deep forces surrounding decision makers (Morgan, 1990, Pauchant and Mitroff, 1992). The settlements crisis exposed a fundamental contradiction in the securities industry. Taurus was driven by a desire to reduce risk and to improve efficiency, yet part of the industry's profits were derived from the very inefficiencies which Taurus aimed to eliminate.

The tensions were reflected in the protracted discussions over the Taurus design. The Bank of England originally allowed two months for an agreement to be concluded. Two years later the industry was still locked in conflict (e.g., Waters 1989; Wolman 1989). Politics may be defined as the pursuit of interest in the face of scarce resources. Political activity is frequently characterized by conflict resulting from competition for such resources (Miller, 1962). The resolution of such conflict may be characterized by negotiation and compromise according to the balance of power between the parties involved (Burns, 1961; Miller, 1962; Crick, 1976; Morgan, 1986). It is good practice when designing an IT system to review existing procedures first (Daniels, 1993). The Stock Exchange's preferred option was to simplify the whole system of buying and selling shares by creating a central register of share ownership. The clearing banks opposed the idea however, because it posed a threat to their registration business.

Unable to dictate to the industry, the Stock Exchange then tried to devise a universally acceptable solution. Since no party was prepared to sacrifice their interests for the common good, a hybrid model emerged consisting of no fewer than 17 alternative Taurus options welded together. Complex designs are more difficult and therefore more risky to build that simple ones (e.g., Schonberger, 1986). The risks are compounded where the technology is both novel and inflexible. Novelty requires trial and error whereas inflexibility restricts the room for manoeuvre (e.g., Etzioni, 1989; Collingridge, 1992; Bowen and Power, 1993). Taurus combined all three elements of risk. It was built round existing business practice which was uniquely complicated and embracing the entire securities industry which involved a myriad of systems and sub-systems (Waters, 1993). Since no blue print for Taurus existed, some of the ramifications of change were incalculable. A project consultant recalls just one of the many debates that took place at the design stage:

What we wanted was (shareholder) visibility. So, we went round saying, 'How can we make this visible? Oh well, what we'll do is consolidate it (the record of transactions) and whiz it off to the registrar periodically.'

There was a great deal of argument about this, 'Well, the network's not there and it's an awful lot of data traffic and God can all the firms (in the securities industry) cope. Is this really the right way to go?'

(Interview, member of Stock Exchange technical team, May 1994.)

Taurus would handle £ trillions of investors' money. The security requirements of the system were likewise unparalleled:

You realized that you were increasingly operating at the edge of available technology. We were constantly moving out to a point where not even the major suppliers could guarantee that what they were supplying would work.

(Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, June 1994.)

Such a complex solution was far from ideal from the Stock Exchange's viewpoint. However, Taurus was perceived as strategically important and there was no alternative. Moreover since the securities industry insisted upon an urgent solution, the Stock Exchange set itself an extremely ambitious timescale of 18 months. Thus did politics over-ride technical rationality (e.g., March and Olsen, 1967; Mintzberg et al., 1976; Bower, 1983; Morgan, 1986).

## The logic of risk-taking: 'I was dead against buying American software ...'

Willcocks and Griffiths (1984) have argued that risk arises from the interaction between a wide range of contextual, organizational and technological factors which come into play during the life of a complex project. The case of Taurus sheds some light upon the nature of such interaction. Risk taking and escalation are sometimes attributed to foolhardy behaviour on the part of those involved (e.g., Teger, 1980; Bazerman, 1994). The case of Taurus highlights how fiascos may result not from recklessness but from rationality itself. What may seem bizarre is actually the unfolding of the destructive logic of the situation (Aram, 1989). To take just one example, it has been suggested that the decision to utilize a software package known as Vista to drive Taurus was a mistake because of the amount of adjustment required (Waters and Cane, 1993). For example, a member of the Taurus monitoring group said:

I was dead against buying American software and changing it because I believed that it would need to be changed far too much. All these off the shelf products are fine if you just tinker round the edges. If you have to re-engineer them to anything more than 20% ... you always have to travel.

(Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, November 1993.)

The then project director Bill Widdis was well aware of the risks involved. Yet his decision to purchase the Vista package was underscored by a clear and defensible rationale. The Stock Exchange did not possess the resources to build from scratch. Sub-contracting the work was not an option as this had proved unsatisfactory in the past. Purchasing ‘ready made’ software was the only option which held out the prospect of meeting the ambitious and critically important timescales. The contract to purchase the Vista package was signed in June 1990, before the requirements were finalized. As the technical team’s understanding of their task deepened, they subsequently realized that the software would require more alteration than was originally thought. For example, a member of the team said:

We said, ‘What is Vista going to do out of this totality of an outline design we think we might have got?’ We had to break it down into chunks, ‘Well, Vista should be able to do all this and then we talked to Vista about various things’ and, ‘Well, we can’t do that. We don’t handle it. Anyway it doesn’t work very well . . .’

(Interview, member of the Stock Exchange technical team, May 1994.)

Thus did the conversion process begin to exceed budget and timescales.

## The paradox of analysis: 'People began to disappear into detailed working groups'

Why did the Stock Exchange not see the risks inherent in such complexity? Broadly speaking risk is predictable provided the probabilities of a given event are known (e.g., Lopes, 1981; Funder, 1987). For example, assuming a perfectly balanced coin there is an equal probability of obtaining a 'head' or a 'tail' when the coin is tossed. Where complex projects are involved, however, probabilities can only be estimated (e.g., Pauchant and Mitroff, 1992; Mintzberg, 1994). Even so, the project management literature argues that analysis based upon intelligent guesswork does at least facilitate some predictive ability (Lock, 1992; Maylor, 1996). This assumption, however, obscures the paradox of rigorous analysis. The greater the rigour, the greater the impression of certainty where none basically exists.

In theory, analysis informs decision making (e.g., Drummond, 1991). In practice, its role is largely symbolic. Analysis legitimates decisions by creating an impression of diligence whilst the assumptions upon which it is based are unverifiable (e.g., Meyer and Rowan, 1978; Feldman and March, 1981). For example, the evaluation procedures which led to the choice of the Vista software package to drive Taurus were immaculate. The critical underlying assumption, however, was that the requirements were clear. In addition to all the other difficulties, the evaluation team could not have foreseen how much they would change (see below). Likewise the legal implications of de-materialization, the business case, the security implications were all examined in detail. A member of the design committee noted: 'People began to disappear into detailed working groups to deal with corporate actions and rules from which essentially they never emerged again.' (Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, September 1994.) For instance:

There were endless arguments about whether Scots' Law had to change and whether the register could be kept outside the company ... whether the register of companies incorporated in Scotland could be maintained on a computer in London ... Enough to try the patience of a saint.

(Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, July 1994.)

Paradoxically instead of highlighting risk, rigorous analysis obscured it because it led the decision makers into ‘first-order’ thinking (Watzlawick et al., 1974). The ‘endless arguments’ signified the risks involved. The decision makers, however, concentrated upon trying to solve the problems instead of questioning their existence. The task was so complex that it defeated them.

## Control as the antidote to risk: 'It used to drive us crazy'

Taurus was a highly risky venture to begin with. Ironically the Stock Exchange's policy of risk management compounded the difficulties. Taurus highlights the potentially destructive logic of control itself (Watzlawick et al., 1974; Morgan, 1990). Power may be defined as the production of intentional effects (Wrong, 1979). The managerial imperative was to maintain the market's commitment to Taurus (Saur, 1993). In order to maintain momentum, however, the technical team commenced construction before the requirements were finalized. Furthermore, the Stock Exchange continued to involve the securities industry and the various project boards in almost every detail of the project. Involvement prompted constant demands for change (Waters and Cane, 1993). A member of the technical team describes what happened as construction commenced:

More records came in, some went out, things on record changed, the description of what the record was used for might grow. That process happened too many times ... It used to drive us crazy. We felt we were giving information too early ... but people were very keen to see the direction we were going, what the records might look like. There was tremendous pressure to produce something.

(Interview, member of Stock Exchange technical team, May 1994.)

No sooner had one modification been agreed in one quarter than another party demanded something different. An investment banker recalls:

There were multiple meetings at multiple levels with different constituencies who would have to interface with Taurus and were constantly impacting on its structure and design. 'We are the custodian community and we would need this sort of information. We are the registrar community and we would need this sort of information and we would input in this way.' So they (the technical team) were constantly saying, 'OK well we will have to recode and redo to cope with that.'

Then software manufacturers would say, ‘Ah but then you have to make this change and you have to make that change and you have to interface these things that are now from different suppliers.’ So the complexity just grew and grew.

(Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, June 1994.)

Thus did control inadvertently create a massive ‘stack-up’ (Perrow, 1984).

## Risk and responsibility: 'We were all progressively amazed at the way costs were building up . . .'

The story of Taurus is reminiscent of the tragedy of the commons. The commons were stretches of land. Every peasant was entitled to graze their beasts freely. Logically it was sensible for each individual to graze one more animal. The land was ruined as an inexorable consequence of the policy of unfettered rights (Hardin, 1968). Taurus failed as an inexorable consequence of the policy of attempting to satisfy all constituents and their conflicting requirements. Failure was not inevitable, however. Taurus might have been stopped if some party had drawn attention to the risks and weaknesses surrounding the project.

Some potentially influential people in the securities industry were sceptical about the venture. They kept their views private, however (Hooker, private communication, 1995). For instance, one member of the design committee said: 'We were all progressively amazed at the way costs were building up ... but it wasn't our money. It was the Stock Exchange's.' (Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, July 1994.) Others were indifferent because they still regarded settlement as an unremunerative chore. The chief executive of the Stock Exchange recalls: 'There was a sort of uncritical acceptance by people, most of whom hadn't engaged their minds to it about (sic) "Let's get on with this."' (Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

## The power of rhetoric: 'Nobody stepped forward and said, "I don't really understand this."

A deeper force lay behind such irresponsibility. The most potent form of power is that which is rarely seen. It rests in ‘taken for granted’ views about what is appropriate (e.g., Berger and Luckman, 1966; Lukes, 1974; Zucker, 1977; Clegg and Dunkerly, 1980). Taurus was supported by a powerful group of international bankers known as the ‘Group of Thirty’ and the Bank of England (Bank of England, 1993). The project became linked to London’s continued pre-eminence and survival. For example, the prospectus noted:

The perceived inefficiencies and risk associated with settlement in the UK have proved a deterrent for international investors and have threatened the continued development of London as one of the world's leading securities markets.

## (International Stock Exchange, 1990, p. 8.)

For the next three years almost every seminar on Taurus, every breakfast briefing, every newsletter and every report espoused the rhetoric (Berger, 1972; Gowler and Legge, 1983; Quaid, 1993; Dyer, 1995). Reputation is an asset, particularly in the financial services industry (Kay, 1993). It was in no one's interests to expose flaws in Taurus for fear of being seen as undermining a strategically vital project. Consequently, 'Nobody stepped forward and said, "I don't really understand this."' (Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, July 1994.)

## Risk and power: 'I did try to stop it and could not stop it'

An exception was the chief executive of the Stock Exchange. He tried to stop the project in early 1990 before construction commenced:

My thesis was we should go back to Taurus One . . . If we are going to have the system changed for christ's sake lets do it properly so we build one central registrar. I said, 'Why don't we do this properly?' The great advantage was that nothing had been built.

(Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

According to decision dilemma theorists, commitment ceases once it becomes clear that expectations will not be met (Bowen, 1987). This was basically the chief executive's case. Drummond (1995), however, has argued that information alone is insufficient to destroy commitment. Power, she suggests, is the key. Although nothing had been built when the chief executive attempted to intervene, Taurus had already become institutionalized, that is, achieved ‘taken for granted’ status. Institutionalization is supported by bureaucracy (Ross and Staw, 1986, 1993). The sheer inflexibility of bureaucracy can lead to bizarre situations. For instance, in the case of the ‘Challenger’ disaster, the engineers were still discussing their doubts about the valves informally but were powerless to act because the problems were officially minuted as solved (Starbuck and Milliken, 1988). A similar thing happened with Taurus. Whilst a central register was potentially the technically correct solution who wanted to re-open the discussions? The chief executive's instructions were clear, ‘No sorry Peter we have had all those arguments. Great idea but no, we have been arguing about it for twelve years, forget it.’ (Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

## The risks unravel

## Estimation of probabilities: 'A niggling doubt'

Despite the ‘endless argument’ when the design committee concluded its deliberations the legal implications of Taurus remained a ‘niggling doubt’ according to the chairman Patrick Mitford-Slade (Interview, Patrick Mitford-Slade, May 1994). What estimation of probabilities did that imply? The prospectus allowed six months for ‘the specific legal structure’ (International Stock Exchange, 1990: p. 14) of Taurus to be finalized. Law is synonymous with precision. John Redwood the minister responsible for overseeing the legislation recalls:

In the beginning they wanted regulatory approval for something that didn't exist on paper ... They hadn't thought through all the details about how it would work. We were constantly striving to get them to define all the details of the system ... You need to know every kind of transaction. It is no good saying you will sort it out later. So when, for example, the Stock Exchange were unable to explain the procedures for rights issues and nationality issues for large corporations, we could only send them back to consider.

(Interview, John Redwood M.P., November 1994.)

A year later, in January 1991, Taurus was postponed for nine months as a result of these unforeseen complications. It was the first of a series of setbacks which would undermine confidence in the project.

## Countervailing power: 'My jaw dropped . . .'

Power is a dynamic and reciprocal entity. No matter how asymmetrical the balance, the weaker party is never completely helpless (Simmel, 1950). The business interests threatened by de-materialization did not withdraw their objections, but pursued them elsewhere. In mid-1991, their influence was suddenly felt.

The first issue to impact upon Taurus concerned the implications of incorporating millions of relatively inactive private investors into Taurus. Although the prospectus said, ‘The needs of the private investor . . . have been given particular attention in our planning,’ (International Stock Exchange, 1990: p.1) the issues had actually received comparatively little attention at the design stage because the large institutional investors dominated the agenda. One member of the design committee said: ‘Companies came second, “we hope they will be satisfied.” As for private shareholders, “they will get what they are given.”’ (Interview, member of Siscot committee, October 1994.)

During 1990 the disgruntled retail-broking constituency began to undermine Taurus by raising questions about the adequacy of the security arrangements. Anxious to avoid a City scandal, the government demanded that Taurus incorporate a system of personal identification numbers. It meant that a unique 13 digit code would be required for every transaction. The technical implications of handling and matching thousands of such codes daily were almost unimaginable. That, moreover, was only the start.

Next the government demanded that the Stock Exchange offer private investors an indemnity.

'My jaw dropped,' said the chief executive when he heard the sum required.

(Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

The minister was determined to protect the public interest, however. 'We have an old-fashioned settlement procedure that works. We couldn't have a new one in London that didn't work,' he said.

(Interview, John Redwood M.P., November 1994.)

The Stock Exchange found itself trapped by its own rhetoric. Peter Rawlins said:

It was a very difficult thing to stand up and say to the world ‘Look, frankly we are not doing this for Mrs Snooks of 22 Acacia Grove and her 100 ICI.’ We were not trying to put her out of business or make it difficult for her but we definitely did not have her as number one priority … Furthermore, Mrs Snooks had a lot of allies in the private client stock brokers who used to lobby hard the politicians, ‘What is going to happen if Mrs Snooks’ shares get ripped off?’

(Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

Nor was that all. The Taurus cost-benefits equation assumed compulsory membership. The listed companies, however, had also taken the battle underground. They utilized the government's consultation procedure to veto compulsion. Consequently, on top of all his other duties, the project director would need to embark upon a tour of the UK persuading shareholders to join Taurus. It was at this point many of the people I interviewed suggested Taurus should have been stopped.

## Escalation

## Institutionalization: 'Lets get the bloody thing done and behind us'

It is a matter of record that the project was not stopped in mid-1991 despite clear evidence that expectations would not be met. The project time-table had slipped 100%. It was known that the cost over-run would be at least 50%. The conversion of the Vista software package was proving highly problematic not least because of the constantly changing requirement. Parts of the prototyped hardware had failed. The security and legislative implications had proved more onerous than envisaged, and, were still unresolved. Private investors were expressing hostility to de-materialization. The market and the government seemed equivocal. Why did this information not destroy commitment to the venture?

It was suggested earlier that Taurus was basically sustained by its ‘taken for granted’ status. Such institutionalization was reflected in the ‘non-decision’ making (Bacharach and Baratz, 1970) on the part of those involved. A ‘non-decision’ may be defined as a failure to question the status quo. Taurus survived as a legacy of, ‘What some committee said we ought to do.’ (Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

One factor which acted to protect the status quo was decision makers' institutionalized acceptance of their roles. Institutionalization is defined here as a form of code. It is a shared expectation about what is appropriate behaviour in a particular set of circumstances. To break the code is to act in a manner which would be regarded by others as unthinkable (Berger and Luckman, 1966).

Decision debacles are sometimes attributed to a ‘can do’ mentality (e.g., Janis, 1989). The present study suggests that what may appear as a ‘gung ho’ attitude may actually reflect the institutionalized acceptance of authority. For instance, the technical team experienced one frustration after another. They persisted because they saw it as their job to build the system and not to question whether it was worth building. Likewise Taurus executives behaved in a manner consistent with the stereotypical image of the manager as ‘problem solver, negotiator, and fixer’ (e.g., Mintzberg, 1973). For example, the chief executive and the project director negotiated over compensation and security arrangements with the government. The monitoring group contributed to the debate. ‘Lets get the bloody thing done and behind us’, they said to one another. (Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

Such behaviour was referred to earlier as first order thinking (Watzlawick et al., 1974). Again decision makers' expertise was directed towards solving the problems rather than questioning their existence. For example a member of the Taurus monitoring group said:

It's easy to look back and say, 'Why didn't you see it all the time?'

Well, most disasters look obvious in hindsight. When you are in the middle of it and your objective is to get to the end you take each issue and you try and deal with it, and then another issue and you try to deal with it, and another issue and you try to deal with it.

(Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, April 1994.)

## Intuition versus analysis: 'There was never a sense that the project was under control'

Management is a ‘right brain’ activity. Decisions supported by analysis are defensible even if they are wrong, whereas intuition is inadmissible even if it is accurate (e.g., Mumby and Putnam, 1992). The charts and graphs and other information supplied by the technical team were impeccable. Yet innately recipients knew something was wrong. For instance, the monitoring group received reports on software development some of it being done ‘in-house’, some of it contracted out to various suppliers:

We said, 'How is all this going to come together?'

And they said, 'Well, we have an integration plan, and we have a testing schedule, and we have all these other things.'

‘And they had a lot of them. Yet there was never a sense that the project was under control.’

(Interview, member of Taurus monitoring group, June 1994.)

To have intervened to stop the project on the basis of ‘gut feel’ would have been unthinkable. So, the decision makers could only wait upon events.

## Conflicting priorities: 'Taurus was ... an irritation'

The chief executive did in fact commission an external review of Taurus in mid-1991. The review, however, was also constrained by first order thinking in that it was concerned with the technical feasibility of Taurus rather than the project's intrinsic worth. The review concluded that even the revised timescales were too ambitious. The earliest possible date for implementation was April 1993. That date, moreover, assumed no further delays. I asked Peter Rawlins why he recommended continuance despite such questionable odds given the history of the project. Rawlins replied that the decision was technically correct given his information at the time. In retrospect, however, he wished he had trusted his instincts.

I can only put that down to the fact that at the time Taurus was frankly a pain in the bum, an irritation ... The other things I was doing were massive, pioneering ... far more challenging. They needed every ounce of my intellectual and professional energy. So, that was one opportunity that I had, and I just didn't like the smell of it.

(Interview, Peter Rawlins, October 1993.)

Escalation is considered most probable where a project is perceived as important (e.g., Rubin and Brockner, 1975; Bowen, 1987). The case of Taurus shows that the opposite may apply. Although the Stock Exchange regarded Taurus as strategically important, it had also briefed the chief executive to implement drastic cost cutting. For the first two years of his appointment Peter Rawlins was busy re-organizing the Exchange. It was only when that mission was completed late 1992, that he felt able to give Taurus his full attention. Nothing had prepared him for the shock in store.

## Discussion and conclusions

The plethora of interests surrounding Taurus might seem atypical. Yet surely that is precisely the point. Taurus serves as a reminder that an IT venture involves more than building a system. It is part of the unfolding of a broader story.

It was suggested earlier that the literature treats risk as a concrete entity which exists ‘out there’ and can be brought under control. The case of Taurus highlights the extent to which risk is something which we create for ourselves. The assumption of risk is part of the political ‘fall out’ of decision making. It is not analysis but politics which determines what risks are run, when and by whom (Lasswell, 1963; Crick, 1976). Project managers therefore require political sensitivity as well as technical expertise.

The case of Taurus highlights the paradox of information, analysis and control. What Taurus teaches us is that it is not the decision maker's information that is important so much as knowing the limits of their information (Toffler, 1992) and indeed the potential of their intuition. Likewise, although the inherent contradictions of management are unresolvable, a crucial element of competence is the awareness that prescriptions for action, like drugs, possess side effects. The art of management is knowing when control is creating chaos. Taurus was supposedly vital to London's continued pre-eminence. Yet after the project collapsed settlement periods were substantially reduced still within the context of a paper driven system. The experience of Taurus shows that there are no absolutes in decision making, only the emergence of a dominant viewpoint. Ventures become sacerdotal because people make them so. No project, however vital it may seem, is beyond challenge or re-appraisal. There is evidence that confronting options reduces the risks of escalation (e.g., Rubin and Brockner, 1975; McCain, 1986). Perhaps the most powerful question in a decision makers' armoury is, 'Why are we doing this?'

The case of Taurus suggests that prime obstacles to re-assessment are institutionalization and first order thinking. A ‘total quality’ approach to designing and building Taurus might have removed the barriers to communication which institutionalization imposes (Deming, 1986; Drummond, 1993). The experience of Taurus also exemplifies the need for including thinking skills in professional education programmes (e.g., De Bono, 1990).

Another question which arises is whether the politics surrounding Taurus could have been better handled. Currie (1994) draws attention to the link between strategy formulation and success and failure in IT ventures, whilst Willcocks and Griffiths (1994)

emphasize the importance of analysis and risk management in predicting the possible impact of vested interests. The case of Taurus highlights the role of time in complex projects. Recall that Taurus was part of the Stock Exchange's strategy of securing a controlling share in settlement services (Stock Exchange, 1989); whilst the securities industry had yet to awaken to the commercial potential of settlement. The awakening brought the Stock Exchange into competition with its own member firms. Moreover, the banks had not yet invested as heavily in the registration business as they subsequently did. The economic recession of the 1990s also curtailed funds for investment. Consequently, by the time the strategy began to be implemented, critical assumptions upon which it was based no longer held.

A related issue concerns the extent to which the project itself changes over time. The Stock Exchange's policy of consultation with the securities industry was intended to maintain support for the project. However, it contained a destructive contradiction in that the cumulative effect of responding to consumer demand was to destroy the original Taurus conception. Many decisions in organizations are only achieved after considerable compromise. A specific lesson of Taurus is the need for managers to maintain a close watch upon the impact of incremental changes as they can result in venture being transformed into something else. This is the point where withdrawal may well be justified as the original objectives cannot be met.

Was Taurus ever worth building? It is surely no coincidence that Crest, the successor to Taurus, offers simplicity (relatively speaking), minimal choice but no compulsion (Bank of England, 1993). Taurus is one more reminder that the odds are stacked against large and complicated projects (e.g., Collingridge, 1992; Pauchant and Mitroff, 1992; Bowen and Power, 1993; Griffiths and Willcocks, 1994). The question to be kept at the forefront is, as risk is difficult to quantify and control, why create it?

## Biographical notes

Helga Drummond is Senior Lecturer at the University of Liverpool's Institute of Public Administration and Management. Her main research interest is a phenomenon known as escalation in decision making. She has published several research papers on the topic. This paper is based upon her study of the London Stock Exchange's decision to abandon project Taurus. The full analysis is published in book form as, Escalation in Decision Making: The Tragedy of Taurus, Oxford, Oxford University Press.
