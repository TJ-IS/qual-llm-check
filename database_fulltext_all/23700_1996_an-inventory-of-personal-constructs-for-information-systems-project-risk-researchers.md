# An inventory of personal constructs for information systems project risk researchers

TONY MOYNIHAN

Dublin City University, Dublin, Ireland

This paper explores the application of personal construct elicitation techniques to the identification of the situational factors that managers of bespoke information system development projects take into account when planning new projects for new customers. It is shown that the personal constructs elicited reflect most of the contingency variables and risk-drivers mentioned in the information systems development literature, but also include some situational characteristics not addressed in this literature. It is concluded that personal construct elicitation techniques have a valuable role to play in information systems development research.

## Introduction

The notion that a developer should ‘tailor’ aspects of an information systems project to achieve a ‘fit’ to important situational characteristics seems to be an unassailable one. It is hard to imagine how a developer could survive otherwise! When tailoring a project to circumstances, a manager must obviously draw on some principles or ‘rules’ for guidance. The theories which people use to guide their actions have been termed theories of action: ‘A full schema for a theory of action would be as follows: in situation S, if you want to achieve desirable consequence C, under cause-effect assumptions a, . . . n, do A.’ (Argyris and Schon, 1978: p. 10).

This paper is about one of the components of project managers' theories-of-action for 'tailoring' projects to circumstances. Specifically, it is about S, the situational factors which managers feel to be of importance to them when thinking through their approach to new projects for new external clients. This paper will not deal with the desirable consequences which project managers seek for projects (C), the cause-effect assumptions (a, ... n) they make when planning projects, or the subsequent actions (A) they take to ensure that C is actually achieved. These components of project managers' theories-of-action will be looked at in a subsequent paper.

An additional ‘peg’ on which the present study can be hung is that of management and organizational sense-making (Thomas et al., 1993). In this framework, adaptation in organizations, from small-scale forms like changing a procedure, to large-scale forms like major strategic or structural change, is seen to entail three key processes: Scanning, Interpretation and Action. Collectively, these processes are described as organizational sensemaking. Scanning involves information gathering, and is usually considered to precede interpretation and action. Decision-makers usually have access to far more information than they can handle, so they must be selective in their choice of information. Interpretation is the process of fitting the information shown into some framework to support understanding and action. Action includes decision-making and implementation of the adaptation. The present study obviously relates to the scanning process.

## The questions to be answered

This paper attempts to answer two questions:

Question 1: Which characteristics of the customer, the application, and so on, do experienced information systems (IS) project managers see as being of importance to them when planning new development projects?

Question 2: How do these characteristics compare with the situational and other project characteristics claimed to be of importance in the IS project planning literature? In particular, how do these characteristics relate to accepted IS project ‘risk-drivers’?

## Method

A group of 14 experienced application systems developers, all located in Ireland, took part in the study. All managed bespoke software-intensive application development projects, originating from external clients. In the remainder of this paper, these developers will be referred to as the project managers (PMs).

All fourteen PMs had at least eight years experience of running projects for external clients. All but one were men. All were in the range 35–55 years of age. Ten of the 14 PMs were owner-directors of their companies. The remaining four PMs reported to an owner-director. The numbers of developers employed in their companies ranged from two to ten persons. The scale of the projects they typically managed fell within the ranges: (i) project duration: 2–18 months, (ii) project effort: 2–36 man-months and (iii) project team size: 1–6 people. All worked with mainstream, current technology. All were in the business of providing bespoke information systems ‘solutions’ to commercial clients.

To identify the factors which the PMs took into account when planning new projects, the technique of personal construct elicitation was used (Stewart and Stewart, 1981; Bannister and Fransella, 1989). A personal construct is a bi-polar distinction, or scale, which a particular individual uses when contrasting different people, objects, situations, and so on. People tend to have multiple sets of many interacting constructs to help them to make sense of the world. The task of identifying the set of constructs used by a person in a particular context is called personal construct elicitation. The technique described below is one of the standard approaches to construct elicitation.

The researcher began the session with each PM by gathering some basic information about the PM and his company. The PM was then asked to make a list of the systems development projects he/she had worked on as project manager. It was explained that the projects need not be very recent, nor need they yet have been completed, nor need they have been undertaken in the PM's current organization. If he/she could identify more than nine projects, he/she was asked to choose the three that were the most successful, the three that were the least successful, and three 'in-between'.

The researcher then said: 'Projects can differ from one another in terms of situational factors that developers must take into account when planning and running them. These factors could relate to the client, the "deliverable", the resources available, or whatever'.

The researcher then randomly chose three projects from the list and said: 'Tell me in what important ways two of these three projects were the same, but different from the third, in terms of important situational factors you had to deal with. Think as broadly as you need to'.

The PM was asked to repeat this task with different triads of projects, until no new situational constructs were being elicited. Finally, the researcher asked: 'Can you think of any other situational factors that you had to take into account when planning these projects?'

A typical construct elicitation session lasted for about one hour. The shortest session was about 30 minutes. The longest session was about 90 minutes. The minimum number of projects chosen by a PM was four. The maximum number of projects chosen was six. The modal number chosen was five. Most sessions went very well. Boredom was not apparent. Most respondents said they found the exercise intriguing.

The interview sessions were tape-recorded and then transcribed onto ‘hard-copy’. From the transcripts, the researcher extracted the constructs used by the PMs. In most cases, it was possible to document the constructs verbatim from the transcript. In some cases, it was necessary to do some minor editing of the transcript to remove hesitations etc. in all cases, the respondents were shown the final wording of their constructs, for approval or amendment. In almost all cases, the respondents said they were happy with the wording, so few amendments were required.

## Question 1: How did the PMs construe their projects?

A total of 201 constructs were collected from the fourteen PMs. The mean number of constructs identified by the PMs was 14.4. The minimum number identified was ten. The maximum was 19. The modal number of constructs identified was 13.

There was a considerable level of apparent overlap of constructs across the PMs. In the light of this, the researcher collected together constructs that, on the surface at least, seemed to be ‘saying the same thing’. In this way, sets of constructs were formed. If the researcher was at all unsure about including a construct in a set, the construct was allowed to constitute a set of one. For each set of constructs, the researcher attempted to construct a single, surrogate construct which it was felt captured the common meaning underlying the constructs in the set. When this exercise was completed, the researcher gave each PM a list of his/her original constructs, which he/she had already approved, together with the corresponding surrogate constructs. The PM was asked to confirm, or otherwise, that the wording of the surrogate constructs captured precisely the intended meaning of his/her own constructs. In the great majority of cases, respondents confirmed their happiness with the surrogate constructs. Where the PM felt that a surrogate construct did not accurately capture the intended meaning of one of his/her constructs, his/her construct was removed from its set, and allowed to stand-alone, with its original wording. This exercise of replacing sets of seemingly identical constructs with single surrogate constructs reduced the total number of constructs from 201 to 113.

## Question 2: How do the constructs relate to situational and other project characteristics highlighted in the IS project risk literature?

The project characteristics which seem to have received most attention in the literature relate to the complexity and uncertainty of user-requirements (Davis, 1982; Mathiassen and Stage, 1990; Fazlollahi and Tanniru, 1991; Saarinen and Vepsalainen, 1993). In essence, the advice is that as the level of uncertainty and/or complexity of user-requirements increases, one should move away from a linear development approach, and towards an evolutionary/prototyping approach. Boehm's Spiral Model of the software development process explicitly incorporates this advice (Boehm, 1988). As one might expect, requirements uncertainty and complexity are reflected strongly in a number of the construct themes.

Barki et al. (1993) conducted a wide review of the literature with a view to building a comprehensive inventory of variables which have been shown to be related to software development project risk. The scope of the literature they reviewed included both in-house development and bespoke development on behalf of external clients. They identified 35 variables, which they subsequently used as the basis for scales in a project risk-assessment instrument.

There seems to be a lot of commonality between the constructs elicited in the present study and the 'risk variables' Barki et al. (1993) found in the literature. In fact, there are quite a number of almost one-to-one correspondences. However, both the constructs and Barki's risk variables vary over a wide range of levels of abstraction. Thus it is impossible to make a strict one-to-one comparison of the risk variables and the personal constructs. To help with comparison, in Table 3 the risk variables Barki identified are assigned to the various themes in the personal constructs. A category ‘other’ has been added to capture risk variables which do not seem to fit under any of the construct themes.

On the surface at least, a number of themes running through the personal constructs do not appear to be represented in the risk variables. Perhaps the most striking apparent ‘gap’ is the absence of risk variables directly ‘tapping’ the level of the client’s apparent knowledge/understanding/clarity regarding system requirements. The fourteen personal constructs falling under this theme could be summarized thus: ‘They seem to know what they want, what they want seems to make sense, and they are able to express their “wants” in IT addressable terms.’ Of Barki’s risk variables, the closest in spirit to this theme seems to be the complexity of the task to be computerized.

The next set of constructs which seem to have no direct counterparts among the risk variables Barki et al. (1993) found in the risk literature are the 14 constructs relating to the distribution of control over the project. Some examples of constructs on this theme are: 'We will have some influence over the requirements/The requirements seem to be already set in stone'; 'If I feel uncomfortable at any point, I'll be able to say "stop" while the problem is addressed/If I feel uncomfortable at any point, I won't be able to say "stop"; 'The client is setting firm checkpoints and deadlines/The client is leaving it to us to set the pace.'

There seem to be no risk variables relating to the 'interface' to the client organization through which the developer must work. Seven constructs related to this theme. Examples of these constructs are: 'We will be dealing mainly with a single individual/We will be working mainly through a committee'; 'We will be working through the client's DP department/The project will be done directly for users.'

Four of the 14 developers in our study declared constructs dealing with the possibility of validating and testing the ‘solution’ before unveiling it to the client. Examples of these constructs are: ‘We will be able to show the client an early prototype or mock-up of the solution/We won't be able to show the client an early prototype or mock-up'; 'We can validly test our solution before showing it to the client/We will need a lot of input from the client to test our solution.' There appear to be no corresponding risk variables.

Other themes running through the personal constructs which do not appear to have corresponding risk variables concern the client's willingness and capability to handle implementation issues, the degree of freedom of choice of platform/environment available to the developer, and the criticality and degree of ‘reversibility’ of the roll-out of the new system.

In interpreting the above findings, it should be remembered that the method used in the present study was designed to elicit the situational factors which the developers took into account when thinking through their approach to the selected projects. The developers were not asked to restrict their responses to situational factors which contributed to project risk. Also, it should be noted that virtually all of the studies that Barki reviewed did not distinguish between projects conducted for in-house clients and projects conducted for external clients. Our study was confined to the latter. This fact could explain the presence in the personal constructs of themes relating to the client's ability to diagnose and express their requirements, the degree of control over the project in the hands of the developer, the 'breadth' of the interface with the client organization and the ease with which the developer's 'solution' can be validated before exposing it to the client.

Examining Table 3 from the other direction, a number of Barki's risk variables do not seem to be explicitly reflected in the personal constructs. These variables deal in the main with personal characteristics of the members of the developer's project team, the degree of technical complexity of aspects of the solution, and the adequacy of the resources available to the project. This does not imply that these aspects of a project are unimportant to our developers. These variables may not have been surfaced because of the particular projects which the developers chose for the construct elicitation process.

## Conclusions

The purpose of this paper has been to learn more about how software project managers construe new projects and their contexts. One hundred and thirteen different constructs were elicited from the 14 project managers. Twenty-one themes were identified in these constructs. As one might expect, the PMs' personal constructs reflect most of the risk-factors identified in the IS project management literature. But the PMs' constructs seem to contain many risk and subtle elaborations of these factors. Also, the constructs allude to a number of additional issues which seem to have received little or no treatment in the mainstream risk literature. Examples of apparent ‘omissions’ from the literature include aspects of ‘requirements uncertainty’ (very much a multi-dimensional notion, to judge by the constructs), the source and nature of control over the project, and aspects of the ‘interface’ between the developer and client organizations. It is hoped that there is new material in the constructs which will be of interest to IS risk modellers and to IS project management researchers.

When one steps back and takes a broad view of the constructs, what overall impression is gained? The constructs strongly suggest that the PMs see themselves as filling their roles of problem-diagnoser, problem-solver and change-agent on behalf of their clients. The constructs seem to capture many of the concerns one could expect to see exercising the mind of an organization development consultant ‘scouting-out’ a new intervention. As self-described ‘solution-providers’, this is not unexpected. So, training in organizational development and change-management skills is likely to be as relevant to PMs as is training in traditional project management skills.

Why use construct elicitation in this study? An alternative approach would have been to prepare a list of the situational characteristics which the literature suggests should be taken into account by PMs when planning new projects. PMs could then have been asked to identify the importance of each of these characteristics to them when setting-up a new project, and to add additional situational characteristics if they wished. This ‘check-list’ approach was not taken because it might have overly constrained the PM’s responses.

Given that it was decided to use construct elicitation, there is then the question of the number of elements (projects) to be used in the construction elicitation process with each PM. To use too few elements is to risk missing salient constructs. So what is an acceptable, minimum number of elements? Reger (1990) suggests that the use of 7–10 triads is sufficient to surface the most salient constructs in most domains. This seems to imply that a minimum of four elements (projects) should be used in a construct elicitation session. The present study achieved or exceeded this minimum number of elements in all sessions.

Perhaps the PMs mentioned some of the constructs they did, not because they really ‘used’ these constructs, but because they felt they would look foolish if they didn’t mention these. Or perhaps they deliberately didn’t mention some of the constructs they actually used, because to mention these might make

them look bad in some light. Ajzen (1988) makes the point that, in reporting on their own behaviour, people tend to over estimate the extent of their ‘socially-desirable’ behaviour, and to under-estimate the extent of their ‘socially undesirable’ behaviour. For behaviour which is ‘socially neutral’, self-reports tend to be unbiased. Given the nature of the topic, there seems to be no reason to suspect the presence of either bias in the constructs.

All of the PMs involved in this study were based in Ireland. Could it be that the findings are country/culture specific? The many close correspondences between the constructs elicited in this study and the risk variables identified in the international software engineering literature suggest that this is not the case. But clearly, this question can only be answered definitively by replicating the study elsewhere.

## Biographical notes

Tony Moynihan has worked as a professor in the School of Computer Applications at Dublin City University for the last thirteen years. Before that, he worked as a Senior Management Specialist in IT at the Irish Management Institute. And before that, in industry as a programmer and systems analyst. His teaching interest is systems analysis and design methods. His research interests include software project management and the development of tools to support software project managers. He has an MSc in computer science and a PhD in statistics from Dublin University.
