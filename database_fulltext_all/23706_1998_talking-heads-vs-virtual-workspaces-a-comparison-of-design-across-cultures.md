# Talking heads vs. virtual workspaces: a comparison of design across cultures

LORNA HEATON

Department of Communication, University of Montreal, C.P. 6024, Succursale Centre-ville, Montreal, Quebec H3C 3T4, Canada

The past decade has seen the development of a perspective holding that technology is socially constructed. This paper examines the social construction of one group of technologies, systems for computer supported cooperative work (CSCW). It compares the design of systems for computer supported cooperative work (CSCW) in Scandinavia and Japan with particular attention to the influence of culture on the resulting products. Two case studies are presented to illustrate the argument that culture is an important factor in technology design, despite commonly held assumptions about the neutrality and objectivity of science and technology. The paper further proposes an explanation for why, despite similar technical backgrounds and research interests, CSCW design is conducted differently and produces different results in Denmark and Japan. It argues that, by looking at CSCW systems as texts which reflect the context of their production and the society from which they come, we may be better able to understand the transformations that operate when these texts are 'read' in the contexts of their implementation.

## Introduction

Although many engineers may continue to support the position that the technologies they build are neutral, it has become something of a commonplace in the social sciences to say that technology is socially constructed. In recent years, numerous instances of how technical artefacts embody political, cultural or economic positions have been identified (see for example the collections edited by Bijker et al., 1987; Bijker and Law, 1992; Winner, 1993).

The challenge for social science, in our view, is to go a step further to examine how this process of social construction is accomplished and to determine which aspects of the black box called ‘technology’ are more or less susceptible to social influences. This paper attempts to do just that for one technology. It compares the design of systems for computer supported cooperative work (CSCW) in Scandinavia and Japan with particular attention to the influence of culture on the design process and product. Given the complexity of the subject matter, and the small number of laboratories actually involved in CSCW design, we adopted a case study approach as an appropriate means of capturing the subtleties of the multitude of situational variables and their interaction. Extensive field observation and interviews were supplemented with document analysis and secondary source data.

CSCW is a particularly appropriate object for this type of inquiry since it is generally recognized as a field which spans a number of boundaries and integrates a variety of perspectives, ranging from those of hard science (engineering) to social science and even philosophy. In the context of this paper, CSCW has been broadly defined as: work by multiple active subjects sharing a common object and supported by information technology. The focus of computer supported cooperative work is less on working with computers than on working with each other through computers. The social ‘content’ of a CSCW system is thus much greater that that of, say, a toaster or even a television. On the other hand, one cannot make abstraction of the very real technical knowledge and constraints that go into building a working system. We believe that the intensive intertwining of the social and the technical in CSCW opens the door to a real contribution from social scientists to understanding the complex relationship between technology and its context of emergence and implementation.

This paper suggests that CSCW systems, like all technologies, can be read as texts. These technological texts contain some elements which are distinctive to their culture of origin, without necessarily being unique to that context. It further offers a plausible explanation for these design choices, basing its argument in the discourse of designers themselves. It draws on the notion of technological frame (Bijker and Law, 1992) to explain how Japanese CSCW designers invoke Japanese culture in general and certain aspects in particular as resources upon which to found technical decisions, illustrating the translation of these ‘cultural’ arguments in CSCW systems. Similarly, Scandinavian designers consistently invoke a group of concepts centring on democracy (user involvement, participation, etc.) to justify certain of their design choices and their systems reflect these democratic ideals.

## Patterns in CSCW Research

A quick survey of the CSCW literature points to an amazing variety of ‘solutions’ or approaches to similar problems. What is more, these solutions seem to follow certain patterns. Not only are there very real differences between the various communities of practice involved in CSCW, the field also demonstrates marked regional differences in emphasis and perspective. A systematic review of the CSCW and European CSCW conference proceedings over the past decade (Heaton, 1997) documents a number of general patterns in how CSCW researchers present their work to the international academic community of their peers (see Table 1 below).

Presentations coming out of Japan illustrate a considerable homogeneity in research interests. All the research presented at international CSCW conferences has centred on the exploration of the possibilities of video, multimedia, and large screen displays. Gesture has a major importance, as does shared view of workspaces. The ‘talking head’ interface so prevalent in teleconferencing applications is seen as unable to support the wide variety of contextual cues necessary to produce a virtual workspace. Moreover, Japanese work tends to present solutions which are technically innovative and which require major investments of technical resources (high bandwidth communication channels, large flat screen displays, a number of video cameras, etc.), and Japanese researchers readily admit to their technical focus and product orientation.

In contrast, video-mediated communication is largely absent in Scandinavian work, which typically focuses on organizational issues. While seeing one's colleagues may be desirable, this takes second place to a suitable framework in which to work; a talking head (i.e. a small view of the partner's head and shoulders) or even an iconic representation is sufficient in most cases. Scandinavian CSCW work is typically presented in the form of cases in which designers have been active participants, and cooperative design, supporting users in their daily work, and looking at work as situated in a specific context are common themes.

The question remains: how can we explain that designers, who have similar technical knowledge and professional backgrounds, choose to explore different issues or questions, and, what is more, appear to answer them in different ways? This is all the more astonishing given the fact that they identify themselves as members of the same research community and are in regular contact with designers from various countries and institutions. Clearly differences between communities of practice alone cannot explain these differences in orientation. Grudin (1991a, 1991b) has outlined a number of partial explanations: institutional support, funding, even cultural norms; others have applied an actor-network approach to analyse the political and cultural regimes in which design is embedded in specific cases (Gärtner and Wagner 1994; Hakken, 1994). This paper adds a comparative perspective with a specific focus on culture as one element. It seeks to ground observations of regional differences in CSCW focus and design process in social theory, and uses field research to provide concrete illustrations.

## Research question and method

We began by asking to what extent different research preoccupations in different countries are the result of different ‘cultural constructions of computing?’ In other words: (a) How do CSCW designers translate their ideas about what people do when they work, and the role of computers in supporting work, into the systems they design? (b) What is the impact of the circumstances in which designers find themselves, on the systems they design? Circumstances here include the larger institutional context, as well as daily work practices, which serve as both resources and constraints on what can be done. While they provide structure, these resources and constraints should not be taken to be immutable.

By asking how ideas and circumstances affect action, we are in fact raising broader issues concerning the relationship between technology and context. As such, this research is part of a nascent body of work struggling to come to terms with this question of growing significance given increasing globalization and the increasing impact of technology (computer-based or not) in our lives (Hales, 1994; Orlikowski and Gash, 1994; Jackson, 1996). It calls for an understanding of how technological artefacts are constructed and how the end result relates to its conditions of construction.

The research presented in this paper is drawn from a larger, comparative study on CSCW systems design (Heaton, 1997). It focuses on one object: computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW), one stage in the process: design, and two different cultural contexts: Scandinavia and Japan. As illustrated above and further supported by data from studies of national characteristics of attitudes to work such as that of Hofstede (1980), there is evidence that these regions have different approaches to work in general and to the design of CSCW systems in particular. Also, they are with North America and Britain the major areas where CSCW research is taking place. They share certain similarities, in that they are relatively small, homogeneous regions (at least when compared with the US). The choice of Japan was to some extent a personal one. Having lived and worked there, the researcher felt that she might more easily approach and understand the workings of a Japanese laboratory than a complete outsider, and that this hybrid perspective could be interestingly applied to the research object.

An interpretive approach was judged most appropriate, given the types of issues explored. We were less interested in determining what designers' culture(s) are actually like than in examining what 'culture' means to our informants and how they construct and reflect it in the way they formulate problems, use representations, etc. Further, given the small size of the CSCW community, it appeared that there would be little to gain from a survey or quantitative approach, and that richer insights might be gleaned from case material.

The researcher is painfully aware that a comparative approach generally requires comparing like things in order to build a coherent whole, however. In our choice of sites and cases for study, we were concerned that the projects be as similar to each other as possible in order to ensure maximum comparability (functional equivalence), although it would be absurd to suppose that, in a field as complex as CSCW, one might find exactly comparable projects.

Within each geographical area, the specific sites were carefully selected. As far as possible, we chose laboratories having demonstrated long term commitment to research in this area, and 'first generation' projects with a view to facilitating interpretation, especially of contextual elements and continuity over time. Following work by Lemaine et al. (1976) on patterns in the emergence of scientific disciplines, we hypothesized that early projects would be less subject to cross-influences from other CSCW research and that it might be easier to identify initial, more culturally dependent inspirations or assumptions (the path isn't as muddy).

(Lemaine, et al. 1976, pp 1-19, suggest that researchers in a new field tend initially to work on loosely defined but similar problems, often unaware of others' work and research results. With the growth of a communication network and the establishment of informal contacts, there is growing consensus; specialized journals are launched, the proportion of references to authors not engaged centrally in the field declines, and a body of 'classics' to be cited regularly is determined. As the field develops, researchers tend to divide it up into research 'clusters' in order to minimize overlap and competition and to ensure exploration of a wide range of issues. This corresponds with our reading of the development of CSCW.) Secondly, there are definite advantages to comparing projects of the same generation in a field which is developing rapidly. Finally, we hoped to find more meat in five years' data than in projects with shorter histories. There were, of course, practical constraints, particularly in Japan. To some extent, we went where we were allowed access.

Earlier typologies of cultures, particularly as they have been applied to the world of work, were used as a starting point and a general guide for observation, although no attempt was made to fit the data gathered into these classificatory schemes. The unity of our subject matter was further reinforced by the fact that we visited site after site, without a break. Of course, the ultimate continuity in this research is the researcher herself. This provides a unifying perspective and gives meaning to the term ‘interpretive research’. We do not pretend to have the definitive interpretation of what we saw, and have taken pains throughout to position ourselves with relation to our sites and our informants. In this respect, the perspective presented here is definitely situated and embodied.

Concretely, data were gathered during five months of observation in several CSCW laboratories (three months in two different Scandinavian laboratories and two months in Japan, based in one laboratory, with visits to several others). Although the time available was insufficient to do a ‘proper’, participant observer ethnographic study (the researcher was not part of the design teams), it was long enough to develop a good idea of how design work is actually organized, as well as of the various contingencies operating at each site.

In addition to observation, the author interviewed over 20 CSCW designers and took part in numerous informal conversations with others involved in CSCW research. The choice of informants was simple: we interviewed all researchers and a selection of graduate students involved in the lab and the CSCW projects studied. This interview sampling thus reflects the actual composition of the laboratories: almost exclusively male (only one female researcher, and three other female informants were interviewed), aged for the most part in their forties. Despite this relative homogeneity, there were occasional differences in perspective on some issues. Our interviews enabled us to note minor differences in perception and emphasis between the accounts of members of the same design team; even if they coauthor an article, they do not necessarily see everything the same way. These differences were not significant enough to merit discussion in this paper, however, and we feel it is justified to treat the design teams as a homogeneous block. For instance, when asked specifically about the impact of being female on her work and working relationships, our female informant (who is familiar with feminist scholarship) did not see her gender as a significant factor, beyond perhaps a concern with human relations, which she ascribed more to her personality than to gender. Nor did we see any evidence of gender effects in the design process we observed.

In Denmark, since a test user group is an integral part of the design process in the laboratory studied, we also had informal conversations with a number of the eventual end users of the CSCW systems being designed. Only one of our informants was a native English speaker, but all the Scandinavians and some of the Japanese were completely fluent. Except for two interviews conducted in Japanese, a number in a mixture of Japanese and English, and one in French, all were conducted in English. Nevertheless, our informants' non-standard, original use of English, made the benefits of close textual analysis of transcriptions doubtful.

Analysis of documents produced by the laboratories in question was also an important part of the process. Some of these documents described the CSCW systems, while others were explanatory in nature. Both internal (working documents, memoranda, project reports) and external documents (scientific publications) were analysed. In addition to using them for the factual information they provide, documents were approached as traces, a series of partial reflections with which to paint as precise as possible a portrait of the CSCW system and its evolution in sociocultural context. The focus was double: to understand how designers perceive their work through what they say and write about it, and to analyse the work itself – work practices (including reporting practices), and the resulting machines and software – the goal being to draw parallels between the two.

## Culture and cultural frames

Although the focus of this paper is on comparing Japanese and Scandinavian CSCW design, this should not be taken to imply simply a discussion of national culture. While a number of studies on work organization and work attitudes have consistently demonstrated significant differences across national cultures, organizational and professional cultures are also vital elements in the mix, as will become clear in the discussion of our cases. Erez and Earley (1993, p. 69) cite a number of empirical studies which suggest that national or societal culture must be considered along with organizational culture in order to gain a fuller understanding of the relation of an organization's culture to its functioning.

Another body of literature has examined differences in attitudes, values and practices between professions. A person's occupation or training undoubtedly has a major influence on how he or she approaches the world. For example, computer scientists likely draw on a similar pool of knowledge and techniques relative to systems development, which in turn calls for and constitutes a particular way of looking at the world. Although the training of computer scientists in Scandinavia, Japan and North America may also differ significantly in terms of 'peripheral' components, with consequent implications for how they see their role. See Dalhlbom and Mathiassen (1993) for a detailed description of the mechanistic, rational worldviews implicit in computer science and systems development. Professional culture becomes a central concern as soon as communication between communities of practice becomes necessary. The notions of communities of practice, boundary practices and boundary objects have been explored by a number of authors, including Star and Griesemer (1989), Brown and Duguid (1991, 1994), Wenger (1994). Systems engineers may be operating from one set of assumptions, while those studying the work practices the system is designed to support or supplant may have a fundamentally different perception of the task at hand, and those who initiated the project (upper management, unions, etc.) yet other objectives and perceptions.

Ulf Hannerz (1992, p. 249) has coined the term transnational cultures, which he defines as 'structures of meaning carried by social networks which are not wholly based in any single territory'. Many transnational cultures are occupational, and Hannerz argues that their real significance lies in their mediating possibilities, since they are 'penetrable to various degrees by the local meanings carried in settings and by participants in particular situations' (1992, p. 251), but also provide points of contact between different territorial cultures.

The important point here is that organizational, occupational and national cultures are simultaneously distinct and interrelated. Those involved in CSCW system design share a common 'CSCW culture' (or, more appropriately, a mix of CSCW influences), but they also reflect and interpret this professional culture within the framework of their territorial cultures, just as professional training and perspectives lead them to interpret elements of territorial culture in certain ways. A given situation, say the design of a particular CSCW system, can be understood in cultural terms as the product of what is unique (national culture) and what is shared by all (occupational culture), enacted in a particular local context or organization (organizational culture). The resulting combination will necessarily differ between cultures and even between systems, because conditions can never be identical.

For the purposes of this research, culture has been defined as a dynamic mix of national/geographic, organizational, and professional or disciplinary elements in constant interaction with one another. We wish to avoid discussion of norms and values as social facts that influence design choices in predetermined ways. Culture changes according to context and over time, and should be understood not in terms of pre-existing, fixed categories, but 'as resources, accumulations of actions, patterns which constitute, reinforce and transform social life. In short, culture is contitually constructed and reconstructed. It is embodied and enacted through the actions of individuals who are the 'product' of multiple cultures and influences.

## Culture in the frames of CSCW researchers

The notion of technological frame provides an interesting way of approaching culture from a constructivist perspective. Law and Bijker (1992, p. 301) use the notion to refer to 'the concepts, techniques and resources used in a community – any community... It is thus a combination of explicit theory, tacit knowledge, general engineering practice, cultural values, prescribed testing procedures, devices, material networks, and systems used in a community.' It is simultaneously technical and social, intrinsically heterogeneous. The related expression 'frame of meaning' as coined by Collins and Pinch (1982) and adopted by Carlson (1992, p. 177) in his study of Edison and the development of motion pictures, more closely translates the specific focus of this paper on how cultural patterns and assumptions inform actions and shape choices:

...in any given culture there are many ways in which a technology may be successfully used ... To select from among these alternatives, individuals must make assumptions about who will use a technology and the meanings users might assign to it. These assumptions constitute a frame of meaning inventors and entrepreneurs use to guide their efforts at designing, manufacturing, and marketing their technological artifacts. Such frames thus directly link the inventor's unique artifact with larger social or cultural values.

Carlson argues that designers attempt to impose pre-existing frames based on previous experience on new products or invention, rather than inventing new frames. This unconscious process of ‘cultural creep’ results because designers create artefacts to fit into the cultural spaces suggested by their existing frames of meaning. It is only after their introduction that new uses and new cultural meanings are developed. Thus, users are present ‘virtually’ in designers’ frames, whether or not an artefact has actually been used (Flichy, 1995) or even produced.

The body of this paper reports on two specific cases which are illustrative of larger tendencies and trends in CSCW design in Scandinavia and Japan. While processual elements are outlined, it focuses primarily on the relationship between designers' justifications for their choices and how these choices are reflected in the design of machines and software. Throughout this report the researchers involved are identified with capital letters e.g. BR, KY etc.

## The Scandinavian approach: quality of working life.

## Case 1: AZ University, Denmark

The development of CSCW in Scandinavia has been highly influenced by the long history of working life democracy in that region, particularly the resurgence of this concern for industrial democracy in the late 1960s and early 1970s, which led to laws on quality of working life and workers' rights to self-determination. It also produced a number of research projects initiated by trade unions on how to involve wage earners in the design and implementation of the tools and machines they would use in their work (Sandberg, 1989). The most influential of these projects was UTOPIA (1981 to 1985), which involved a number of the people now active in CSCW at AZ University. The focus in UTOPIA on concrete, local conditions and practical experience led to the development of a number of methods for communicating in the design process. Implicit in the approach was the need to try out tools; experiments, prototyping and the involvement of those who have the tacit knowledge and skills, the 'end-users', were therefore essential.

Over the past fifteen years, a small group of researchers in the Computer Science Department of AZ University has worked extensively to develop the cooperative design techniques pioneered in the UTOPIA project. They have developed sets of techniques or 'toolboxes' of methods for doing design that they apply in all their projects (see Greenbaum and Kyng, 1991). With the advent of CSCW, this group began exploring CSCW systems development from this perspective. In 1995, the group consisted of three professors, two of whom also worked on the UTOPIA project, one post-doctoral fellow, two PhD students, and occasionally some programmers.

Given the historical and political tradition of UTOPIA, it is not surprising that the work of AZ University's CSCW group has a strong ideological component. Indeed, the group is always careful to situate its work in the continuity of this tradition; virtually all the texts it produces refer explicitly to the trade union roots of systems development in Scandinavia. While this package of beliefs and principles is forcefully promoted at AZ University, it is not limited to this site and we found evidence of it throughout Scandinavia (Heaton, 1997). In the introduction to his thesis, for example, Grönbaek (1991) (who did not participate in UTOPIA) states certain of his assumptions 'as a Danish citizen possessing certain political points of view'.

Firstly, workers have fundamental democratic rights to influence the conditions under which they are working. This implies that users of technology, such as computer systems, have democratic rights to influence the development and use of technology for their workplace. Secondly, the goal of developing new technology for a workplace should be to increase the quality of both work processes and resulting products. This implies that computer systems should enhance both the individual and collective skills of workers rather than substitute human skills with automatized procedures. (Grönbaek, 1991, p. 10)

The discourse on involving users, and on design as a cooperative activity between designers and users, is omnipresent in the AZ CSCW group. A number of techniques, such as future workshops and metaphorical design, have been developed to get participants to understand current practices in new ways. These techniques would not be out of place in a group dynamics context, but their use in the design of computer systems is striking. They are perceived as essential by the group, however, since the introduction of new technology is seen as an opportunity for organizational change. Brainstorming and role-playing activities go hand in hand with cooperative prototyping, a cyclical process which often starts with mock-ups, simulations and 'throw-away' prototypes to clarify requirements, and produces increasingly realistic and appropriate prototypes. Unlike laboratory design, this approach emphasizes the benefits of interaction between designers (who contribute technical knowledge and experience) and users (who bring to bear knowledge of current practices).

Another defining characteristic of AZ's approach is the insistence on supporting work in the actual contexts, being responsive to local needs. They reject categorically the possibility of modelling or designing from theory, since 'the system development process does not follow a rational ideal and thus cannot be accomplished according to given guidelines. Every development project is unique and development processes are contingent on the context in which they are situated.' Grönbaek (1991, p. 10) writes 'I don't . . . believe in modelling as a way of doing design.' (BR)

Many of the ideological stances of the AZ group are also reflected in the way they organize their work. The group's organization is democratic and nonhierarchical; although the senior team member is largely responsible for defining the direction the group takes, the group prefers to interpret this as a division of responsibilities according to the strengths of each member of the group, rather than a leader/subordinate relationship.

Working together is another important aspect of working in CSCW at AZ. The group members place a high priority on doing things together. KY explains: 'I think that in all the things that we have done, we have tried to find a vision shared among a lot of people because it's more fun to achieve a lot together with other people than do exactly what you think is the right thing to do with a few people.' BR concurs: 'We don't work together all the time but we do take care to do, to cooperate a lot. And I sense that we all feel that this is very important and none of us really want to be left alone doing our own piece of research.'

In short, relationships between team members are characterized by flexibility, informality and equality. Because they share common assumptions and know each other and each other's interests and capabilities well, there is little need for formal meetings or organization. Their offices are very close to each other, and they see each other daily, popping into each other's offices to settle details as they arise.

Despite different degrees of interest in the social or technical aspects of CSCW, all the members of AZ University's CSCW group are trained computer scientists. Members of the group tend to feel that they can embody different disciplinary perspectives inherent in the field of CSCW, and take on all aspects of problems, ranging from the extremely 'social' (organizational development) to the highly 'technical' (as in a new type of hypermedia), themselves. As they see it, the holistic approach to system development using participatory design is incompatible with thinking about things in terms of sociotechnical 'divides'.

I find it quite difficult to appreciate what that Great Divide is a divide between.... I think you have to come up with ways of working in the specific projects where you try to reconcile all the various contributions in one fashion or the other. I think that's a more fruitful way of looking at it. (BR)

The democratic ideals of AZ's CSCW group also impact significantly on what they design, as the following two examples illustrate. Despite widely different contexts, scope and technical complexity, both attempt to integrate new technology into the workplace by setting up frameworks for collaboration. What is more, these frameworks are designed to be as flexible as possible so that workers will be able to use the computer system as a tool to accomplish what they want, in the way they want, thereby increasing their self-determination and enhancing their quality of working life.

The first example is a project which involved customizing Word Perfect to support work at the local branch of the National Labor Inspection Service. The AZ group 'found the organization first without any idea whatsoever to what computer support might be developed. That was up to the project to find out.' Although the researchers were interested primarily in applying the diversity of techniques developed in earlier projects within a single project, 'as it turned out, we made a lot else than just making computer support. Organizational change was the prime issues that were worked through (MN).

Starting in 1990, the project followed the typical AZ pattern: an initial period of interviews and observation, followed by an initial prototype, based on the current paper forms used by inspectors. This exploratory phase wound up with a three-day seminar, during which 11 employees and the researchers explored organizational issues and made mock-up prototypes. The next step was to develop an ‘action plan’ covering both proposed organizational (introducing semiautonomous groups integrating a variety of professionals) and technological (replacing the central computer and terminals with networked personal computers) changes.

The bulk of the researchers' involvement focused on the introduction of personal computers. In addition to teaching employees how to use word processing software and the graphical interface, the researchers 'tailored' the purchased applications: configuring the network, developing templates for form letters, macros, etc. Finally, in co-operation with two inspectors and two secretaries, they continued prototyping to explore some of the more advanced potential uses.

The organization continued to develop its own system after the researchers left the project in 1993, with more advanced users designing button panels, macros and standard forms for their colleagues to use. This ongoing design (tailoring) would appear to be the ultimate success from the Aarhus researchers' standpoint: workers have assumed full responsibility for ongoing technical development, and development activity remains completely embedded in a professional work life.

The second example is a much larger scale, more technical project, under the aegis of the European Community Esprit programme. Its aim was to develop generic CSCW applications, giving this project a more product-oriented focus than any of AZ's previous CSCW work. The group's technical contribution to this multipartner project was the design of a hypermedia system to link various third-party applications.

The project's test site was one of Europe's largest technical projects, a complex multi-site construction project involving approximately 400 employees and consultants. The major problem, as the group saw it, was the mismatch between several large independent, vertical systems for various tasks: time management and tracking progress, financial management, quality control, management of approximately 35 000 drawings, and a daily journal system, and the complex interrelation of the various elements in daily work. 'As the analysis at the GB proceeded we saw that . . . the main thing they needed to get their daily working cooperation going was tools to integrate the heterogeneity of the systems they had in their branch there' (GK).

Although the project objective was not the development of a system for the construction project in particular, the AZ CSCW group insisted on the importance of analysing a specific site. They approached the GB with a period of initial analysis, including site visits, interviews and study of documentation, followed by the generation of ‘visions’ on how to overcome identified problems and bottlenecks, and a period of iterative prototyping.

The first primitive prototype of the hypermedia showed some of the possibilities for supporting a networked structure of the material. GB's response was enthusiastic, and the AZ team began working on the actual hypermedia in 1991. It is important to note that the focus of the hypermedia changed considerably as a result of feedback and ideas developed in workshops at the Great Belt.

In the beginning we thought that we were going to focus very much on editing, multiple authors editing, writing a report together. But as the analysis at the GB proceeded we saw that ... the main thing they needed to get their daily working cooperation going was tools to integrate the heterogeneity of the systems they had in their branch there. (GK)

Once the requirements had been generated, much of the work actually developing the hypermedia took place in the laboratory. A series of prototypes followed, with periodic visits to the site to test out intuitions.

The resulting design enables workers to continue using the software applications they are familiar with. It allows the whole organization, a department or a smaller group of people to share materials and networks of links. The link system is tailorable, so that users can define their own links and their own way of navigating through data and between previously distinct software applications. For example, a worker can follow a link from a sentence in some minutes describing an anomaly in casting a bridge element to a set of photographs of the element in question and the construction drawing. The result thus more closely mirrors the way one might want to consult documents and switch from one to another. In short, the hypermedia was designed with a maximum of flexibility in mind, as one 'building block' to be used with others to help users move smoothly between individual and cooperative work and between different tasks throughout their day. It provides a framework which structures collaborative group work, while taking into account individual users' comfort.

## The Japanese Approach: Quality (and quantity) of work

## Case 2: MY University Lab, Japan

Our second case examines briefly MY laboratory in the Instrumentation and Engineering Department of a prestigious private university located near Tokyo. Workplace hierarchy is omnipresent in MY laboratory, as it was at all the CSCW laboratories visited in Japan, and it is accepted as a fact of life. Two professors supervise some 50 graduate students. Decision-making and the definition of priorities within the laboratory is unproblematic: the senior professor (invariably a man) decides and everyone, including the associate professor, obeys. Students identify themselves as students of Professor MY and therefore members of his laboratory (which bears his name), and there is a clear ranking of seniority, based on age, among them.

Design work is done exclusively in the laboratory, and any evaluation takes the form of controlled laboratory experiments. The focus is on technical design proficiency, giving students a basic grounding in instrumentation engineering that will be filled out with on-the-job training in the companies which eventually hire them. As one might expect in an engineering department, the base criterion for judging a system is whether or not it works. There are no end users although, because of the educational context, there is a sort of iterative prototyping of student projects based on feedback from the professors and senior students.

The MAJIC system developed at MY University laboratory illustrates many research themes characteristic of Japanese CSCW. To a large extent, MAJIC builds on earlier Japanese work on eye contact and gaze awareness (Ishii, 1990; Ishii and Miyake, 1991; Ishii et al., 1993), adding a multiparticipant dimension and a more explicit focus on the surrounding environment. This relationship to earlier work is both professional and personal: one of the designers told us specifically that he was influenced by Ishii's work on gaze awareness and that another of the Clearboard designers was his sempai (upperclassman) at university. The MAJIC team explains clearly why they feel this line of inquiry is important:

When we have discussions in face-to-face situations and people approve of a statement, we can tell by their attitude, tone, eye movements, gestures and so forth, whether or not they approve wholeheartedly. It is difficult, on the other hand, to estimate how strongly they approve when we read only the minutes without attending a meeting. Hence, one of the purposes and/or advantages of face-to-face meetings is that all of the participants are aware of the speaker's intent and the other listeners' reactions based on both verbal and nonverbal communication. (Okada et al., 1994, p. 385)

The MAJIC designers refer repeatedly to the importance of context, orientation to the other (how what you say is being received), and a focus on interpretation of intention rather than surface meaning. The key design issues for MAJIC were defined as (i) support of multiway round-table meetings and multiple eye contact; (ii) maintenance of peripheral gaze awareness; (iii) seamless presentation of life-size images of participants to achieve a sense of reality; and (iv) a shared work space (Okada et al., 1994, p. 385).

The creation of a seamless environment and sense of presence in MAJIC relies extensively on nonverbal behavioural information, such as eye contact, gaze awareness, gesture and body language, and on contextual cues such as image size, perceived distance and background. References to these elements are extremely specific. For example, the MAJIC team refers to symmetrical or asymmetrical postures and body orientations as important cues: 'In this way we sense the atmosphere in the meeting room and the aura of the participants, and, consequently, we can understand the opinions of the participants clearly and make the meeting productive' (Okada et al., 1994, p. 386). They cite gaze as a means of controlling a meeting: 'A chairperson sometimes gazes at participants to urge them to speak when there is silence in a meeting' (p. 386), and discuss the social uses of eye contact: 'Of course eye contact is very important in communicating with one another, as mentioned above, but especially in Japan it is impolite to look into someone's eyes for a long time' (p. 387). In their observations of face-to-face meetings, the designers noted that participants most commonly averted their eyes by looking down at material on a table in front of them, and decided to incorporate such a table in their design (p. 390).

The central element of MAJIC is a large (4 feet × 8 feet), curved semitransparent screen. Each MAJIC unit also contains a workstation (with a recessed, tilted monitor), two video projectors, two video cameras, two directional microphones and two loudspeakers. Video images of the participants are projected on to the screen and captured from behind it. Each participant sees the frontal view of the others and the edges of the images overlap slightly. In actual fact, the backgrounds must be 'matched' at the seam. But this is only a prototype; MAJIC proposes doing away with the actual background altogether and replacing it with an artificial one that can be chosen to create a desired mood, to relax or to inspire (p. 386 and personal communication). This would be done using a chromakey blue background. The researchers hypothesize that 'if users are surrounded by other participants with a seamless background, they can feel as though they are together' (p. 386). In a similar vein, they have been experimenting with elements which may affect 'virtual distance', the sensed distance among participants. In this sense, MAJIC represents a curious mix of virtual or imaginary space and an interest in closely simulating reality, including providing direct physical feedback whenever possible.

With so technical a focus, it is not surprising that the main justifications for design choices are technical ones. There is however, another, more social, element to Japanese design choices, that of Japanese culture. Japanese CSCW researchers, like most Japanese people, clearly believe that Japanese culture and the Japanese way of working are different from the Western ways (see Mouer and Sugimoto (1986) for a thorough history of the ideology of Japanese uniqueness). How to reflect or cope with this difference when designing technology is a constant leitmotif among Japanese CSCW researchers. They must continually try to reconcile this tension between their professional engineering or computer science culture, which tells them that science and technology are culturally neutral or universal, and their Japanese intuitions and knowledge of the society in which they live.

On top of this is the context in which they work: generally for companies whose objective is to produce and sell products. This institutional context goes a long way towards explaining the product life cycle approach and the absence of contact between designers and potential users. It also influences some design choices. For instance, we have already noted that much Japanese work incorporates video and other high-tech components. This is less surprising when we consider that it is the major electronics companies that are building CSCW systems; state-of-the-art components produced in-house are thus more readily available (or at lesser cost) for experimenting and testing out research intuitions. NTT, the dominant Japanese telecommunications company, has been a leader in CSCW design, and its solutions are very video-intensive. One NTT researcher there told me that 'NTT's policy is to waste bandwidth' (YI), a strategic choice when you consider that if NTT can sell their bandwidth-intensive systems, their customers will be generating revenues for the phone system. And a researcher working for a major electronics firm had this to say about NTT's CSCW research: 'Yes they have to promote broadband ISDN (Integrated Service Digital Network) so I think he made such research. The requirements are different to us and to him' (WE).

Japanese designers must keep in mind that their work should ultimately generate profits for their companies. But there is something of a dilemma here: while the Japanese market for CSCW systems is potentially enormous, electronics companies have traditionally followed a policy of export-led growth. When CSCW systems leave the laboratory and hit the market, they should be suitable for American and European customers and organizations, even if they have been designed by Japanese or with Japanese workers in mind. The researchers we interviewed were divided in their interpretations of how this affected their work and design choices. Some state that they are designing for anyone and everyone, others that what they design is most suitable for the Japanese way of working. Most insisted that they are simply researchers doing their jobs and trying to build the best possible system, and that marketing the result is someone else's problem. 'I am a researcher. I like to do research. If I can no longer concentrate on interesting research, I will leave' (KA).

There is also another kind of profitability associated with doing research – what sells academically in the international CSCW community. Japanese companies have been willing to invest in ‘promoting’ the work of their laboratories, for example by investing in the production values of demonstration videos presented at international conferences’. The positive reception of early Japanese CSCW work by the CSCW community has likely had some impact on decisions by other teams to follow similar lines of work, as well as to focus on innovative, imaginative solutions. The resulting Japanese CSCW ‘style’ may thus be both reflective of what researchers feel is appropriate for supporting Japanese work groups and of what they have been praised for in the past by members of their professional community (and which has positive consequences for them in their companies).

If all agree that Japanese are different and that Japanese groupware must reflect cultural particularities if it is to be successfully implemented, let us pursue precisely what is different, according to CSCW researchers, and how this may impact on their work. The dean of groupware in Japan, Professor MY, cites five specifically cultural reasons why he believes groupware must be different if it is to be used in Japan: cultural differences in views on co-operation versus competition, negotiation style, degree of context, the importance of human relations, and the relation of the individual to the group (MY). While his was the most concise, systematic listing, the same elements came up again and again in our interviews and conversations with other Japanese designers. These points are borne out by our observations and by secondary source data.

Characterizations of Japan as a society in which human relations are all-important, relationships are dependent on positioning people on vertical (hierarchy) and horizontal (in- or out-group) axes, and where communication is highly indexical or context-dependent have been widely discussed in the business and sociological literature on Japan (see for example Stewart, 1987; Ito, 1989; Barnlund, 1989 specifically on interpersonal communication in organizations). The extent of agreement in the literature suggests that they are firmly grounded in reality.

## Comparison and discussion

The specific cases discussed above are illustrative of larger tendencies in Scandinavia and Japan. Figure 1 summarizes the major points of comparison between CSCW design in these regions. While only two laboratories have been discussed in this paper, the figure is based on document analysis of all CSCW work presented in international forums (CSCW and ECSCW conferences) from 1988 to 1997 by Scandinavian and Japanese laboratories, as well as in-depth study of two Scandinavian university laboratories and six CSCW laboratories in Japan (two university and four industrial).

CSCW researchers have entered this relatively new field from different perspectives: from a highly politicized action research tradition, focused on enhancing the quality of working life in Scandinavia, and from the electronics, engineering and telecommunications fields in Japan. In Scandinavia, supporting workers in what they are doing is a key justification for CSCW activities. At AZ University, for instance, starting from a use perspective to give users a tool which will enable them to do their work suggests a holistic approach in which users are involved in the specific contexts of their jobs. Indeed, over time, participatory design has come to be closely identified with the ‘Scandinavian approach’, and AZ University in particular has refined a set of participatory design techniques that they are able to apply, even in a highly technical multipartner project. Although the political context has changed considerably since the 1970s, the AZ group does not find its approach any less valuable.

I think, well, first of all there seems to be you know 'why do we want to involve the users?' That question has been given a different answer today than it did in the 70s [when] it was sort of quite a political thing, but then if you look at for instance the designer's workbook, it does argue very much in terms of quality of the product. And today it's still quite an argument in terms of the quality of the product but I also think there is a different kind of emphasis on networks and grass roots movements and things like that, that in a sense legitimate that people have different perspectives and different interests in an organization or in a culture.... The other thing of course, one major difference I see is the role of the trade union movement.... And I think that what has changed I think was if we looked at the early 70s it was obviously that the labour movement, the unions, they weren't in any way perfect, but they were sort of the best counter power to the way organizations were and in that respect looked as the most promising for people to getting something else going.... And I think that picture is far less clear today,... so if we started over we probably wouldn't choose the unions as the main partner in cooperation.... And I think what we have chosen is then to try to work with more, more explicitly with groups in an organization in a workplace and see how that sort of plays out. (BR)

Scandinavian CSCW also typically involves working with people from a variety of perspectives, including different disciplines. The AZ CSCW group has collaborated regularly with social scientists in the past, notably on the Labour Inspection project, (see Markussen, 1993), and over the past decade has developed something of a tradition of inviting guest researchers to work with them. The enrichment of perspective that visitors bring is appreciated by all the group's members:

We are confronted with a lot of different people with slightly different approaches....When open discussions become more frequent, of course that influences what goes on here. We are influenced by the people we cooperate with, and we are definitely also influencing them, so it's good.... That means a lot for our projects here that we have some good contacts that like to be here, like to return here. (GK)

Working on a more traditional, multi-partner ESPRIT project was also a learning experience. KY describes what they learned through participation in this project:

we have learned a lot about collaborating with people who don't have any prior, or not much prior knowledge about participatory design. So I think that compared to five years ago, we have a much better understanding of how you could sort of guide non-PD computer scientists into working in a PD-like fashion. And secondly we have gained a lot of experience with a more traditional project approach.

In sharp contrast, elements such as quality of working life, supporting the individual user, participation and involvement do not enter into the Japanese CSCW equation at all. CSCW is a development of the telecommunications, electronics and engineering industries and is thus closely identified with a product, rather than a research orientation. Although there is CSCW research taking place in universities, the main players are the major electronics companies. A 'hard' science approach dominates. Virtually all those involved in designing CSCW systems in Japan are engineers or computer scientists. They identify strongly with their profession, building a good system, that is one that works, is reliable, state-of-the-art, original, is both the goal and a measure of their capabilities as engineers. They are not generally concerned with who will use their systems, or how they will be implemented. Although there is increasing interest by social scientists in the impacts and use of these systems, while we were in Japan multidisciplinary collaboration was not considered, let alone practised.

The cases presented earlier illustrate significant differences in the way CSCW researchers organize themselves and their work in Japan and Scandinavia. This organization, both day-to-day and institutional, is in direct relation with the ideas underlying some of the choices they make. Democracy is very much a part of the social psychology of Scandinavian CSCW researchers; they frame many of their CSCW choices in these terms: participation, building up local resources, helping the ‘resource-weak’, cutting across boundaries, hierarchy, etc. This is also evident in the way they work together, as equal members of a team, who place a high priority on working together. In contrast, respect for authority, and taking one’s place in the overall scheme of things seem to drive the actions of many Japanese CSCW researchers.

Not only do designers' ideals impact significantly on the design process, they are also reflected in the resulting products, or CSCW systems. While it is not our intention to treat these norms and values as social facts which predetermine design choices, they are present, with other things, in designers' frames and designers draw on them to guide their actions.

In particular, Japanese culture is regularly invoked as a justification for decisions to focus on contextual awareness and non-verbal communication in Japanese CSCW systems. This emphasis on the contextual translates into research on spatial awareness, gaze awareness rather than eye contact, gesture, interpersonal distance, physical feedback, large displays. Furthermore, considerable attention is paid to creating a pleasurable physical environment or a 'shared' environment, as in MAJIC, with tones of virtual reality. It is judged important that a sense of atmosphere or transpire through the system. For instance, a Japanese social psychologist whose research interest is group dynamics explained to us that the most important thing in Japanese groups is face-to-face communication, which creates atmosphere, or kuuki. He believes that the biggest challenge facing CSCW systems is how kuuki might be transmitted at a distance.

Edward T. Hall, an author often cited by Japanese CSCW researchers, uses high- or low-context culture to refer to a culture's preferred communication style: the degree to which the meaning of a message can be abstracted from the situation in which it was produced and received. A high-context message is one in which 'most of the information is either in the physical context or internalized in the person, while very little is in the coded explicit transmitted part of the message'; a low-context message is one in which 'the mass of information is vested in the explicit code'(Hall, 1976, p. 91). The concept has implications for implicit/explicit, verbal/non-verbal, affective or intuitive/fact-based, and relational/absolute communication. In a society like Japan where most behaviour and the use of language is highly codified, the form is standard. It is important to look beneath the surface to interpret the meaning of an exchange, hence the importance of 'positioning' and the emphasis on 'atmosphere'. Much of the content of a message will be implicit; interpretation will often be based on intuition rather than facts; and relationships will continually shift and be redefined. When language cannot convey all meaning, non-verbal communication becomes more important. There is clear evidence of this in the emphasis on video communication, large displays, attention to gaze and gesture in MAJIC. One informant even went so far as to insist that physical feedback must be integrated into the interface design because 'it is impossible for Japanese to have a purely intellectual relationship with the computer' (OA).

Talking heads vs. virtual workspaces: a comparison of design across cultures

A corollary of not trusting a computer system to model all instances of human communication or to successfully translate the subtleties of day-to-day interaction, is the focus of many Japanese CSCW systems on providing channels for communication which can be used to complement, or supplement, traditional ways of working. This channel should transmit as much information as possible (hence the widespread use of video and large displays), but should avoid specifying procedures or ways of doing things. This is clearly the case with MAJIC in which research and evaluation have focused exclusively on the physical environment. Clearly, Japanese designers, fully conscious of the highly relativistic approach to relationships in their society, do not believe that all types of communication can be supported by groupware systems. All our informants readily admit that there are limits to supporting the more ‘subtle’ or situationally dependent aspects of work. Given the constant fluctuations and redefinitions involved in any activity which is out of the ordinary, they view the task of trying to support ‘delicate’ communication, such as negotiation, as an impossible one. ‘People don’t or haven’t used such a system for their real decision-making. They are doing, they’re making decisions with all the traditional ways’ (WE). Another researcher, YI, points to the impossibility of ‘catching’ pieces of information that fly around an office and which are grasped through peripheral awareness.

Another feature of Japanese CSCW systems is that they are careful to provide support for traditional, paper-based forms of working, and ways of integrating paper and electronic information. Designers view the systems they design as complementary to, not replacements for standard practices; their aim is to support groups, not to replace or reconfigure all their activities The MAJIC system integrates a desk that people can work on. This is the logic underlying MY Laboratory's research programme, as well as that of a number of other groupware or CSCW systems.

In contrast, Scandinavian CSCW systems focus on providing frameworks for collaboration that facilitate communication and decrease social distance between all those using the system, thereby improving the quality of working life. AZ's solution for the Labour Inspection Service targeted better coordination of work by reproducing existing paper forms as templates in computerized form, and cross-registering the information they contained, so that earlier entries could be easily retrieved by any team member and the need to enter the same information in many different places was reduced. The entire inspection team could thus share information generated by any individual team member. While Scandinavian CSCW solutions may be highly technical, as in the case of the AZ hypermedia, they are not overtly so; it is felt that too much technicity would represent an obstacle to ubiquitous use of this new type of tool.

## Conclusion

This paper has illustrated the interaction of process and product in the design of CSCW systems. It has explored significant regional cultural differences in CSCW design and has proposed an explanation, grounded in the notion of cultural frame, for these observations. This explanation focuses on the interaction between the specific situation in which design is taking place, its larger social, cultural or institutional context, and the unique actions of designers. Based on how they understand the world around them, designers make assumptions that guide their design choices. As participants in their larger professional, organizational and national cultures, individual designers link their creations with larger social or cultural values. In particular, 'Japanese culture' is highly significant in the frames of Japanese CSCW designers, while a group of ideas centring on 'democracy' plays an equivalent role in the frames of Scandinavian designers.
