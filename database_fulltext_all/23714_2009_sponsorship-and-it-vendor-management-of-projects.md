## Abstract

Project sponsorship is assumed to be critical to the success of information systems development and implementation. However, there is limited research on the effect of vendor-based sponsorship on the management of information technology (IT) projects. This paper contributes to that emerging body of evidence. Drawing on literature on psychological contract, management control and project sponsorship, the authors propose that vendor-based sponsors influence project performance in two main ways. One is to protect project quality by renegotiating contract with the client; and the other is to resource the project. Based on a survey of senior managers in IT services vendor organisations, it reports that project sponsorship improves the quality, but not the cost or timeliness, of vendor delivery of IT projects. The findings suggest that IT services vendors can improve their project delivery capability by appointing project sponsors. Journal of Information Technology (2009) 24, 46–54. doi:10.1057/jit.2009.2

## Introduction

P <sup>roject</sup> <sup>sponsorship</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>frequently</sup> <sup>cited</sup> <sup>as</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>critical</sup>factor influencing information technology (IT) project factor influencing information technology (IT) project performance (Love and Brant-Love, 2000; Schmidt et al., 2001; Ross and Weill, 2002; Graham and Englund, 2004), and lack of executive support is consistently ranked high among factors leading to poor project performance (Johnson, 1995; Johnson et al., 2001; Schmidt et al., 2001; Sharma and Yetton, 2003; Hayes, 2004). Yet, there is limited empirical research on the effect of sponsorship on IT project performance.

In particular, there is very limited research on project sponsorship in IT services vendor organisations. Instead, the existing literature focuses on project sponsorship in client organisations (see, e.g., Hall et al., 2003; Graham and Englund, 2004). Anecdotal evidence suggests that vendorbased sponsorship plays an important role in supporting vendor project delivery (Sauer et al., 2001). This study investigates this issue, examining the effect of sponsorship in IT services vendors on their management of projects.

Drawing from the literature on psychological contracts (Koh et al., 2004; Miranda and Kavan, 2005) and management control (Daft and Macintosh, 1981; Simons, 1990, 1991, 1994, 1995), we examine the role of project sponsorship in IT services vendors and argue that the role has two key objectives. One is to support the performance of project teams within vendor organisations, providing additional resources and political cover as required (Graham and Englund, 2004). The other is to support collaboration between vendor project teams and their clients and, where necessary, to renegotiate the contracts with the clients (Miranda and Kavan, 2005).

Research has begun to examine how formal and psychological contracts complement each other (Saunders et al., 1997; Willcocks and Kern, 1998; Zaheer et al., 1998; Poppo and Zenger, 2002; Koh et al., 2004; Argyres et al., 2007). In particular, Koh et al. identify the mutual obligations that client- and vendor-based project managers expect of each other. Here, we contribute to that literature and examine how sponsors also deliver on those obligations.

Typically, research on project management has adopted performance on an individual project as the unit of analysis. Here, instead, the focus is on the effect of sponsorship on the management of projects, defined as the performance across the portfolio of projects delivered by an IT services vendor to its clients. Adopting that definition, the subject of this research is the management of projects rather than individual project management. This is consistent with the shift in current research towards issues concerning project delivery capability and away from the earlier dominant research focus on individual project performance.

In the following section, the literature is reviewed and two hypotheses are developed. The research design, a survey of senior managers in Australian IT services organisations, is then described and the analysis presented. The results show that vendor-based sponsors affect the quality, but not the cost or timeliness, of the services delivered to clients. Finally, implications for research and practice are discussed.

## Literature review

In examining how project sponsorship within IT vendor organisations affects the delivery of IT projects to clients, this paper is a departure from the dominant literature in which the focus has been on the role of sponsors within client organisations (see, e.g., Turner, 1999; Hall et al., 2003; Helm and Remington, 2005; Englund and Bucero, 2006). Within those organisations, sponsors mobilise stakeholder support, facilitate organisational change and champion business cases to deliver business outcomes and benefits (Volkoff et al., 1999; Sauer et al., 2001; Englund and Bucero, 2006).

While appointing project sponsors is frequently recommended as a mechanism to keep projects responsive to their stakeholders’ requirements (Love and Brant-Love, 2000; Schmidt et al., 2001; Ross and Weill, 2002; Graham and Englund, 2004; Englund and Bucero, 2006), the performance of sponsors is usually studied from a customer- or client-centric perspective. The exceptions include Currie and Seltsikas (2001), Levina and Ross (2003), Feeny et al. (2005) and Argyres et al. (2007), who research vendor strategies and core competences, and Koh et al. (2004), Sabherwal (1999) and Willcocks and Kern (1998), who investigate the mutual relationship between vendor and client. Here, we contribute to the vendor-based research sub-stream, adopting a vendor perspective of sponsorship, with vendor-based sponsors typically taking one of a number of project management oversight roles within the vendor organisation. These would include, for example, joining a vendor’s value review board, responsible for contractual obligations, pricing and returns, appointing project managers and approving project resourcing.

The literature review below begins by examining the influence of formal and psychological contracts on project performance. Then, four potential threats to project performance are described. The adoption of interactive control systems (Simons, 1990, 1991, 1994, 1995) to mitigate those threats is explored.

This is followed by an analysis of vendor project performance. We adopt the golden triangle of cost, time and quality (Gardiner and Stewart, 2000) to measure project performance, with vendor project performance measured for a portfolio of projects rather than for an individual project. Two hypotheses, differentiating between the positive influence of sponsorship on the quality of vendor project performance and its null effect on the cost and time components of that performance, are derived. With a limited literature on vendor sponsorship, we take the literature on sponsorship in client organisations as our point of departure.

## Formal and psychological contracts

Typically, outsourced IT projects are governed by both formal outsourcing contracts and post-contractual relationships or psychological contracts (Koh et al., 2004; Miranda and Kavan, 2005). The formal contracts specify the obligations of the parties, including provisions for contract variations, dispute resolution processes and governance structures. When the expected outcomes can be fully specified ex ante and the processes for achieving those outcomes are well structured, reliance on formal contracts is sufficient to deliver the required project performance (Turner and Simister, 2001).

Frequently, however, it is difficult to fully specify IT outsourcing contracts ex ante. This is due to the complexity and unpredictability of organisational, business, technological and requirement changes that typically occur during major IT projects (Koh et al., 2004; Miranda and Kavan, 2005). Under those circumstances, reliance on formal contracts is inadequate and psychological contracts play important roles in facilitating the necessary on-going cooperation between vendors and clients (Ring and Van De Ven, 1994; Sabherwal, 1999). Here, we adopt the Koh et al.’s (2004) definition of a psychological contract: ‘the contractual parties’ mental beliefs and expectations about their mutual obligations in a contractual relationship, based on perceived promises of a reciprocal exchange’.

Therefore, one of the major challenges for vendor-based sponsors is that, with IT projects subject to high levels of business and technical uncertainty, vendor project teams need flexibility to respond to changes in client requirements (Markus, 2004; Miranda and Kavan, 2005). Inevitably, this flexibility brings actions by vendor project teams into conflict with the formal project contracts, requiring agreement by both parties to variations to those contracts. It is necessary, therefore, to have both prior agreements on dispute resolution mechanisms (Miranda and Kavan, 2005; Argyres et al., 2007) and frequent exchanges between sponsors and client senior management to facilitate speedy resolution of conflicts.

The implicit and incomplete nature of psychological contracts, combined with frequent changes in project team membership, puts the ‘mutual obligation’ aspect of psychological contracts between vendors and their clients at risk, when resolving contractual conflicts, with potentially serious consequences for both parties (Conway and Briner, 2002; Deery et al., 2006). Sabherwal (1999), in one of his case studies, describes how senior executives from both the vendor and client met informally the night before the regular vendor–client project meeting to address and resolve any contentious issues. This was to reduce any threats to the joint project team’s friendly cooperation. He goes on to note: ‘Moreover, the disagreements, which were now aired in a small group of senior executives, did not adversely affect the trust of even these executives’.

It is also important to have mechanisms to ‘repair distrust from ‘breaches’ to the contract. Again, Sabherwal (1999) gives an example of this. When a vendor was failing to deliver on one aspect of the contract, ‘the client’s Vice President of IS went directly to the vendor’s President, the issue was addressed quickly’. Sabherwal calls this the ‘focused use of top management pressure’.

Typically, both of the above examples would fall within the areas of responsibility of vendor-based sponsors. In the former, potentially disruptive issues are considered outside of the routine joint vendor–client project team meeting to protect the psychological contracts among the vendor and client team members. In the latter, the sponsor would be the designated point of contact for the client’s information systems (IS) Vice President with the authority to resolve the issue. In both cases, building and sustaining effective formal and healthy psychological contracts between vendor and client team members and organisations are critical responsibilities of vendor-based sponsors.

## Threats to vendor project performance

A review of the client sponsorship literature identifies four threats to vendor project performance. As noted above, this analysis draws on the research on individual project management rather than the management of projects. However, each of these threats would fall within the responsibility of a vendor-based sponsor and can be used to begin to model the management of projects.

First, project performance depends on the commitment and involvement of stakeholders beyond the control of vendor project managers, requiring the support of senior management to secure commitments from those stakeholders (Graham and Englund, 2004).

Second, vendor project managers are not members of the executive team. Therefore, they are unlikely to be familiar with their organisations’ strategic intents and oversight from senior executives on the conduct of projects is needed to ensure projects stay on track (Ross and Weill, 2002). Guidance or interpretation of dispute resolution rules and contract terms, and direct negotiations with clients over the business/systems requirements are important for the effective delivery of projects. Typically, these are the responsibility of vendor-based sponsors and not of project managers.

Third, projects compete with other projects and priorities for resources. Typically, project managers do not have strong organisational power bases. Therefore, with the resources required for projects subject to competing demands (Pinto, 2000), it is necessary to have vendorbased sponsors to ‘provide air cover for the troops’ (Graham and Englund, 2004). In addition, where significant changes in business and technical requirements are expected, vendor-based sponsors negotiate with clients to continuously align vendor resource commitments and client requirements (Miranda and Kavan, 2005).

Finally, strong and visible commitment from vendorbased sponsors is important to motivate vendor project teams and to remove potential barriers to their performance (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1991; Grover, 1993; Koh et al., 2004; Miranda and Kavan, 2005). This behaviour by sponsors may also reassure clients of vendor commitment to their projects.

To mitigate the four threats, sponsors, implicitly or explicitly, adopt interactive control systems, defined by Simons (1987) as ‘formal systems used by top managers to regularly and personally involve themselves in the decision activities of subordinates’. Interactive control systems are specifically designed to operate in situations of high strategic uncertainty, which are present in many IT projects. Here, strategic uncertainty refers to uncertainty that is fundamental to achieving business goals, creating threats or opportunities as circumstances change (Daft and Macintosh, 1981; Simons, 1990, 1991, 1994, 1995). With vendor-based sponsor involvement, formal contracts can be renegotiated, when necessary, for project teams to explore innovative solutions.

For example, at the beginning of a project, clients frequently have conflicting and incomplete objectives embedded in their system requirements. The vendor business analysts work with clients to clarify their particular business needs. These are then translated into requirements by systems architects and project teams (Remenyi and Sherwood-Smith, 1998; Markus, 2004). The involvement of senior vendor executives as sponsors in high uncertainty, fluid business environments supports project team’s flexibility by facilitating adjustment to contract terms, client expectations embedded in the psychological contracts and resource commitments.

## Effects of sponsorship on vendor project performance

Drawing on the above brief review of the psychological contract, sponsorship and control theory literatures, there are two basic arguments to support the positive effect of sponsorship on vendor project performance. One concerns the role of sponsors in developing and sustaining interorganisation relationships. The other is the support by sponsors of project teams within vendor organisations. These two roles combine with the role of vendor project managers to deliver on client requirements. Specifically, vendor project managers deliver on the contracts, while vendor-based sponsors interpret and renegotiate those contracts, and, then, identify and commit the resources necessary to deliver on the revised contracts.

To explore the different roles between sponsors and project managers, consider the analytical framework proposed by Nidumolu (1996). He identifies two roles that affect project performance within an organisation. One is the horizontal relationships between IS project managers and user representatives in the same organisations. The other is the vertical relationships between those project managers and their own managers.

Here, we extend this analytical framework to model inter-organisational project performance, partitioning the horizontal role into project managers’ responsibilities to deliver on the contracts and the sponsors’ responsibilities to develop the ‘best’ contracts for their clients. Within the vertical relationship between sponsors and project managers, sponsors are given the temporary, limited line authority necessary to adjust contracts and resource project managers to deliver on the renegotiated ‘best’ contracts. In this framework, project managers run the projects, and sponsors manage and resource contract variations.

For example, based on the initial requirements and specifications, project managers and project teams begin to design, schedule, build and deliver the project. However, requirement changes have consistently been identified as a major cause of project failures (Boehm, 1991; Johnson, 1995; McConnell, 1996; Schmidt et al., 2001). In particular, studies show that requirements-analysis phases continue to impact on later phases (McConnell, 1996). Therefore, it is the responsibility of sponsors to agree requirements and specification changes and, where necessary, negotiate adjustments to formal contracts.

In addition, under conditions of strategic uncertainty, it is important to have flexibility in resource commitment to support the flexibility of vendor project teams in responding to emergent client needs. Project delivery under these conditions requires the active support of senior vendor management (Simons, 1990, 1991, 1994, 1995). Specifically, vendor-based sponsors not only negotiate contract variations, they also resource and support vendor project teams to deliver on the revised contracts.

The above shows that the form of the relationships between vendor-based sponsors and clients, and between vendor-based project managers and clients are different. The contents of these relationships are also different. When clients initiate requests for changes in requirements, they do not ask to extend project schedules or increase project costs. Rather, they generally want to include features not specified in the extant contracts. Therefore, the discussions are frequently about the value of the additional features to the clients compared with the estimated increased costs and potential delays to the projects.

Sabherwal (1999) quotes an example of how a sponsor could help the client evaluate the benefits of features and not waste money. He quotes the client in one of his cases as saying: ‘In most cases, the vendor’s consulting expertise gave it the credibility to convince us we didn’t need what we said we wanted. And most of the time, the vendor was right’. The sponsor’s focus is on protecting the quality of the service provided to the client and, therefore, the vendor’s reputation in the market. Appointing vendor-based sponsors provides a specialised mechanism to protect the quality of client solutions. Formally:

Hypothesis 1: Vendor-based project sponsorship has a positive effect on vendor project performance [quality].

In contrast, vendor project managers work with client project managers or other client representatives to deliver on the extant contract. This includes resolving unforeseen cost increases and schedule delays. Only in extreme situations would these issues be escalated by either vendors or clients up to the level of vendor-based sponsors and their client equivalents.

In addition, two arguments restrict the influence of sponsorship on vendor project performance against time and cost. One is that, with quality being the clients’ first priority, the importance of time and cost performance is secondary. Furthermore, anecdotal evidence suggests that vendor-based sponsors have both limited time to assist project managers with their day-to-day running of projects and limited project management experience on which to base that assistance. Therefore, attention to and influence over the time and cost components of vendor project performance by sponsors is limited.

The other argument is based on the project management literature that warns against interference in project managers’ day-to-day running of projects (Graham and Englund, 2004). Combining the two arguments, sponsorship has a limited effect on the time and cost components of vendor project performance. Formally:

Hypothesis 2: Vendor project performance [time, cost] is independent of vendor-based sponsorship.

Essentially, the above argument assumes that the cost and time dimensions of the iron triangle do not co-vary with the quality dimension for vendor-based sponsors effects on vendor performance. Sponsors renegotiate, as required, the contracts that project managers deliver and for which their delivery may be constrained by the iron triangle. As noted in ‘Discussion’ section, this raises questions for future research in relation to the iron triangle.

## Research design

Hypotheses 1 and 2 are tested using survey data collected from senior managers in the Australian IT services industry.

## Unit of analysis

Project management capabilities are likely to vary across business units within a divisionalised corporation. In contrast, within a business unit across projects, the differences are likely to be small. Therefore, the business unit was selected as the appropriate unit of analysis.

## Sample selection and data collection

A mailing list of IT services organisations was provided by an information service provider. Two hundred and twentyfour IT services organisations were identified as both belonging to the industry classification SIC 7371-7379 and having a turnover of A\$10 million or more. These criteria defined IT services vendors large enough to employ multiple project managers.

A fax was sent to the Chief Executive Officer or Managing Director of each vendor asking them to assist the research by providing the names and addresses of the senior managers responsible for business units to participate in the survey. Twenty IT services organisations provided the names and contacts of 52 senior managers. Questionnaires were mailed directly to those managers. Follow-up phone calls were made to encourage them to complete the questionnaires. Thirty-nine responses were received.

Response rates can be calculated against two bases: the number of companies invited to participate or the number of managers nominated to participate. Using the former base, the initial response rate to the fax was 20/224 ¼ 8.9%. The low response rate to the fax, while similar to other studies (Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski, 1991; Nidumolu, 1996; Yetton et al., 2000), signals a potential non-response bias validity threat to the findings (Schwab, 1999). The response rate to the questionnaires was high, 39/52 ¼ 75%. Multiple responses from the same business unit were aggregated, using the mean of the responses, resulting in an effective final sample size of 36.

To evaluate the potential validity threat from nonresponse bias, the representativeness of the sample from the population of IT service organisations was examined. To do this, the populations defined by SIC codes in the first paragraph of this section were identified using Dunn &

Bradstreet’s ‘Business Who’s Who of Australia’ database, and the distributions of the variable – number of employees – in the population and sample were compared using a $\chi ^ { 2 } \cdot$ test. The analysis shows that there is no evidence of a nonresponse bias for the sample.

## Instrument design and validation

## Dependent variables

Project performance capability is a vendor’s capability to satisfy expectations on cost, time and quality, as measured by each respondent’s perceptions of overall performance, relative performance against competitors, and client satisfaction. In particular, project performance capability [quality] is measured on perceptions of systems quality and specification fulfilment. Cronbach’s alphas for the measures of performance are acceptable: project performance [quality] ¼ 0.90, project performance [cost] ¼ 0.72 and project performance [time] ¼ 0.71.

## Independent variables

Project sponsorship is measured in two ways. One is the percentage of IT projects within the business unit that have been assigned a project sponsor (Sponsor %). The other, Sponsor responsibility, is the aggregate across four questions measuring sponsorship responsibilities: having formal decision-making power and command over resources; overseeing projects for corporate control; taking care of stakeholders’ interests; and being assessed on project outcomes (see Appendix). The former measures the level of vendor commitment of executive resources to the management of projects. The latter measures the responsibilities assigned to the vendor-based sponsors.

An added benefit from measuring project sponsorship using two separate instruments, one using a continuous scale (Sponsor %) and the other a Likert scale format (Sponsor responsibility), reduces the potential common method variance (CMV) validity threat to the findings. CMV poses a validity threat to empirical findings when dependent and independent variables employ similar scale formats (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

The Cronbach’s alpha for Sponsor responsibility is 0.80. The bi-variate correlation between the two constructs is $r = 0 . 4 5 \ ( P { \leqslant } 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Therefore, vendors who assign sponsors to a high proportion of their projects are also likely to hold them accountable for the four responsibilities described above. Of the 36 business units, 14 (38.9%) assigned project sponsors to all their IT projects, while 3 (8.3%) did not assign project sponsors to any IT projects. On average, business units assigned project sponsors to 75% of IT projects.

## Discussion

In this section, the results are summarised, validity threats to the findings are reviewed, and the implications for theory and practice are discussed.

## Findings

Consistent with the predictions derived from management control theory, and the formal and psychological contract literature, Table 1 reports that Hypothesis 1 is supported. Sponsorship, the use of project sponsors (Sponsor %) and sponsor responsibilities, is significantly related to vendor project performance [quality], a vendor’s capability to deliver projects to client needs. In addition, supporting Hypothesis 2, there is no significant effect of sponsorship on vendor project performance [time, cost].

External validity is concerned with the generalisability of the findings. There are two distinct populations to which these findings could be generalised. The absence of any evidence for a nonresponse bias suggests that the findings do generalise to IT services vendors. As discussed below, the authors do not believe that these findings generalise to other industries and, in particular, to the construction industry. Rather, models of the effects of vendor-based sponsors and project managers on the management of projects are likely to be contingent on the context.

## Implications for research and practice

This study contributes to the emerging literature on contingent models of project performance (Shenhar, 2001; Pich et al., 2002; Liu and Yetton, 2007). The specific finding, the positive effect of IT services vendor-based sponsorship on vendor project performance [quality] but not on vendor project performance [time, cost], provides further evidence for the contingent nature of the effect of management support on project performance (Sharma and Yetton, 2003).

In addition, the paper refines Sabherwal and King’s (1992) and Yetton et al.’s (2000) findings, showing that vendor-based sponsorship has a direct effect on project performance [quality] of IT projects but not on project performance [time, cost]. When system quality is the key project performance criterion, high-risk projects are associated with high levels of user participation (Yetton et al., 2000; Barki et al., 2001). In those situations, the appointment of a project sponsor within the vendor organisation supports client (user) participation to improve the quality of the systems delivered (Yetton et al., 2000).

The findings also provide support for Turner and Keegan’s (2001) view that the two roles of broker and steward are critical to effective project management. The role of a broker is to bid for and win work, liaise with clients, win follow-on work and ensure clients are satisfied. In contrast, the role of a steward is to make sure the resources needed to deliver a project are available when they are needed.

In the IT vendors researched here, both roles are vested within the role of the project sponsor, with the broker role frequently supported by an account manager in either sales or business development. Sponsors are better positioned than project managers to engage and negotiate with clients over variations to the project deliverables and to marshal resources to support those deliverables, with the day-to-day running of those projects remaining with their project managers.

Specifically, vendor-based sponsors facilitate the fulfilment of the four vendor obligations identified by Koh et al. (2004). First, in consultation with clients, sponsors define and fine-tune the authority structures for managing projects. Second, sponsors protect project teams from interference from both client and vendor organisations, enabling project teams to ‘take charge’ of projects.

Third, through political influence and position power, sponsors mobilise organisational resources, including effective human resources management, required for successful project delivery. Fourth, sponsors co-ordinate, support and develop a sense of common purpose with client organisations, building effective inter-organisational teams. In addition, sponsors influence client organisations to fulfil their obligations in project ownership, project specification and project monitoring.

To capture the benefits identified above, senior management needs to address two issues. One is the appointment of vendor-based sponsors to projects with high levels of strategic uncertainty. The other is that sponsors should be specifically assigned the four responsibilities identified by Koh et al. (2004).

In its basic form, the model presented here is similar to that employed by many general management consultancy practices in which relationships with client organisations are the responsibility of partners and the conduct of projects is the responsibility of engagement managers. This is a different model for managing projects than the model adopted in the construction industry, where project managers typically report to project directors. This also supports the emerging contingent model of project management (see Shenhar and Dvir, 2007) and further cautions against accepting uncritically the findings for project management in the construction or other industries as applicable in the IT industry and vice versa.

Theoretically, this interpretation motivates an interesting speculation for future research. Zaheer et al. (1998) report a significant path coefficient between inter-organisational trust and performance and a significant path coefficient between inter-organisational trust and inter-personal trust but, controlling for inter-organisational trust, there is no significant path coefficient between inter-personal trust and performance.

One interpretation of these findings is that the effect of inter-personal trust, the psychological contract between vendor and client project managers, on performance reported in the IS literature is, at least in part, a function of the effect of vendor-based sponsors on inter-organisational trust. In that case, the effect on performance of sponsors could be more important than the effect of project managers.

For example, were the project managers in the research reported by Koh et al. (2004) identifying the responsibilities of vendor- and client-based sponsors and their organisations, or, as reported, the responsibilities of the project managers in each of those organisations? The complementary roles of sponsors and project managers in IT vendor and client relationships, and their joint effects on performance should be the subject of future research.

Accepting Hypotheses 1 and 2, there would be a strong correlation between performance [cost] and performance [time], r ¼ 0.51 (Po0.01), while the co-variation between performance [cost] and performance [quality], r ¼ 0.18 (n.s.); and performance [time] and performance [quality], r ¼ 0.12 (n.s.), remain low and not significant. These results suggest that using the iron triangle as the success criteria, changes in the quality dimension are not associated with changes in time or cost outcomes. This raises an important question about the implications of the iron triangle, when the contract on which the iron triangle is based is open to being renegotiated.

Renegotiations of the initial contracts by vendor-based sponsors take many forms. The benefits from the resultant improved performance [quality] are not uniquely linked to time and cost penalties as in the iron triangle. For example, in the illustration given above from Sabherwal (1999), the sponsor persuades the client that it does not need its proposed changes to requirements, with their expected cost and time penalties.

A number of studies provide support for the independence between the time/cost (process performance) dimensions and the quality (product performance) dimension of IS projects (Nidumolu, 1996; Atkinson, 1999; Yetton et al., 2000). Future studies are needed to investigate the relationships between the three dimensions of the iron triangle for other types of projects.

Finally, this study extends Nidumolu’s (1996) analytical framework to model the effect of project sponsorship on inter-organisation control, raising the question: With whom should vendor-based sponsors interact within client organisations? Three of the four vendor obligations, along with the additional client obligations discussed above, involve interactions between vendor-based sponsors and clients. Here, we speculate that the appointment of a vendor-based sponsor should be matched by the appointment of a client-based sponsor.

The alternative strategy to appointing a sponsor is to reduce the level of strategic uncertainty. Two steps would contribute to that goal. One is the coordination of resources, roles and responsibilities to improve task programmability and, therefore, reduce organisational uncertainty. The other is the development of methodologies and project management tools to reduce strategic uncertainty by reducing technical uncertainty. In that case, senior management could be relieved of the need for direct intervention to protect project quality. Instead, they would be able to focus their attention on other strategic opportunities.

## Summary

This paper shows that, in the IT services industry, vendorbased project sponsorship protects vendor project performance by improving vendor project performance [quality] but not vendor project performance [time, cost]. This contributes to the emerging literature on the contingent nature of project performance. The implications for both theory and practice are to reinforce the need to refine and extend the contingent models of IT project performance.

The findings provide insight on how to focus senior management attention and actions to improve project performance. To generalise the findings to other industries, further studies are needed to identify strategic project outcomes that need the protection of executive sponsors and to test the effect of project sponsorship on project performance in different contexts.
