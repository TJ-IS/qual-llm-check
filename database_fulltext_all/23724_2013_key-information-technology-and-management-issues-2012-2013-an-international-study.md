## Abstract

The importance of the impact of IT for organizations around the world, especially in light of a very slow recovery from the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis, has ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed the need to provide a better understanding of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c geographic similarities and differences in IT managerial and technical trends. Identifying these in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors is one thing; the paper also looks at the challenges in addressing them, taking into account both local responsiveness and global pressures. By comparing and contrasting IT trends from different geographies, this paper presents important local and international factors, including management concerns, in<sup>fl</sup>uential technologies, budgets/spending, and organizational considerations). In addition these trends help to prepare IT leaders for the challenges that await them. The IT trends also serve as an indicator as the respective geographies address their dif<sup>fi</sup>cult economic environments. The research is based on data from four geographic regions, namely United States, Europe, Asia, and Latin America. The same questionnaire (albeit translated for the respective respondents), based on the lead author's well-respected and long-running Society for Information Management survey, was applied across the geographies. This paper presents the major <sup>fi</sup>ndings based on survey responses from 787 organizations (195 U.S., 360 European, 55 Asian, 41 Australia, and 136 Latin) in 2012. The top <sup>fi</sup>ve management concerns were: (1) Business productivity & cost reduction, (2) Business & IT alignment / integration / fusion, (3) Business agility & speed to market, (4) Business process management/reengineering, and (5) IT cost reduction.The <sup>fi</sup>ve most in<sup>fl</sup>uential technologies were: (1) Business intelligence, (2) Cloud computing, (3) Enterprise resource planning, (4) Apps developments, and (5) Customer relationship management. Overall, the paper <sup>fi</sup>nds that while the economic climate is improving at different rates around the globe – albeit at a slower pace than anticipated – ITs role continues to evolve as it provides organizations with a fundamental vehicle for reducing business expenses and new opportunities for increasing revenues.

## Introduction

he Society for Information Management (SIM) has been conducting a survey of senior IT executives since 1980, to help IT and business leaders understand and prepare for important issues and trends. There are a number of IT trends Tdescribed by survey results reported in the literature, but most are either speci<sup>fi</sup>c to a certain industry, such as IT (Herlihy and Ptak, 1997) or airlines (Airline Business, 2009), or concentrate on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspect of an industry, such as IT salaries (Ziff Davis Enterprise, 2011). There have been a limited number of surveys covering various aspects of the IT industry (Herlihy and Ptak, 1997; O’Toole, 2003; Baker, 2006; Airline Business, 2009; Ziff Davis Enterprise, 2011), most with limited geographic reach (Li et al., 2005; Li et al., 2010).

The SIM survey, focusing on IT in the United States and started in 1980, remains one of the longest running surveys covering various aspects of the IT industry, with the lead author facilitating it since 2000. Organizations around the world have used the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the previous SIM surveys as a barometer to prepare for the future, albeit the annual SIM survey, prior to 3 years ago, has been based on survey responses just from organizations located in the United States.

The recognition of the global reach of IT, especially in light of the impact of the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis, has ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed the necessity to obtain responses from organizations around the globe to understand and benchmark similarities and difference across geographies. Hence, since 2010 the same survey has been expanded to include Europe, Asia, and Latin America, in addition to the United States.

This paper presents the major insights gained from the 2012 survey in Europe, Asia (including Australia), Latin America, and the United States, the same geographies covered in our previous years’ study (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011; Luftman et al., 2012). This paper is based on responses from IT executives representing over 750 organizations (see Tables 1 and 2 for participant geographies and demographics).

While the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the United States (with some minor comparison with Europe and Asia/Australia) have been published in the December 2012 issue of MISQ-Executive (Luftman and Derksen, 2012), this article, using an updated set of data from other geographies, includes the major insights gained from Europe, Asia, and Latin America, and uses the overall global responses as the benchmark for a range of fresh insights.

## Top 10 IT management concerns

The top 10 management concerns tend to evolve slowly except for concerns such as IT cost reduction, which appears to be directly related to each region’s short-term economic perfor mance. Despite the prevailing global economic conditions, management concerns such as IT and business alignment, and business agility and speed to market are both globally and locally the consistent top three management concerns.

Interestingly, IT cost reduction remained a more important IT concern in Europe than in all other geographies. This has now been the case for the last 3 years and is a result of the ongoing economic turmoil that has plagued Europe. A second reason is the budget control focus of Western European organizations, which are well represented in the European part of the survey. Most of these organizations are focusing more on IT costs rather than return on IT investments.

## Business productivity and cost reduction

Business productivity and cost reduction has been ranked as one of the top three management concerns since the global economic downturn, and it appears as the top concern in all geographies except for Latin America and Asia, where it is still ranked very highly: second and third, respectively.

Although there is a general consensus on the importance of business productivity and cost reduction using IT, IT itself is still largely perceived as an expense within many companies. This is demonstrated by the continued appearance of IT cost reduction as one of the top 10 management concerns. However, as noted in our report last year, this economic downturn has most organizations leveraging IT to reduce business expense more than merely reducing IT expenses. This year’s data clearly show this by having business productivity and cost reduction as well as business and IT alignment ranked higher (in all geographies) than IT cost reduction. This, in concert with an increasing focus on leveraging IT for revenuegenerating initiatives (discussed later), is very different from previous economic downturns.

After many years, last year was the <sup>fi</sup>rst in which IT cost reduction was pushed just outside of the top 10 management concerns globally, but continuation of the slow recovery has seen IT cost reduction returning as one of the top management concerns.

In line with last year’s <sup>fi</sup>nding, instead of simply cutting IT budgets, business and IT leaders seem to have responded to the recession by focusing on IT as an enabler/driver of business productivity (Luftman and Ben-Zvi, 2010; Luftman and Zadeh, 2011). It seems business productivity and cost reduction have remained major management concerns as economies slowly move into a post-recession phase. As in 2010, it is noted that this trend is present in all geographies except in Asia, where issues such as business process management (BPM) and revenue-generating IT innovations are ranked higher than business productivity and cost reduction. Since Asia has not been as severely affected by recession in the past few years, placing more focus on strategic issues instead of cost reduction is understandable. It is noted that business productivity and cost reduction still rank very high in Asia, albeit not as high as in the other geographies. The United States is also seeing an increasing trend in focusing on leveraging IT for revenue-generating initiatives, as will be discussed later.

## Business and IT alignment

Despite being a top concern of IT managers for over 30 years, alignment of IT and business remains an ultimate but elusive goal. After climbing to the top spot in 2011, compared with the second spot in 2010, it once again appears in second place (Luftman et al., 2012). In all of the geographies, IT and business alignment ranks in the top 10 management concerns, ranking second in the United States and Europe, <sup>fi</sup>rst in Latin America, and sixth and fourth in Asia and Australia, respectively.

IT and business leaders continue to see IT as an integral driver/enabler of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency/effectiveness throughout the business and therefore focus on initiatives that enhance the maturity of alignment between IT and business (Luftman et al., 2012). As CEOs and other business executives require direct support from CIOs to deliver demonstrable value (Eul et al., 2010), it is not surprising to see once again alignment of business and IT as one of the top three management concerns.

Despite this long-standing concern, however, less than one in <sup>fi</sup>ve enterprises believe their effort in aligning IT and business has been successful (Lochan and Shah, 2010). The lead author’s research on IT business alignment maturity suggests that this is not just a question of success or failure, but the effectiveness of the relationship and how it can be enhanced.

## Business agility and speed to market

Business agility and speed to market is ranked No. 3 in the United States, Europe, and Latin America; No. 2 in Australia; and fourth in Asia, bringing the total ranking to third in 2012, exactly the same ranking it had in 2010, vs 2011 when it was ranked at No. 2.

Business agility and speed to market are essential for business growth in a competitive economy. This is especially true in this slow recovery from recession, demonstrated by the continued high ranking of business agility and speed to market.

This management issue was introduced in the US survey in 2003 (before the recent recession) and was ranked in the midteens (Luftman and Derksen, 2012). Since the recession, however, business agility and speed to market has consistently ranked very high in the US survey (Luftman and Derksen, 2012). Our global survey started after the recession started in 2007, and business agility and speed to market has appeared among the top concerns each year. Business agility was ranked No. 2 in Asia last year, but interestingly this year it has moved down to the fourth spot (it was sixth in 2010). It seems that due to the inherent cultural and organizational agility, IT managers there seem not as concerned with agility and speed to market as their counterparts elsewhere.

As we have been noting, business agility and speed to market along with business productivity and cost reduction are the foundation for long-term competitive advantage (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011; Luftman et al., 2012), and therefore it is anticipated that these concerns will retain their high ranking as various geographies try to maintain and improve their global competitive advantage.

## Business process management and reengineering

BPM and business process reengineering (BPR) are ranked in the list of top 10 concerns in all of the geographies (ranked seventh in the United States, sixth in both Europe and Latin America, third in Australia, and the top spot in Asia). This should not be surprising in that BPM and BPR are fundamental vehicles for leveraging IT to reduce business expenses.

As discussed previously (Luftman et al., 2012), BPM and BPR have appeared every year in the list of top 20 concerns in the United States and Europe, and it was noted that Asian, Australian, and Latin American companies are catching up and hence that BPM and BPR are ranking higher in these geographies. This is still true, but as business processes improve and the focus is moved to strategic contribution of the new processes to business productivity and agility, the ranking of BPM/BPR was set to decrease eventually, and it has indeed decreased in all geographies since last year (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011) except for Asia, where it has moved to the top spot. It is noted that BPM and BPR continues to rank higher in Asia and Australia than the rest of the geographies, pointing to the more developed and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient organizations in these geographies, which have seen a reduction in their vulnerability to the impact of the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis through structural changes, production specialization, and improved resource ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Dutta and Mia, 2011; Luftman et al., 2012).

As the economic recovery progresses sluggishly, and as corporations need to compete in a globally linked market place, we have been predicting, and still believe, that BPM and reengineering will remain a top management concern globally in the near term. The very high ranking of ERP (enterprise resource planning) as an important application and technology in all geographies except Australia (discussed later in this paper) provides further support for this important consideration.

## IT cost reduction

IT cost reduction re-enters the top 10 list of concerns for IT managers. Considering the longer than expected economic sluggishness in the United States and Europe, it is understandable that the importance of IT cost reduction is higher in these geographies, where it was ranked <sup>fi</sup>fth and fourth in the United States and Europe, respectively.

With the economic environment being more robust in Australia and Latin America, it is no surprise that IT cost cutting was ranked lower in Latin America (10th) and outside of the top 10 in Australia (14th). Asia seems to buck this trend, because despite the economic environment not being as volatile as it is in Europe and the United States, IT cost reduction still ranks relatively highly at No. 5. This is probably due to the fact that staf<sup>fi</sup>ng salaries are on average lower in this region, making IT infrastructure costs a larger proportion of the total IT budget. This means thay IT infrastructure cost reduction is seen as a viable and tangible way to reduce company expenses. Interestingly, this is balanced in Asia by having revenue-generating IT innovations higher than all other geographies.

IT cost reduction and revenue-generating IT innovations are direct indicators of how business leaders view IT organizations, whether it is a business expense or a business partner that can help reduce business costs and increase revenues (please see the discussion on revenue-generating IT innovations below).

## IT reliability and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency

The growing complexity of IT systems along with the everincreasing reliance of business operations on IT brought a surge in the importance of IT reliability, availability, and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency last year to No. 6 globally (Luftman et al., 2012). Even though the global ranking has remained at sixth place, it has been pushed down to the bottom of the top 10 in the United States. The large ranking drop in the United States might not be because of the lower importance of this issue, but could be due to the persistence of <sup>fi</sup>nancial uncertainty in this geography, causing other pressing issues such as business cost reduction and IT cost reduction to take the limelight.

Two years ago we predicted a rising ranking for IT reliability and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in Europe, due to a number of very high-pro<sup>fi</sup>le cases, including the migration of the WikiLeaks website hosting from the United States to Europe as well a the well-publicized cyber-attacks on eCommerce sites such as PayPal and Mastercard (DarkNet.org.uk, 2010; IslandCrisis .net, 2010; Luftman and Zadeh, 2011). Our prediction did come to fruition not only in Europe, but also in Asia and Latin America as well (Luftman et al., 2012). This year, however, we believe there are other issues and opportunities with more immediate business return that will dominate the top 10 management concerns throughout 2013, and therefore its global ranking will remain within the top 10 but might see a slow slide toward the bottom of the list.

## IT strategic planning

IT strategic planning was the top-ranking concern in the inaugural US survey in 1980, and has remained in the top 10 ever since. It is in the top 10 yet again, albeit it has moved down a bit to seventh place. It is ranked sixth in the United States and seventh in both Europe and Latin America. As was discussed last year, a trend is emerging within Europe whereby an annual uni<sup>fi</sup>ed business strategic plan, incorporating strategic planning for IT, is developed (Hinssen, 2008). This not only ensures that business and IT are aligned, but also that IT strategic planning remains a focus for business and IT leaders throughout the year. Europe registering the highest ranking for IT strategic planning among all geographies is testimony to this emerging trend.

In contrast to their European counterparts, Asian and Australian IT leaders have ranked IT strategic planning just outside of their top 10 concerns. It seems the economic uncertainty elsewhere has brought other issues to the fore in these geographies, where the application of IT to improve the bottom line (i.e. business productivity and cost reduction, BPM, and revenue-generating IT innovations) has taken the limelight away from longer-term strategic initiatives such as business and IT alignment, business agility, and IT strategic planning.

## Security and privacy

Security and privacy is ranked in the top 10 across the board except in Asia and Australia, where it is ranked as No. 14 and 12, respectively. Security and privacy was ranked just outside of the global top 10 two years ago (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011), and moved to the top 10 last year (Luftman et al., 2012). We have been predicting that security and privacy will improve its ranking for a couple of years, and it has generally followed our prediction. This increase has been due to a number of highly publicized incidents in the past few years (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011, Luftman et al., 2012), and it seems there continues to be daily news about security or privacy breaches. In Asia and Australia, however, such news seems to have somehow moved from headline items to second or subsequent page items. The fact that it was a new entrant in Asia’s list of concerns last year was interesting in itself, but it is intriguing that it has now moved once again well outside of the top 10, especially as the 19th APEC Economic Leaders meeting in Honolulu in November 2011 listed ‘implementing an APEC Cross Border Privacy Rules System’ as one of the key steps in strengthening regional integration and expanding trade (APEC, 2011). It is rather dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to demonstrate the return on investment from security and privacy, and without major headline news, security and privacy seem to have moved slightly off the radar in Asia and Australia. We do not anticipate any major security or privacy incident in these geographies, but not paying close attention to and preparing for such events would inevitably adversely impact these organizations.

## Revenue-generating IT innovations

Revenue-generating IT innovations has remained in the No. 9 spot globally, but it showed huge year-over-year variation in different geographies. In Europe and Latin America, where it was within the top 10 concerns last year, it has moved outside of the top 10. However, it has improved its ranking substantially from ninth in 2011 in the United States to fourth in 2012 and from 16th in 2011 in Asia to the second spot in 2012.

It is interesting to see that this year revenue-generating IT innovation and enterprise architecture (EA) has ranked higher in Asia than IT reliability and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, IT strategic planning, and security and privacy. Similarly, in the United States, revenue-generating IT innovation has ranked higher than BPM, IT reliability and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and security and privacy. With the United States tending to lead other geogra phies, perhaps this trend will expand.

## Enterprise architecture/infrastructure capability

EA and infrastructure capability is ranked 10th globally. Although ranked 10th in Europe and in Asia, it is ranked eighth in the United States and continues to be outside of the top 10 in Australia (13th) and Latin America (16th). This management concern has shown quite a bit of variation in the United States from year to year, and it seems this trend is not con<sup>fi</sup>ned to the United States alone.

EA is important in developing business architectures to integrate and standardize enterprise functions and processes. It seems that the top technical investments of Cloud and Big Data are having an impact on the amount of focus organizations are placing on EA. We will track this concern, and its trends in different geographies, with great interest next year.

## Top <sup>fi</sup>ve applications and technology investments

Survey respondents were asked to rank the importance of applications and technology developments by selecting their top <sup>fi</sup>ve from a list of 51. Similar to the SIM survey, respondents were also asked to nominate new technologies if they were not already on the list. Table 5 lists the top <sup>fi</sup>ve application and technology rankings for 2012 across the geographies. Similar to last year’s results, some of the top <sup>fi</sup>ve applications and technologies, such as business intelligence, are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the top <sup>fi</sup>ve across all the geographies, while the ranking of some others, such as cloud computing, vary greatly across the geographies. This is indicative of the different cultural, technological, infrastructure, and human resource variation in these areas. The top <sup>fi</sup>ve applications and technologies for 2012 are discussed below with compar isons across the surveyed geographies.

## Business intelligence

Business intelligence remains the top technology investment in 2012. This is the fourth consecutive year that it has been ranked <sup>fi</sup>rst globally (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011; Luftman et al., 2012). It has been ranking the highest in the United States every year since 2003, and in Europe since 2010. It is now also ranked as No. 1 in Australia and Latin America as well. The only geography that registered a reduction in the importance of this IT technology was Asia, where it moved from the <sup>fi</sup>rst place in 2010 to the second in 2011 and to the <sup>fi</sup>fth in 2012.

However, IT leaders in Asia and Australia have this year ranked knowledge management far higher than any other geography. This can be a sign that they are gearing up to collect enough tailored data for knowledge management, and over the coming years these data could be leveraged for business intelligence.

Global high ranking of this technology is showing, yet again, that since business intelligence leverages data mining to identify valuable insight, IT leaders still believe their organizations are data-rich and insight-poor (Luftman and Ben-Zvi, 2010). In addition, recognizing the set of skills (e.g., data management, programming, statistics, tools, and industry/company) required to successfully leverage this technology, along with the limited number of candidates possessing these skills, is driving its persistent high ranking.

## Cloud computing

Cloud computing improved its global rank from the third place in 2011 to the second place in 2012. It is, however, very interesting that it has plummeted to the eighth spot in Asia and has moved well outside of the top 10 in Latin America. This seems to mirror its ranking in Europe last year, when it dropped to the eighth place compared with the previous year when it occupied the top spot. The implications of cloud computing are yet to be fully understood, and the slow pace of recovery in the United States and the economic volatility in Europe have forced Asian IT managers to look in their immediate organizational vicinity for certainty, where applications and technologies such as knowledge management, apps development, CRM, and BPR ranked higher than cloud computing (intriguingly analogous to the relative rankings in Europe last year). As a new generation of skilled labor enters the workforce in Asia, they may bring with them the added value of <sup>fl</sup>exibility, and therefore afford Asian CIOs the ability to have employees focus more on unique internal initiatives brought about with the Cloud rather than the Cloud itself.

## Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems

As has been the case for the past few years, the high ranking of leveraging IT to reduce business expenses and BPM/BPR as a management concern drives the associated applications and technologies such as ERP and Customer Relationship Management (CRM). ERP ranked third globally this year (one lower than last year), the same ranking it has enjoyed in the United States since 2010. In other geographies the ranking was mixed. It was lower in Europe and Latin America (fourth from the second and second from the <sup>fi</sup>rst, respectively) and it was higher in Asia (fourth from the seventh).

The ranking of BPM has risen substantially in Asia in 2012 (<sup>fi</sup>rst spot), in line with pronounced improvement in the ranking of ERP (fourth) and CRM (third) in that geography. These rankings suggest that the aggressive growth of BPM in Asia continues to drive the deployment of ERP and CRM.

The only geography where ERP was not ranked in the top 10 IT technologies was in Australia. ERP systems and their implementation were in the news in Australia for all the wrong reasons for quite a few years in the mid-2000s, and the stigma of those experiences still resonates with IT leaders in that region. Most IT leaders we spoke to in Australia are either still trying to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of their ERP systems or are very reluctant to embark on new implementation. As ERP as a technology matures and is proven elsewhere, we believe its ranking in Australia should improve.

As predicted last year, fragmented adoption and various market dynamics will continue to keep ERP as an important technology in the region in the coming years.

## Apps development

Application (apps) development covers a whole computing platform as a service across computer networks. With pervasive mobile devices (and Bring Your Own Device – BYOD), apps development goes hand in hand with cloud computing and can greatly bene<sup>fi</sup>t from it.

Apps development is a new entrant to the top <sup>fi</sup>ve IT applications and technologies this year, having been ranked No. 3 in Europe and No. 7 in Asia, making it globally fourth overall. Apps development still seems not to be on the radar in Latin America (ninth), the United States (ranked 11th), and Australia (22nd). With all indicators pointing to a growing proportion of smartphones in all geographies, and a rapidly increasing proportion of staff and clients wishing to access relevant data remotely, the pressure is on IT departments to develop applications allowing such access. We envisage that apps development will be one of the issues keeping many IT managers awake at night over the next couple of years. The support of BYOD is likely impacting the speed for Apps deployment around the globe.

## Customer relationship management (CRM)

CRM tools are ranked in the <sup>fi</sup>fth place globally and in Europe. This is the same position it has been ranked in the United States since 2010.

CRM was on the list of top 10 technologies in Latin America last year, but this year it has plummeted to number 20. CRM remains a relative newcomer in this region, and the same issues discussed last year still hinder its wide adoption. A main issue remains the lack of well-established data warehouses that limits mass adoption of tools that require detailed historical data.

As also discussed last year, Asian companies have been facing pressure to increase exports, which in turn means more importance placed on CRM tools. As a result, the ranking of CRM remains in the third spot in this region (also see the discussion on ERP and BPM in Asia). On the other hand, Australian IT leaders are more focused on improving internal processes (e.g. business intelligence, cloud computing, BPM, and collaborative and work<sup>fl</sup>ow tools) than external relationships (e.g. CRM, and customer portals and external community platforms).

Although different geographies continue to perceive improvements in the economy differently, an important message is that, overall, they are all indeed increasing investments in IT. As shown in Figure 1, CIOs reported increased IT budgets in 2012 compared with 2011 in 31% of the companies in Europe (45% last year), 45% in the United States (same as last year), 47% in Australia, 61% in Latin America (64% last year), and 80% in Asia (74% last year).

## IT budget as a percentage of revenue

The often-benchmarked average IT budget as a percentage of revenue for the United States had been relatively stable for a few years (around the 3.8% mark), except for last year when it decreased to around 3.5%. This year the same <sup>fi</sup>gure has increased to 4.9%, an unusual surge. This could indicate that IT leaders have had to have higher budgets this year to account for the uncharacteristically low budget last year, in concert with lower revenues. At the same time, European enterprises have decreased their IT budget from 6.4% of their revenue in 2011 to around 6.1% in 2012, due to budget reductions in concert with increases in their revenue. A similar pattern is evident in Latin America, where IT expenditure increased from 5.8% of enterprise revenues in 2010 to over 10.85% in 2011, and it came down to around 6.3% in 2012. Percentage of IT budget as percentage of revenue of Asian enterprises had soared in 2011 to over 11.8% compared with around 5.8% in 2010, and similar to the trend in Europe and Latin America, it has come down to a more reasonable level of around 7.1%. It seems the better-than-the-rest economic conditions have kept Asian enterprise leaders bullish in their IT expenditure; they have had to have a lower IT budget as a percentage of revenue, but the <sup>fi</sup>gure of 7.1% is far higher than any other geography. Analogous to results in previous years, an examination by industry reveals that some sectors, such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, real estate, insurance, and telecommunications, have IT budgets of around three times that of sectors such as manufacturing, construction, retail, and food and beverages. Interestingly, Asia and Australia are the only geographies where IT budgets in healthcare services are well above their respective average IT budget as a percentage of revenue.

## IT staf<sup>fi</sup>ng and compensation matters

Staf<sup>fi</sup>ng (internal and external) remains the largest single component of IT budgets (53% in both the United States and Europe, 41.7% in Asia, 46.4% in Australia, and 49.9% in Latin America). It should be noted that staf<sup>fi</sup>ng as a component of IT budget had shrunk in 2011 in all geographies. This seems to be a direct result of increased infrastructure spending and consolidation in the face of global economic uncertainties.

Offshoring of internal staff (elaborated on later in this paper) has really taken off in Asia (6% in 2011 vs 18.7% in 2012) and Australia (6% in 2011 vs 23.6% in 2012). It also has shown some increase in the United States (from 2% in 2011 to 6% in 2012), and has stayed at 2% in Europe over the same period. This has brought offshoring of internal staff up from 1.8% in 2011 to 6.3% in 2012. This trend is set to continue, with surges forecasted by IT leaders in the United States to 11% (from 6% in 2012), Europe to 13% (from 2%), and Latin America to 11.1% (from 4.1%). Asia and Australia anticipate a slight drop; from 18.7% in 2012 to 15.2% in 2013 in Asia, and from 23.6 to 18.5% in Australia.

This trend is not con<sup>fi</sup>ned to offshoring of internal staff. Employee expenses is set to generally increase in the United States, Europe, and Latin America between 2012 and 2013, while it is set to decrease in Asia and Australia. IT leaders are preparing to compensate for this change in expenses by altering their budget allocated to consulting. This is set to decrease in the United States (from 11 to 8%), Europe (from 14 to 4%), and Latin America (from 13.3 to 9.5%), and increase in Asia (from 6.9 to 11.8% a) and Australia (from 2.9 to 11.5%). This is yet another indication of the recovery as US, European, and Latin American companies have started to employ IT staff after having reduced internal resources at the peak of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis. In Asia and Australia, however, the reduction was never as pronounced or as harsh as in the other geographies, but now IT managers in these regions are trying to spread their risk by outsourcing more of their resources.

Salary increases for IT staff in Asia is still buoyant, with 79% of IT staff expected to have salary increases between 2012 and 2013 (compared with 79 and 74% in 2011–2012 and 2010– 2011, respectively). Salary increases in the United States are also set to continue (60% in 2012–2013), albeit not at quite as high a rate as last year (66% in the previous year). The same pattern is evident in Europe (34% this year vs 44% the year before). The only geography aside from Asia that has managed to maintain the stronger increases is Latin America, where again 64% of IT staff are expected to have salary increases. For reference, the pre-recession levels of salary increases in the United States were at 78% in 2008. The recent pattern may be an indication that salary increases are leveling off in different geographies to a more sustainable level (for each geography), rather than just continuing to escalate year on year, and this derives largely from expectations about the economic climate.

As indicated in our previous reports (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011; Luftman et al., 2012), the region with the lowest rate of expected salary increases is Europe. The 2010 ruling of the European Court of Justice allows unhindered access of the Eastern European worker to Germany’s job market, and this has resulted in an in<sup>fl</sup>ux of skilled migrants, with the ensuing downward pressure on salary increases in Germany. More broadly, with the current depressed economic environment in Europe, the resulting downward pressure has kept salary increases at bay for some time.

As in previous years, the respondents were also asked to indicate the anticipated percentage of their IT budgets allocated to education and training. The percentage of IT budget allocated to education and training is on the increase across all of the geographies after a slight dip in the United States and Europe in 2010, which could be an indication that organizations are willing to spend more money to prepare and retain their talent. It is also an indication that IT managers are warming to the idea that it is better to spend money on training staff even if it means they might leave, rather than not training them and having them hanging around.

Finally, with regard to the rate of IT staff turnover there were strong staff retention rates across the globe last year and these have generally improved in 2012. Staff turnover was 4.7% in Europe (5.56% last year), 5.2% in the United States (5.5% last year), and 4% in Australia (6.1% last year). The only geographies with increased staff turnover in 2012 are Asia (9.3% in 2012 vs 6.1% in 2011) and Latin America (6.7% vs 4.9% last year).

The decrease in staff turnover in the United States was at the back of an upswing in staff turnover in 2011. Staff turnover has been a very good barometer of economic outlook, as employees are far less likely to leave during economic downturns than they are during times of high growth. Instead of pointing to a slowdown in the economy, we believe the latest decrease is an indication that there is a large proportion of new employees in organizations who are less likely to want to move elsewhere after just starting a new job. We will be closely watching staff turnover trends in the next couple of years to see whether staff turnover as an indicator has indeed run its course and now it is maturing to a point where it is no longer directly proportional to economic outlook.

## IT organization considerations

This section discusses the survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings related to IT organizational structure, CIOs, and IT outsourcing.

## IT organization structure

The IT organization can have a major impact on the performance of a company. IT organization structure is the degree to which it is centralized, decentralized, or federalized (Luftman et al., 2012). The percentage of organizations with either extreme (fully centralized or fully decentralized) has, historically, been steadily declining in all geographies. For example, the percentage of enterprises with a centralized IT structure has gone down from 68% in 2010 to 61% in 2011 in the United States, stayed steady at 66% in Europe, and gone down from 69 to 57% in Latin America. In Asia, however, the percentage was 45% in 2010, and it has dramatically gone up to 78%.

As noted earlier (Luftman and Zadeh, 2011; Luftman et al., 2012), the two extreme organizational structures (i.e. centralized and decentralized) are becoming less prevalent in enterprises in nearly all geographies. A federated (or hybrid) structure can realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of both centralized and decentralized structures. Corporate-wide standards are enforced in an effort to maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of economies of scale, while providing <sup>fl</sup>exibility to business units to maximize unique application opportunities at the business unit level. It is easy to see why IT leaders across the globe would want to move toward federated/hybrid IT organizational structure. Indeed, 34% of the European companies are reported to have federated IT organizational structure in 2011 compared with 23% in 2010. Similar signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increases are evident in Asia (37.5% in 2011 compared with 25% in 2010) and in Latin America (31% in 2011 compared with a mere 14% in 2010). Bucking the trend was the United States, where the percentage of enterprises with federated IT structure was slightly reduced from 28% in 2010 to 26% in 2011. However, the long-term trend is the same as all the other geographies, 28% in 2010, 18.4% in 2009, 22.2% in 2008, 18% in 2007, 15.7% in 2006, and 15.8% in 2005 (Luftman and Ben-Zvi, 2011).

## CIO tenure

The average CIO tenure, on the rise in the United States since 2006, continues to climb, reaching 5.04 years in 2012. In 2011, the respondents in Asia reported a decline in CIOs with more than 3 years at the job of 42.42% of all CIOs, compared with 52.3% in 2010. But Latin America shows a reverse trend where, in 2011, 60.43% of CIOs are reported to have tenure of more than 3 years (compared with 48% in 2010).

The survey also asked respondents to indicate where CIOs were hired from. In all, 58% of the US and 56% European, 30% of Asian, and 34% of Latin American respondents stated that their CIO was hired from outside the company. This is a drop across the board compared with 2011 (65, 76, 46, and 44%, respectively). Hiring CIOs from within the company is up in all geographies, probably due to the perception that a new-to-organization CIO is less likely to understand the organizational business strategy and therefore less likely to quickly implement immediate changes to reduce business expenses and increase revenues, ranked among the top management concerns. The insight from these data is that, even though the odds of getting a CIO position in your current/own company have not been in your favor for a while, the odds are in fact improving.

When asked what background CIOs had before taking their current position, the respondents indicated that in the United States, 91% of CIOs were recruited from IT organizations (either internal or external). In Europe this was 82%, in Latin America 62%, and in Asia 55%, for CIOs being recruited from

IT organizations. In other words, you are not likely to become the CIO in a company without an IT background, although the odds here are also improving. For example, in 2011 a solid 100% of CIOs hired in Asia had IT backgrounds.

## CIO time on activities

CIOs continue to spend most of their time dealing with non-technical issues, but this year this represents a reduced proportion of CIO time: 76% in the United States in 2011 compared with 77% in 2011; 78% in Europe compared with 83% in 2011; 58% in Latin America compared with 63% in 2011; and 56% in Asia compared with 74% in 2011. Interestingly, CIO time spent on software development issues remains around 4–6% across all the geographies barring Latin America, where it has slightly declined from the high of 14% of CIO time in 2010 to around 11% in 2011, and 10% in 2012.

Because of the relatively less mature infrastructure in Asia and Latin America, IT leaders in these areas used to spend more time on technical issues than their counterparts in the United States and Europe, but this trend is converging. It seems that this metric is not solely dependent on infrastructure maturity, but also on issues such as staff skills and business focus (short-term returns vs longer-term strategic issues).

## IT outsourcing

IT leaders globally have long been looking to outsourcing as a vehicle to reduce costs as well as to <sup>fi</sup>ll skills gaps. The recession accelerated this even further, especially with the reduced optimism for a rapid turnaround. The result? Outsourcing continues to expand. Considering total outsourcing, which includes offshoring, near-shoring, and consulting of non-internal staff (notwithstanding the fact that different geographies de<sup>fi</sup>ne these differently), the allocation of the respective IT budgets are 29% in Europe (33% in 2011), 48% in Asia (31% in 2011), 32% in Latin America (18% in 2011), and 26% in the United States (18% in 2011). As highlighted above, it is important to note how rapidly consulting is expanding in Asia and Australia. Accounting for 6.9 and 2.9% of total IT budget in 2010, respectively, these <sup>fi</sup>gures are predicted to jump to around 11% in 2013.

Offshoring seems the most attractive in Australia and Asia, where the local currencies (namely, Australian dollar and Chinese yuan) have continued to hold their value at or near record highs compared with the US dollar. The Australian dollar has almost plateaued, albeit at near record highs, and we anticipate that offshoring activities will pick up even further at the expense of domestic outsourcing. The upward trend in the Australian dollar might continue even further as many ratings agencies are providing advice that the Australian dollar is the currency investment of choice based on IMF assessments of sustained GDP in Australia and its comparative stability in relation to the US and European markets (Economy Watch, 2010). This could be exacerbated even further if the predicted increases to interest rates (Sweeney, 2013) in 2013 eventuate, resulting in an even stronger Australian dollar and making offshoring an extremely attractive option for IT managers in that geography.

As noted in our report last year, the current US pressure on China for re-valuation of the Chinese yuan (Morrison and Labonte, 2008) has seen the Chinese yuan creep upwards, resulting in the above-mentioned pick-up in offshoring of staff. There are con<sup>fl</sup>icting opinions on the real value of the yuan vs the US dollar, and it remains to be seen whether the value of the yuan will continue to appreciate against the US currency.

Outsourced domestic staff follows mostly the same general pattern as for offshore staff – increasing in most geographies in 2012 compared with 2011and tapering off in 2013, except in Australia and Asia, where it is set to skyrocket in 2013 for the same reasons mentioned above.

Consulting service providers seem to be in for a tough year in some geographies, especially in Europe, where it is set to plummet from 14% of total IT budget in 2012 to 4% in 2013. On the other hand, consulting services are set to surge in Asia and Australia as IT leaders in these regions are trying to spread their risk by having a balanced portfolio of internal as well as external expertise at their disposal. Moreover, as explained above, the strong local currencies make offshoring of outsourced staff a very attractive option in Asia and Australia, which means that Asian and Australian IT leaders have a viable alternative to consultants in addressing their staff skill shortages.

Overall, our prognosis last year is equally applicable to consulting this year, as consulting is in for a period of consolidation. Meanwhile, volatility of exchange rates in the short term will dramatically change the attractiveness of offshoring for IT managers.

## Conclusion

As an interesting indication of continued global <sup>fi</sup>nancial uncertainty, IT cost reduction has returned to the top 10 global management concerns. This management concern had been pushed outside the top 10 management concerns last year as the prospect of an end to the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis was perceived to be just over the horizon – something that has become more elusive. Furthermore, business productivity and cost reduction have moved to the top position across all geographies except for Latin America, signifying that organizations are more interested in utilizing IT to increase productivity and reduce the cost of doing business as well as focusing on the reduction of IT costs. We also see the appearance of organizations starting to leverage IT to increase revenues.

There are clear signs of improvements in IT budgets and spending, with a steady progression toward, and in some cases even exceeding, pre-recession levels.

The pace of recovery from the recession is indeed sluggish, but it does vary greatly in different geographies as each area has its own set of characteristics and therefore varying management concerns and responses are observable. As highlighted in study last year, unique characteristics of local markets in<sup>fl</sup>uence management responses of enterprises operating in a globally linked environment. By comparing and contrasting different geographies, this research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the many similarities and dissimilarities that confront managers. Clearly there are regional in<sup>fl</sup>uences that are powerful enough to reduce the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of global trends.

In closing, it is important to point out that IT managers are working in a highly interconnected world, and therefore certain patterns span different geographic locations. However, this research found that while there are many similarities, there are also important local trends that managers must be sensitive to. In fact, in some regions such as Europe, local considerations are so prevalent that they have taken precedence over global in<sup>fl</sup>uence and interconnectedness. Overall, IT continues to evolve in its importance to organizations of all sizes, in every industry, around the globe.

The authors envision extending the survey’s participation to all geographies and welcome proposals to assist in obtaining additional data.

## Survey methods

The SIM survey has been conducted since 1980. Surveys prior to 2000 focused just on the top management concerns. Since 2003, the survey has been extended to pursue more speci<sup>fi</sup>c insights regarding the key IT issues of the day. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant strength of this research is its ability to identify important trends by comparing survey data from previous years. Beginning in 2008 the survey has been extended to IT executives from around the globe.

The 2011 survey was similar to previous ones in methodology and process. The questions were based on previous surveys, with questions modi<sup>fi</sup>ed based on previous results and suggestions from respondents and researchers (academic and industry). In addition, some questions were updated and new questions were added based on (1) lists from other similar research, (2) input from Board members from sponsoring organizations, and (3) the lead author’s experience.

SIM members represent the US respondents, while CIOnet members represent the European respondents. The results from S.E Asia and Australia were facilitated by Hossein S. Zadeh (co-author), and those from Latin America were facilitated by Martin Santana (co-author) and Guillermo Rodriquez (co-author). Senior IT executives were invited to take the online survey. The key US <sup>fi</sup>ndings were initially presented during the 2012 SIM annual conference (SIMposium) in late 2012. The US <sup>fi</sup>ndings and the initial international <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been published in the fourth quarter issue of MISQ-E. The purpose of the JIT paper is to provide important international insights.

The authors anticipate extending the reach of this important research to a more complete set of countries and geographies, and invite leading researchers with a strong network of IT executives to contact them.
