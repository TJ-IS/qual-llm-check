# Limitations to building institutional trustworthiness through e-government: a comparative study of two e-services in Chile

Matthew L Smith

Information Systems and Innovation Group, Department of Management, London School of Economics and Political Science, London, UK

Correspondence:

ML Smith, Information Systems and Innovation Group, Department of Management, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

E-mail: msmith@idrc.ca

## Abstract

There is much interest in the application of new information and communication technologies (ICTs) to improve the quality, performance, and responsiveness of public sector institutions thereby potentially improving trust. However, there is little empirical evidence about the interaction of ICT implementations on institutional trust of the public sector. This article extends our understanding of the interplay of ICTs in the public sector and institutional trust through an in-depth comparative case-study of two successful Chilean e-services. The article begins by developing a relational conception of institutional trust of which institutional trustworthiness is a component of that relationship. It then analyses the two cases separately with respect to three dimensions of institutional trustworthiness: good performance, aligned incentives, and the values of fairness and distributive justice. Following this, a comparative analysis of the two cases shows that while the implementation of technology may bring anticipated efficiency, effectiveness, and transparency gains, there are fundamental limitations to improving institutional trustworthiness through technological interventions. In particular, where points of discretion and ambiguity remain, or power asymmetries are exacerbated, trustworthiness is challenged. Furthermore, drawing on the cases and the relational conception of institutional trust, the article argues that enhancing trustworthiness is a constrained optimisation problem in an environment of competing and often conflicting values.

## Introduction

rust in government and trustworthy institutions of governance are arguably fundamental components of healthy democracies. Trust is considered especially important for countries in transition looking to consolidate democratic forms of governance (Offe, 1999: 43). Trust provides stability and a space for concerted government action that is critical to effective governance (Norris, 1999: 27). For example, without a requisite level of legitimacy and trust, citizens may withdraw voluntary compliance of governmental demands and regulations, or even actively resist governmental policy, rendering the government incapable of performing the tasks required of it (Nye Jr. and Zelikow, 1997; Levi, 1998: 88).

E-government is touted as one means to help establish trustworthy institutions and to build or restore the public’s trust in government (Bellamy and Taylor, 1998; West, 2005). Empirically, however, there is little evidence of how e-government helps to achieve these ends. This lack of empirical data is partially because of the relatively contemporary nature of e-government implementations that has meant limited time and opportunities to study the wider social, economic, and political implications of e-government projects (Weare, 2002; Avgerou et al., 2006: 2). The limited evidence is also a function of the fact that a majority of the research on e-government and trust in government has been macro-level survey work that focuses on citizens’ perceptions of particular e-services (e.g., Moon, 2003; Parent et al., 2004; West, 2004, 2005; Tolbert and Mossberger, 2006). These studies do not delve into the internal institutional dynamics resulting from implementing information and communication technologies (ICTs) in the public sector and how they contribute to changes in bureaucratic processes and government services that ensure a trustworthy operation that then may work to build trust.

To contribute to the understanding of how e-government affects trust in government, this research explores the first part of the process of building trust: how and in what circumstances does e-government contribute to creating trustworthy institutions of governance? This research is a substantial and substantive extension of Avgerou et al.’s (2005, 2006) comparative case study of four e-services in Chile and Brazil that looked at how e-government influences the development of trustworthy institutions of government. This study revisits the Chilean e-services with the benefit of added data sources and a novel conceptualisation of institutional trustworthiness.

The goal of the research is to advance our empirical and theoretical understanding of the interaction between e-government implementations and institutional trust. In particular, this research focuses on how e-government affects the attributes of a trustworthy government, and not what builds trust itself. However, the analysis maintains awareness of the fact that trustworthiness itself is one component of a larger institutional trust relationship. Although such an approach is necessarily incomplete, advancing the understanding of trustworthiness attributes of government is recognised as important to the development of theory of trust in government (Levi and Stoker, 2000; Hardin, 2000: 152, 2002).

The paper proceeds as follows. The following section examines the concept of institutional trustworthiness that provides the theoretical underpinning to the research. The article then presents the two Chilean cases: an e-tax administration and an e-procurement system. Each case contains a within-case analysis of changes in institutional trustworthiness influenced by the introduction of the new technology. Following this, a comparative analysis explores the limitations to developing trustworthy institutions through the implementation of ICTs in the public sector. Finally, the article concludes with thoughts on the implications of this research.

## Institutional trustworthiness

Academic literature generally focuses on the concept of institutional trust rather than institutional trustworthiness. This section develops a conception of institutional trust and trustworthiness that provides the theoretical framework for the study. This conception of institutional trust integrates theory from across a wide range of literature, including political science, sociology, information systems, inter alia. For more on the process of developing the conceptualisation, see Smith (2010).

## Trust and trustworthiness

Trust is understood here as a social relationship where A trusts B to do C (Hardin, 2002: 9); that is, A will only trust B if A believes B to be trustworthy with respect to C. For B to be trustworthy requires that B has both the competence and the motivation to satisfy the requirements of C (Kee and Knox, 1970; Hardin, 1999: 28). These two categories of competence and motivation subsume the list of trustworthiness features. For example, credible commitments, benevolence, and honesty can all be seen as traits that reflect motivation.

This definition of trust highlights the important relational nature of trust, implying that trustworthiness is but one component of a larger social relationship of trust with two purposive actors (Coleman, 1994: 96; Harre´, 1999; Reed, 2001). This is a crucial, but not entirely uncontroversial, distinction. In fact, the two concepts (trust and trustworthiness) are commonly conflated in the literature (Hardin, 1993: 512, 1996, 2002; Solomon and Flores, 2001: 56). This conflation is a simple slippage as trustworthiness can never be fully abstracted out of the context of the trust relationship within which it is embedded. However, the relational conception here suggests that trustworthiness and trust are both related and distinct; trustworthiness is both an objective feature of the relationship and simultaneously a subjective feature of that trust relationship (Hardin, 1993, 1996; Mayer et al., 1995; Levi, 1998; Levi and Stoker, 2000; Avgerou et al., 2005). This implies that one has to be very careful to disentangle the two to be able to understand their individual characteristics, their forms of mutual interdependence, and how they interact through time (Archer, 1995).

There are two other important and relevant aspects of trust. First, the motivation to trust can come from a variety of sources, both egoistic and non-egoistic. There is a split in the trust literature between rational self-interest accounts (egoistic) (Hardin, 1991, 2002; Cook et al., 2005) and normative, moral, and emotional (non-egoistic) accounts of trust (Lewis and Weigert, 1985; Blackburn, 1998). Selfinterested accounts of trust assert that people will trust in others whose interests are aligned with theirs (Hardin refers to this as encapsulated interests) (Hardin, 1991, 2002; Cook et al., 2005). Non-egoistic accounts are those that transcend individual and rational self-interest. These are values linked with emotional cares and concerns that are motivating (Lewis and Weigert, 1985). Such motivations can stem from several sources. They may exist as a cultural norm for trustworthy behaviour, such as the value of ethical reciprocity or a general duty of fidelity (Williams, 1988: 10; Levi, 1998: 88–89), or emerge from the emotional desire to be seen as trustworthy (Dasgupta, 1988: 53; Petit, 1995; Tyler, 1998: 288).

Importantly, the self-interest and moral or emotional mechanisms to trust are not mutually exclusive. For any trust relationship, any combination of these elements may be simultaneously coactive. Indeed, the simultaneous cooperation of these mechanisms is often necessary and different types of trusting relationships may be based upon different mechanisms to varying degrees. For example, trust in institutions may be based more on rational considerations of structural incentive systems and self-interested calculations than knowledge of individual motives, emotional concerns, or the norms of fidelity (Lewis and Weigert, 1985: 793).

Second, the interaction of trust and trustworthiness is linked through expectations (Barber, 1983; Sztompka, 1999). Expectations, with respect to C (A trusts B to do C), set the boundaries for the trust relationship. It is through the shaping of expectations that different values (egoistic or non-egoistic) are projected onto the trustee by the truster. Consequently, understanding values is always central to understanding any trust relationship (Barber, 1983: 66).

## Institutional trust

Institutional trust can be understood as a specific type of trust between a person and a social institution (Harre´, 1999; Offe, 1999; Sztompka, 1999). In the literature, institutional trust is primarily conceptualised as trust in the rules, roles, and norms of an institution independent of the people occupying those roles. It is this institutional arrangement that provides the motivations for those acting out those roles to perform their tasks in an appropriate manner (Cook et al., 2005). Consider Zucker’s (1986) definition of institutional trust, one of the most commonly referenced conceptions in the information systems literature. For Zucker, institutional trust emerges when ‘formal mechanisms are used to provide trust that does not rest on personal characteristics or on past history of exchange’ (Zucker, 1986: 61). Warren (1999, 2004) extends Zucker’s notion of institutional trust to understand trust in democratic institutions. For Warren, institutional trust is trust placed in the institutional norms and the effectiveness of accountability mechanisms. This requires three conditions: (1) institutions are defined by norms that both the truster and trustee know and the trustee is expected to follow; (2) the truster can effectively monitor (or know that others are monitoring) the trustee; and (3) the institution has effective accountability mechanisms if the trustee diverges from the norms. Likewise, O’Hara (2004) argues that trust in institutions comes from a specific and generally inflexible framework of codes of practice and rules (e.g., impartiality) and the credible threat of sanctions, and the institutions that impose sanctions must have sufficient power and authority.

Drawing from the relational understanding of trust, it is clear that the above understanding of institutional trust falls short on two accounts: (1) it conflates the concepts of institutional trustworthiness and institutional trust, and (2) in doing so, it does not allow for the analysis of the interaction of institutional trust and trustworthiness through time. However, a conception of institutional trustworthiness as a component of larger institutional trust relationship addresses these deficiencies.

## Components of institutional trustworthiness

At any given point in time and from a given particular perspective, institutional trustworthiness can be understood as an objective feature of an institution that determines its motivation and competence with respect to some domain. As a component of an institutional trust relationship, the competence, motivation, and domain are determined by the citizen’s expectations (Levi, 1998). This paper explores how e-government interacts and shapes trustworthiness, and does not explore the citizens ‘street-level’ epistemology of institutional trust (Hardin, 1993). However, any study that seeks to understand how ICTs are implemented within government alter the trustworthiness of government in terms of its structure, processes and outcomes, must be done with respect to a particular perspective of what constitutes trustworthiness. In this paper, this perspective is established by drawing on the existing literature and trust theory to develop propositions of the features of a trustworthy institution. In this way, the propositions can be subject to empirical scrutiny and crosscase analysis to deepen the conceptual understanding of how ICTs alter institutional trustworthiness, one component of the institutional trust relationship. This analysis also allows for reflection on the institutional trust theories themselves. Keeping in mind that the trustworthiness features are ultimately interpreted by citizens, the propositions of trustworthiness are expressed as indicators (or trustworthi ness cues) of trustworthiness (Gambetta and Hamill, 2005).

The discussion of trust above allows for the development of three general propositions about institutional trustworthiness: institutional competence, institutional interests, and institutional values. These components stem from the two dimensions of trustworthiness: competence and motivations (interests and values).

Proposition 1: Good institutional performance indicates a trustworthy government.

The competence of an institution, as portrayed by institutional performance, is one indicator of a trustworthy institution. Empirical data from trust studies suggest that effective performance increases the public’s trust in government (Mishler and Rose, 1997; Chanley et al., 2000: 254; Espinal et al., 2006). Conversely, ineffective government will tend to breed distrust (Offe, 2006: 10). For example, the most common reason Americans give for not trusting the government is that the government is inefficient and wastes money (Blendon et al., 1997). In this way, a good track record of delivering on promises is an indicator of a trustworthy government (Levi, 1998: 93).

Proposition 2: An institution with structures that align the institution’s policies (and the behaviours of the civil servants) with citizens’ interests indicates a trustworthy institution.

The self-interested account of trust in institutions hypothesises that citizens will trust in institutions whose interests are aligned with citizens’ interests. More concretely, trustworthy institutions are those institutions that pursue policy goals that people agree with (Hetherington, 1998: 793). Arguably, this implies that the structure of the institution contains the ‘procedures for selecting and constraining the agents of institutions so that they are competent, credible, and likely to act in the interests of those being asked to trust the institution’ (Levi, 1998).

Expressions of this phenomenon are captured in surveys when people respond that ‘the government is run by a few big interests looking out only for themselves’, ‘public officials don’t care about what people think’ (Orren, 1997), or the government ‘spends on wrong things’ (Blendon et al., 1997: 210). For example, citizens may trust welfare service providers because of the congruence of interests, whereas it may be significantly more difficult to trust legislatures because of the widely divergent interests of its members (Warren, 2004: 23–25).

One method for keeping the interests of an institution aligned with citizens’ interests is through mechanisms of transparency and accountability. Arguably, new ICTs provide a mechanism for promoting accountability of the public sector, opening up new channels of downward communication (Weare, 2002) and empowering citizens to monitor the activities of government (Wong and Welch, 2004: 276; Meijer, 2007). For example, governments can publish online information regarding public sector performance (Bhatnagar, 2004) and policies and policy intentions (Gelders, 2005). From this, citizens have the opportunity to influence the institution’s policies through mechanisms such as exit and voice (Hirschmann, 1970).

Proposition 3: An institution that embodies procedural values of fairness and/or distributive justice indicates a trustworthy institution.

Two values potentially embodied by the institutional structure, processes, and outcomes that indicate trustworthy institutions are procedural fairness (treating people fairly) and distributive justice (Mishler and Rose, 1997; Orren, 1997; Levi, 1998: 93; Sztompka, 1999; Levi and Stoker, 2000; Kumlin, 2004; Murphy, 2004). These authors theorise that an institution that holds and demonstrates these values is more trustworthy and will consequently generate trust. Conversely, breaking from these values is indicative of an untrustworthy institution that will ultimately build distrust and scepticism. For example, consider the powerful impact of scandals to destroy trust (Chanley et al., 2000: 254). Scandals represent a breakdown in procedural and distributive justice. Trust that is based on these values can be thought of as trusting in the ‘ethos’ of an institution; that is, the ‘implied normative meaning of institutions’ provides the grounds for trust in an institution despite the fact that the institution exists of unknown and unknowable strangers (Offe, 1999: 70).

The three component theories of institutional trustworthiness are summarised in Table 1. Each component should be understood as an abstract ideal-type theory that will have many different manifestations depending upon the type and quality of the e-service and institution. For example, aligned interests could manifest in a variety of forms such as improved e-service quality, increased user benefits, or decreased corruption.

There are other potential indicators of institutional trustworthiness that are not considered here, such as security (Avgerou et al., 2009), citizen privacy, and credible commitments (Farrell, 2004; Smith, 2010). However, no data were collected to reflect these potential attributes of institutional trustworthiness, and therefore they are not examined here. Having established the working propositions, the article now turns to the two e-service cases.

## Methodology

The motivating research question is: how does e-government contribute to creating trustworthy institutions of democratic government? To tackle this question a comparative case study research strategy was chosen (Yin, 2003; Gerring, 2007). Two cases of e-government services in Chile were studied: an e-tax administration and a public sector e-procurement system (ChileCompra).<sup>1</sup> These two e-services were selected for theoretical reasons as they provide an opportunity to study ‘successful’ e-government implementations. Success is, of course, a relative and constantly shifting concept as the process of ICT implementation and uptake, especially within government, is subject to a wide variety of interests and motivations that extend above and beyond the original stated purpose of the implementation (Kling and Iacono, 1984; Noir and Walsham, 2007). Success here, however, is defined with respect to the fact that the two e-services have both achieved, to some extent, improvements along the dimensions of the institutional trustworthiness dimensions. Consequently, these case studies provide an excellent opportunity to study in-depth and comparatively the dynamics that occur when ICT implementations realise to some extent the institutional trustworthiness propositions.

## Data collection

The data collected for these two cases come from three separate sources. The first source is the set of interviews and institutional documents from a 2004 study of e-governance and trust in which the author took part (see Avgerou et al., 2005, 2006). This was a comparative study of four e-services, two in Chile (the same two studied here) and two in Brazil that focused on the institutional changes resulting from the implementation of the e-services. Concerning the two Chilean e-services, 28 in-depth semi-structured interviews (with 37 subjects) were conducted of prominent stakeholders of the two e-services. The interviews included top government officials, administrators of the tax agencies, administrators in the government agency running ChileCompra, NGO activists, international development aid organisations, actors in government agencies responsible for policies promoting the use of ICTs in government and across Chile, and officials in various levels of government administration such as in the Chilean Chamber of Commerce.

The second data source was research conducted in early 2006 for a World Bank Project on the social impacts of e-government. The research looked at the e-tax and e-procurement systems in Chile and consisted of 14 semi structured interviews and seven surveys. The interviews were of 28 subjects including employees of the Chilean tax authority (SII),<sup>2</sup> employees at the national and regional levels, managers in the ChileCompra controlling authority, and public sector and private sector users of ChileCompra. These interviews followed a pre-designed template to assess the social impacts of the new technologies across a series of dimensions of social impacts. There were also seven different surveys, four of which are relevant for this study. The surveys were conducted of SII managers and employees, as well as public and private sector users of Chile-Compra. The surveys were adapted and made relevant for the Chilean e-services from similar surveys constructed by a World Bank team in India. Tailored for each different user group, the surveys asked a series of questions regarding the use and outcomes of the services including costs and benefits, personal assessments of the technology, and changes to work practices and the internal organisa tional processes. A majority of the questions used a five point scale to assess the respondent’s opinion. The surveys were conducted electronically. The ChileCompra survey was conducted using the online survey service, Survey-Monkey, and the SII survey was conducted using the SII’s internal survey software. For the SII survey, 61 managers and 226 employees responded (the total number of employees who received the survey was unavailable). The ChileCompra surveys were sent out using a mailing list provided by the state agency in charge of ChileCompra. A total of 7433 emails were sent to registered businesses with 322 responses (4.3% response rate) and 9538 emails sent to public sector users with 654 responses (6.8% response rate). Given the low levels of responses, the data from the surveys may be highly biased and thus should be treated as tendentious support for the arguments made in this paper. However, the survey data correspond to the interviews and other third party survey data providing somewhat more confidence in the findings.

The third set of data comes from research conducted by the author in 2005 and 2006 on the two e-services. This research augments the above data by delving deeper into issues surrounding the interaction of new ICTs in the public sector, institutional trustworthiness, and trust. The research consisted of a series of interviews and focus groups with public sector employees (including in the Chilean tax authority, the public sector entity in charge of ChileCompra, and public sector purchasing officers), citizens, and businesses that use the e-services. There were a total of 56 interviews, with 76 subjects, of which nine interviews were with the DCCP managers and employees, and 16 were with the SII managers and employees at the national, regional, and municipal levels. Although many of the interviews with e-service users were focused on institutional trust rather than trustworthiness, the interviews at times provide excellent data points to triangulate claims made by those who work in the public sector.

## Data analysis

Data analysis began with independent within-case analyses. The goal of the within-case analyses was to answer the question: how does the introduction of new e-government implementations interact with the trustworthiness of the public sector? The trustworthiness propositions provide the structure for answering this question. The data are first analysed with respect to the relation of the new ICTs impacting upon the three dimensions of institutional trustworthiness: institutional performance, aligned interests, and values. The basis of the data analysis is the interviews and the internal documentation which was then supplemented with the survey data.

Note that the within-case analyses are from an institutional perspective meaning that trust outcomes are not considered. Given the relational nature of trustworthiness, the analysis considers trustworthiness towards the society as a whole, rather than to a specific citizen or group. That is, the analysis looks for the areas that have been theorised to indicate trustworthiness to citizens. How and for whom this translates into trust perception is the target of another study.

The within-case analysis is extended with a comparative case analysis (Eisenhardt, 1989). The analysis consisted of comparing and contrasting similarities and differences between the results of the two case analyses along each of the three dimensions. It is at this stage where it is possible to begin to deepen the understanding of the ICT and institutional trust relationship, and to tease out more generalisable themes.

## Cases

This section presents the two e-service cases that explore the impacts of these services on institutional trustworthiness. This section begins with an overview of the Chilean social and political context within which these e-government implementations are embedded. The two e-services and their within-case analyses follow.

## Chile: social, political, and ICT policy context

Chile is considered a Latin American ‘success story’ (e.g., World Bank, 2001). In the last 20 years Chile’s per capita GDP more than doubled, poverty fell, and social spending doubled, especially in the sectors of education, health, and housing (Espinal et al., 2006). There is, however, still a high level of inequality and an estimated 20% of the population lives at or below the poverty line, with some arguing that the real figure should be higher (The Santiago Times, 2006). Chile also is an aberration in the region in terms of its institutions and traditions of governance. Chile has a relatively efficient state apparatus with ‘a fairly long tradition of professionalism, efficiency and independence from political interference’ (Sa´nchez, 2005: 233) and strong democratic traditions such as the norms of give-and-take, tolerance, and respect for fundamental liberties (Tiano, 1986; Valenzuela, 1995: 86).

In the mid-to-late 1990s, Chile began to look to new ICTs to improve governance as well as to modernise and rationalise the state (Dı´az and Rivas, 2005: 481). E-government was a main component of the larger public reform and modernisation programme that included ‘institutional and political reform, public management modernisation, decentralisation, transparency, and participation’ (AED, 2003: 373). The push has had significant impact, earning Chile a position atop Latin America and in the top 25 countries in the world in e-readiness (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2003). E-government transactions exploded from non-existent in 1999, to 34 in 2002, and over 300 by 2005 (Dı´az and Rivas, 2005: 503). Particularly impressive examples of this e-government rollout are the two e-services that are studied here that have received extensive national and international recognition (e.g. Panzardi et al., 2002).

## Case 1: E-tax administration

The administration of taxes is central to the operation of any state as it provides the central revenue source that funds public spending. It is especially critical in developing countries to get the tax regime and administration right as revenue is already limited. The Chilean tax authority (SII) is responsible for tax administration including tax compliance procedures, audit, and enforcement. The SII’s powers of enforcement entail jurisdiction over the interpretation of tax laws and the tax tribunal, the first judicial stage in tax disputes.

## The e-tax system

The e-tax administration system consists of three interconnected components. The first is an intra-SII network that provides internal communications, an online publication system, and Internet applications for SII auditors. The second component is an electronic platform that connects public sector organisations enabling them to communicate and exchange information. This amounts to massive agency interoperability that enables the gathering of third-party information about taxpayers such as salaries, interest earned, and investment holdings. The third component is the online SII-taxpayer system where taxpayers can perform a myriad of tasks such as obtaining a password, checking third-party information with respect to collected tax return information, checking tax status, submitting annual and monthly tax returns, making tax payments, and retrieving legally valid e-invoices for both products and services rendered. A relatively new service offered is the presentation of an income tax return proposal that is prefilled with the taxpayer data collected by the SII. This system completes taxes for a wide range of taxpayers, but does not complete the process entirely for those who own their own business. The SI tries to make it clear that this completed form is a simply a proposal and that it is the legal responsibility of the taxpayer to confirm the accuracy of the data before accepting it.

## Quantitative outcomes

The transition from a paper-based to a computerised bureaucracy is almost complete. The system is moving towards achieving its goal of putting all major tax transactions online. As of 2006, after only seven years, the SII now receives 100% of the third-party information electronically (Barraza and Hervias, 2006), and approximately 97% of taxpayers filling electronically, the equivalent of 2,068,001 received e-documents (Gigli, 2006).

The system has proven to be both efficient and robust. Its efficiency is reflected in the reduced tax processing time from 25 working days to just 12 h. This means that online taxpayers receive their return in terms of days or weeks rather than months. The system has also proven to be remarkably robust. Not only is it Chile’s number one Internet destination, but it also has been able to process large amounts of forms in short time periods, especially around the income tax deadline. For example, on 9 May 2006 the SII received and successfully processed over 25,000 tax declarations between 23:00 and midnight.

## Institutional trustworthiness analysis

Theory 1: Good institutional performance indicates a trustworthy government.

The objective outcomes described above suggest that the SII has consistently increased its bureaucratic capacity and performance from the early 1990s until 2006. Both the interview and survey data indicate that the administration and tax service has been directly enhanced in the expected ways by the implementation of the system:

1. SII employees stated almost unanimously that the introduction of the transactional stage where citizens can complete the tax forms online has contributed to increased efficiency and effectiveness of the SII. This has occurred primarily through three means: (a) reductions in human input errors (98% of the managers reported that the accuracy and consistency of data had improved); (b) reducing lost information from transported documents; and (c) reducing manual data entry. Employees report that their work processes are simpler (68%) and a reduced load of data handling and record keeping tasks (62%). Managers also experience benefits: 86% of managers report a reduction in time to generate reports and 78% of them believed the cycle time of key processes has been reduced.

2. In interviews, managers were confident that their ability to monitor internal processes has greatly increased. This is supported by the survey data. Ninety per cent of managers responded that IT has facilitated supervision over employees (69% strongly agree, 31% somewhat agree), and 86% believe that the ability to monitor performance has increased (56% strongly and 30% somewhat agree).

3. Managers also expressed that the increased information sources regarding administrative performance improved the ability of the organisation in tactical decision and policy making to achieve organisational goals. Eightyeight per cent of managers believe that the SII provides good decision support for the government and 93% believe that the policy formulation and implementation has improved with IT.

4. The auditing capacity has been greatly enhanced with the symbolic processing capabilities of computers. The SII has in place algorithms that effectively audit every tax form, with algorithms designed to identify potentially high-return audits. Internal auditing has also increased with the development of a new department of administrative control which monitors employees’ and disparate agencies’ activities through the new system. This new department monitors the tax processes and performance with the system generating ‘monthly management reports’ that include indicators for goals for offices and individuals. It also allows for central control and monitoring of the different regions and regional performance. As this happens automatically, it also saves time for the managers at the regional levels.

Theory 2: An institution with structures that align the institution’s policies (and the behaviours of the civil servants) with the interests of citizens indicates trustworthiness.

The SII places two types of information online. First are the internal rules of operation and transaction information that includes the tax procedures, rules, fines, etc. The SII has done their best to assure that all necessary information is available to avoid hidden costs that might surprise a taxpayer. This increased transparency is reflected by survey respondents. Eighty-five per cent of SII employees surveyed found the rules and procedures of the SII online to be very or somewhat transparent. Second is transaction information. When a taxpayer logs into the web page with their unique account and code, they have access to all of the information that the SII has on the taxpayers.

Although there is increased transparency, it is not clear that this transparency is linked to effective accountability mechanisms. This information potentially provides some level of accountability, of both the SII and of accountants. The clarity of the rules, regulations, and information make the citizen’s or business’ individual tax process open to scrutiny by the user making it possible to notice irregularities in the process. For example, if an accountant is used, all the information is easily available to the citizen to verify, if desired. However, if and when there is a significant dispute, the only recourse for citizens is to resort to the SII tribunal. In this instance, the SII is both the judge and adjudicator, and thus is questionable as an independent accountability mechanism.

Theory 3: An institution that embodies values of procedural fairness and/or distributive justice indicates a trustworthy institution.

One way to achieve procedural fairness is through ‘impartial’ processes. Here computers appear to make a relatively significant difference as they automate processes and remove the potential for misused discretion. The shift to online tax services also reduces personal interaction between citizens and SII employees. Taking the responsibilities of the interaction out of the hands of SII personnel and putting them online constrains the street-level discretion of front-line civil servants. This works to inhibit administrative flexibility in both situations. For example, interviews indicated that the civil servants at the tax offices would use the output of the technology as justification for their actions. It appeared to limit their flexibility at times that the taxpayer might prefer it. It also, at times, constrains the arbitrary exercise of discretion, something particularly important in the administration of taxes in Chile. A central concern of many of the users of the system interviewed was with the seemingly arbitrary use of power by the tax authority agents. This was expressed by one interviewee:

It [the SII] does not have a good reputation. Because the user is a condemned person when they make a mistake – they forgot something or whatever, the accountant – something – if you have a problem, you are condemned in advance y The taxpayer is afraid of the SII. It is an institution of fear. Because you know you will lose. They are going to charge you no matter what.

This issue was well understood by an municipal SII manager interviewed who was highly aware of how the quality of the street-level bureaucrat transaction is highly dependent on the personal factors such as the mood of the bureaucrat that day, what has happened in their lives recently, etc. He viewed computerisation favourably as computers are not subject to the same whims.

In terms of distributive justice, the benefits of the e-tax service do not accrue evenly across the Chilean society, and in particular, it does not directly benefit those of lower socio-economic status. Although the online tax service does potentially bring the benefits of increased ease of use as well as time and cost savings to over two million tax payers, another six to eight million people of working age do not earn enough to file taxes. Furthermore, the use of the online system presumes a degree of comfort with computers and the tax system that many Chileans might not have.

The SII has also aggressively looked to incorporate new taxpayers, especially small businesses that have traditionally operated outside the formal system. Of course, this is both a benefit and a cost to these businesses, which may become formalised, but now also have to pay taxes. On the other hand, increased tax compliance provides the government the means for the increase in social services and other social development policies that have benefited many Chileans over the last two decades. In this way, improved tax compliance and collection indirectly benefits many of the non-taxpayers who do not derive direct benefits of the system.

## Case 2: ChileCompra: public sector e-procurement

Public sector purchasing plays a behind-the-scenes, yet pivotal, role in the management and operation of government and the achievement of social and economic development goals. A more efficient and effective purchasing process generally implies more cost effective service delivery, something of special concern for cash-strapped governments. In contrast, poor or corrupt public procurement practices can have serious detrimental impacts on state finances, economy, health and human safety, environment, innovation, values, and finally trust in the government itself, among others (Transparency International,

2006). In Latin America, the state is the largest purchaser of goods and services (Hussmann, 2004). In Chile, between 2000 and 2006, public sector purchasing accounted for between 8 and 10% of Chile’s GDP. These transactions involved over 600 public sector procurement entities conducting approximately one million transactions per year with over 40,000 domestic and foreign suppliers (DCCP, 2002, 2006a). Even small savings of a few percentage points translates to savings in the hundreds of millions of dollars.

## Chilean public sector purchasing system

Chile’s current public sector purchasing system (PSPS) has undergone fairly significant institutional changes since the beginning of this century, many of which are partially attributable to ChileCompra. Two contextual factors have shaped the path of change over the last 7 years. First, the public procurement function in Chile was characterised by relative institutional weakness especially at the regional and municipal levels (World Bank, 2004). Second, much of the current PSPS in Chile was legally defined by the relatively new 2003 Public Procurement Law. This law provides the legal framework for the electronic procurement of goods and services. The 2003 law placed ChileCompra at the heart of the PSPS by making the purchase of all goods and services through ChileCompra mandatory (IADB, 2004; World Bank, 2004: 16) for all public agencies, with some exceptions.

## ChileCompra

ChileCompra is an electronic system developed to make public sector purchasing in the Chilean public sector more efficient, effective, and transparent. The main function of ChileCompra is as an e-marketplace bringing together purchasers and suppliers. There are two types of procurement processes executed through the ChileCompra platform: e-market and e-catalogue. The e-market is the online instantiation of the standard public sector purchasing process. The public sector procurement process can be broken up into six basic steps: (1) definition of requirement, (2) selection of a purchasing mechanism, (3) tendering and reception of bids, (4) bid evaluation, (5) bid selection and formalisation, and (6) the execution and management of the contract. Currently, almost the entire purchasing cycle can be completed electronically through ChileCompra, from the presentation of a tender, to emitting a purchase order, to billing and electronic payment. A central background feature of the platform is the security component including encryption and electronic bid capabilities to help ensure the integrity and confidentiality of both the system and bids (Avgerou et al., 2005).

The e-catalogue offers a more direct and time-saving approach to making routine purchases. The e-catalogue acts as a virtual store where public sector organisations can purchase goods and services, and is the first stop for public sector purchasing. The catalogue has products and services for set prices, purchasing conditions, and other specifications of the products and services, for a determined time-frame, as negotiated by the DCCP, the controlling governmental agency of ChileCompra. The purchasing process is straightforward. A purchaser selects what they want and puts the selection in their e-shopping cart. They then complete the transaction online and ChileCompra creates a purchase order which is then sent to the chosen suppliers. As of 2006, there were more than 130,000 goods and services available with around 490 suppliers (national, regional, large and small) across the country.

ChileCompra also functions as an information publishing portal making available a wide range of information such as invitations to bid, procurement plans, a database of past public sector purchases, as well as providing search and statistical functions (World Bank, 2004: 3).

## Quantitative outcomes

After a relatively unsuccessful first launch (Hussmann, 2004; World Bank, 2004: 4), ChileCompra was redesigned in 2002 and has since taken off in terms of use. By the end of 2004, around 84,000 providers were registered in the system supplying 655 public entities (including 123 municipalities) (IADB, 2004; Avgerou et al., 2005). Since the end of 2004, ChileCompra has continued consolidating and augmenting its gains (see Table 2). Perhaps as a mark of ChileCompra’s coming of age, from 2004 to 2006 it was ranked as the best transactional site in Chile by the Centre of Digital Economy Studies, beating out the second place SII website.

The operation of ChileCompra has had distributional impacts, opening up the playing field to a wider variety of suppliers. In 2006, sales from small and micro enterprises (SMicEs) increased to 35% of the total number of ChileCompra transactions, up from 31% in 2005 (DCCP, 2007). ## Institutional trustworthiness analysis

Theory 1: Good institutional performance indicates a trustworthy government.

ChileCompra, along with institutional reform policies and changed purchasing practices, has increased competence in terms of the efficiency and effectiveness of the public sector purchasing process (PUCV, 2006). The most immediate efficiency improvements, more directly traceable to the technology, were realised by reducing (or replacing) costly and time consuming manual work, and increasing competition between suppliers. This increased competition is evidenced in the jump in average suppliers per purchase tender from an average of 1.7 bids per tender in 2002 to 5.3 bids in 2005 (DCCP, 2006a). This has resulted in both cost savings and improvement in the quality of services. Two different procurement officers expressed their beliefs regarding these improvements:

y in my experience, we save sufficiently compared to the system before. Because, y before we worked with a group of providers who weren’t necessarily the cheapest, but now, no. y In terms of economic criteria, many more competitive offers arrive, because the providers have access and they can go online and compare, see what their competition has offered y so they will lower their process, or do what they need to in order to compete. So, we have spent less overall.

There is the perception of the market that the quality of the products is better than before y There is no analysis that I can give you to tell you with certainty that there has been a reduction in prices or an improvement of quality of the offers. y the perception [is] that they have more competition that makes the provider perform better, offer a better service.

The e-catalogue reduces transaction costs even more, substantially lowering costs for the search, order, and payment functions, as well as eliminating the waiting time for the solicitation and evaluation of bids. The DCCP has calculated that it is about \$50 cheaper per transaction, in terms of procedures. In 2005, the amount of transactions made through the e-catalogue was just under half of all total transactions realised through ChileCompra (DCCP, 2006a). Furthermore, by acting as a centralised purchaser, the DCCP is able to negotiate highly competitive prices for a large variety of goods, as would be expected (Anderson, 2004; Panayiotou et al., 2004). One municipality remarked that they had seen products up to 50% lower through the e-catalogue. Overall, this has been calculated to result in significant savings for the public sector to a tune of an estimated USD\$70 million in 2004 (DCCP, 2006a).

Another component of improved purchasing practices has been the move to professionalise the procurement officer position with new incentive structures and training. Although there are no data that directly attest to the improvements brought about through the training programme, the head of the Programme to Improve Public Sector Management at the DCCP stated that there is a big change between those who have completed the training compared with those who have not, especially when comparing the budget management practices to those who have not attended the programme. The data indicate that the procurement practices have become, in general, more rationalised and planned, rather than ad-hoc and reactive. For example, there was a reduction of urgent purchases, generally considered an indicator of poor management, from just above six percent of all purchases in February of 2004 to just over 2% in December of 2005 (DCCP, 2006a).

There are trade-offs to moving online, however. Before ChileCompra, experienced procurement officers with wellestablished procurement routines could simply pick up the phone and call a familiar supplier finalising a deal in a matter of minutes. There was no minimum wait time for the procurement process or a need to make a detailed specification of the product or service desired and establishing criteria for bid evaluation. Also, during the transition time, there are costs associated with the acquisitions officers changing their practices to adapt to the new procurement law and the new technology, that may initially appear more difficult, complex, or even unnecessary.

Another unintended outcome of ChileCompra is that the public sector finds itself often with an increased informational gap with respect to many of the new suppliers who enter into the newly expanded market space. This stems from the movement away from a more personal purchasersupplier relationship based on reputation and a history of past interactions to a more anonymous online relationship. The only information that is available online is the identification number of the business, and there is nothing about the company’s infrastructure, capacities, or past history. This makes it more difficult for the procurement officer to judge the bids, and sometimes results in poor service or product delivery. This information asymmetry also works in the other direction, where new providers are not used to the demands and expectations of the purchaser. This expanded field of providers increases the risks of engaging in transactions with untrustworthy providers, resulting in increased costs associated with an incomplete purchase tender. These include the losses to the public sector entity not having the product/service available in a timely fashion and the transaction costs in terms of time and manpower to repeat the tender process. Indeed, some procurement agents took it upon themselves to establish a parallel paperbased information system to track the quality of suppliers. One head of a procurement office explains:

We have a system – our own departmental system. We have a book of providers that we keep marking down. We give three opportunities y we put down, provider X didn’t complete the offer, and the people who choose orders know, and now some orders come in and they [the purchasing officers] don’t even pay attention to them, they throw them out.

The e-catalogue provides one solution to these problems. A challenge undertaken by the DCCP, rather than the purchasers, is to ensure that providers comply with their agreements. To do so, they have a point system they maintain based upon supplier performance. Furthermore, the e-catalogue provides more information about products, such as brand name, that provides additional insight into the expected quality of the product. Thus, purchasing through the e-catalogue solves two major information asymmetries – the quality of the product and the supplier.

Theory 2: Aligned interests between an institution’s policies and the interests of a citizen indicate a trustworthy government.

The almost entirely opaque purchasing process is now considerably opened up to outside scrutiny. This provides the theoretical possibility for accountability towards the public. For example, businesses can use this information to litigate any disputes through the tribunal. Interestingly, the procurement officers were well aware that the information was openly available and could be examined for discrepancies. As one noted:

Now they have more tools to be able to check on us. Before they didn’t do much auditing of this department. Now with ChileCompra they have more opportunities. Why? Because the system is open to whomever – so they can go in and see what we are doing.

Despite improved transparency of some aspects of the procurement process, the quality of the information has its limitations. One factor is that the information entered into and displayed on ChileCompra is a necessarily superficial representation of a sometimes complex and cognitively subjective process. Of the procurement stages, the bid evaluation is typically one of the most difficult stages to be carried out fairly and transparently (Transparency International, 2006: 42). In Chile, the procurement law obliges the public services to say what criteria they are using to decide between bids. It does not, however, oblige them to show how and why they chose particular criteria, nor how they used the criteria to make their decision. Often this means that the public sector purchasers provide terse explanations of their bid selection rather than detailed explanations. As one DCCP employee explained:

y they [the procurement officers] are supposed to give a reason to the provider about their decision, but they are ready-made sentences, for example, ‘because it matches best the criteria’ and that is all, it doesn’t really tell the provider anything.

Furthermore, the quality of the online information with respect to purchasing decisions is a function of the amount of subjective variables employed to make the decision. This means that, in general, the evaluation of services is less transparent than the evaluation of many other products. For example, printing paper can be easily specified in terms of its size, type, and price. There is not a large variation in other aspects such as quality. However, more subjective judgements are needed to evaluate providers of some services such as research consulting or job training. This fact was well understood by the procurement officers, as one related:

For example, if you want to hire a consultant – experience, creativity, they are not objective variables.

Reducing corruption also indicates aligned interests between the citizen and the institution. ChileCompra has introduced two mechanisms that work to reduce corruption: (i) increased access of the internal auditors to purchasing process information and (ii) the reduction of provider-supplier personal interaction. For any Chilean public sector agency, there are two main auditors: the internal controllers that are a part of the institution, and the Controller General of the Republic who is the main federal controlling agency. Both have access to ChileCompra and can use the system to aid in their duties. This has meant a general improvement in the quality and timeliness of information used for auditing, improving their ability to hold the purchasing officers accountable. Interviews indicate that this has an effect on the incentives of the civil servants who are quite aware of the auditors and their increased capacities.

The other means of reducing corruption comes through the decrease in personal contacts that provide situations ripe for corrupt activities. Personal interaction has been, for the most part, replaced by interactions through the portal, reducing the potential for the development of personal relationships that enter into the bid decision-making process. This, however, has not entirely eliminated the interaction between suppliers and buyers who will sometimes resort to using other forms of communication to maintain a personal relationship.

Theory 3: An institution that embodies values of fairness and/or distributive justice indicates a trustworthy institution.

The procedural justice value of the institutions and their procurement processes that is most profoundly altered by ChileCompra is the value of impartial institutions. This happens through the standardisation of the process that occurs because of a combination of the new technology, procurement officer training, and new regulations. For example, the imposition of the evaluation criteria, the regulatory framework, and a yearly purchasing plan work to constrain the activities of the procurement officer and force them to rationalise and justify the purchasing process. Purchasing officers admitted that there were always loopholes to get around the system if desired. Indeed, there were always means to justify the purchase ex-post. When asked if the system was transparent, one purchasing officer responded:

100% [transparent]? No, because, like I said, someone can always continue with particular providers y One can always say that they worked with these particular providers because these providers have responded well.

However, they also felt that too much abnormal behaviour would quickly be noticed with associated punitive consequences. As another procurement officer stated:

y you know that if you pass the first obstacle there will be other barriers that you can’t control and afterwards they can demand the clarification of the information that is obscure.

In terms of distributive justice, a major selling point of ChileCompra was the promise of increased business opportunities for small businesses, a component of the Chilean social and economic development strategy. The data suggest that small businesses have in fact gained in public sector market share through the use of ChileCompra. However, while ChileCompra does supply increased opportunities to small businesses it has also meant increased competition and lowered prices, causing difficulties for some suppliers. Furthermore, the DCCP has had a hard time negotiating deals with small businesses for the e-catalogue. Experience has told them that there is a tradeoff between increasing opportunities for small and medium businesses and obtaining good prices and the required level of service.

## Comparative analysis and discussion

This section draws on the within-case analysis above and other relevant theory to illustrate several areas where the introduction of ICTs in the public sector runs up against inherent limitations to promote trustworthiness. There are two limitations that emerge from the interaction of technology and the public sector:

 limitations to improving competence and procedural fairness, and

 limitations to improving transparency and accountability.

However, crucially, even if improvements are made in the above areas, there is a third limitation that modulates the resultant trustworthiness:

 Given that trustworthiness is a function of citizens’ values, the inherent competing or conflicting value systems in a democracy imply that institutional trustworthiness is subject to constrained optimisation.

These three limitations are discussed below.

## Competence and procedural fairness

Both e-services brought about improvements to the efficiency and effectiveness of public sector entities along a variety of dimensions. The greatest gains for the SII were capacity improvements resulting from the automation of previously manual processes, such as the income tax declaration and auditing processes. The gains from ChileCompra came mainly from the improved ICT-enabled information flow that brings together a wider variety of suppliers, improving market competition and leveraging centralised buying (e-catalogue).

Gains from automation, however, differed depending upon the process being automated. For example, the SII was able to almost completely codify their online income tax process. However, the purchasing process of ChileCompra could not be entirely automated. Human discretion was still necessary for the definition of specifications and decisionmaking stages. This difference is most clearly expressed in the two basic types of public sector bureaucracy: machine and professional. Machine bureaucracies, like a tax administration, are prototypical Weberian bureaucracies with a strong hierarchy and a focus on standardisation of procedures and processes. For this reason, a tax administration is generally a clearly articulated financial process that is amenable to objective codification and, therefore, to a relatively straightforward translation and implementation in technology. Consequently, it is not surprising that e-tax administration systems are some of the first, most common, and most successful e-government applications (Dunleavy et al., 2006).

In contrast, professional bureaucracies such as schools, hospitals, and social services departments are filled with civil servants whose daily activities are dominated by streetlevel interactions that are both non-legal and non-routine, requiring larger levels of discretion (Bovens and Zouridis, 2002; Dunleavy et al., 2006). It is not clear how far new technology can work to codify and automate such activities (Bovens and Zouridis, 2002). Consider the use of technology to rationalise and make transparent public sector purchasing. ChileCompra is able to rationalise the purchasing process up to a point, through the structuring of the process and provision of oversight mechanisms. Human intervention is ultimately required to provide the input to the processes such as the creation of the big specifications and an appropriate set of evaluation criteria and the ultimate bid decision making. Even with the technology, there are always ways to game the system. Creative procurement agents can overly specify particular product characteristics, or weight the evaluation criteria in such a way that only one particular bidder can possibly win. Currently the state of technology does not allow for the complete removal of human discretion from this process. The specifications for highly technical products or services, for example, require a high degree of specific expertise that is often beyond the knowledge of the average procurement officer.

Interestingly, even within the procurement process not all public procurement transactions are the same in terms of the amount of discretionary activity that goes into their tendering and selection (Talero, 2001: 9). This can be seen when comparing basic products with more complex services. Particular products, such as purchasing a chair or wood, constrain the discretion of the bid decision process more than for services, such as the hiring of a consultant. The reason for this is that for such services, there is an increased reliance on subjective selection criteria (e.g., relevant experience, quality), whereas for products, objective criteria such as the technical specifications and price are the central evaluation criteria. In other words, the public purchasing process retains an element of professional bureaucratic activity with subjective elements and expertise that make objective codification difficult. However, even within professional activities particular activities may be more amenable to codification and automation than others.

Even though the SII represents a quintessential machine bureaucracy, this does not imply that there is no discretion in the internal bureaucratic processes. Rather, discretion is better understood as shifting from the street-level bureaucrats who ultimately acted as policy creators and enactors (Snellen, 1998; Bovens and Zouridis, 2002) to software engineers and system designers who encoded the processes (Bovens and Zouridis, 2002; Reddick, 2005). This shift is seen in the case of the e-tax administration where the shift occurs from auditors who before chose who they would audit, to the managers, analysts, and software engineers who determined and encoded the automatic auditing algorithms. Given the complexity of some processes, other points of arbitrariness may emerge. For example, overly specified algorithms may not have the flexibility to deal with special circumstances. In this way, arbitrariness emerges through the back door because of the excessive rigidity and inability to take account of contextual details (Bovens and Zouridis, 2002: 182). Conversely, if not all details are specified in advance, the discretion falls to the system programmer. In such situations, policy decisions made at the edges are done in an ad-hoc manner and can result as an unintended consequence of the complex interaction of the software.

## Transparency and accountability

Meijer (2007) identifies two types of accountability: internal and external. The e-tax and ChileCompra implementations do provide mechanisms for increased forms of internal accountability. This is the accountability of the institutions towards a central accountability agency (such as the General Accountability Office in the US), as well as of civil servants towards their managers. In contrast, the two e-services studied here provide only a limited amount of external accountability. The increased transparency of the e-tax system and ChileCompra provide accountability at the micro-level of the individual or business interacting with the government. Although the e-services do not fundamentally change the accountability mechanisms available to the citizens, the transparency acts as a form of credible commitment (Farrell, 2004) that ensures that the state comports its activities as it should. For example, businesses can complain to a tribunal if there is a perceived illicit purchasing practice. Citizens can legally complain if they are overcharged for taxes or for other tax disputes, and it is easier for taxpayers to find the relevant rules and regulations. What makes this type of accountability possible, however, is that at the micro-level where the performance of the public sector is less ambiguous, there is an obvious principal who can dispute the outcome. The outcomes are salient for the interacting taxpayer or business and they can pursue the issue as needed. Of course, there are often resource limitations that inhibit SMicEs and citizens from enacting these accountability mechanisms.

Moving away from this micro-level of interaction, the link between transparency and accountability becomes more tenuous. Several important limitations emerge. The information offered through transparency, such as performance indicators, becomes increasingly ambiguous. Developing performance indicators and measuring them is problematic (Noordegraaf and Abma, 2003); it is impossible to know counterfactuals, some elements of government performance are not quantifiable (Bok, 1997), and performance often depends on a variety of potentially incommensurable scales of achievement (Sztompka, 1999: 83). Of course, external accountability also requires an active media or civil society organisations with the expertise to assess the performance indicators. In the case of Chile, there were several instances of confusion in the media where, through a misunderstanding of the role of the technological system, the media misrepresented instances of poor public purchasing behaviour, placing blaming on ChileCompra and the DCCP rather than on the purchaser who is responsible. However, even with an active media and civil society, what the public sector should do is a matter of contested debate (Nye Jr., 1997). For example, what is the appropriate normative judgement of the financial expenditures of the public sector in Chile? Should the public sector attempt to sell more to small businesses outside of Santiago for distributive justice purposes, or do they attempt to increase efficiency?

There is also a legitimate concern that new services might alter the citizen-government relationship in terms of exacerbating the citizen-government power asymmetry. With the push for hi-fidelity government services comes an increased need for information gathering (Giddens, 1990; Webster, 2002). As governments move towards improving services through the increased gathering of information, such as is necessary for the SII to complete a tax form automatically, a by-product is a movement towards a surveillance society (Bellamy and Taylor, 1998: 88). The SII collects an amount of personal financial information that would most likely evoke privacy concerns in Western states. The ChileCompra and SII systems have also become intertwined to expand the power of the state. For example, in order to enrol as a supplier in ChileCompra, a business must be registered. This has allowed the increased inclusion of small and micro businesses on tax registers that previously had operated informally. Furthermore, if tax payments are late, the SII can revoke your ability to get SII stamped invoices which in turn limits your ability to do business. This power asymmetry is particularly problematic for developing trustworthy institutions. The reason is because, in powerful relationships, the more powerful party can renege on an earlier promise at a low cost (Tschannen-Moran and Hoy, 2000; Farrell, 2004; Cook et al., 2005). This implies that as an institution increases its power vis-a\`-vis the citizenry, they have less incentive to put in place mechanisms to ensure their trustworthiness.

Public sector trustworthiness: a constrained optimisation problem A central feature of democratic governance is the institutionalisation of processes to settle conflicts of interest discursively rather than coercively (Warren, 1999). In other words, democracy by its very nature is a mechanism to balance conflicting interests and values, such as striking a balance between improving services, responsiveness to citizens, and the values of democracy (Pegnato, 1997; Glaser, 1999). Even the inherent values of democratic institutions themselves are not entirely uncontested as there are a variety of notions of public interest and consequently competing claims to trustworthiness (Barber, 1983: 67).

The structure of public sector bureaucracies embodies some democratic values such as impartiality and egalitarianism (Bellamy and Taylor, 1998: 88; Kallinikos, 2004). The introduction of technology in the public sector can work to alter these values. It is well understood that technology can embed political values (Winner, 1986; Introna and Nissenbaum, 2000), and in the public sector, these political values ‘have constitutional importance’ (Nye Jr., 2002, p. 14). E-government represents a policy choice that determines who gets what and when (Brewer et al., 2006). For example, Cordella (2007) shows how new public management models that incorporate private sector ideals in the public sector do so at the expense of democratic values. The increased personalisation of services as a means to increase the efficiency and effectiveness of government, as well as to predict and even influence behaviour (Pieterson et al., 2007), can run directly counter to the goal of equal treatment. In the same way, seeking to advance accountability through e-government implementations will at times alter the balance of values expressed by the public sector (Smith et al., 2010).

These two cases offer support for Cordella’s general argument, that democratic values might be sacrificed or at least shifted towards other values when implementing ICTs in the public sector. An example helps to illustrate. There are an inherently contradictory set of values that underlie ChileCompra. On the one hand, ChileCompra promotes increased efficiencies in the public sector procurement process through the enhancement of market competition. On the other hand, the DCCP was committed to improving the ability of SMicEs to compete in sales – the value of distributive justice. However, the reliance on market forces to increase competition and the goal of increasing opportunities for SMicEs are in conflict from the perspective of the supplier (Hussmann, 2004: 34). Not all SMicEs can win bids. This tension is also found in the use of the e-catalogue to obtain higher efficiencies. The most efficient approach for the DCCP is to incorporate several large suppliers into the e-catalogue and not bother with the small businesses that may not have the resources to make large-scale deals with the government at reduced prices. However, this runs counter to the one of the stated goals of the DCCP, and is an area of difficulty for the DCCP managers in charge of negotiating for the e-catalogue.

This analysis illustrates that any attempt at improving institutional trustworthiness in the public sector is subject to constrained optimisation. The implementation of technology to improve trustworthiness may indeed be effective along certain dimensions (such as improving government finances and lowering corruption), in particular bureaucracies, or for certain processes. However, the current state of technology does not allow for the complete removal of discretion from the public sector. Many of the more professional bureaucracies require a wide range of discretion – in contrast to the codifiable nature of tax administration. Furthermore, the link between the outputs of e-services and accountability is tenuous at best, especially when dealing with power asymmetries, aggregated data, and complex domains. However, even if it were possible to somehow hold the public sector directly accountable, and completely remove discretion from the public sector, technology itself cannot directly address the varying interpretations of trustworthiness that flow from the conflict of interests that are inherent in a democratic society. Further complicating this dynamic is the fact that people will often attribute human characteristics to technology, interpreting their interactions with technology as social interactions (Marakas et al., 2000).

## Conclusion

This article seeks to expand our conceptual and empirical understanding of the relationship between e-government and institutional trust. It introduces a conception of institutional trustworthiness as a component of a larger institutional trust relationship. In doing so, it argues for disaggregating existing institutional trust theory and analysis into three components: institutional trustworthiness, institutional trust, and the interaction between the two over time. Using this theoretical framework, the cases focus principally on the dynamics of introducing ICTs in the public sector and their interactions with institutional trustworthiness.

The analysis highlights what appear to be structural limitations to the continued improvement of institutional trustworthiness through ICT implementations. At the level of institutional trustworthiness there were at least two limitations: automation, at least with today’s technology, can only go so far; and digital transparency can only reveal so much. Furthermore, new ICTs might work to further tilt the power asymmetry in favour of government institutions. These dynamics are critical points where developing institutional trustworthiness through any e-government implementation is challenged.

Keeping in mind the embedded nature of institutional trustworthiness within a relationship of institutional trust, the comparative analysis goes further to suggest that there is a more fundamental constraint on improving trustworthiness through e-government implementations. Trustworthiness is as much a political and perception issue as it is a technocratic matter of improving institutional competence through efficiency and effectiveness gains. Consequently, building institutional trustworthiness and trust is ultimately a constrained optimisation problem: you can’t please all of the people all of the time.

The analysis here also suggests that theories of ICTs and institutional trust must reflect the sometimes seemingly contradictory nature of trustworthiness and trust outcomes; that is, when the same ICT implementation can both build and destroy trust. Theories of building institutional trust through e-government that connect particular implementations to trust outcomes need to be disaggregated into more concrete hypotheses that explore these dynamics. For example, hypotheses of institutional trustworthiness should spell out the different tendencies that build and limit improvements to trustworthiness in different contexts (see Smith, 2006: 203–206, 2010), as well as generate the competing dynamics of constrained optimisation. Methodologically, this suggests the need for developing, testing, and refining opposing hypotheses (e.g., when, why, and in what contexts do ICTs tend to build and destroy trustworthiness) and an openness to multiple interpretations (Robey and Boudreau, 1999).

This analysis has implications for thinking about the role of e-government implementations to build trustworthy institutions and ultimately to build trust. It becomes imperative to know when and how these implementations shift policy in ways that alter underlying value choices expressed by the public sector. Considerations of the impacts of e-government must get away from the view of technological implementations as value-free, apolitical tools of rational technological progress (Danziger et al., 1982; Schot, 2003; Bekkers and Homburg, 2007). This is a re-politicisation of e-government implementation and theory. This means shifting the evaluation of e-government outcomes from a universal and thereby acontextual notion of trustworthiness, or other conceptions of public sector management such as the good government paradigm or new public management, to a contextualised evaluation space, such as the concept of public value (Kearns, 2004; Grimsley and Meehan, 2007).

To further complicate matters, institutional trustworthiness exists relative to a forever shifting value base. Culture and values are not static; they shift (perhaps slowly) through time dependent upon a wide range of demographic, social, and other factors. Such a situation means that, much as political parties must re-invent themselves at times to adjust to demographic shifts and new political contexts, public sector bureaucracies will also have to be attentive and responsive to shifting values of the public they serve. Interestingly, this may be an area where in the future new ICTs enable a more flexible and responsive bureaucracy through the improved participatory feedback loops.

In conclusion, if the goal is to develop trustworthy institutions with the help of technology, perhaps it is more realistic to work within a framework of refined expectations. Building trust in government through new technologies will probably never resolve the points of tension that emerge from necessary discretion and incomplete transparency. Nor does it address the constrained optimisation problem. Consequently, institutional trust theory and research should reflect these tensions. Such an approach raises a new set of questions beyond notions of efficiency, effectiveness, and transparency: How do ICTs and associated reform programmes alter the fundamental values of the bureaucracy? What do they conceal and disclose? How might they alter the loci of power and points of discretion? Answers to these questions will provide insight into ways that information technology is impacting on the forever shifting goal of institutional trustworthiness.
