# Evaluating the impact of CASE: an empirical comparison of retrospective and cross-sectional survey approaches

RT Coupe and NM Onodu

Operational Research Group, School of Management and Mechanical Engineering, University of Birmingham, Edgbaston, Birmingham B15 2TT, UK

The purpose of this paper is to evaluate two methods of assessing the productivity and quality impact of Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) and Fourth Generation Language (4GL) technologies: (1) by the retrospective method; and (2) the cross-sectional method. Both methods involve the use of questionnaire surveys. Developers’ perceptions depend on the context in which they are expressed and this includes expectations about the effectiveness of a given software product. Consequently, it is generally not reliable to base inferences about the relative merits of CASE and 4GLs on a cross-sectional comparison of two separate samples of users. The retrospective method that requires each respondent to directly compare different products is shown to be more reliable. However, there may be scope to employ cross-sectional comparisons of the findings from different samples where both sets of respondents use the same reference point for their judgements, and where numerical rather than verbal rating scales are used to measure percep tions.

## Introduction

Managers are typically cautious when taking decisions about the adoption of new development software. This is because the likely gain to the organization must be traded against the cost of its purchase, and of training or securing new personnel. These gains are likely to include improved developer productivity, and better quality applications software that, in turn, may enhance operational efficiency and market competitiveness. While potential purchasers can examine a new software product and evaluate the results of bench testing, they must generally rely on information from other sources to find out how far productivity and quality are likely to improve when new software is actually being used in normal operational circumstances by typical developers and end-users. One important source is to assess the opinions of developers already using the product, using questionnaire surveys. Two approaches, the retrospective method and the cross-sectional method (Coupe, 1994), may be used to measure developers’ views.

Most published studies of the impact of Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) have used the retrospective survey method, which involves developers comparing CASE and the software technologies previously used for software development. However, these studies do not always make the bases for comparison clear, that is, whether CASE is being compared with the Fourth or Third Generation Language (4GL, 3GL) technology that the respondent previously used, even though the productivity achieved with these technologies is likely to inherently differ. This makes it difficult to reliably assess the productivity gain from adopting CASE software. Vendor product surveys can also present problems because they commonly consider one product in isolation, and do not involve respondents directly comparing different technologies or products.

The purpose of this paper is to evaluate the crosssectional and retrospective methods for measuring the impact of CASE software. It is principally concerned with developers who compare CASE with 4GL software, and these respondents are distinguished from others who made comparisons between CASE and 3GLs, and between 4GLs and 3GLs, thereby making bases for comparison explicit. The study uses data from questionnaire surveys whose primary aim was substantive: to assess the relative impact of CASE and 4GL development technologies on developer productivity and applications software quality. The objectives of this paper, however, are methodological. They are to determine the extent to which the findings from the retrospective data analysis and those from the cross-sectional analysis point to the same conclusions, the ways in which they differ, and to provide some insight into their relative reliability.

## The research designs

The cross-sectional and retrospective research methods can be illustrated by using a comparison between the productivity of developers using CASE against using 4GL software.

## Cross-sectional method

The cross-sectional method involves the comparison of the responses given by a sample of current CASE users with those of a different sample of users of 4GL software. The two sets of software developers are asked to rate the development software they are currently using, so the perceptions of both types of product are contemporaneous. Higher productivity ratings from CASE than 4GL developers would support the conclusion that CASE was superior in this respect. Although individual developers may express their perceptions differently, so that comparable levels of productivity could be rated by one developer as ‘high’, and by another as ‘very high’, it is assumed that this would vary randomly between the CASE and 4GL developers. The drawback of the approach is that it is inferential: the individual developers do not make a direct comparison between the two software technologies. It is quite likely that the context in which their views are expressed, and any other experience they may have had of other technologies, would influence how they respond. Thus, a CASE tool user with only prior 3GL experience might well rate its productivity higher than one with prior 4GL experience.

For both this and the retrospective method, other factors that influence productivity may vary systematically between the samples being compared. Whichever of the methods is being used, if this were the case, it would be necessary to control for them in order to isolate the impact of CASE on productivity compared with 4GL technology.

## Retrospective method

A single sample of current CASE users who have previously used or are also using 4GL software is questioned about the productivity differences between the two software technologies. Each respondent directly compares the two development technologies, and therefore conclusions as to which is better or worse can be made with more confidence than with the cross-sectional method. The retrospective method provides evidence about the relative standing of different development software, rather than about their absolute status. Even though CASE software might be rated as relatively better than 4GLs, respondents might nevertheless, feel that both technologies produce applications software of low quality, and this would not be apparent from their responses. Although the contexts in which developers experienced the two technologies may have differed, and this may have affected productivity levels in contrast with the cross-sectional method, developers responding to a retrospective survey are able to take account of this when making judgements based on direct comparisons between the technologies.

When using a retrospective design, it is generally necessary to rely on developers’ recollections of past software development. This may mean that memory lapse is a problem, while it is not generally possible to take account of the other factors that may have affected productivity in prior development environments. When this type of survey design is used, it is quite common for information about prior software technologies used by respondents to be omitted from questionnaires, and this makes the basis for comparison unclear, and the findings difficult to interpret. Despite this, it is perhaps the most common method of assessing the impact of a new policy or new technology.

## Methodologies used in existing studies

Published studies of the impact of CASE software have almost always used a retrospective method. Norman and Nunamaker (1989), Hayley and Lyman (1990) and Selamat et al (1994) all surveyed CASE users and used ‘retrospective’ questions to establish the productivity or quality impact of CASE tools; while Khosrowpour and Lanasa (1989) used a retrospective approach to compare 4GLs with 3GLs. In contrast, further studies did not seek to compare CASE with other software technologies, but asked CASE users (Wijers & Van Dort, 1990) or current and prospective CASE users (Burkhard & Henster, 1989) for their opinions about, and reasons for acquiring or wanting to acquire CASE tools. There are few, if any, published studies that compare results from a survey of CASE users with those from a survey of 4GL or 3GL users cross-sectionally, in order to assess their relative merits.

Supplier evaluations of new software use retrospective questions less frequently. They tend to rely on asking users how they view an item of software in isolation, without any explicit comparison with other software. However, there are problems in taking findings of this kind at face value. The risk of drawing the wrong conclusions would be enhanced rather than reduced if a potential adopter were to place the results of a number of separate product surveys ‘side by side’ and drew inferences based on informal cross-sectional comparisons. If one software product were typically rated as ‘very good’ by its users, and another as ‘good’ by its users with respect to some attribute, it might appear perfectly reasonable to conclude that one is better than the other. However, this practice has inherent dangers because perceptions are conditioned by the circumstances in which they are made, and these factors will not be taken into account if survey respondents do not make direct comparisons between different products or technologies. Comparable problems of interpretation are faced when placing the findings from separate retrospective surveys ‘side by side’. In reviewing published papers on CASE that involve unrelated questionnaire surveys, for instance, it is often difficult to know the extent to which findings are conditioned by the context in which the studies were carried out.

The purpose of the following analysis is to determine whether or not cross-sectional inferences, which involve comparisons between unrelated surveys, should be relied upon when evaluating different software technologies. The issue of whether developer perceptions about changing productivity are better measured using numerical questions rather than verbal rating scales is also addressed.

## Research methodology

The two research methods were evaluated using data collected from larger UK organizations. A sample was drawn from the population of companies operating at or above £50 million at the end of 1993, from the key British Enterprises directory (Dun & Bradstreet, 1992). Questionnaires were mailed to organizations’ information technology (IT) managers, with the request that they be completed by software developers actively using CASE, or, if CASE were not in use, a 4GL developer. In all, 1400 organizations were targeted and 127 companies returned a fully completed questionnaire, a 9% response rate. Fifty-three (41%) of the questionnaires were from active CASE developers, while the other 74 were 4GL users. Over 5% of the questionnaires were returned incomplete because the organization concerned used neither CASE nor 4GL technologies, and it is likely that this was also true of a significant portion of the other non-respondents.

Both the ‘CASE questionnaire’ and the ‘4GL questionnaire’ contained questions that elicited retrospective comparison from developers, and both included questions that could be used for cross-sectional comparison. Each respondent, therefore, answered questions about the ‘absolute status’ of the productivity and quality of either CASE tools or 4GLs. In addition, developers answering the 4GL questionnaire made comparisons with their former 3GL development environments, while the CASE developers were asked to make comparisons with prior 4GL work, or with 3GL development if this was what they had previously used. This meant that four distinct comparisons could be made (Figure 1):

(1) a retrospective comparison of CASE with 4GLs;

(2) a retrospective comparison of CASE with 3GLs;

(3) a retrospective comparison of 4GLs with 3GLs;

(4) a cross-sectional comparison of CASE with 4GLs.

## Sample representativeness

To verify the representativeness of the sample, the Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) of the sample companies was compared with that for the population. Overall, the survey respondents were reasonably representative, though responses from mining and light industrial companies were slightly under-represented, while those from service industries were somewhat over-represented.

## Findings

The findings from the two methods are inconsistent. While the ‘retrospective findings’ indicate that CASE software is better than 4GLs, the cross-sectional method suggests the converse is true. This conclusion was based on analyses using measures of productivity and quality that were available from both the cross-sectional and retrospective samples.

## Analysis of productivity

Developer productivity was measured in three ways:

(1) The speed of development: a measure of overall productivity, that indicates how quickly a software application can be designed and completed by the whole project team ready for the end-user to operate in the working environment.

(2) Analyst/programmer productivity, specifically concerns the productivity of the developers who design the system and produce the code.

(3) Ease of maintenance, which concerns maintenance productivity, the ease to alter and make corrections if the system is not satisfactory once in operation.

## Speed of development

While retrospective analysis showed CASE to be little or no better than 4GLs, cross-sectional comparison indicated that 4GLs were superior (Table 2) in terms of speed of development. Using cross-sectional data, CASE received poorer ratings than 4GLs tools (Mann-Whitney, P = 0.027), whereas retrospective analysis suggested there was little to choose between them: CASE was almost as likely to degrade as improve productivity and performance (Table 2).

## Analyst/programmer productivity

Retrospective analysis suggests that productivity is better with CASE tools than with 4GLs: 56% viewed CASE as better, and only a quarter as worse (Table 3) than 4GLs. In contrast, cross-sectional analysis shows the opposite (Mann-Whitney, P = 0.02); findings are virtually identical to development speed, with 77% of 4GL users giving a ‘good’ productivity rating, compared with only 57% of CASE users.

## Maintenance productivity

As perhaps might be expected, there is a similar pattern of contradictory results for maintenance productivity. Whereas CASE was superior in the retrospective comparison, 4GL technology was significantly more highly rated (Mann-Whitney, P = 0.037) when using cross-sectional data. When respondents directly compared CASE with the 4GLs they had previously used, 57% thought CASE better, and only 18% worse (Table 4). In contrast, when the separate samples of CASE and 4GL developers are compared cross-sectionally, 68% of 4GL users, but only 47% of CASE users, regarded the development software as ‘good’ in terms of ease of maintenance.

## Analysis of quality

A measure of overall applications software quality was included in the survey and can be used to compare findings from the two methods of analysis. It was based on developers’ perceptions, and enables the impact of CASE and 4GLs on applications software quality to be assessed.

While retrospective analysis showed that better quality applications software is produced with CASE tools than with 4GLs, cross-sectional analysis suggested the converse (Table 5). When directly comparing CASE and 4GLs, 33% thought CASE superior, and only 7% inferior; while, using the two separate samples, 4GLs were rated as better (Mann-Whitney, P = 0.047), with 80% of 4GL developers rating 4GLs as ‘good’ or ‘excellent’, compared with only 62% of CASE developers.

The comparative evaluation of CASE tools and 4GLs, using retrospective and cross-sectional methods, has resulted in conflicting findings. In general, when developers directly compare CASE tools with the 4GLs they had previously or were concurrently using, CASE was rated as superior in terms of productivity and quality. In contrast, every cross-sectional comparison, involving two different samples, showed CASE to be inferior to 4GLs (Table 6).

## Explaining the contradictory results

There are a number of possible explanations for the inconsistent results:

I Sampling error might have affected the representativeness of one or more of the samples.

I Memory lapse might have affected the judgements of developers making retrospective comparisons.

Differences in developers’ expectations may have affected their absolute ratings of productivity and quality, and thus distorted the findings from the cross-sectional analysis.

Developers’ views of productivity and quality are likely to hinge on the context in which they are being experienced and judged.

## Sample representativeness

There are a number of factors that have an important influence on the effectiveness with which CASE tools are used, especially with respect to productivity and quality. These include:

I the number of years experience using the software technology;

I developers’ skills, knowledge and abilities;

I experience of the organization in which they work;

I knowledge and working experience of structured methods;

the nature of the required applications software, since some software development tasks may be easier with CASE tools while others may be more difficult;

the survey respondent’s job, whether project leader, analyst, programmer, or analyst/programmer might also affect perceptions about different software technologies;

the amount of training respondents have received in the CASE or 4GL technology they are using could affect software production.

These factors can improve or lower productivity and software quality, and if any of them were over- or underrepresented in either of the CASE samples or the 4GL sample, then this could account for the discrepancies in the findings from the two methods. Thus, sampling error might have resulted in a retrospective sample of survey respondents weighted in favour of those more able to use CASE effectively, while, in contrast, cross-sectional samples might tend to exaggerate the value of 4GLs and undervalue CASE tools.

In practice, however, the two CASE samples, the retrospective and the cross-sectional, did not differ with respect to any of these factors. Nor was there any difference between the cross-sectional 4GL sample and the retrospective sample. So, for example, although the number of years a developer has used CASE technology, and his previous use of structured methods, and the number of years working in the organization influenced his productivity and quality ratings, none of these factors varied significantly between the relevant pairs of samples. Therefore, the differences in the results of the cross-sectional and retrospective methods are not attributable to sampling error.

In addition, survey respondents were specifically questioned on the importance of the hardware platform in influencing software production. They felt it was the least important factor affecting productivity and quality.

## Memory lapse

Memory lapse did not affect the responses of developers making retrospective comparisons. In fact, 90% of developers were still using 4GLs as well as CASE tools. Moreover, responses did not differ between CASE developers who had stopped using 4GLs, and those who were still using them.

## Developer expectations

Many prospective CASE users can have inflated expectations about the value of CASE (Burkhard & Jenster, 1989), particularly its productivity (Hayley & Lyman, 1990), and this can influence their perceptions (Rushinek & Rushinek, 1986) when they eventually use the technology. This may, in part, reflect the nature of some CASE suppliers’ literature. Wijers & Van Dort (1990) and Parkinson (1990) also stress the importance of realistic expectations if the effects of a negative client response on sales are to be avoided, since the higher the expectations, the more difficult it is to impress the developer (Liang, 1986).

If many CASE developers have expectations that have not been fully met, then it is quite likely that they would have rated productivity more harshly than comparable 4GL developers, particularly in the ‘cross-sectional’ samples, where no direct comparison between the two technologies is required. Consequently, a CASE user’s ‘quite good’ might be equivalent to a 4GL user’s ‘very good’. However, when using a retrospective design, an explicit comparison is needed, and, even if a developer has unsatisfied expectations, the extent of any undervaluation is likely to be constrained by the need to relate CASE productivity perceptions to the comparable 4GL experience and perceptions.

The reliability of the views expressed about productivity by developers directly comparing CASE and 4GLs, retrospectively, can be judged by seeing how well they fit into the overall pattern of direct comparisons between CASE, 4GL and 3GL software.

Verbal and numeric measures of productivity change Survey respondents were asked to answer questions about numeric as well as verbal differences in productivity between different software technologies. This makes it possible to gauge the extent to which developers classify on similar scales, and to appreciate the way the meaning of verbal descriptions alters according to the types of software being compared. It also provides an insight into the relationship between developers’ perceptions of the degree of change in productivity, and the way this is verbally expressed.

In general, there is a fairly good association between the verbal, descriptive ratings and the perceived productivity changes expressed as percentages on each of the three diagrams, and on each there is a marked hierarchical trend. This suggests that developers comparing, for instance, CASE tools with 4GLs, classify on a similar scale. Developers comparing CASE and 4GL technology associated verbal and numeric measures the most distinctly, since there were no overlaps between the percentage figures for productivity change between adjacent descriptions on the verbal rating scale. Where developers compared CASE or 4GLs with 3GLs, they described much larger changes in productivity, and there was some overlap. Thus, some respondents comparing 4GLs and 3GLs who cited a 100% increase in productivity expressed this as a ‘large increase’, while others expressed it as a ‘slight increase’. Although similar amounts of productivity change are, therefore, sometimes described verbally in different ways by developers comparing the same two types of software, a great deal of consistency, nevertheless, is evident in the hierarchical trend.

In contrast, there is much less consistency in the numeric, productivity ratings and the verbal descriptions made by developers comparing different pairs of software, that is, across the different diagrams. So, the meaning of a ‘large increase’, for instance, depended on the context in which it was being used. For developers comparing CASE with 4GLs, it meant a productivity increase of only 30% to 100%, while for those comparing either CASE or 4GLs with 3GLs, it meant increases of between 20% and 1500%, or between 10% and 900%, respectively.

## Discussion

The size of the difference, for instance, in what developers mean by a ‘large increase’ in productivity between CASE and 4GLs on the one hand, and between CASE and 3GLs on the other (Mann-Whitney, P = 0.024), confirms that perceptions about software technologies and the way they are described depend on the context in which they are used and evaluated.

It is, therefore, clear that the meaning of verbal ratings is not fixed and absolute, but varies systematically according to context. This context includes the characteristics of the software being compared, as well as the impact of developer expectations. The variable and unstable character of verbal ratings has been revealed by contrasting three sets of retrospective data. However, it appears certain that the verbal ratings expressed by respondents in answering questions about software productivity and quality in the ‘cross-sectional’ samples will similarly depend on the context in which they were made. They may indeed vary even more because no comparison with other software was involved.

It follows, therefore, that drawing inferences using a cross-sectional method is hazardous, and should be used with great caution. This is because the two samples will not be compared directly but will often contain perceptions expressed in different contexts, and under circumstances where developer expectations can be very different. The comparison of numerical survey questions with verbal rating scales also indicates that the responses to numerical questions about productivity changes tend to be context specific, since the values cited depend on the types of software technologies being compared. This means that the use of numerical rather than verbal rating scales would help to make drawing inferences from the cross-sectional comparison of unrelated survey findings less risky.

## Comparing findings from different samples

Findings from different samples may be reliably compared, cross-sectionally, if they include the same reference point for comparison. A comparable baseline for evaluation would force respondents to use a similar scale for classifying productivity and quality changes. This can be illustrated with an example that also confirms the greater reliability of the retrospective method over the cross-sectional method. This concerns the developers that retrospectively compared CASE with 3GLs, and those that retrospectively compared 4GLs with 3GLs. Both these sets of respondents used the same baseline for comparison, namely their experience and views of 3GL technology. There were two relevant findings:

1. There was no significant difference in the amount of improvement in productivity cited by CASE developers and that cited by 4GL developers compared with the 3GLs that both groups had previously used, ie A compared with B in Figure 1 (verbal ratings: Mann-Whitney, $\begin{array} { r } { P = 0 . 2 0 5 8 ; } \end{array}$ numeric ratings: Mann– Whitney, $P = 0 . 7 5 4 6 )$ . This is in line with the findings based on retrospective analysis (Table 6), but, like this finding, is at odds with the finding from the crosssectional analysis.

2. CASE developers cited significantly greater overall improvements in applications software quality than 4GL developers, when both groups compared with the 3GLs that they had previously used, again A compared with B in Figure 1 (Mann-Whitney, $\overset { - } { P } = 0 . 0 2 4 3 )$ . This result also confirms the previous retrospective finding but contradicts the cross-sectional one (Table 6).

Therefore, analyses based on a cross-sectional comparison of two sets of respondents, each making retrospective comparisons using the same reference point, validates the retrospective method rather than the crosssectional method.

## Conclusion

Caution should be exercised in selecting the right research method when investigating software developers’ opinions. The method that is most appropriate will depend on the particular survey objectives. Surveys that aim simply to assess developers’ views about a given software product, without any comparison with other alternatives, can be reliably used to determine consumer views.

However, it is important to appreciate that the opinions expressed in surveys of this kind will be influenced by the context in which the respondents are making judgements, and this will include their expectations and prior experience. It is because of this that, although it might appear a perfectly reasonable thing to do, the findings from two such surveys, one covering CASE and the other dealing with 4GL technology, should not be put ‘side by side’ and compared cross-sectionally in order to draw inferences about their relative merits. For assessing relative benefit or impact, or for comparative evaluation, a retrospective method that requires the respondent to directly compare different products is more reliable. However, it may sometimes be possible to employ two cross-sectional samples if both sets of respondents use the same reference point for their judgements, while the use of numerical rather than verbal rating questions is likely to make cross-sectional comparisons less hazardous.

The decisions that organizations make about investment in new software technology need to be based on reliable information. The views of existing users can help with this by informing potential adopters about the likely benefits and drawbacks. The productivity and quality impact, the ease of implementation and how long it takes to learn about a new product are important issues that questionnaire surveys can help to resolve. However, their design and interpretation can be a little more complicated than might at first appear. If an appropriate research design is not adopted, there is a risk that we will misread the evidence and end up with the wrong conclusions.
