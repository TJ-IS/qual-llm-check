# Understanding transfer pricing: a case-based reasoning approach

O Curet<sup>1</sup> and J Elliott<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>City University Business School, Frobisher Crescent, Barbican Centre, London EC27 8HB; <sup>2</sup>Department of Accounting and Management Science, University of Southampton, Highfield, Southampton, SO17 1BJ, UK

This paper introduces the domain of transfer pricing, followed by an overview of case-based reasoning. Then, case-based reasoning is used to analyse the results of a questionnaire survey in transfer pricing. Finally, some conclusions are drawn and opportunities for future research identified.

## Transfer pricing

## Introduction to transfer pricing

Transfer pricing is currently one of the most topical subjects in the international taxation arena. In a recent survey, Ernst and Young (1995a)<sup>1</sup> interviewed a sample of over 200 multinational enterprises (MNEs) from the Global 1000. It showed that transfer pricing was considered to be the main international tax issue by 82% of the sample (and 92% of UK MNEs). In addition, the OECD and the US have published updated regulations on the tax treatment of transfer pricing.<sup>2</sup> In particular, the OECD (1994) recognised that the role of MNEs in world trade had increased dramatically over the last 20 years, and commented on the tax implications:

‘The growth of MNEs presents increasingly complex taxation issues for both tax administrations and the MNEs themselves since separate country rules for the taxation of MNEs cannot be viewed in isolation but must be addressed in a broad international context. These issues arise primarily from the practical difficulty, for both MNEs and tax administrations, of determining the income and expenses of a company or a permanent establishment that is part of an MNE group that should be taken into account within a jurisdiction, particularly where the MNE groups’ operations are highly integrated’.

Transfer prices are established where physical goods, intangible property and services are charged between companies within the same group. There are a number of internal variables that influence the transfer pricing method that are used to achieve corporate goals. These goals include controlling subsidiary operations, and motivating, evaluating and rewarding subsidiary performance (Arpan, 1988; Abdallah, 1989). Once transactions cross international borders, transfer pricing takes on an added dimension: when MNEs’ subsidiaries and branches operate in different tax jurisdictions, the transfer prices cease to be solely an internal concern. The corporate goal for MNEs is often perceived to extend beyond internal objectives to the maximisation of global after tax profits. This objective may be achieved by using transfer prices to utilise corporate tax rate differentials and shift income to countries where the corporate tax rate is lower.

One of the effects of the latest transfer pricing developments has been a shift in the balance between internal (such as motivation or performance evaluation) and external factors (such as tax), seemingly making the external factors more influential. Bonfiglio (1995) contended that the ‘Internal Revenue Service made it clear in the final regulations that from its perspective, intercompany pricing is a tax determination, not a financial or a business decision. Thus, the final regulations essentially will force the tax department (within the company) to take a more prominent role in the development of the company’s international tax strategy and the implementation process than has previously been the case’. If this prediction turns out to be true, it will be insufficient for companies to claim that internal prices are set in the course of normal business activities. Companies must be able to demonstrate that their pricing policy falls within the pricing methods permitted by tax regulations.

## What is the appropriate transfer price for tax purposes?

It is often held that there are two approaches available to tax authorities concerning taxation. One is that their major objective is to maximise tax revenues; the other is to assess tax on a fair level of profit and avoid double taxation. Assuming that the latter assumption prevails, the tax authorities need to be able to verify that the transfer prices have been computed in a reasonable manner generating a ‘fair’ level of profits in each country in which a group of companies is operating.

The arm’s length principle (ALP) is viewed by the majority of parties involved in transfer pricing as the most appropriate method.<sup>3,4</sup> ALP requires that the result of an inter-corporate transaction should be similar to the result of a transaction which would have taken place between unrelated parties in similar circumstances. ALP is a simple concept in theory but difficult to arrive at in practice.<sup>5</sup> OECD (1994) describes two different sets of methods that are available for computing an ALP:

Transaction-based methods ‘compare the prices charged in controlled transactions undertaken between those enterprises with prices charged in comparable transactions undertaken between independent enterprises’. OECD (1994) identified three transaction-based methods: (i) The Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP); (ii) The Resale Price method; (iii) The Cost Plus Method.

Profit-based methods ‘examine the profits that arise from controlled transactions of one or more of the associated enterprises participating in those transactions’. Two methods were identified in OECD (1995b); (i) The Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM)); (ii) The Profit Split Method.

## Case-based reasoning (CBR)

Introduction to artificial intelligence (AI) and CBR Rule-based expert systems (RBES) have been perceived to be the AI system most applicable to decision making in business domains (Curet & Killin, 1991). However, since the mid 1980s, the design, applicability and use of these systems in certain domains have been questioned (Coates, 1988; Connell & Powell, 1990) on the grounds of a lack of a general and well-accepted methodology in RBES design, and a high dependence on somewhat rudimentary models (such as the rule-based formalism).

A new approach, CBR, is one of the latest AI technologies. It has been suggested that this technique is more suitable than traditional expert systems techniques in experience-rich domains but when there is no model. One can find a comprehensive review and bibliography of CBR systems in Kolodner (1993), Marir and Watson (1994) and Watson and Marir (1994). Riesbeck and Schank (1989), generally recognised as the originators of this concept, claim that CBR comes closest to how human learning and reasoning operates. Given that people learn and reason from past cases, CBR allows the storage of cases relating to specific problems which have been solved already. Once the past cases have been stored in a case library, it is possible to compare and match a current unsolved case with a past one for which a solution was found. In addition, as new ‘solved’ cases are added to the library, a fuller understanding of problem solving emerges. This learning process allows the system to circumvent one of the shortcomings of traditional RBES, namely the problems associated with defining and modifying the rulebase. Other advantages of CBR include the ability to reason in domains that are not completely understood and to evaluate solutions when no algorithmic method is available. A list of some of the differences between traditional rule-based and CBR systems can be found in Curet and Jackson (1995).

CBR has been shown to support human judgement in fuzzy domains for which rules and procedures have not been determined, such as top management fraud detection (Curet, 1995). As CBR involves reasoning about the current situation on the basis of pinpointing and resuscitating past instances, the user can look at previous case details and associated decisions, quite unlike the rule base required in traditional expert systems.

Many papers have compared classification algorithms on large data sets. One of the most recent and comprehensive studies can be found in King et al (1995), who compared sixteen algorithms (six from symbolic data, eight from statistics and two from neural networks) on twelve large real-world classification problems.<sup>6</sup> One of the conclusions of this study showed that

‘symbolic algorithms are a good choice for maximising accuracy if the data have an extreme distribution (. . .) and/or if there is a high percentage of binary/categorical attributes. If ease of use and human understandability are of high priority, symbolic algorithms should also be chosen’.

## Limitations of the CBR approach

The suitability of a problem area for a case-based approach: cases may be perceived as being too pragmatic or instrumental and therefore, collecting them may be pointless. For example, a CBR approach may not be appropriate for income tax calculations which is a rule-based domain (ideal for a rule-based system).

Defining a case description: A case is only a snapshot at a point in time. It is supposed to encapsulate a problem definition and a past solution to the problem. There are many cases that can be used to represent a solution space for a specific problem domain. This is why it is important to assess and control the types of information that should be contained in the cases. The information includes the data types (numeric, Boolean, symbolic or even text), and their definition influences the way cases are represented and retrieved.

Collecting relevant cases: case elicitation is not problem-free: Memory is fallible, and so the case library may contain noisy information or even invalid cases. A case wrongly identified may carry exactly the same weight as a case which has been identified correctly. Case collection also raises the question of the number of cases to be gathered to provide a valid case library. On the one hand, there may be too few cases to collect, and in this instance some domain knowledge may be missing. On the other hand, there may be too many cases, and in this instance the most relevant ones may become ‘diluted’.

Organizing and indexing cases needs attention: This concerns the selection of cases to be stored, and the types of indexes to be used. This will influence mainly the retrieval process. Whichever way the appropriate cases are retrieved, the approach cannot take shortcuts to solve the problem, unlike rules that lead to a specific solution would. Since the generation of similar cases and the selection of the closest one to the present problem that supports the user’s decision-making processes, the organizing and indexing of the case library is fundamental to the retrieval and adaptation process.

The need to define the level of the applicability of a former case and its adaptation to the present problem is also important. Adaptation is often considered as the most difficult part in CBR research. It may also appear that a set of most similar cases does not contain the most appropriate case. At the same time, cases may have lower levels of similarities on some specific features, but overall they are more relevant to the present situation although less similar to it. In other words, there is no obvious answer derived from the most similar cases, just various levels of approximation.

Deriving rules from cases: Once cases have been stored in the case-based system, performing induction for example on these cases may result in rules in a form of a decision tree that will not evolve much with time. This will be the situation where new cases closely match cases already in the case library. In such situations it can be argued that it would be more appropriate to build a rule-based system embodying the rules elucidated by the case-based approach.

## An introductory example to induction using CBR

The following hypothetical example considers whether there is a relationship between the levels of internal transfers (IT) in relation to sales in companies, their levels of turnover (TO) and whether the Inland Revenue made investigations into their transfer pricing policies. The example consists of 25 hypothetical companies with turnovers ranging from £100 million to more than £5 billion, their respective levels of IT (less than 1% to more than 50%) and whether there has been an investigation (Yes/No). Each of these companies is a unique combination of one of the five levels of turnover and one of the five levels of IT.

Looking at a two-dimensional representation of the population, a few facts can be deduced. It is apparent that there is some influence of size and number of internal transfers on the audit decision (where ‘+’ represents a transfer pricing audit and ‘−’ represents no transfer pricing audit). First, there are always investigations for companies with more than 25% of internal transfers (regardless of size). Second, for companies with IT of less than 10%, there has been only one investigation (for the largest company).

Using induction it is possible to identify a more formal statement of the relationships. As suggested above, the decision tree in Figure 2 confirms that the level of IT is the key discriminator (less than 10%). Once this key discriminator has been detected, it is possible to refine the analysis by clustering further the 25 cases. The 10 cases with a level of IT of less than 10% need to be divided into the nine instances without audit versus the one instance with audit. The next discriminator for these 10 companies is turnover above or below £5 billion. The 15 cases with internal transfers exceeding 10% also need to be subdivided further. When they are classified into companies with levels of internal transfer above or below 25% it identifies 10 cases which have had an audit.

This way clustering helps the user to access a resolution to problem-solving.

## The applicability of CBR to transfer pricing

One of the main strengths of CBR systems is that the case inferencing process provides a framework for exploring the domain and concrete examples (cases) to support a decision. In addition, the user is exposed to questions (or variables) which may have been overlooked, forgotten or mis-specified.

Transfer pricing is a difficult problem area, for which expertise exists mostly in the form of cases rather than plans and operations which can be converted easily into rules. In the context of transfer pricing, the existence of numerous competing objectives makes it difficult to develop a coherent set of rules. Therefore, CBR might present a viable medium for learning about transfer pricing decision making within an organization. As well as its potential as a system for decision support, CBR might also offer a way to evaluate survey responses on transfer pricing from an external user’s perspective.

Rushinek and Rushinek (1988) observed that ‘one of the major sources of frustration in studying environmental variables of multinational transfer pricing is the multiplicity of associated variables and the inherent data redundancies’. Often there is multicollinearity between many of the variables<sup>8</sup> (violating any assumptions of independence) and the variables themselves exhibit skewed (rather than normal) distributions. These violations may bias some multivariate statistical analyses (Hair et al, 1995). Previous empirical studies of transfer pricing practices in MNEs have struggled to cope with the richness of the data collected from surveys and have had to restrict the information content.<sup>9</sup> One approach is the use of factor analysis (Burns, 1980; Al-Eryani et al, 1990), and another approach is to reduce the number of variables by careful selection (Yunker, 1983). Other studies have also used probit (Al-Eryani et al, 1990) and regression analyses (Cravens, 1992).

Cravens (1992) carried out a recent study because it was believed that ‘researchers do not have a clear picture of how the multinational firm considers multiple objectives in determining transfer pricing policy’. Borkowski (1996) emphasised the need for more research into the factors affecting transfer pricing method selection because in existing studies there is a ‘lack of consistency in factors studied and statistical testing and/or reporting (p 50). These arguments suggest that CBR might provide the ideal means to explore and learn about the domain.

Borkowski (1996) supported the use of questionnaires for evaluating the transfer pricing domain<sup>10</sup> and there has been some support for using CBR to evaluate survey data (Morgan & Bond, 1989; Curet, 1995). Morgan and Bond (1989) advocated using CBR techniques to analyse and understand market research data in a number of domains. They identified some of the shortcomings of both statistical and inductive (CBR) analyses, but concluded that ‘it seems possible that the deterministic, statistically based modelling we are used to (and have used for years) may have to make room for the new “rule of thumb” heuristic procedures’. Transfer pricing could be a suitable domain which could be explored and explained using the inductive reasoning of CBR.

## Analysis of the transfer pricing survey

## The transfer pricing survey

Following the publication of OECD (1994) and US (1994), Elliott (1995b) conducted a survey of UK MNEs.<sup>11</sup> The literature review demonstrated the extent to which statistical techniques have been used in previous empirical studies, but CBR has never been used to explore this domain. Therefore, the overall objective of this survey was to evaluate the potential of CBR to explore and explain some fundamental transfer pricing issues.

The main research question addressed here is ‘what are the key factors which determine which companies have a transfer pricing audit?’<sup>12</sup>

The questionnaire survey<sup>13</sup> was sent to a sample of UK-based MNEs taken from the Company Analysis database (a global accounting database). Two selection criteria applied:

The company is a chemicals, pharmaceuticals, electronics or electricals company.<sup>14</sup>

I The most recent annual turnover exceeds £100 million.

After identifying the Head of Taxation and sending out an initial and a follow-up questionnaire, 24 completed surveys were returned out of 108 surveys sent out (representing a 22.2% response rate). These responses allowed a descriptive analysis of the survey questions and illustrated the power of using cases to understand the transfer pricing domain and learn about the stated research questions.

Table 2 describes the composition of the sample in terms of annual sales and proportion of internal trade (as a percentage of total company sales). The sample contains a higher proportion of smaller companies and there is only one company with annual sales exceeding £5000 million. The majority of the companies have internal trade between 1% and 25% of total sales, although two companies have internal trade of less than 1% and two companies greater than 50%.<sup>15</sup> This table shows that two of the larger companies are responsible for the two instances of internal trade exceeding 50% and that two of the smaller companies account for the two instances of internal trade below 1%.

Focusing on company operations, the respondents were asked to assess the following descriptions of their companies (where 1 = not at all appropriate and 5 = very appropriate). Table 3 partitions the sample into those companies with internal trade either greater or less than 10% of annual sales. The results conform with prior beliefs about that set of companies with higher levels of internal trade. They are more vertically integrated, more technologically sensitive and their products are much more speciality with no intermediate market.<sup>16</sup> As expected, most of the companies did not view themselves as having a predominantly UK presence (only one company considered that a predominant UK presence is a very appropriate description).

Transfer pricing audits are becoming more of an issue for MNEs.<sup>17</sup> For instance, Richards (1994) described the increase in transfer pricing audits in Australia and ‘there now seems to be almost an expectation that the Taxation Office, through its transfer pricing audits, will find that multinational taxpayers are ripe for the picking’. In the sample, eight companies indicated that they have been subject to a transfer pricing audit in the last 5 years.<sup>18</sup> Table 4 disaggregates transfer pricing audit (yes/no) by the type of transfer pricing method ranked as most applicable by the 20 MNEs which replied to both sets of questions. The four audits of market-based pricing involved resale minus. As suggested by Arpan (1988):

‘cost-based prices have one distinct advantage over marketbased prices: flexibility. Because any cost element that enters into the computation of the base price can be changed as well as the percentage mark-up, it is much easier to change a transfer price based on cost than one based on market’.

For this reason, one might expect more instances of transfer pricing audit in companies which use predominantly cost-based transfer prices. However, the instances of transfer pricing audit in the sample used are distributed fairly evenly across market-based and cost-based transfer prices.

Finally, from the selection of determinants suggested in the survey, there appear to be no dominant factors which appear particularly important in the determination of an ALP (see Table 4 for the list of factors). Indeed, most of the factors have some influence on the ALP. In the survey (based on mean scores), local market conditions, the provision of a fair return on assets to the subsidiary and the competitive position of that foreign subsidiary are viewed as the more important of the identified factors in determining an ALP.

## CBR results

The results described here use ReMind’s induction process to produce decision trees. These decision trees help the user to learn about the domain. The main objective is to determine, using induction, what influence the following groups of factors (their variable names, as included in the decision tree, are stated as well) have on whether or not companies have a transfer pricing audit?

I Organizational factors

I Internal and external factors

I Pricing

I UK tax authorities

I Dividends

## Organizational factors

Organizational factors include annual sales, the proportion of internal trade (as a percentage of total company sales) and the four descriptions of company operations. Respondents were asked to assess whether their company’s operations were vertically integrated; technologically sensitive; speciality products with no intermediate market; and predominantly UK presence (where 1 = not at all appropriate, and 5 = very appropriate).

## Internal and external factors

Table 4 describes the internal and external factors presented in the questionnaire. These were rated from 1 = very unimportant to 5 = very important in the determination of a transfer price. Then the five-point Likert scale was reclassified for this analysis as low (1,2), neutral (3) or high (4,5).

## Pricing factors

There were three specific questions on the pricing method used, identifying an arms’ length transaction and on advance pricing agreements:

TPM: The transfer pricing method (TPM) nominated as the most applicable arm’s length pricing method for each company (cost-based or market-based);

ARM: Is it possible to identify a comparable arm’s length transaction in your industry? (where 1 = not at all, and 5 = to a great extent);

APA: How important do you consider the possibility of obtaining an advanced pricing ruling? (where 1 = very unimportant, and 5 = very important).

## UK tax authorities

There were two specific questions on the UK’s tax authorities approach to transfer pricing and documentation:

UK1: How satisfied are you with the UK’s approach to transfer pricing issues? (where 1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied);

UK2: How onerous are the documentation requirements needed to justify an arm’s length pricing structure in the UK? (where 1 = not at all onerous, 5 = very onerous).

## Dividends

There were two specific questions on surplus ACT and foreign income dividends (FIDs):<sup>19</sup>

DIV1: Does your company have a stock of surplus ACT? (Yes/No);

DIV2: To your knowledge might your company distribute FIDs in the future? (Yes/No).

## Transfer pricing audit based on all factors

The decision tree in Figure 4 begins to offer some explanation for which companies had transfer pricing audits. First, all respondents with internal trade exceeding 25% of company sales (three in the sample) had audits. Second, companies could be subdivided on the basis of the importance of intra-group negotiations in the determination of the transfer price. The seven companies that considered negotiation to be of low importance did not have a transfer pricing audit. Third, six of the remaining companies could identify a comparable arm’s length transaction to a medium or high degree. These six companies did not have transfer pricing audits. Five of the remaining eight companies that could identify a comparable arm’s length transaction to a low degree had transfer pricing audits. These eight cases are less easy to interpret<sup>20</sup> but might be resolved with a larger case library.

On the basis of this decision tree, the majority of the factors do not appear to be important discriminators for the current sample (including TPM, UK tax authorities’ variables and dividend variables). The UK tax authorities are more likely to audit either companies with high levels of internal transfers or companies that use negotiation to set their transfer prices and have no obvious comparable arm’s length transactions in the industry. These results appear sensible and therefore for a larger sample the CBR approach could provide a legitimate and logical explanation of the problem domain. Because the qualitative features are an essential component of transfer pricing cases, statistical techniques would not provide a similar degree of explanation.

## Conclusion

One of the critical aspects of any CBR library is to collect as many representative cases as possible. For a study of any significance on the application of CBR to transfer pricing decision, many cases are required so that statistical tests and cluster verification can be performed. Nevertheless, the main objective of this study was to evaluate the potential of using CBR in a business domain such as transfer pricing. So far the use of statistical multivariate analysis of survey findings to establish causal models has provided decision support of limited applicability and it is necessary to find a better way to understand the domain. As such, the small sample provides sufficient data and interpretation to suggest that

CBR’s inductive retrieval process is a suitable technique for exploring a larger sample of cases.

One of the strengths of CBR is that it provides a better understanding of patterns of pricing decisions and corporate profiles associated with transfer pricing investigations. Within an organization, CBR could be used by companies to support their pricing decision based on the analyses of past cases. This approach would allow a more detailed and meaningful analysis and becomes more likely with the increased interest in data warehouses in many organizations. An even more radical step would be to design and use shared case libraries (preferably by a number of companies in the same industry) which would incorporate global knowledge, which is crucial in the area of transfer pricing.

## Summary

To the authors’ knowledge this is the first study to use CBR to explore the domain of transfer pricing. Data on transfer pricing was collected from a sample of UK MNEs. This paper illustrates the applicability of CBR to explain the relationship between a range of variables and whether or not the company had a transfer pricing audit. Initial findings suggest that CBR may have a valuable part to play in learning about this problem domain.
