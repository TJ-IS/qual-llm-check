# System dynamics in software project management: towards the development of a formal integrated framework

AG Rodrigues and TM Williams

Department of Management Science, University of Strathclyde, 40 George Street, Glasgow G1 1QE, UK

Successful software development is becoming increasingly important to many companies. However, most projects fail to meet their targets, highlighting the inadequacies of traditional project management techniques in this unique setting. Despite breakthroughs in software engineering, management methodologies have not improved, and the major opportunities for better results are now in this area. Poor strategic management and related human factors have been cited as a major cause for failures, which traditional techniques cannot incorporate explicitly. System dynamics (SD) aims to model the behaviour of complex socio-economic systems; there has been a number of applications to software project management. SD provides an alternative view in which the major project influences are considered and quantified explicitly. Grounded on a holistic perspective it avoids consideration of the detail required by traditional tools, looking at the key aspects of the general project behaviour. However, if SD is to play a key role in software project management it needs to be embedded within the traditional decision-making framework. The authors developed a conceptual integrated model, the SYDPIM, which has been tested and improved within a large on-going software project. Such a framework specifies the roles of SD models, how they are to be used within the traditional management process, how they exchange information with the traditional models, and a general method to support model development.

## Introduction

The increasing rate of change and the complexity of new technologies and markets has motivated organizations to adopt ‘management by projects, as a general approach to development (Turner, 1993). As software-based systems are at the core of most companies’ new products, successful software development has become a critical issue. During the last decade, surveys have shown that software costs are large and rapidly increasing worldwide, with an average 12% annual growth in the US (Boehm & Papaccio, 1988). While studies indicate that the demand for new software systems has been increasing beyond our development abilities, the growth in the software development activity has been marked by major overruns (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991). A recent Massachusetts Institute of Technology-PA Consulting survey shows that more than half of development projects fail to meet their targets (Cooper & Mullen, 1993). This ‘software crisis’ emerged with greater impact in the defence industry, but the problems also affect the private sector of commercial software development.

Despite the major breakthroughs in the technical aspects of software engineering, much less attention has been given during the last decades to improve management methodologies (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991). Frederick Brooks (1987) suggests that “. . . there is no single development, in either technology or in management technique, that by itself promises even one orderof-magnitude improvement in productivity [. . .] we see no silver bullet”. He identifies the most promising strategic ‘attacks’ on the technical difficulties of software development: (1) ‘buy versus build’: use cheaper offthe-shelf reliable packages instead of spending effort in developing costly home-grown software; (2) requirements refinement and rapid prototyping: an effective way of preventing costly changes; (3) incremental development: the conceptual structures underlying the software product are nowadays too complex to be specified accurately in advance. A continuous software ‘growing’ process appears to offer considerable advantages against the classic single-shot building approach; and (4) ‘great designers’: the conceptual design is at the core of a successful software system. Being essentially a creative process, growing great designers is a key priority.

However, adopting the right technical strategies is not sufficient: effective planning and control are essential functions to guide a project towards the objectives. The importance of effective management has been recognised by Boehm (1981): “Poor management can increase software costs more rapidly than any other factor”, and Thomsett (1980): “We ran into problems because we did not know how to manage what we had, not because we lack the techniques themselves”. The general awareness that the major breakthroughs are now to be achieved in the management arena (Abdel Hamid & Madnick, 1991) highlights the need to improve the techniques.

## Traditional software project management

Software management comprises the several functions responsible to keep the project within the targets of cost, quality and duration; project management is therefore the specific activity of achieving project control (Rook & Wingrove, 1990). This comprises the interfacing with the client and subcontractors, and managing the interactions between planning, monitoring, technical development, quality assurance (QA), and configuration management. In this paper we focus on the three main functions of project control: (1) estimating; (2) planning; and (3) monitoring of progress.

The discipline of project management emerged in the early 20th century from the construction industry. Over the years a large collection of procedures has been developed to support the manager with the practical problems of implementation. Most of these techniques have been transferred to other industries, and in particular to the management of large software projects: network models based on the work breakdown structure (WBS) became of widespread usage to support planning and monitoring (Moores & Edwards, 1992), while empirical estimating models are the preferred among software managers (Edwards & Moores, 1994). A major concern has been the rigorous definition of the software development process based on the principles of the classic life cycle model. Rook and Wingrove (1990) proposed a formal method called the process definition diagram (PDD), based on an object-oriented approach, to describe in detail the processes within the life cycle. To face the limitations of the life cycle model within fast moving IT business environments, an alternative paradigm for the software development process, called rapid application development (RAD), has emerged early in this decade, based on incremental development (Martin, 1991).

## Problems and limitations of the traditional approach

Traditional techniques are aimed at providing support to operational issues, where detailed decisions about the implementation process have to be taken. Despite their valuable contribution, however, studies in several industries suggest that the main causes lie in strategic areas which have not been the concern of traditional techniques, such as the political/social environment, legal agreements, and human factors. There has been a relative lack of emphasis on strategy and it is now recognised there is the need for a strategic model (Turner, 1993) based on a more holistic view of the project system, integrating management with technical development. In the software field, the lack of such global understanding has been cited as a major obstacle to improving management procedures (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991). The inadequacy of the current management approach to cope with the softer human aspects of software projects has also been recognised to be a major problem: “Most problems have to do with the way people in the project behave” (Weinberg, 1982).

Two main reasons can be identified for why traditional techniques do not consider explicitly most of these human factors (so called soft factors): (1) their intrinsic subjectivity seriously restrains an appropriate quantification, particularly at the level of detail assumed by the techniques; (2) their local impact on the individual elements of the project system is perceived to be of minor relevance and hence empirical assumptions can easily be made; their long-term impacts result from less visible compounding effects that ripple through the project life cycle. These same arguments support the idea that an appropriate analysis should require a strategic perspective. As an example, the use of schedule pressure to ensure high staff productivity might look a simple issue when the analysis is focused on a single individual activity: while the small team might have been able to meet the tight schedule, the long-term effects of staff exhaustion and low work quality are not yet visible. To cope with these effects the manager needs a more holistic view of the problem and hence a strategic perspective: how should schedule pressure be used throughout the project life cycle to provide a beneficial outcome? Reinforcing this idea, the explicit definition and possible quantification of many human factors such as staff attrition, training and communication overheads, also demands such an aggregated view. While other soft factors might take place at lower levels of detail, the assessment of their impacts on project performance still demands a strategic perspective to which traditional techniques are not aimed.

The failure of the traditional network-based models to cope with the higher-level but complex dynamics that result from these softer strategic issues stems from four key limitations: (1) while the concern is to capture the project in great detail, the resulting complexity does not enable quick and reliable strategic analysis; (2) as mentioned before, they do not incorporate explicitly the influence of human factors; (3) they do not consider explicitly the rework phenomenon; and (4) they fail to capture the dynamic interactions within and between technical development and management policies. The specific characteristics of software development exacerbate these problems: (i) the main resource is people; (ii) the software product remains intangible throughout most of the development life cycle; and (iii) a rigorous definition of the product requirements is usually very difficult until the later stages. Staff productivity and work quality are continuously affected by several factors such as learning, schedule pressure, training and communications overheads. Variations in work quality among programmers can reach a magnitude of 10:1 difference in the error rates (Boehm & Papaccio, 1988), while the difference in designers’ productivity can reach a 2:1 order of magnitude (Brooks, 1987). On the other hand, management performance in undertaking decisions can be badly damaged by work pressure: “an overloaded manager is usually a bad manager” (Weinberg, 1982). The intangible nature of the software product, in particular its quality, encourages poor QA performance. High error detection rates are often seen as poor technical development, and because software quality is not tangible in the early stages staff can easily skip QA efforts. On the other hand, such a large amount of defects would probably require schedule extensions, which are also perceived as poor management (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991). This poor QA poses a serious obstacle for organizations to improve the quality of the designs, a key requisite for productivity improvement (Brooks, 1987). Finally, unstable system requirements exacerbate the introduction of changes. The side effects of these changes, in particular work being done out of its natural sequence, often create dramatic problems of rework in the later stages (Cooper, 1994).

## The need for a new approach

Considerable efforts have been directed towards the improvement of the existing traditional models. Inadequacies of network-based tools have been recognised and Petri net based extensions have been proposed for software projects (Liu & Horowitz, 1989; Lee & Murata, 1994; Lee et al, 1994). Improvement of estimating models, like the COCOMO, has also been the focus of attention (Gulezian, 1991). However, such developments have been directed towards an increasing complexity. A strategic view has not been achieved while the influence of human factors remains ignored or is handled by simplistic assumptions. Furthermore, the models often demand detailed information to which managers cannot relate practical meaning. Finally, while rapid application development based methods have been emerging with success in certain domains of software applications (for a real case see Kerr & Hunter, 1994), the approach is not suitable to other areas, in particular to real-time large-scale systems (DSDM Consortium, 1995), so commonly developed within the military industry.

As managers find little help in the traditional techniques to cope with the higher-level complex problems, they apply crude rules of thumb and intuitive judgement based on past experience (i.e. their mental models). Under constant pressure, they search for the usual problems and revert to problem-solving strategies that they believe have worked in the past (Weinberg, 1982). Management faults remain uncovered and organizations fail to learn effectively from one project to the other. This problem requires a more formal systemic analysis at which traditional tools and techniques are not aimed.

## Application of system dynamics

System dynamics (SD) was first introduced by Forrester (1961) and emerged as a new methodology for modelling the behaviour of complex socio-economic systems, based on the principles of feedback control theory (Roberts, 1964). The approach is based on a holistic perspective of managerial problems and focuses on the human aspects of a system’s behaviour. There has been a number of applications to project management, particularly in the field of software development (a review can be found in Rodrigues & Bowers, 1996). For the purposes of this paper, three of these studies deserve particular attention.

The first application of SD to software project management was proposed by Abdel-Hamid and Madnick (1982) leading to the development of a ‘generic’ model for the software development process (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991). The testing and practical application of this model was based on the post mortem analysis of a real software project at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Centre.

At NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory similar work was developed (Tausworthe et al, 1983) and an SD model for the software development process was later proposed (McKenzie et al, 1984). Further improvements led to a generic simulation model of the software life cycle embedded within an expert system (SLICS/HESS) (Lin & Levary, 1989). The testing and validation of this model was based on a real project (a NASA space shuttle software development project). Further research has been reported by the authors on other models (SEPS) (Lin, 1993).

Cooper and Mullen (1993) report major practical applications of SD models to software development projects. Coopers’ work focuses on assessing the impacts of the factors ‘work quality’ and ‘time to discover rework’, based on the generic concept of the rework cycle (a generic structure which considers explicitly that rework is generated, remains undiscovered for some time, and is then discovered re-joining the initial pool of ‘work to do’ (see Cooper & Mullen, 1993; Cooper, 1994 for details)). The models were set to recreate the past behaviour of completed projects and quantitative measures for these two factors were extracted. His findings suggest that gains in project performance can only be achieved by directing efforts to increase work quality and to detect errors earlier. Although the major practical applications reported in the literature refer to cases of post mortem analysis, the author claims that the models (for example, the PMMS – see Pugh Roberts Associates, 1993) have been used with success to support the management of large ongoing programmes.

## Current models and the need for improvement

The work of Abdel-Hamid and Madnick (1991) forms an excellent survey on the human interactions taking place within a software project, in particular the factors affecting staff productivity (e.g. learning, communications overheads, and use of ‘slack-time’). The authors provide reasonable evidence of empirical validation for the quantification of the relationships within the model. Another important issue is that the model considers explicitly a continuous flow of errors escaping throughout the life cycle, until the later testing stages. This is an important concept since in software development errors are reworked in the phase where they are detected. The model avoids the ‘iteration of phases’ and is therefore consistent with the basic principles of the classic life cycle model: (1) precise phases-ends; and (2) continuing activities (Rook, 1990). However, the model does not consider any breakdown of the project work, assuming the highest possible level of aggregation. This way, it cannot provide a detailed analysis of the intermediate schedule milestones. This consideration also prevents the model to consider a planned staff profile for the project and, hence, in replicating the ‘Raleigh curve’ (Boehm, 1981; DeMarco, 1982), there is no explicit consideration for the ‘natural’ changes in the work intensity. Finally the model also considers stable requirements for the project, something extremely unlikely in most medium– large size software projects.

The models proposed by Lin (1993) and Lin and Levary (1989) consider an explicit breakdown of the project work into the classic life cycle stages, providing a more detailed analysis for the schedules, budgets and staff allocation to the project. The SLICS model focuses on the problem of requirements changes being introduced throughout the life cycle. An interesting feature of this work is the use of an expert system to support model application. The SEPS model provides an important perspective of a software project described as a dual life cycle process of engineering (i.e. product development) and management (i.e. decision-making). Also important is the proposed procedure to support empirical validation of the model based on several tests. However, the breakdown of the project work is still restricted to a single sequence of the classical life cycle phases, whereas in large projects several software components with different characteristics are developed by different teams, entering the integration phase at different times. Although the authors suggest the use of SLICS to support continuing projects, evidence of major practical applications would be desired.

The work developed by Cooper (1980) (see also Cooper & Mullen, 1993), in particular the PMMS, introduced the important concepts of the rework cycle and monitoring ramps (an XY plot of the ‘real progress’ against the ‘perceived progress’). This considers explicitly that rework is generated in the project and remains undiscovered until the later stages. The consequent gap between the perceived and the real progress explains the occurrence of the ‘90% syndrome’. The PMMS system provides a more flexible way of capturing the project work structure. A model is developed based on generic ‘building blocks’ to capture the major project activities. This includes specialised sub-models for design, construction, procurement, testing, staffing categories, and programme management. The procedures to apply the model in practice are based on the calibration for a ‘problem-free’ scenario, followed by a ‘what-if analysis where disturbances are introduced. However, the models still assume a high-level view aiming to support the strategic management of large design and construction programmes, which often include several projects being implemented in parallel. The models are not specialised to capture the many specific and unique characteristics of software development. It is, therefore, unlikely that they are suitable to provide support at the lower tactical level.

In summary, the above developments represent important contributions for the application of SD to software project management. They introduce valuable concepts and ideas that should be considered in the future. In particular, the work developed by Cooper, at Pugh-Roberts Associates, provides evidence about the practical credibility of the approach. However, most of the reported cases refer to post mortem analysis. Here, the model is used to reproduce the behaviour of completed projects and helps to investigate the causes for deviations. In other cases the models are used within fictitious scenarios as training tools to support policy analysis. A major step forward would be to apply SD models to support the management of continuing software projects at both strategic and tactical levels (Cooper claims the application of PMMS to major programmes at the strategic level). However, such an application requires the approach to be embedded within the traditional project management framework. Despite the limitations, project managers still need operational tools to support planning and monitoring at the tactical level. Integrating system dynamics models with the traditional tools also raises the question of whether quantitative links can be established between both types of models. In the next section we propose a conceptual model to integrate system dynamics with the traditional approach.

## A conceptual integrated framework

The need to integrate SD within the traditional project management approach has been discussed elsewhere (Rodrigues & Bowers, 1996). The conclusions drawn from this study suggest that SD have potential to provide a distinctive and complementary contribution. Both approaches address common managerial needs (e.g. estimating overall project duration, cost, and staff profile), but their perspective of a project is different. Networkbased models are based on a top–down process of decomposing the project into its constituent elements in a structured way. They consider the management problems at the operational level and focus on the detailed logic of the project work structure and resource requirements. On the other hand, SD models are based on a bottom–up approach to aggregate the many details of the project into a whole system dominated by internal interactions and resulting feedback loops. They consider the managerial problems at the strategic level and their main priority is to capture the more general aspects of the project behaviour that result from its internal feedback structure. Ideally, an integrated framework should consider the benefits of both approaches and establish information links between the two levels of analysis.

## A conceptual integrated model (SYDPIM)

Preliminary work on the development of a system dynamics project-management integrated model (SYDPIM) has been reported by the authors (Rodrigues & Williams, 1995). As a practical framework such a model should specify in some detail: (1) the specific roles of SD models; (2) how the SD models are used within the management process, in particular how they exchange qualitative and quantitative information with the traditional models; and (3) a general description of the required characteristics of the SD models, in particular their structure, and procedures for validation and calibration. Our current research focus on the improvement and testing of the SYDPIM (Figure 1), within a large software project, at BAeSEMA Ltd.

The SYDPIM considers the use of SD models at both strategic and operational management levels providing support to the planning and monitoring functions of the project control cycle. In planning, the role of the models focuses on estimating future results and performing risk analysis, while in monitoring they are aimed at diagnosing the past behaviour of the project. At the strategic level we consider the use of a high-level SD strategic model (SDSM), which covers the whole project life cycle and captures the major software development milestones. At the operational level, a more complex model (SDOM) captures in more detail the individual life cycle phases. The structure of both models is based on an appropriate breakdown of the project into major subtasks, consistent with the traditional WBS. The main aim of the SDSM is to provide quick and preliminary assessment of major strategic decisions and risks, before a detailed plan is produced. This is particularly important at the early stages when a detailed plan for the whole life cycle is not available. The SDOM focuses on the project sub-tasks in more detail providing quantitative data to support work scheduling and resource allocation. As this model requires the availability of detailed information, in practice, it might not cover the full project life cycle until the middle development stages have been reached.

Underlying principles and concepts of SYDPIM The integrated use of SD models within SYDPIM is grounded upon some basic principles and concepts:

(1) Projects are complex systems incorporating many physical and human elements. A project being implemented changes its status continuously over time. This change of status can be represented by the behaviour patterns exhibited by some set of its characteristics (e.g. staff level, current budget, cumulative defects found, estimated completion date). This selected set of behaviour patterns over time forms the project behaviour.

(2) A network plan explicitly assumes changes in the project status over time and hence it portrays, implicitly, the image of a project evolving successfully towards constant targets of cost and schedule. Because the targets are kept constasnt, the planned behaviour portrayed by a network is called ‘steady’.

(3) As the project evolves, progress data is collected in the monitoring system, some of which is recorded in the network. This progress data captures changes in the project status and hence it also portrays, more or less explicitly, the project past behaviour. When changes in the targets occur (e.g. delays, overexpenditure, use of extra staff), the project behaviour is called ‘unsteady’.

(4) An SD project model represents and simulates a project being implemented, and hence produces quantitative behaviour patterns (i.e. the values of the model’s variables over time), which form the simulated behaviour. Within SYDPIM the model’s parameters and equations are continuously readjusted to reproduce two reference modes of behaviour: past behaviour, and planned future behaviour. The process of identifying and implementing the changes required in the model’s parameters and equations to reproduce one of these reference modes, is called ‘calibration’.

Some of these concepts are illustrated in Figure 2. The graphic representing the project behaviour plots the pattern over time of four cost control characteristics of the management process. It is also assumed that the project has evolved so far in a steady manner in terms of cost, as represented by the constant value of the ‘target’ variable ‘cost at completion’ (CAC). The planned behaviour (shaded region), is of course also steady. Finally, it is important to note that in many control systems the progress data collected in the monitoring sub-system is updated in the network, and hence the network itself also captures some characteristics of the past behaviour.

In order to apply these principles within SYDPIM, some key requirements must be met within the project management process. First, there is the need to define clearly what are the relevant characteristics of the project which will be used to represent the project behaviour. This must consider practical restrictions in terms of quantification and measurement: when the characteristic of concern is intangible, or in practice its measurement is too expensive or inaccurate, measurable alternatives must be searched in order to achieve an acceptable representation of the original cause of concern. The characteristics of a project fall in two main categories: management and technical development. Management characteristics include human perceptions of progress (e.g. estimated effort to complete), and changes implemented in the plans (e.g. schedule adjustments, reallocation of resources). The characteristics of the technical development process focus in the physical work accomplished: size of the code developed, defects detected and reworked, test cases passed, cumulative code integrated, and others.

Another key requirement is the availability of costeffective procedures in order to extract the planned behaviour from a network plan. Since network plans can be extremely complex, incorporating thousands of nodes, computer-based automated procedures should preferably be used. Equally important is that the required metrics are collected from the project in order to derive the past behaviour. An effective metrics programme must be in place, and automated tools should also be used whenever possible. This is not to say that these two procedures are necessarily expensive, as this will depend largely on the appropriate definition of the project characteristics to be monitored (as an example, the metrics plan developed by the first author for the case study, at BAeSEMA, included 15 main metrics, some of which were already part of the company’s monitoring procedures and their collection was already semi-automated).

## Use of the models within the SYDPIM

The use of the SD models within the SYDPIM is based on a process of continuous recalibration to reproduce the two reference modes of behaviour, as the software project moves through the development life cycle. Figure 3 provides an overview of how the SDOM model is used to support planning and monitoring (a similar process applies to the SDSM).

In planning, the model is used as a ‘test laboratory’ to assess the performance of the current plan and to identify risks. Once a detailed plan has been produced into a logical network the next step is to extract the dynamic characteristics of the planned behaviour. Calibrating the SD model to reproduce this steady behaviour requires the explicit definition of several metrics, which otherwise would remain ‘hidden’ in the plans. In fact, we do not impose the planned results directly in the model; instead, the quantitative parameters and relationships (i.e. equations) within the model’s feedback structure must be able to produce such behaviour. This uncovering of metrics is an important exercise as it helps to identify unrealistic assumptions and thereby suggest readjustments in the plan. As an example, if the error generation required for the model to reproduce the desired project objectives is too low, then perhaps extra rework activities should be considered in the network plan. After this stage, the model is used to assess the plan’s sensitivity to risks through the analysis of ‘what-if’ scenarios. Disturbances are introduced in the model and this provides explicit description of possible unsteady behaviours (e.g. schedule slippage, cost over-runs). Planning alternatives can be tested, and further readjustments are carried out to reduce the plan’s sensitivity. As major planning changes suggested by the SD analysis are accepted by managers, they can be translated into the network plan. When the plan is perceived to be realistic and robust, implementation may take place.

In monitoring the model is used as a ‘test laboratory’ to diagnose the past behaviour helping to identify the causes for possible deviations. Once progress monitoring data has been collected using traditional procedures, the next step is to derive from these data the dynamic characteristics of the project past behaviour. As in planning, calibrating the model to reproduce the past behaviour provides the uncovering of result metrics. As an example, a low error discovery rate might suggest either an exceptionally good development quality or a poor performance of QA activities. Where the numbers are not realistic, the conclusion might be that progress data is not reliable and hence needs to be reviewed. Another important output from this calibration is the estimation of the number of undiscovered defects that escaped to the future life cycle stages. This provides awareness about the amount of rework required in the later stages avoiding ‘over-optimism’ in terms of progress. After the progress data has been eventually revised, the model can be used to investigate the causes for eventual deviations and to test whether alternative planning and control policies could have provided better results.

Each time the model is recalibrated to reproduce and diagnose new ‘segments’ of past behaviour, it also provides new estimates for the future behaviour regarding the current plan. As an example, the model might suggest that, due to a high number of errors escaping, the schedule of the next stage is likely to over-run. This is the starting point of a new control cycle in the planning function. Again, the SD model is re-calibrated, the network plan is readjusted, and the whole process is repeated for each control cycle.

In summary, within the planning function the models are used to estimate the project outcome and help to identify better planning alternatives, ensuring that the plan is robust and based on realistic assumptions. In monitoring, the models are used to uncover several metrics about the project status, and as a diagnosis tool they help identify possible causes for observed deviations, and support policy improvement. The SYDPIM considers that both models (SDOM and SDSM) are used in this way, but at different levels of detail. A crucial issue within this process is the potential ‘loss of data’, which may threaten the validity of the models’ calibration. Furthermore, effective procedures must also be in place so that both the planned behaviour can be extracted from a network plan, and the past behaviour can be derived from progress data within an acceptable time-scale. In order to overcome these difficulties it is essential that: (1) a software process definition and maintenance, and (2) a metrics programme, are explicitly considered as key activities within project control, and that these are ‘culturally’ accepted and well established within the project organization. This is in itself a complex issue which goes beyond the scope of SYDPIM, but we shall discuss it further in this paper, in more detail.

## Definition of the model structure

The structure of an SD project model should capture the basic characteristics of the software development process. Several techniques can be used to provide a rigorous definition of this process (e.g. PDD, Rook & Wingrove, 1990), which is also the basis for the development of traditional models, like the WBS and logical networks. The framework here proposed to define the structure of the SD models is based on four basic principles: (1) dual life cycle view of management and engineering; (2) breakdown of the project into major sub-tasks; (3) dual life cycle of work and defects within the engineering process (and hence associated rework); and (4) single high-level project management and human resource management function. This framework is intended to support the development of new SD models, specialised in a specific project (or category of similar projects), and within a specific organization.

The principle of the dual life cycle emphasis on the interaction between engineering and management implies that the SD model should capture both characteristics of technical development (e.g. life cycle phases, product components, rework, work dependencies, reviewing techniques), and the characteristics of the management process: this includes the procedures used to monitor progress and the managerial policies employed in replanning the work schedules and in reallocating of resources.

The principle of breaking down the project work structure into a set of sub-tasks assumes that each of these tasks holds enough complexity so that planning and control might be problematic. Each task will be simulated by an individual SD sub-model with a structure specialised on the type of work being performed. For the engineering process of a software development project we propose three main categories of generic tasks to which correspond three types of sub-models: (1) development; (2) testing; and (3) integration. A development task refers to the process of developing a particular sub-product based on pre-defined requirements and eventually using some sort of input resources. This includes the activities of developing, reviewing and reworking (e.g. design and coding fall in this category). A testing task refers to the process of checking the functionality of a sub-product and, if necessary, changing it. In practice this usually consists of running a set of predefined tests, identify and diagnose failures, and rework the defects found (unit testing, group testing and system testing fall into this category). Finally, integration is essentially a transformation process which uses two or more sub-products as an input and delivers an integrated product as the output. This usually includes developing or changing existing interfaces (i.e. connectivities between software sub-components), testing these connectivities, and making the necessary changes in the existing sub-products so that the final integrated product delivers the basic required functionality. While this classification is intended to be generic for the software development process, other more specific approaches may be adopted, depending on the purpose of the model.

The breakdown of the engineering process is based on three main sources of information: the life cycle definition, the product structure, and the structure of the technical development team. The lowest level of decomposition would consider a single task for each life cycle phase of each software sub-component, being implemented by an individual team. For most software projects such level of detail, very close to the network plan, would not be appropriate for an SD model. However, using this decomposition as a starting point we now need to aggregate these elementary sub-tasks into more complex tasks. We define two types of aggregation:

(1) horizontal aggregation: i.e. ignoring the intermediate schedules of a set of sequential tasks, and consider them as a single task with a single schedule which delivers a single cumulative output product;

(2) vertical aggregation: i.e. ignoring the individual schedules of a set of parallel but inter-related tasks, and consider them as a single task with a single schedule which delivers a single cumulative output product.

The definition of the appropriate level of decomposition/aggregation of the engineering process into tasks is based on four main criteria: (1) type of work being performed; (2) major schedule milestones; (3) level of detail required for management purposes; and (4) minimum level of complexity required. The type of work being developed within a task should be of a similar nature and strongly inter-related. As an example, the design and testing of a software component should not be captured by the same task, while the design of two tightly coupled software components may be aggregated into a single design task. The major schedule milestones of the development process should impose limits on the level of horizontal aggregation so that they are not overlooked in the model. In general, most managers would like a decision support model to consider explicitly the individual schedules of the major life cycle phases. Finally, each task must comprise a minimum level of complexity so that the use of an SD model is appropriate. SD models focus on the interactions among a system’s components. Decomposing the software development process into highly detailed elements tends to eliminate the effects of these interactions: the individual behaviour of such elements becomes characterised by discrete uncertain events dominated by exogenous forces, to which the higher-level, continuous and endogenous perspective of a model is not appropriate.

Having decomposed the engineering process into a set of individual tasks these can be linked according to precedence relationships. This defines a network of tasks to which a network of SD models will correspond (the SD-TNet). Here, the implementation of sequential tasks can overlap depending on the specific characteristics of the software development process, and on management decisions. As an example, it might be decided that the coding phase can start after 50% of the design phase has been completed. Each SD sub-model comprises an internal management process responsible for monitoring progress within the task and taking corrective actions when necessary. This includes scheduling the manpower available among the activities within the task (e.g. development, review and rework), and adjusting the schedule within a contingency range.

The third principle for the model structure is to consider explicitly that the engineering process comprises the continuous flow of two inter-related entities: work and defects. As the work is developed, reviewed/tested and reworked throughout the life cycle, defects are also generated, detected, and reworked. However, some are not detected by the review and testing activities and hence escape, incorporating the sub-product passed onto the next task, as shown in Figure 4 (the broader arrows indicate the life cycle phases where error introduction and removal usually occur with more intensity).

Finally, each of the models in the SD-TNet continuously reports progress to a high-level management subcomponent (SD-GMan), which overviews the whole project. This information includes work progress, estimated completion date, estimated cost at completion, and manpower needed to meet the schedule. Based on this information, reported from the currently on-going tasks, the SD-GMan mimics the high-level decision-making in the project. This includes: (1) adjusting the completion schedules of the individual tasks; (2) reschedule staff among the tasks; (3) increase the degree of overlapping between sequential tasks; and (4) hire more staff into the project. The process of hiring/firing staff is modelled by an additional human resource management sub-component, which considers recruitment and training issues and staff turnover. The complete global model for a software project (SD-GModel) is now defined by linking all submodels. The conceptual structure for the global model developed for our current case-study is shown in Figure 5 (the ‘KDCOM’ is an intensive software project, being carried out at BAeSEMA Ltd, which aims to develop a Command and Fire Control System which is part of a combat system to be installed and integrated into a destroyer of the Republic of Korea Navy). In terms of vertical aggregation, the development of the nine software sub-components was aggregated into three major functional areas (Radar, SSGT, and Core). In terms of horizontal aggregation, the nine different stages of the specified life cycle were aggregated into five main development phases (design, coding, group testing, integration, and system testing). A practical application of this model is discussed in Rodrigues and Williams (1996), where an SD model is used to assess the impacts of introducing changes in the later stages of the design phase, and to select appropriate rescheduling policies. In this experiment, several scheduling policies were tested against the final schedule, cost, and defects escaped from the design phase: whilst schedule compression would prove effective for the first two, the negative impacts in the quality of the designs (i.e. escaped defects), were unacceptable. A final policy of extending the original schedule in 60% of the proportion of the changes (also defined in percentage), appeared to offer the best timequality-cost compromise.

## Information links between SD models and traditional models

Both SD and traditional network-based models use quantitative data as input (e.g. staff profile, budget) and produce quantitative data as output (e.g. final schedules). Furthermore, both models also consider common structural aspects of the project, although sometimes at a different level of detail (e.g. work dependencies, resource requirements). The use of SD models within the SYD-PIM assumes a close relation with the traditional models in two ways: (1) structure, and (2) data: input estimates and results produced. The structure of the SD models should be according to the project characteristics, and their application in supporting project control is based on the exchange of information with the traditional tools. In terms of data, the important relationship is between the modes of project behaviour (i.e. past and planned future), produced by the SD model and portrayed by the network model.

As described in the previous sub-section the structure of the SD model is based on the breakdown/aggregation of the project work. Similar decomposition of the project work also applies to traditional models, and in particular the WBS, which captures the whole project. Therefore, a formal link can be established: to each task of the SD-TNet (i.e. an SD-Task), should correspond a specific set of work packages in the WBS. Of course, this relationship becomes stronger if the majority of these packages are unique to a certain SD task. In such case, the translation of data between the SD model and the network model is easier to implement, and eventually more accurate. The relationship between the WBS and the SD-TNet should be defined by a matrix identifying which WBS packages correspond to each SD-Task, and in case a package is not unique, the proportion of effort should also be identified. As an example, a certain WBS package relating to general design support to the coding phase of several software components may specify that say 20% of the effort booked to the package refers to a certain component.

The technical development is implemented and managed by project sub-teams as specified in the organization breakdown structure (OBS). These teams are usually specialised in a certain software development activity (e.g. designing, coding, testing), which are performed continuously throughout the life cycle. Therefore, each team usually works on several life cycle phases. Although the work performed within each task of the SD-TNet might be dominated by a certain team, it will inevitably incorporate the contribution of other teams. The relationship between the OBS and the SD-TNet should also be specified in a matrix identifying which teams work in each SD-Task. This is important to support the translation of staff levels between the SD model and the logical network.

The software development process adopted in the project is usually specified in terms of a life cycle document, often using formal techniques (e.g. PDD). This model specifies in detail the life cycle phases/stages and the processes to be followed within each phase. The structure of the SD model should relate to this information by a clear identification of the life cycle phase to which the SD-Tasks belong. This should also identify the processes within the phases being incorporated in each SD-Task.

The use of SD models within the SYDPIM assumes the exchange of qualitative and quantitative information with the traditional models. These models include estimating models (e.g. COCOMO), and network-based planning and monitoring models (e.g. PERT, PERT/Cost and Earned Value, see Nicholas, 1990).

As the project progresses, the network plan is continuously updated to incorporate both the past results and the plans for the future. The past behaviour of a project includes: (1) information about the technical development process, specifying the actual effort expenditures, schedules, staff levels, and possible structural changes in the development process (e.g. eliminating or splitting testing stages); and (2) information about the management process specifying replanning decisions of schedule adjustments and hiring more staff, and progress estimations produced by managers. The past behaviour recorded in a network does not capture the second component, because of the static perspective of the model. When the SD model is calibrated to reproduce the past behaviour it must reproduce with acceptable accuracy the final results of the engineering process, as recorded in the network, but it must also reproduce the decision-making pattern occurred within the management process (i.e. perceptions of progress and replanning actions). The calibration for this second component of the project behaviour is more difficult since replanning decisions are often not recorded in the project information system. Replanning often implies changing the structure of the network, typically by increasing concurrency (as reported by Williams et al, 1995). Therefore, the unsteady past behaviour of a project may well be characterised by a continuous evolution of the network structure, reflecting those decisions. Where accurate data about the management component of the project behaviour is not available, managers’ judgement is essential to validate the calibration. Equally critical are the possible structural changes in the software development process, usually not recorded in the information system, but which usually imply significant changes in the network structure. Whenever structural changes occur in the development process, the appropriateness of the high-level structure of the SD models (i.e. the SD-TNet) should be revised, adjusted if necessary, and validated.

The future behaviour of the project portrayed by a network assumes a ‘planned success’, in which either deviations do not occur or management corrective actions are sufficiently effective to avoid over-runs. When the SD model is calibrated to reproduce this steady behaviour it must reproduce the expected results from the engineering process (i.e. schedules, effort expenditure, staff levels). Again, there is usually no information available about the likely decision-making pattern within the management process: as a project is a perceived complex, the tendency is to adopt a reactive attitude where deviations and management reactions are not anticipated. Once the future behaviour is assumed steady, the SD model must reproduce a behaviour with little deviation and management actions quickly solving the problems. As an example, even within a ‘successful scenario’, low productivity levels may occur when the nature of the work is more complex (e.g. formal detailed design), or when learning is slow. Here, schedule pressure, use of overtime, or even reduction of QA intensity, all may occur with the perceived schedule delay eventually being eliminated.

In summary, the global structure of the SD project model, represented by the SD-TNet, should be integrated with the WBS, OBS, and with the specification of the life cycle development process, through the use of appropriate matrixes. An SD-Task should: (1) incorporate a specific set of work packages in the WBS; (2) capture the work performed by several development teams identified by the OBS; and (3) relate to a specific life cycle phase incorporating some of its internal processes. The relationship between the SD project model and the network model is characterised by both models producing or portraying the same quantitative behaviour patterns and final results for both observed project past and planned future. This quantitative correspondence is central to the SYDPIM application and hence it must be considered within a well defined criterion of acceptable accuracy. Figure 6 provides an overview of these relationships. Preliminary estimates produced by traditional models, like the COCOMO, may also be tested in the SD model and if successful are translated into the network plan. Monitoring tools provide the necessary information to derive the project past behaviour. The quantitative links here identified are at the core of a rigorous integration of SD models within the traditional approach, as described by the SYDPIM.

Finally, and since both types of models produce or portray common behaviour patterns for the project, it is important to stress the key differences: SD models assume a higher level view of the whole project focusing on human factors and managerial policies. They have an inherent flexibility which enables them to incorporate a wide range of influences specific to particular scenarios. The network based models used in the traditional approach focus on the project work structure and are more specialised, assuming a detailed view of the individual parts of the project. Consequently, they are more rigid, enforcing a particular view of the project; this can ease their implementation but at the expense of some reality: while ensuring rigorous monitoring of the project past, their view of the future is focused on a ‘planned success’. In contrast, SD simulation models provide a laboratory to test several different scenarios for the project, delivering a clearer and perhaps more realistic view of the possible futures.

## Critical issues for the application of SYDPIM

The successful implementation of SYDPIM within a real project environment depends upon some critical factors. The framework, as proposed in the previous sections, assumes that SD is applied within a well structured project management environment: traditional operational techniques and tools are in use, there is a well defined WBS specifying the work scope, an OBS identifying the organizational elements, and a formal definition and maintenance of the life cycle development process. It can be argued that, more often than not, these ‘pre-conditions’ are not met within many project organizations. However, SYDPIM is a high-level framework and hence inherently holds enough flexibility to be implemented at different levels of formality. Nevertheless, there are some main critical factors that deserve special discussion.

Figure 6 Information links: relationship between a SD project model and the traditional models.

A key requirement in SYDPIM is the availability of accurate data, which is required to: (1) define the two project models of behaviour; and (2) calibrate the SD model. The availability of data in the project depends upon the existence of a well structured metrics collection programme. Metrics collection is in itself a complex issue, which goes beyond the particular support to SYD-PIM. It is known that the benefits of metrics programmes are not widely accepted within the software industry, because of the often high costs involved and the difficulty in obtaining accurate results (Kitchenham, 1990). On the other hand, while it has been argued for some time that measurement is essential for effective control (DeMarco, 1982; Gilb, 1982), practical experiences have shown that, if properly applied and maintained, real benefits can be achieved from metrics programmes (Grady & Caswell, 1987; Stark et al, 1994), while structured approaches have been proposed (Fenton, 1991; Grady, 1992). Within SYDPIM, regarding (1) above, while extracting the planned behaviour from a network plan is unlikely to pose major difficulties, deriving the past behaviour may be problematic when the structure and contents of the network plan itself are changed. This is usually due to management replanning decisions as a reaction to perceived delays. These decisions may include the increase of parallelism, creation of extra activities, or removal of activities. The problem becomes more complex if structural changes in the life cycle development process occur. In these situations, the current version of the network plan does not hold information about how the network evolved over time, what were exactly the management decisions, the underlying problems, and the structural changes in the development process. A first approach to overcome this difficulty is to keep a historical record of all versions of the network plans, a procedure commonly used (e.g. at BAeSEMA). Adding to this, progress reports and other documentation from the project management information system, like life cycle maintenance reports, provide extra information. Despite all the political ‘biases’, management and staff judgement should also be considered, possibly through the use of structured techniques (Wright, 1985). A company committed to use and implement SYDPIM at a certain level of formality, must ensure that the necessary data will be made available within a level of accuracy, and this must be agreed and accepted a priori. SD simulation modelling is a structured technique. Its application in SYDPIM is only suitable within project environments able to comply with some level of structured management.

Regarding point (2), calibration of SD models is also a complex issue which again goes beyond the particular application of SD within SYDPIM. The problem of calibration relates to model validation, which in SD is based on the principle of confidence. Whilst there is not a single test or set of tests that, per se, can ensure model validity, a set of confidence tests has been proposed (Forrester & Senge, 1980), while further extensions have been made towards the establishment of a more formal validation approach (e.g. Homer, 1983; Barlas, 1996). The calibration process consists of adjusting the model’s parameters and equations so that the model is able to reproduce the behavioural characteristics of a specific real world scenario (occurred or estimated). Whilst many parameters and equations in an SD project model can be directly extracted from reality (e.g. planned schedule, staff profile), others are intangibles or may require unmeasured data (e.g. effect of pressure on error generation). In these cases, confidence tests can be used to ensure an appropriate calibration (for more details on the KDCOM case study see Rodrigues & Williams, 1996). Furthermore, the ability of a specific calibration to reproduce the expected behaviour patterns can also be assessed employing formal measures of ‘goodness-of-fit through the use of an appropriate set of statistic (see Sterman, 1984).

Another critical issue is the range of applications for SYDPIM. In the first place, and as already mentioned, the framework assumes a certain level of structured management within the project. The availability of such an environment depends, mainly, upon the complexity and size of the project. Small-size projects are less likely to use highly structured management techniques, but for this type of project it is also unlikely that long term interactions and feedback effects are relevant and hence problematic under a managerial perspective. On the other hand, these effects can be overwhelming in complex projects, and it has recently been recognised that highly structured management approaches must be in place (NATO Advanced Research Workshop, 1996; Cooper, 1996; Conrad, 1996; Williams, 1996).

A second possible restriction in the application of SYDPIM is the type of software development process being adopted in the project. In this paper we have assumed the use of the traditional life cycle model. Despite its usefulness, this approach is now being challenged by rapid application development based methods, a particular example being the dynamic systems development method (DSDM), which emerged as an attempt to provide a rapid application development standard for the software industry (DSDM Consortium, 1995). The rapid application development approach is fundamentally an attempt to move away from the static sequence of phases of the life cycle model, to provide a more flexible approach based on rapid iterative delivery, and where it is explicitly assumed that it is inappropriate to attempt a full definition of the system’s requirements before some core functionality has been delivered to and tested by the user. The DSDM method is based on the use of incremental prototyping and close user involvement, where the requirements and the system itself evolve throughout several deliveries of increased functionality. However, whilst the new principles offered by this method look attractive within fast moving business environments, it is clearly stated by its developers that its sustainability is restricted to some types of software applications. The system to be developed should be: (i) highly interactive, where the functionality is fully visible at the user interface; (ii) have a clearly defined user group; and (iii) not computationally complex. In particular, it is cited that “DSDM is unsuitable for real time applications” (DSDM Consortium, 1995), so unsuitable for many types of military system (e.g. combat systems).

But even for those projects where a rapid application development approach is suitable, there is still potential to apply some principles of the SYDPIM framework. A major problem with incremental development is the identification and planning of the increments, and the further definition of the development strategies to be followed within each of the increments (e.g. some increments may focus on the refinements of the designs, while others may focus on some particular aspects of the code functionality and performance). One could envisage the use of SD models to assess the robustness of the incremental plan, and to support monitoring of the whole project. Alternatively, if an increment is complex enough, the SYDPIM framework could be applied within the increment as described in this paper. These are, however, hypothetical routes since no theoretical or practical attempt has been made by the authors to test such possibilities.

Finally, it is important to note that SYDPIM is intended neither to exhaust the full range of possible applications of SD in Project Management, nor to replace the use of any other different tool, technique or procedure, that can provide a useful contribution to the project management process. Other possible applications of SD, like interactive gaming for training managers, or developing simple models to create learning environments (Morecroft & Sterman, 1994), may also provide a complementary and distinctive contribution, outside the scope of SYDPIM. Within a specific organization, this framework can be implemented more or less formally, depending on how structured the management process is, on the information made available by this process, and on the experience gained and benefits perceived by the organization while using SYDPIM.

## Conclusions

Traditional software project management and analysis tools have proven inadequate, since they do not provide a strategic overview, and because they fail to capture both the influence of human factors and also many important project interactions and feedback effects. SD has been found to offer important benefits to the analysis of software project management. However, much of this work is post mortem; to provide support to ongoing projects, SD models need to be embedded within the framework of traditional project management.

This paper has described a conceptual SYDPIM, which has been improved and tested over a long period of intensive work within a large software project at BAe-SEMA Ltd. The SYDPIM uses SD models at both the strategic and operational management levels. In order to aid planning, the models are used to assess the current plan and identify risks. In monitoring, the models are used to diagnose the past behaviour, help identify causes for deviations, and support policy and process improvement.

Specific generic model structures for the software development process have been described; these are based on the dual life cycle view of management and engineering, breaking the project into major sub-tasks, considering the engineering process as a flow of work and defects, and a single high-level management function. The structure of the SD model, based on a breakdown of the project work, allows a formal matrix relationship to be established between the WBS and the SD model; a similar relationship can be defined between the OBS and the SD model; information is also exchanged between the SD model and traditional estimating and network-based models. Each time the model is recalibrated to reproduce and diagnose segments of the past behaviour it provides new estimates for future behaviour. This calibration reproduces not only the final engineering result, but also the management decisionmaking pattern (i.e. perceptions of progress and subsequent replanning).

The authors consider that this integrated tool provides a powerful support to the management of major software engineering projects as they are ongoing. This has been proven by the improvement and use of the tool during an actual project.
