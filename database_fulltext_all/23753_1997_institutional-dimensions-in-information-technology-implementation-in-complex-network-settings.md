# Institutional dimensions in information technology implementation in complex network settings

E Christiaanse<sup>1</sup> and J Huigen<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Nijenrode University, Breukelen; and <sup>2</sup>Tilburg University, Tilburg, The Netherlands

It is increasingly recognised that an institutional perspective can offer explanations for the success and failure of the implementation of information systems. In this paper an institutional perspective is applied to the analysis of two inter-organizational information systems in two very different institutional environments. The analysis of cultural biases, the structural order which supplies coordination mechanisms, and the driving forces of the innovation points to issues which must be taken into account during implementation of inter-organizational information systems. It is concluded that the institutional perspective offers new and promising directions for the analysis of these systems.

## Introduction

This paper analyses the implementation of inter-organizational systems (IOS), (Barret & Konsynski, 1982; Huigen, 1993; Christiaanse et al, 1995). The implementation of IOS are seen as social and political processes in which institutional factors can play an important role. The paper presents interesting evidence regarding the implementation of information technology (IT) in complex interfirm and social networks. We argue that IT development is an ongoing social process, in which networks of actors produce outcomes. However, these actors involved in information systems development do not operate in a vacuum: their interactions are influenced by institutional contexts (King et al, 1994). We argue that especially where IT innovations surpass organizational boundaries and where they change boundaries between organizations as is usually the case in IOS, these institutional contexts are particularly important.

We thus focus on inter-organizational information systems from an institutional point of view and present two cases from two very different institutional environments. Based on previous research a set of dimensions is identified which will be used to analyse these cases.

Our research method is intentionally explorative given the present state of the discussion. The cases describe the process of the development and implementation of the information systems. This paper should be seen as an attempt to identify some of the important factors that might account for the different outcomes of each of the cases. A process perspective is used to be able to provide the details of the dynamics of the implementation of the systems in institutional terms.

We make three claims:

(1) IOS implementation is complex: not so much technically as organizationally. In particular because it surpasses organizational boundaries (Christiaanse, 1994).

(2) An institutional point of view is not often applied to analyse the implementation of inter-organizational information systems. It is more often applied to analyse and evaluate the development of information systems within organizations (Kling & Iacono, 1984).

(3) An institutional perspective, however, is crucial to fully understand the dynamics of IOS implementation and this perspective may offer promising analytical instruments for research.

In this paper it becomes clear that the outcome of IOS developments varies with respect to factors which cannot be comprehended without institutional dimensions. We will start with a discussion of the institutional dimensions we draw from various sets of literature. This will be followed by a description of the cases. Then the success or failure of each of the cases will be presented in terms of these dimensions. We will conclude with some generalisations of our research results and some suggestions for further research.

## Framework: institutional dimensions in complex social networks

Institutional theory is dispersed over a heterogeneous body of literature. Many disciplines have institutional strands of theory. Economics (Williamson, 1975; North, 1981), political science (Evans, 1985; Wildavsky, 1987), sociology (Scott, 1994), business sciences (March, 1989), and anthropological sciences (Douglas, 1986) all have their particular representatives of institutional thought. Common to these different disciplinary approaches is that they all point out that interactions and choice are both enabled and restricted by habits, procedures and other embodiments of structure. Interaction and choice have over time solidified into these structural arrangements.

From this wealth of literature we focus on three main aspects. These aspects all shed a different light on the ambiguities facing parties during the implementation of IOS. Similar to other implementation processes (Huigen, 1994) the implementation of IOS present operational problems which must be solved during interaction. The design of information systems cannot answer all possible problems in advance. Moreover, circumstances may change which show earlier decisions in a new and unfavourable light.

A crucial question concerning the operationalisation of IOS is the identification of what parties to the system (can) aspire to. All parties will try to get as much gain as is possible, but they will have to determine what possible gains or losses may arise during implementation. The (politico-institutional) cultural theory of Douglas (1982) holds that preferences arise because of cultural biases. Parties adhere to cultures which prompt certain positions and preferences in social processes. Below, we will elaborate this first aspect of our analytical framework.

The second aspect concerns the way interactions are influenced by the social and political framework in which they occur. Interactions cannot occur in a vacuous space: they are embedded in coordination mechanisms, in what can be called fundamental structural arrangements (Fischer, 1980).

A third aspect focuses on an important part of the context of inter-organizational information systems. These systems are developed because of, and in the context of, belief systems on the part of the buyers and developers of these systems. Whether in market niches, in business communities or in technical communities there are ideas concerning the need for, or the inevitability of, these systems. In other words an important explanatory factor of the implementation of these systems lies in the mix of belief systems regarding the ideal supply-push or demand-pull forces for innovation (King et al, 1994). This and the previous aspects will be expanded below.

## Biases in perceptions

Cultural bias theory is based on extensive anthropological research (Douglas, 1986). It is a theory of preference formation. Currently, it is increasingly used in technological environments (Schwarz & Thompson, 1990; Hoppe, 1993). The central question of the theory is the origin of goals and preferences of people and organizations, and it states that goals and preferences are to a large extent suggested by the culture to which they belong. Culture, Douglas and Wildavsky (1982) state, can be distinguished on two variables: the strength of prescription and the cohesion within the group. These grid–group dimensions produce four cultures (see Table 1). Cultures with weak cohesion and prescription are market-type cultures. The opposite is a hierarchical culture. Egalitarian cultures are characterised by strong cohesion and weak prescription. Fatalists do not care much for group ties, but they do subject themselves to authority.

From cultural theory it follows that phenomena which occur in social interaction will be judged according to the cultural bias of the actors. Implementation of information systems must therefore account for these biases.

## Coordination mechanisms

The second institutional aspect we focus on is the structural order which underlies interactions between parties. The operationalisation of structural order is difficult. In neo-institutional economics the concept of property rights is used (e.g. North, 1981); in political theory the concept of rational order is used (Fischer, 1980). Although these concepts are very important, we will try to become a little more operational.

IOS are usually tightly coupled systems. The output of one actor’s work may be the input of another. Even more tightly coupled information systems perform parallel tasks. At the end these processes may be assembled in a combined product. The point is that during the implementation of the information system underlying these activities the actors must closely coordinate their actions. This is the case even when the IOS is not tightly coupled: the implementation of the information system is usually too expensive in funds, manpower and time to be able to afford a linear, gradual implementation process.

However, the coordination of action cannot be seen apart from the structural order underlying the interactions between the actors in a business or government network. This structural order enables, but it can also inhibit certain avenues of action and communication. In the analysis of the two cases we will identify the structural order and assess its effect on the implementation of the information system.

## The supply-push demand-pull dimension

Influence and regulation can play different roles depending on the ‘supply-push’ or ‘demand-pull’ forces driving any innovation (Utterback, 1974; King et al, 1994). Supply-push forces come from the production of the innovative product or process itself. Demand-pull forces arise from the willingness of potential users to use the innovation. An initiative can be characterised as a supply-push intervention where users need to be stimulated to implement the system instead of a demand-pull intervention with an articulate demand from users (Utterback, 1974; King et al, 1994). Most often the initiative of an innovation responds to some requests and/or needs that are present in the community that the innovation is going to be applied to. King et al (1994) note in this regard that:

There can be no innovation without a new idea or artifact to adopt and apply, but innovators usually respond to established needs and these forces shape institutional actions regarding innovations (King et al, p 149).

Research on innovation through the mid 1970s culminated in the view that demand-pull forces were dominant in the innovation process (Utterback, 1974), with expected institutional implications (King et al, 1994). However supply-push factors were found to be influential in technical industries where the innovation itself created a demand. We argue that the fact whether an innovation can be either characterised as supply-push or demand-pull has an important impact on the type of institutional action and outcomes of these initiatives. We will illustrate how implementation of IOS is shaped in two cases. We will demonstrate that one of the crucial factors that differentiate the implementation between the GBA case and the EMP case were related to the characteristics of the dimensions, and that these to a certain extent were responsible for the failure and success of the innovation.

## The cases

## Case 1: GBA

The GBA system stands for the intermunicipal network for the exchange of population information in the Netherlands. In the 1960s the Ministry of the Interior considered and developed the idea of a central administration of Dutch citizens (In Dutch: ‘De Wet Centrale Persoons Administratie’). Population data are gathered by municipalities and used by them and other government organizations for various purposes. The idea was to administer the data more efficiently. It was only in September 1994 that this information system was implemented. Moreover, the information system that was implemented was completely different from the one that was originally devised. In the original 1960s design it was a star-like network, a many-to-one configuration, which is more or less hierarchical. The design which was implemented was a many-to-many design, a decentralised information system, in which municipalities could electronically communicate with each other. The information system that was implemented was an electronic network in which municipalities could communicate with each other and with organizations that used population data (Huigen, 1993). The question of interest in the light of this paper is in what way this change can be explained, and to what extent an institutionalist perspective can offer help in this explanation.

Population data concern many government organizations. Municipalities use them for preparation and implementation of policies. Departments at the national level use them for the same purposes. The data are also very important with respect to the financing of local government. Dutch municipalities receive the major share of their finances from the national government. The Ministry of Finance allocates these funds from a general Municipality Fund on the basis of a complex distribution key. Most important in this key is the number of inhabitants. Other examples are many earmarked subsidies, which are distributed proportionate to the number of inhabitants of a municipality. Many departments have informational ties with local governments in this fashion. Population data are therefore of great importance in the Dutch administrative system. The data is one of the blood streams for the operation of the administration.

Historically, the municipalities are responsible for the collection, storage and distribution of these data. This responsibility is one of several symbols of the autonomy of local governments in the decentralised unity state of the Netherlands (Daalder, 1987). The distribution of extensive powers to the municipalities is a cornerstone of the Dutch administrative system, and can be traced back to the desire to realise self-rule in fairly closed communities differing in religion, and to some extent in social class, from the 1700s until the present. In this tradition every break of municipal autonomy can be expected to meet severe opposition. And in fact, the plan to centralise the storage of population data was rejected by the municipalities. For example, many municipalities were alarmed when they realised that the centralised system would allow other government organizations to acquire their data freely, without asking the municipalities. Their lobby organization, the Union of Dutch Municipalities (VNG, Vereniging van Nederlandse Gemeenten), came with a counterproposal. It was designed after years of fruitless discussion, the Ministry of the Interior nevertheless presented a Bill in the House of Representatives based on the centralised functional design. However, the House voted against the Bill. The House agreed with the municipalities that the plan would break the principle of local autonomy. Apart from that, it was judged too great a risk to impose this plan on the municipalities. Even in the centralised plan, cooperation of the municipalities would be of such importance that implementation of this plan was deemed impossible. An alternative was called for.

In 1984 the Ministry of the Interior emulated the design of the Union of Dutch Municipalities. The GBAproject, as it was called then, aimed at the interchange of municipal population data between local and national government. However, this interchange is achieved by a network that allows municipalities to communicate with each other. Interestingly, when this solution was decided upon, the technology was still at an experimental level. The network solution using an X.400 protocol was not fully developed. The two main functions in this network, maintenance of the system and the quality of the information are split between the two layers of government. System maintenance is centralised, but information quality is a local responsibility.

In other words, the new design of the system mirrors the egalitarian nature which is ingrained in Dutch administrative politics and cultural context. In the decentralised unity state, local administrations have autonomy in certain areas which make them equal to other government layers. This egalitarian nature of the plan to establish this network, and the implementation of the network can be characterised in a number of instances.

In the first place, municipalities do not have to send data automatically. They must first receive a request from another party, e.g., a national government department. Then, they must authorise the exchange of the requested data. Finally, these data can be sent via the network. It is understood that the receiver can only use the data for purposes outlined in the request. In this way municipalities are able to retain control over their data.

Another problem of implementation was the definition of the data in the system. This problem arose vertically between the Ministry of the Interior and local government, and horizontally between Ministries at the national level. To give an example of the latter, the Ministry of Housing wanted to issue regulations about addresses. It maintained that addresses belong to houses. Of course, this was understandable from its particular point of view. Subsidies from the Department of Housing are usually given to buildings rather than to persons. But the Ministry of the Interior preferred addresses to be linked to people. In the end the issue was ‘solved’ pragmatically: the automisation of population data was further developed by the Ministry of the Interior, and the project followed its definition of an address. Subsequently, this definition could be, and was used, without many problems by the municipalities.

A third problem of the GBA project was that smaller municipalities hardly benefitted from the informativeness of population data. Small municipalities with some thousand citizens or less have to invest heavily in hardware, training, and administrative development. But usually, they have only very few mutations to perform. The project leader for the Ministry of the Interior remarked that:

“. . . it is clear to me that redrawing boundaries between municipalities is necessary. Otherwise the smaller municipalities could at least hook on to their bigger neighbours. In the present situation, however, they go at all lengths on order to avoid cooperation” (Huigen, 1993).

But this would go against municipal autonomy. As we saw earlier this type of development was effectively blocked by the initiatives of the municipalities, but the project nevertheless added to an already increasing pressure to reform the Dutch municipality structure.

These three problems during the informativeness of population data point to some ambiguities inherent to (the design and implementation of) informative projects. Nothing is obvious or unequivocally clear in informativeness. Political forces fighting around these ambiguities produce a certain infrastructure which shapes public administration. In the above we saw that information and communication technologies (ICT) may change routings of information flows. In the pre-GBA period, departments had direct lines of communication with their counterparts in municipalities. The GBA makes these relations more indirect. Departments have to ask for these data formally, and via a channel which is run by the Ministry of the Interior. Also the project contributes to increasing pressures to reduce the number of municipalities. Considering the fact that this is the first electronic data interchange (EDI)-project in a row (information systems exchanging information about real estate, environment, and finance may follow), EDI thus may very well produce profound changes in the informational infrastructure of the Dutch administrative system.

The confrontation between two institutions during the implementation of these complex network settings is central to our paper. The following differences and institutional aspects are critical in the case discussed above.

First, there is the difference in the standard operating procedures in the Dutch administrative system and the professional expertise of the information design experts. Initially, the design procedures inherent in information systems development led to a design which was hierarchically oriented. This design conflicted with some powerful images ingrained in the Dutch administrative system of autonomy of government layers. It was only when information systems consultants outlined the possibility of new network technology that system design and administrative culture could be reconciled.

A second important institutional aspect of the case is that the activity during implementation entailed close coordination of actions of various organizations: municipalities, software suppliers, the Ministry of the Interior and the organizations that use population data (e.g., Ministries). This meant that these organizations had to be tightly coupled during implementation, whereas in their ordinary activities these organizations are to a large extent dependent on each other. This going against the standard operating procedures effected high costs of coordination, not only in financial terms, but also in terms of time.

## Case 2: The Reuters EMP initiative

In 1992 Reuters, the worldwide press agency and supplier of business information services, started developing an electronic information system on behalf of parties in the spotmarkets for air cargo space. The initiative to place an electronic market system in the market for air cargo space has been developed by the Department of Airline Economics of the University of Amsterdam (Roos, 1993). Reuters was assigned to facilitate the necessary equipment.

The so-called Reuters-initiative is an international initiative with terminals in Amsterdam, London, Paris and Frankfurt. The traditional air cargo community consists of three broad functional domains: airlines (passengers/cargo carriers as well as cargo-only carriers), ground transport companies (truck and rail transport companies), and freight forwarders that coordinate the door-to-airport and airport-to-door activities at each end.

## The carriers

In traditional distribution the only activity carriers perform is flying goods from airport-to-airport. Many airline companies have decided to expand the percentage of direct distribution. Direct distribution means that carriers do business with shippers directly, thereby eliminating the forwarders. Two reasons for this choice mentioned during field-work are: (1) Changing needs of the customers. Due to intense competition between various kinds of transport (high degree of substitution) on the one hand, and intense competition between air cargo carriers on the other, there is an increasing need of delivering guaranteed services. Customers need a reliable, flexible and time-definite delivery at the lowest possible price. This asks for an extensive supply of various connecting kinds of transportation. There is a growing need of full logistic services; (2) Zero profits in the core-business (airport-to-airport transport of goods). Air carriers are on the extreme left corner of the distribution channel. Between logistics and forwarders there is price elasticity. On the other hand, between forwarders and carriers there is price inelasticity (Roos, 1993). This distinction between price elasticity and price inelasticity can be explained by dividing the market for air cargo in two sub-markets, a market for air cargo services and a market for air cargo space.

## The forwarders

The activities of the forwarders are usually highly differentiated. Most of the forwarders have five or six different functions within their own system. They sometimes act as carrier, broker, consolidator and agent for the airline, as agent for the client and as logistic service center (Vervaat, 1994). Brokers and consolidators buy space from airlines and sell this space to shippers.

## The integrators

Recently a new kind of player has emerged in the air cargo community: the integrator. Integrators (e.g. Federal Express, DHL) do everything, including flying the cargo in their own aircraft. In the US the integrators grew very rapidly by transporting small, high-value, time-critical packages, usually documents. They gained 92% of the US domestic air cargo shipments with relative ease. However, these statistics are misleading in that the 8% remaining with the traditional carriers proved to be worth 25% of the total air cargo revenues (Forster & King, 1994). Moreover, much of the integrators’ growth was due to a large increase of document and small parcel shipment that the integrators stimulated. The actual position of traditional air cargo was not fundamentally altered (King et al, 1996).

The content of the information system consists of three parts: (1) Scrolling news page consisting of general and specific air cargo news that can influence the market of air cargo space; (2) Summary of all available business information, such as oil prices and exchange rates. This information is helpful in increasing the insight in the influence of these factors; (3) Summary of indicative price quotes imputed by sellers. These quotes show to which degree sellers would like to buy or sell space. By changing the indicative prices contributors can give signals to other parties involved. In this way prices are assigned the function of information carrier. The three parts together provide a complete overview of the spotmarkets for air cargo space. Changing circumstances can soon be made visible, and in this way it is possible to react to these changes more quickly.

The implementation process and reasons for its failure The information system has run on trial at the airport at Schiphol from August 1993 to January 1994. In the information system there was no trade. The system was completely filled with pre-trade information. The Reutersinitiative is focused on creating a symmetric transparency in the air cargo market. In the situation before the Reuters-initiative the markets for air cargo can be called extremely non-transparent. There are a great number of different market parties, each with his own exploitation problem. Uncertainty about their own situation of capacity compared with the market situation, and fear for mutual price underbidding cause mysteriousness and intransparency between airlines. On the other hand there is much more transparency for buyers in the markets for air cargo. For them there are replaceable alternatives. They have knowledge of prices and price differences by shopping around. This higher transparency for buyers than for sellers causes a heavy price competition, because sellers react (too) slow to changes in market conditions. Higher transparency causes a decrease in uncertainty and because of this a more efficient tuning between demand and supply. The creation of a symmetric transparency causes the possibility for the sellers to react to changing circumstances more quickly. In micro-economical terms this means the sellers are capable of being more price-elastic because of a better view on the market (Vervaat, 1994). It is important to realise this is the opinion of the initiators of the Reuters EMP, not that of other partners in this network.

One of the reasons for its failure, is that the information system has not been given much support. Only in the financial world were the reactions very positive. However, in the branch of industry itself parties were very opposed to the Reuters-initiative. The following objections were provided during field interviews.

At first the forwarders had serious doubts, and in the end the support for the system was totally withdrawn. Some of the reasons provided during field-work were: fear for the elimination of the forwarder, fear for decreasing margins due to increasing transparency of the market, and the negative attitude in general towards electronic business.

The reaction of the carriers hasn’t been positive either. During fieldwork the following reasons were provided that stand in the way of realising an electronic market for air cargo. (From an interview with the authors (1994) with Grin B, Director KLM Cargo, Strategic Development). (1) According to the carriers it is impossible to split the market for air cargo into a market for space and a market for service. Carriers think there is no clear cut distinction possible between the definitions of space and service. (2) Air carriers claim the demand and supply for air cargo space cannot be separated. This means in the short-term, the demand for and supply of air cargo space cannot easily be changed. Carriers will not immediately buy new aeroplanes if demand for space increases. On the other hand, if the price of space rises goods will have to be transported as well, so demand will not fall. (3) Alias-effect: According to the carriers it is not possible to make a clear-cut distinction between different parties. Some forwarders act like carriers by buying large bulks of capacity and then reselling it.

As a result of these conflicting interests the system ‘has been put on ice’. It was concluded that there is no viability for the system if the parties concerned refuse to cooperate. The system is related to pre-trade information. It must constantly be updated, which asks for an active role of the parties involved. The initiators had thought of a solution for this problem. Whenever a party would fail to make changes in the information during a whole day, this parties’ page would be made invisible. However, all the parties have collectively chosen to refuse the system and this choice has made the measure completely pointless. Meantime Reuters is busy looking for parties that might be interested in the system elsewhere in the world.

## Institutional dimensions and cases

## The cultural bias dimension

Bias plays a distinct role in both cases. Interestingly, the turn-around in the GBA case produced an implementation that was reconcilable with the egalitarian bias of the municipalities. This has two aspects. In the first place the information system itself mirrored a more egalitarian design than the previous one (the CPA): a many-to-many design instead of a many-to-one design controlled by the national government (see Table 2). In the second place the municipalities acquired an equal role during implementation. Changes during implementation were discussed with representatives of municipalities as well as with software producers and the users of population data.

In the EMP case, the information system failed to take biases into account. It was imposed on the various actors in the air cargo business. This implementation procedure was bound to run into difficulty. Not only because hierarchical procedure went against a heavily market-oriented community, but also because it was never clearly demonstrated whether the actors could benefit from the system. On the contrary, as it was set up the operation of the system disturbed a delicate balance in the market which had taken years to develop. This negligence contributed to ending the experiment at an early stage of its development.

## The structure dimension

In the first case, the intermunicipal government network, the interactions between government layers are embedded in the concept of the decentralised unitary state (Daalder, 1987), which is the cornerstone of the Dutch administrative system. In this structural order the three government layers are to a large extent autonomous. They are dependent on one another, but each has its own legal powers, financial means and expertise. The first version of the GBA went against this principle in a number of ways. It was implied in the design that the national government would use the municipalities as a mere collector of population data, and they would not be able to influence the implementation of the system. This went against the principles of coordination of action in the administrative system: the sharing of power and the need for consensus about measures in the light of new problems or developments. When the GBA was implemented, the continuous coordination of the municipalities, national government and the other organizations involved was much more in line with the coordination principles of the decentralised unitary state.

In the case of the EMP the interactions of customers, forwarders, integrators and carriers are based on the distinction of the two major activities in the business: transport space and shipping services. The market is structured along the lines of the so-called ‘space-capacity’ principle, meaning that carriers provide space and forwarders and integrators provide services to fill capacity. This principle, however, is increasingly contested among the main parties in the market. The airlines are of the opinion that they offer not only space, but also services. The forwarders and integrators, however, maintain the strict distinction of activities in the market between airlines and themselves. Both parties, however, agree that the market itself is not (yet) a commodity market in which competition is conducted mainly on price. In contrast, the initiators of the Reuters concept claimed that the market for air cargo space can be separated from the market for air cargo service. They claim that the market for air cargo space has developed itself from a differentiated market to a commodity market. If this is the case the only important information is price information.

In this difficult situation the EMP system provided new problems. Since it could only contain price information and no product or service information, it treated the market as a commodity market where parties could only compete on price instead of in service attributes. In the system the sellers (the airlines and the integrators) are explicitly considered sellers of air cargo space. According to the initiators, buyers of space are the same as the sellers of air cargo services (Vervaat, 1994). The air cargo market was thus regarded as a commodity market by the Reuters initiative and thwarted the space capacity coordination mechanism used by the parties involved. We believe the system did not take these issues into account during the design of the system and that this was an important reason for the parties not to participate.

## The supply-push demand-pull dimension

We discussed that it is important to realise that supplypush forces come from the production of the innovative product or process itself, while demand-pull forces arise from the willingness of potential users to use the innovation. An initiative can be characterised as a supplypush intervention where users need to be stimulated to implement the system instead of a demand-pull intervention with an articulate demand from users (Utterback, 1974; King et al, 1994).

Clearly in the GBA case the initiative was coming to a large extent from the community itself: the Ministry of the Interior developed the idea of a central administration of Dutch citizens. The distribution of extensive powers to the municipalities is a cornerstone of the Dutch administrative system, and information distribution according to these egalitarian principles is considered an important feature of the social relationships in this network. It was in the interest of the municipalities that information flows among them were facilitated and it is important to realise that it was not a third party initiative to introduce a system, to apply a strongly centralised infrastructure that was not congruent with the social and political structures in this community. In fact the system was drastically adapted before the actual implementation to adopt these ‘artifacts’ that were such an important aspect of the social configurations in this network.

In the EMP case the initiative for the system was a collaboration between Reuters and the University of Amsterdam. The role of intermediary in this case is performed by the newsagency, Reuters. The development or implementation of the system were not initiated by the carriers, forwarders or integrators. In fact the initiative can be characterised as a supply-push intervention where users need to be stimulated to implement the system instead of a demand-pull intervention with an articulate demand from users (Utterback, 1974; King et al, 1994).

The fact that the ownership of the system would be with Reuters means that neither buyers nor sellers would have control over the system. The carriers and the forwarders expected that the system would further erode their margins and that the implementation of the system was not in their interest. Reuters obviously tried to move in to this market with the idea that by providing the operation of the system and the additional services to the parties in this business, network profits could be obtained. The electronic market initiators tried unsuccessfully to convince the parties to use the system during 1993 even though from the beginning the parties were reluctant to participate (Roos, 1993).

## Conclusions

Inter-organizational information systems link, by definition, several organizations together. This linkage is always complex, since these organizations are usually mutually dependent in various ways. On top of that, linkage by ways of information exchange usually means the adaptation or redesign of internal operations (Davenport, 1993). This type of information systems often confront the participants with issues of an economic and political nature. As organizations are usually active within a structural or institutional framework, the implementation of inter-organizational information systems means that power structures, action frameworks, and the distribution of competencies may be challenged. This paper has shown that when this institutional context with its existing power structures and competitive implications are not taken into account in the implementation of these information systems, they will be up against major problems.

In the case of the EMP initiative, institutional characteristics finally lead to failure, even though this market in terms of characteristics (in transparency, volatility, high transaction and search costs) provided the conditions for the use of a successful EMP system. However, as information asymmetries that were favouring the sellers in this network were eliminated, it had severe impacts on power relationships in this industry. Moreover, the buyers were of the opinion that the system did not facilitate satisfactory guarantees that the system would not reduce their importance and role as forwarder by facilitating direct deals between airlines and customers. Also, the way the system went against the cultural biases of the actors and the way their actions are usually coordinated, contributed to the explanation of the failure of the EMP initiative. The more successful GBA system scored better on these institutional dimensions. Although this system had difficulty in the beginning the participants later agreed on a plan in which implementation was congruent to its institutional environment.

In the institutional perspective on IOS the role of the various participants takes on a new meaning. As these systems almost always entail a combination of private and public actors with different institutional settings, the implementation of IOS have to be carefully designed and monitored. This is especially so, if it is generally agreed that these innovations are necessary to further socioeconomic and political goals. For example, in the air cargo case the absence of government intervention in terms of restructuring markets may have been an important omission. On the other hand stringent regulations and standardisation might decrease innovativeness of parties as in this business network, and in a country like the Netherlands, authoritarian interventions like in Singapore would be unimaginable. Government intervention can be a key factor in the success of complex IOS initiatives [Singapore Tradelink case; King et al, 1994].

More research is needed to fully understand the institutional factors that might play a role in the implementation of complex IOS initiatives involving multi-party agreements. We hope that this paper stimulated further empirical and theoretical research along these lines.
