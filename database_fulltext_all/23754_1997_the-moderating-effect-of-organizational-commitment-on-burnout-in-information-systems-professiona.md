# The moderating effect of organizational commitment on burnout in information systems professionals

RC King<sup>1</sup> and V Sethi<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>College of Commerce and Business Administration, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Champaign, IL 61820; and <sup>2</sup>CIS Department, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, MO 65804, USA

The study examines the moderating role of organizational commitment on the relationship between role stressors and burnout in information systems (IS) professionals. Two role stressors are examined in this study – role ambiguity and role conflict. In addition, two dimensions of organizational commitment – affective and continuance commitment – are examined for their moderating influence. The emotional exhaustion subscale of the Maslach Burnout Inventory is used to measure burnout in 312 IS professionals. Both role stressors were found to correlate positively with burnout. In addition, affective commitment was found to moderate the relationship between role stressors and burnout. Therefore, in employees with high affective commitment, role stressors had a lesser effect on burnout. This study, therefore, supports the argument that commitment acts as a buffer against stress and its antecedents.

## Introduction

Organizational commitment and job stress have been noted as factors that can affect the work performance and tenure of information systems (IS) professionals. Previous research has examined these variables in order to identify the consequences of commitment and job stress such as turnover and performance effectiveness. For example, Igbaria and Greenhaus (1992) found that committed employees are less likely to leave an organization than those who are less committed. Mathieu and Zajac (1990) determined in their meta-analysis that commitment is negatively related to withdrawal behaviour; i.e., intention to search for job alternatives and an intention to quit.

Several practical consequences of stress and burnout have also been identified in the literature. Kahill (1988) grouped these consequences into five categories: physical, emotional, interpersonal, attitudinal, and behavioural. These categories include a variety of mental and physical health problems (Burke & Deszca, 1986); physiological strain (Kaldenberg & Becker, 1992); deterioration of personal relationships (Jackson & Maslach, 1982); the development of negative attitudes toward the job (Burke et al, 1984); and a decrease in the quality of job performance (Maslach & Jackson, 1985).

While the antecedents and consequences of burnout and commitment are important, these variables must also be examined in the broader framework of organizational stress. Motowidlo et al (1986) proposed a model of occupational stress in which the subjective experience of stress is caused by specific work-related events and leads to negative effects such as anxiety, hostility, and depression. These negative effects are often associated with lower job performance. In this model, the cumulative effects of the frequency and intensity of stressors produce the stress experience. Revicki and May (1989) also determined that occupational stress (as a result of organizational climate, supervisor behaviours, and work group relations) had a direct impact on the development of depressive symptoms in nurses.

Although it is important for organizations to enhance commitment because of its relationship to turnover, as Reilly (1994) has noted, commitment is a multifaceted construct with implications for performance and organizational membership. However, is there a relationship between burnout and organizational commitment? Jackson et al (1986) first proposed such a link. In a study of elementary and secondary school teachers, they reported significant relationships between emotional exhaustion and turnover intentions (i.e., thoughts of leaving the school, actually leaving the school, and training for a new career). Therefore, it becomes reasonable to expect that organizational commitment will have an impact on burnout and the process by which organizational factors (stressors) cause burnout. However, these relationships have not been examined previously in the IS literature. This study is designed to examine whether organizational commitment helps IS employees respond to stressors in the work environment.

Previous research has indicated that commitment can act as a moderator variable between stress and its potential outcomes (Mathieu & Zajac, 1990). There are two competing arguments in the organizational behaviour literature regarding this moderating influence (Begley & Czajka, 1993). The first perspective is provided by

Mathieu and Zajac (1990), who propose that highly committed employees feel the effects of stress more than less committed employees. They argue that highly committed employees identify with an organization and have a higher emotional attachment with it and that this increases their vulnerability to psychological threats (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Thus, more committed employees take problems more personally and are more disturbed by them, and therefore negative consequences of stressful organizational situations are greater. Less committed employees are more detached from an organization. This allows them to avoid the negative consequences of stressful situations because of a lack of personal interest.

A second perspective proposes that commitment protects individuals from the negative effects of stressors because commitment enables employees to remain focused on their roles (Kobasa, 1982). Committed members of an organization confront threats but are better able to deal with organizational insecurity. For example, Mowday et al (1982) note that organizational commitment “can provide employees with stability and feelings of belonging” (p 139), which can provide a buffer against the negative effects of stress on job attitudes. Similarly, Antonovsky and Antonovsky (1974) argues that commitment is a critical resource that enables individuals to resist the effects of tension in their environment and connects them more closely to their social networks. Employees committed to their company can be expected to benefit from this buffer.

This study also examines two different dimensions of organizational commitment. Two views of commitment have dominated the organizational behaviour literature: attitudinal (or affective) commitment and behavioural (or continuance) commitment (Meyer et al, 1993). Affective commitment is defined as an emotional attachment to an organization characterised by strong links (Mowday et al, 1982). Other studies have described affective commitment similarly. Buchanan (1974) defined it as “a partisan, affective attachment to the goals and values of an organization” (p 533) and Etzioni (1975) proposed the concept of moral commitment as the internalisation of norms and identification with authority.

Continuance commitment and the Side-Bet Theory of Commitment were popularised by Becker (1960). According to this theory, employees make certain investments or side-bets in their organizations; for example, tenure toward pensions, promotions, and work relationships. These investments are sunk costs which reduce the attractiveness of other employment opportunities. Commitment is, therefore, an outcome of inducements or exchanges between an individual and an organization.

Organizational commitment has been measured using an instrument (called the Organizational Commitment Questionnaire (OCQ)) developed by Mowday et al (1982). Although the OCQ instrument was primarily intended to examine affective commitment, it also included items relating to an employee’s involvement, willingness to exert effort, and desire to stay (DeCotiis & Summers, 1987). Subsequently, Meyer and Allen (1984) clarified the properties of the organizational commitment construct. McGee and Ford (1987) empirically validated the two-dimension theory of commitment proposed by Meyer and Allen (1984), and examined the properties of two scales to measure affective and continuance commitment. These scales – the affective commitment scale (ACS) and the continuance commitment scale (CCS) – have since been extensively studied in the organizational behaviour literature (Angle & Lawson, 1993; Meyer et al, 1993; Hackett et al, 1994).

In this study we propose to: (a) measure burnout in IS professionals by using the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI); (b) measure organizational commitment in IS personnel by using the ACS and CCS scales; and (c) examine the relationship between organizational stressors and burnout and the moderating influence of ACS and CCS on this relationship.

It is expected that role stressors will be positively related to burnout in IS professionals. We also expect both the ACS and CCS to moderate the relationship between stressors and burnout.

## Literature review

## IS research related to burnout, organizational commitment, and role stressors

Burnout among IS professionals has been examined to a limited extent in the IS literature. McGee (1996) notes that virtual-office technology, top management demands, and downsizing are some of the key factors leading to burnout. She argues that stress and burnout are more severe for IS professionals than other employees because IS employees are expected to ensure that “technology and systems work 24 hours a day”. Similarly, King (1995) notes that stress is a result of more companies deploying increasingly new and complex technologies. For example, help-desk workers are prone to burnout after 18 months. Similarly, Ludlum (1989) cites long and odd hours, unrealistic deadlines, and the proliferation of technology as the main reasons for job dissatisfaction and burnout among IS professionals.

Sonnentag et al (1994) surveyed 180 software professionals from 29 software development teams and found that work stressors were positively related to burnout. They also found that a lack of control at work, work complexity, and openness to criticism within teams were significantly and negatively related to a lack of identification.

Several IS studies have focused on the antecedents and consequences of organizational commitment. These studies have, however, examined only the affective component of commitment as measured by the OCQ instrument.

For example, Igbaria et al (1991) determined that a match between career orientation and job setting is critical to the development of commitment (measured by the OCQ). Their study of 517 IS professionals found that managerially-oriented employees in technical jobs, and technically-oriented employees in managerial jobs displayed negative work attitudes including lack of organizational commitment.

Job-related characteristics have also been found to affect commitment. Igbaria et al (1994), in a study of 112 IS employees in South Africa, determined that job characteristics, salary levels, and job satisfaction had a direct impact on commitment (measured by OCQ). Taskbased rewards (e.g., working with competent colleagues, working on professionally important projects, and having the freedom to be creative), and organization-based rewards (e.g., salary increases) were both determined to have a positive impact on organizational commitment. The study also found that ‘intention to stay’ is negatively related to commitment.

Perceived acceptance by the organization and fulfilment of expectations have also been determined to be antecedents of commitment (measured by OCQ). In a study of 138 IS professionals, Igbaria and Wormly (1992) found that commitment was highest for employees who felt accepted by their organizations or who felt their career prospects were favourable.

Finally, two role stressors have been examined previously in the IS literature: role ambiguity and role conflict (Igbaria & Siegel, 1992). In a study of 107 software engineers, Igbaria and Siegel (1992) found that these role stressors had a direct negative impact on job and career satisfaction. Igbaria and Guimares (1993) also examined 76 information center employees and found that role ambiguity was the most dysfunctional element in relation to job satisfaction.

## Theoretical development of job burnout in the literature

The term ‘burnout’ was coined by Herbert Freudenberger (1974), a clinical psychologist, to refer to stress responses exhibited by staff members in free clinics and halfway houses. Research on burnout began as a result of work conducted on emotion, arousal, and the way in which people cope with, or manage the arousal (Maslach & Jackson, 1984). This research led to the realisation that emotional stress in some occupations can often lead to excessively harmful consequences. Under the general label ‘burnout’, this state of emotional stress has been examined in social workers, nurses, teachers, lawyers, physicians, police officers, and line managers (Jackson et al, 1986).

The most commonly accepted definition of ‘burnout is that it is a reaction to role stress and “is a syndrome of emotional exhaustion and cynicism” (Maslach & Jackson, 1981). The three-component conceptualisation used by Maslach and her colleagues is a popular representation of the phenomena (Maslach, 1982; Maslach & Jackson, 1981). One component of burnout, emotional exhaustion, is characterised by a lack of energy and a feeling that one’s emotional resources are used up. Emotional exhaustion generally coexists with feelings of frustration and tension, as employees feel that “they are no longer able to give of themselves at a psychological level” (Maslach & Jackson, 1981). The second component of burnout is depersonalisation or dehumanisation. This is characterised by a tendency of employees to treat people as objects. Employees display a detached and an emotional callousness, and they may become cynical toward co-workers and clients. The third component of burnout is diminished personal accomplishment, which is characterised by a tendency to evaluate oneself negatively. Employees may experience a decline in feelings of job competence and an increase in feelings of inefficacy.

The emotional exhaustion component of burnout resembles the standard stress variable (Jackson et al, 1986). Studies by Koeske and Koeske (1989) demonstrate that “emotional exhaustion is the essence of burnout” (p 132) and that it is an indicator of strain resulting from the demands of work and the resulting stress. Through a series of five different studies, Koeske and Koeske (1989) show that the emotional exhaustion component of burnout mediates the relationship between stress and outcomes. Therefore, following Reilly (1994), the current study focuses on emotional exhaustion and treats it as synonymous with burnout.

Prior research (Maslach, 1976, 1978a,b, 1979) has shown that burnout can lead to lower commitment, turnover, absenteeism, and low morale. Other studies such as those by Jackson et al (1987), Leiter (1991), and Reilly and Orsak (1991) have shown that emotional exhaustion is highly correlated with measures of organizational commitment.

Several antecedents of burnout have been outlined in the literature. These include factors related to interpersonal relationships, job context, and personal characteristics. One dominant theme in burnout research is the presence of job stressors, defined as those characteristics of the job environment that make considerable demands on the abilities or resources of employees (Abramis, 1994). Stressors typically involve a lack of predictability, control, and understandability of environmental conditions (Cohen, 1980; Sutton & Kahn, 1983).

Two stressors that have been previously noted to be important are role ambiguity and role conflict (Cordes & Dougherty, 1993). Role ambiguity is associated with the need for certainty and predictability with regard to one’s goals and the means of accomplishing them. It may occur if an individual lacks adequate information to accomplish required activities (Jackson & Schuler, 1985). It may also occur in the absence of proper procedures for performing tasks and uncertainty about evaluation criteria (Miles & Perreault, 1976). Role conflict occurs as a result of incongruity or incompatibility of expectations communicated to an individual (Kahn, 1978). According to Rizzo et al (1970), role conflict may occur: (a) between an individual’s internal standards and required job behaviour; (b) between time, resources, or capabilities of an individual and required behaviour; (c) between several roles for the same person which require incompatible behaviour; and (d) because of conflicting expectations and organizational demands.

## Theoretical development of organizational commitment in the literature

The organizational commitment construct has been studied extensively and has been the subject of several critical reviews in the organizational behaviour literature (Mowday et al, 1982; Bar-Hayim & Berman, 1992). Various definitions and measures of organizational commitment have also been proposed. Commitment to an organization is considered to reflect the relative strength of an individual’s identification with, and involvement in, that organization (Steers, 1977). According to Randall (1987), it incorporates three main components: (a) a strong belief in, and an acceptance of, the organization’s goals; (b) a willingness to exert considerable effort on behalf of the organization; and (c) a desire to maintain membership in the organization.

The common theme across most definitions of commitment is that it represents a bond between an individual and an organization. How that bond develops is, however, explained differently. Two distinct perspectives have become well established in the organizational commitment literature and are: affective attachment to an organization and the perceived costs (also known as continuance commitment) associated with leaving the organization.

## Affective attachment

Several authors view commitment as an affective orientation toward the organization. Kanter (1968) termed this “cohesion commitment,” and Mowday et al (1979) describe this as:

“the relative strength of an individual’s identification with and involvement in a particular organization”. (p 226)

What is the basis for an employee’s psychological attachment to an organization? One important mechanism in the development of affective attachment is the process of identification (Bowlby, 1982) with the attitudes, values, or goals of an organization. These attitudes are accepted by the individual and become incorporated into his/her cognitive response set (Kagan, 1958).

Thus, affective commitment proposes that an employee remains a member of an organization because he/she wants to, enjoys membership in that organization, and “identifies with it” (O’Reilly & Chatman, 1986). However, the OCQ instrument, based on studies by Porter (1974) and used to measure affective commitment, includes items that are considered to not adequately capture the affective component of commitment. For example, in the OCQ instrument, affective content is less important than an employee’s desire to stay and willingness to exert effort (DeCotiis & Summers, 1987). Therefore, Allen and Meyer (1990) proposed an alternative formulation for affective commitment and developed the ACS scale to measure it. This scale focuses on an employee’s degree of psychological attachment to an organization and emphasises the positive elements of attachment; e.g., loyalty, affection, belonging, and happiness (Jaros et al, 1993).

## Perceived costs

Other authors view commitment as resulting from a recognition of the costs associated with leaving an organization. Becker (1960) termed this continuance commitment and described it as a “disposition to engage in consistent lines of activity” (p 33). Costs result from ‘side-bets which would be lost due to termination of employment. Value is assigned to factors such as pension and seniority, which are contingent upon continued employment in that organization. Kanter (1968) defines “cognitivecontinuance commitment” as that which occurs when there is “a profit associated with continued participation and a ‘cost’ associated with leaving” (p 504).

Continuance commitment is thus a conscious psychological state shaped by environmental conditions (e.g., the existence of side-bets) and has implications for behaviour (e.g., continued employment with the organization). Allen and Meyer (1990) developed the CCS scale to measure continuance commitment, and the instrument focuses on the costs such as monetary, social, and psychological, that compel an employee to remain in an organization.

Both types of commitment reflect ties that develop between an employee and an organization; and both dimensions describe links that provide reasons for an employee to remain in an organization. However, the nature of the links is different. Studies in the organizational behaviour literature also suggest that affective and continuance commitment are both predictors of turnover and the withdrawal process. However, a meta-analysis by Mathieu and Zajac (1990) shows that affective commitment has a higher correlation with job involvement and job satisfaction than continuance commitment.

## Research hypotheses

This study has three main objectives: first, to measure burnout in IS professionals by using the emotional exhaustion sub-scale of the MBI and to examine the relationship of burnout with role stressors; second, to measure organizational commitment in IS personnel by using Allen and Meyer’s (1990) ACS and CCS instruments; and third, to examine whether organizational commitment (as measured by the ACS and CCS scales) moderates the relationship between role stressors and burnout.

As described above, role conflict and role ambiguity are two role stressors that can lead to burnout. Schwab and Iwanicki (1982) found that these stressors accounted for a significant amount of the variance in the emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation dimensions for a sample of 469 teachers. Brookings et al (1985) reported significant relationships between perceived role conflict and role ambiguity and all three burnout components. While most of these studies have been undertaken for human service professionals, role ambiguity and conflict have been found to affect IS professionals as well (Igbaria & Greenhaus, 1992).

We examine the relationship between role stressors and burnout in IS employees and propose that:

H1: Role stressors (role ambiguity and role conflict) will be positively related to burnout in IS employees.

In addition, we examine the moderating role of organizational commitment on the stressor–burnout relationship and propose that:

H2: Affective and continuance commitment will moderate the relationship between role stressors and burnout.

Hypothesis 2, as noted previously, is partly based on an argument for the positive aspects of commitment as described by Kobasa (1982). According to this perspective, commitment protects individuals from the negative effects of stress because it allows them to attach direction and meaning to their work.

## Research methodology

## Procedures

One hundred and sixty-one firms were randomly selected from the local chamber of commerce listings of five US metropolitan areas (two midwestern, three eastern). IS directors of these firms were contacted by phone and asked to participate in the study. This was followed by a letter explaining the project. Eighty-nine organizations agreed to distribute questionnaires to IS employees. This resulted in a total sample of 312 responses.

Several precautions were taken in the design of this study to ensure a representative sample and to avoid any bias related to industry, geographical region, or type of work. This was done by geographical randomization: selecting companies randomly from five different geographical areas of the United States; industry distribution: selecting the initial sample of 161 companies so that a variety of industries were represented in the sample; and work distribution: selecting five employees randomly in each of the 89 organizations in our final sample (in order to achieve an item to response ratio of 1:10). Therefore, we have increased confidence in the representativeness of the study sample.

All respondents were assured of confidentiality and anonymity and that only aggregate data would be reported. A variety of industries were included in the sample: insurance (7.7%), manufacturing (18.9%), financial services (12.5%), consulting (15.1%), retail (12.8%), utilities (6.7%), healthcare (12.2%), transportation (5.1%), information technology (7.4%), and others (1.6%).

Job titles of individual respondents included system programmers (13%), project leaders (8%), application programmers (17%), systems analysts (20%), IS managers (8%), software engineers (13%), consultants (12%), and other (13%). Sixty-one per cent of the respondents were involved in system development activities, and 31% performed end-user computing support.

The average age of the 312 respondents was 31 years; average tenure in the organization was 4 years; and average tenure in the IS area was 5.3 years.

## Measures

This section discusses the measurement and operationalisation of the socialisation tactics and outcome variables.

## Experienced burnout

The emotional exhaustion sub-scale of the Maslach Burnout Inventory was used to measure the burnout syndrome. The sub-scale consists of nine items and its reliability coefficients have been reported to be 0.88 in previous studies (Maslach & Jackson, 1981; Reilly, 1994).

## Organizational commitment

Commitment was measured by the eight-item affective and the eight-item continuance commitment scales used by Meyer and Allen (1984). Responses were measured on seven-point, strongly disagree to strongly agree scales, and scale scores were computed by averaging across items. Internal consistency of these scales has been reported previously to be in the range of 0.84 to 0.88 for affective commitment, and 0.70 to 0.84 for continuance commitment (Meyer et al, 1989).

## Role ambiguity and role conflict

Role ambiguity and conflict were measured using a modified version of the scale developed by Rizzo et al (1970). The original scales for these constructs contained an eight-item measure of role conflict and a six-item measure of role ambiguity. However, there has been criticism of these scales due to the confounding effects of scale wordings (Tracy & Johnson, 1981), and attributional bias (Smith et al, 1993). Also, Smith et al (1993) advise that some of the items from both scales be dropped and encourage further experimentation with scale items based on “theoretical, not empirical considerations.” For this study, we examined the original set of thirty items that were factor analysed by Rizzo et al (1970) and used to develop the eight- and six-item scales. We selected six items (RC1–RC6) from the role conflict group to represent conflict between several roles. Five items (RA1–RA5) were selected from the role ambiguity group to best represent properties suggested by Breaugh and Calihan (1994) – work method ambiguity, scheduling ambiguity and performance criteria ambiguity. These items are: “I feel certain about how much authority I have on the job” (RA1); “I know what my responsibilities are” (RA2); “I know what is exactly expected of me” (RA3); “Job explanation is clear concerning what is to be done” (RA4); “I perform work that suits my values” (RA5); “I have to do things that should be done differently” (RC1); “I work under incompatible policies and guidelines” (RC2); “I work with two or more groups who operate quite differently” (RC3); “I receive incompatible requests from two or more people” (RC4); “I work on many unnecessary assignments” (RC5); and “I have to work under vague directives or orders” (RC6).

A factor analysis of these items suggests the presence of two constructs. Items RA1–RA5 loaded on one factor with factor loadings above 0.58. Items RC1–RC6 loaded on one factor with factor loadings above 0.53. One item RC6 showed a cross loading of 0.53. However, since RC6 can be better interpreted as relating to role conflict, all six items RC1–RC6 were used to measure role conflict.

## Dimensionality of the commitment scales

In order to assess the dimensionality of the commitment scales (affective and continuance), the 16 items comprising them were factor analysed using maximum likelihood estimation followed by varimax rotation. The eight ACS items loaded on the first factor. Six of the eight continuance-commitment items loaded on the second factor. Two items – CCS7 and CCS8 – did not load. These results mirror previous analyses conducted by McGee and Ford (1987). The factor analysis suggested deleting the two CCS items and recomputing the remaining six items as one scale. The final reliability estimates for the ACS scale were 0.88 (8 items) and 0.84 for the revised CCS scale (6 items).

## Hypothesis 1

As indicated in Table 1, zero-order correlations show that both role stressors – role ambiguity and role conflict – are positively related to burnout (r = 0.44 and 0.53 respectively). In addition, ACS is inversely related (r = −0.45) and CCS positively related (r = 0.29) with burnout. These correlations support Hypothesis 1.

Low affective commitment was defined as a median score less than 32 (n = 155); and the high affective commitment group had median scores greater than 32 (n = 143). While the groups differ in mean values of ACS, CCS, and burnout, there are no significant differences in age, number of years in the IS field, and the number of years in the company.

## Discussion

This study was conducted with three specific objectives: first, to measure burnout in IS professionals by using the emotional exhaustion sub-scale of the MBI and to examine the relationship of burnout with role stressors; second, to measure organizational commitment in IS personnel by using Allen and Meyer’s (1990) ACS and CCS instruments; and third, to examine whether organizational commitment (as measured by the ACS and CCS scales) moderates the relationship between role stressors and burnout.

The emotional exhaustion sub-scale of the MBI has an internal consistency of 0.88. Table 4 summarises average values of the sub-scale as reported in other studies. While the average values are similar, respondents in the current study sample report higher emotional exhaustion.

The results also indicate that organizational commitment is a multidimensional construct. Its dimensions – affective and continuance – are clearly distinguishable. The maximum likelihood factor analysis shows the distinction between the two commitment components. The ACS scale has good internal consistency and was unidimensional. In the CCS scale, two items showed loadings less than 0.50. These items are: “It wouldn’t be too costly for me to leave my organization in the near future”; and “I am not afraid of what might happen if I quit my job without having another one lined up”. Previous factor analysis by McGee and Ford (1987) also resulted in these items being dropped. The remaining six-item scale had an acceptable alpha value of 0.84. Overall, we find support for the conceptual distinction between ACS and CCS in the case of IS professionals.

There is also significant support for each of the study hypothesis. Role ambiguity and role conflict were expected to be positively related to burnout. Table 1 provides evidence for this hypothesis. As proposed, role ambiguity and role conflict were positively related (r = 0.44 and 0.53, respectively) to burnout.

In addition, we also examined the moderating influence of ACS and CCS on the relationship between stressors and burnout. As shown graphically in Figure 1, ACS was found to be a moderator variable in this relationship. Based on Tables 2 and 3, we conclude that the level of ACS affects how an employee responds to role stressors. Highly committed IS employees tend to show lower burnout due to role stress than do employees with lower affective commitment.

## Conclusion

IS literature has previously examined stress and burnout only to a limited extent. In this study, we use a burnout measurement scale to assess burnout in IS employees. This instrument – the Maslach Burnout Inventory – has been previously used with human services employees. We found the psychometric properties of the emotional exhaustion sub-scale of the MBI to be similar to those reported in previous studies.

The burnout construct originated because of research that examined stress responses in professionals involved in “people work” (Maslach & Jackson, 1981). Several different models of stress – the P–E model (French et al, 1974), the Beehr and Newman (1978) model, and McGrath’s (1976) model of stress effects – focus on people-oriented work. Based on this idea, it is possible that IS employees involved in user support and other activities that involve direct contact with users will demonstrate a higher level of burnout. Although this proposition was not the direct focus of this study, we divided the sample into two groups loosely labelled, ‘the enduser support group’ and ‘the development tasks group’. The mean values of the emotional exhaustion subscale were 26.3 and 24.7 for the two groups, confirming our preliminary proposal. Further examination of the MBI and its sub-scales is therefore highly recommended to explore these relationships.

In addition, we find support for the negative influences of role stressors – role ambiguity and role conflict. In previous studies by Igbaria and Greenhaus (1992), these stressors have been shown to increase turnover intentions. Employees who experienced high levels of role ambiguity and/or role conflict have been found to be dissatisfied with their jobs and careers, tended to be uncommitted, and to have strong intentions to leave their organizations. This study also finds a strong positive correlation between role stressors and burnout, indicating yet another reason for organizations to better manage information flow and communication in order to clarify individual roles within the organization.

In addition, this study provides evidence for the generalisability of the two-component model of organizational commitment in the IS context. Adequate support for two distinct components of commitment as theoretical constructs was found. This supports previous research by Meyer and Allen (1984) and suggests that both these components should be included in future IS research. The value of commitment to an organization is unquestionable. However, this study has found that this value may well depend on the nature of the commitment.

Finally, on the basis of this study, we find that organizational commitment can moderate stress reactions in employees. Past studies have mostly included organizational commitment as a mediator variable, for example, the causal models of turnover as proposed by Igbaria and Greenhaus (1992). This is true in the organizational behaviour literature as well (Mathieu & Zajac, 1990). For example, Podsakoff and Organ (1986) found that commitment mediated the influence of role ambiguity on alienation among professionals and non-professionals. Similarly, Ferris (1981) used commitment as a mediator variable to link employee personal characteristics to job performance.

However, in this study we found that organizational commitment moderated the impact of role stressors on burnout. It is not that low commitment employees show more burnout, but that the impact of role stressors on burnout is greater in low commitment employees. This is graphically shown in Figure 1. These results are similar to those found by Begley & Czajka (1993), who, in a longitudinal study of 102 clinical staff, showed that commitment can buffer the impact of stress variables on job satisfaction and turnover intentions.

It should be noted that the current study sample included a variety of IS professionals such as systems programmers, applications programmers, systems analysts, project leaders, and IS managers. This was done because burnout is a problem faced by all IS employees (Mcgee, 1996). In addition, one objective of this study was to examine the general relationships between role stressors, organizational commitment, and burnout in IS professionals. It is possible that the antecedents of these constructs for each group of IS employees may be different; i.e., antecedents of burnout for programmers may differ from those of systems analysts. However, the results of this study show that the general relationship between the three study constructs – role stress, ACS and CCS, and burnout – will be applicable to any IS group.

Finally, the above studies point to the need for an examination of commitment as a stress buffer similar to constructs such as social support (Beehr et al, 1995) and coping mechanisms (Parkes, 1990). Although we have noted two models that have been proposed to explain the moderating influence of commitment, we have found support for the positive impact model; i.e., that commitment shields employees from the negative impacts of stress.
