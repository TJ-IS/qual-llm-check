# Determinants of EDI adoption in the transportation industry

G Premkumar<sup>1</sup>, K Ramamurthy<sup>2</sup> and M Crum<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, IA 5001; <sup>2</sup>College of Business Administration, University of Wisconsin Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI 53201; and <sup>3</sup>College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, IA 5001, USA

This study examines the impact of various environmental, organizational, and technological factors on the adoption of innovation in the context of electronic data interchange (EDI). Based on research in information technology (IT) adoption, organizational studies and marketing a comprehensive research model is developed. The model identifies eleven variables, under three broad categories (environmental, organizational, and innovation characteristics), that could potentially influence the adoption of EDI. The research variables are operationalised using multi-item indicators and data collected from a large scale field survey of 950 firms in the trucking industry. A total of 181 firms responded to the survey with near equal number of adopters and non-adopters. Multivariate discriminant analysis is used to determine the impact of each of these variables on EDI adoption. The results of data analysis indicate that four factors are important to discriminate adopters from non-adopters of EDI in the transportation industry. They are: size of the firm, competitive pressure, customer support, and top management support.

## Introduction

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is significantly changing the way business is conducted between organizations and these changes are having a significant impact on most industries. A substantial amount of information flow occurs across various organizational entities – suppliers, customers, banks, and transportation carriers – for the completion of a business transaction in the supply chain. A variety of media is used for these communications including paper, face-to-face, telephone, fax, and electronic communication between computers. Since the information communication activity is a labour intensive process, a switch to electronic communication would significantly improve the efficiency of the process. EDI, an automated and structured form of transaction communication between trading partners, provides the technological solution to improve the efficiency of operations (McGee, 1987). The initial electronic links were established between customers and suppliers. However, the growth in just in time (JIT) and quick response systems has triggered the need to include other agencies, such as transportation carriers, in the EDI network to ensure the efficiency of the overall supply chain. In the transportation industry EDI can help to provide electronic bills of lading, delivery tracking, freight billing, electronic funds transfer, etc, resulting in reduced paper processing and immediate access to information. Firms are increasingly feeling the competitive pressure to use EDI in order to maintain the existing customer base. EDI is expected to result in improvements in operational efficiency, quick response, greater accuracy, less paperwork, and lower operational costs (Emmelhainz, 1988; Hansen & Hill, 1989; Johnson et al, 1992).

Although trade literature and vendors profess significant benefits from EDI and claim that many firms use it, in reality the adoption of EDI by carriers has been rather slow. A variety of factors may have influenced the slow adoption rate. EDI, unlike traditional information systems (IS), is an inter-organizational system (IOS) that requires the cooperation and commitment of all participating firms. These firms have complex economic and business relationships that influence the adoption of EDI. There is also a need for a critical mass among trading partners for EDI to provide significant benefits. Firms are motivated to adopt EDI only if a majority of partners in the distribution chain – suppliers, customers, transportation carriers, and banks – adopt it. This helps them to gain economies of scale and thereby derive the benefits from using EDI technology. Organizations may not obtain adequate benefits from EDI if they have to maintain both a paper-based system and an EDI system in parallel.

An analysis of the factors that influence the adoption of EDI will be useful in providing guidelines for evaluating EDI and in suggesting strategies to overcome the constraints that inhibit its adoption. A few empirical studies have examined the adoption of IOS (Reich & Benbasat, 1990; Grover, 1993) and adoption of EDI (O’Callaghan et al, 1992; Saunders & Clark, 1992; Iacovou et al, 1995). Most of these studies, except Grover (1993), have looked at a small subset of four to five variables. A few studies have examined EDI adoption in the transportation industry (Johnson et al, 1992;

Williams, 1994) but they did not attempt to develop a comprehensive model to study EDI adoption. This study attempts to build on their studies by developing a comprehensive model for EDI adoption that identifies three broad categories of variables, and examines their impact on EDI adoption.

## Background

EDI can be defined as the direct computer-to-computer communication between an organization and its trading partners of business documents and information in a machine-readable, structured format that permits data to be processed by the receiver without rekeying. This definition precludes other electronic communication such as fax and e-mail.

There are primarily two areas of research that provide the theoretical bases for this study: innovation adoption research in the context of information technology (IT) adoption and marketing/organizational behaviour research related to inter-organizational relationships. There are other research disciplines such as transaction cost economics, that are used in EDI research to evaluate the impact of EDI on market structure. However, most of the research in this area is related to post-implementation impacts of EDI and does not directly relate to the primary objectives of this study, i.e., factors influencing the adoption of EDI. Hence, we do not extensively review that literature in this paper. A brief review of the relevant research in the first two areas is presented below.

## Innovation adoption research

The innovation adoption literature provides a very strong theoretical base for this study. Innovation is an idea, product, programme or process that is new to the adopting unit (Zaltman et al, 1973). Rogers (1995) identified eight broad categories of research on the adoption and diffusion of innovation from studies in sociology, engineering, marketing, communication, and organizational behaviour. They are: earliness in knowing about adoption, rate of adoption and innovation attributes, innovativeness of members, opinion leadership, diffusion networks, rate of adoption in different social systems, communications channel use, and consequences of innovation. Studies on organizational innovation adoption have been an important subset of this broad stream of research (Damanpour, 1991). While there are similarities with research in other areas there are differences that are specific to organizational innovation.

Studies of innovation have focused on two different contexts, organizational and individual adoption (Fichman, 1992). The determinants of innovation adoption vary depending on the context. Organizational adoption decision is a group decision process where many other organizational factors play a role in the adoption decision. The adoption and subsequent diffusion of innovation are normally explained through a stage model. A number of stage models have been proposed by researchers (Kwon & Zmud, 1987; Cash et al, 1992; Rogers, 1995). Although there are variations in the naming of the stages among researchers, most agree that there is no clear demarcation between adjacent stages. Most organizational researchers agree that there are two important stages, adoption and diffusion (Rogers, 1995). In the adoption stage the firm collects information, builds knowledge of the innovation, examines its relevance and appropriateness to the organization, and makes a decision whether to adopt the innovation. In the diffusion stage the innovation is implemented, and its usage is diffused among users over a period of time. Typically, researchers focus on one or the other stage in their studies, primarily due to the long time duration for the adoption and diffusion process and difficulty in collecting longitudinal data. In this study we focus on the adoption stage and examine the factors that influence the adoption of an innovation in the context of EDI.

Kwon and Zmud (1987), based on prior research in innovation adoption (Baldridge & Burnham, 1975; Tornatzky & Klein, 1982; Rogers, 1995) and IT adoption (Zmud, 1984), identified five broad categories of variables – innovation, environmental, organizational, task, and individual characteristics – that could potentially influence adoption of an innovation. They highlight the possibility of different factors being relevant in different contexts and for different innovations. For example, individual factors, such as education and age, may not be relevant for organizational adoption of innovation where the adoption decision is a group decision process and often not decided by the actual users. In this study we are considering three broad categories of antecedent variables – innovation, organizational, and environmental variables. Individual characteristics are believed to be not directly relevant to this study as we are examining the organizational adoption of innovation. Since EDI affects a broad category of tasks in an organization, we do not include specific task-related variables. Also, the inconsistent findings on the influence of task variables such as task variety and autonomy discouraged us from considering this category of variables (Kwon & Zmud, 1987).

Researchers have examined the influence of various innovation characteristics on innovation adoption (Rogers, 1995), but the results have not been consistent. Only three variables – relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility – were found to be significantly related to adoption on a consistent basis (Tornatzky & Klein, 1982). In the context of IT adoption, organizational factors play an important role in the adoption decision (Kwon and Zmud, 1987). Research on IT adoption identifies a variety of organizational factors that are critical facilitators or inhibitors to innovation adoption. Early studies on the organizational adoption of innovation have identified many structural factors, such as formalisation, centralisation, integration and size that influence adoption (Hage & Aiken, 1967; Moch & Morse, 1977; Zmud, 1982; Ettlie, 1986). However, recent studies, particularly in IT adoption (Grover, 1993), have not found much support for these variables, except size. Organizations have become so complex that simplistic operationalisation of organizational structure variables is unable to capture adequately the organizational complexities and therefore does not explain adoption decisions. Recent studies of IT adoption have found support for IT-related factors that directly influence its adoption such as top management support, IT champion, IT infrastructure and resources, strategic IS planning, and strategic orientation of IT (Lucas, 1978; Sanders & Courtney, 1985; Beath, 1991; Fuller & Swanson, 1992; Grover, 1993).

## Organizational behaviour and marketing

There is an extensive body of research in organizational behaviour and marketing that examines the role of interorganizational relationships on organizational decisions. This research stream is based on the premise that organizations form inter-organizational linkages primarily to gain control over critical resources and thereby reduce uncertainty in their environment. The inter-organizational relationship is described in terms of the sociopolitical processes reflected by the transaction climate that exists in the relationship and the socio-political structure captured through the power-dependence relationships in the dyad (Reve & Stern, 1986). A favourable climate between two firms facilitates greater information flow, better inter-organizational coordination, and greater cooperation leading to better decision making (Williamson, 1975; Dwyer, 1980; Reve & Stern, 1986). Inter-organizational power-dependence, according to Thompson (1967): “an organization is dependent on some element of its task environment (1) in proportion to the organization’s need for resources or performances which that element can provide and (2) in inverse proportion to the ability of other elements to provide the same resource or performance”. Using similar notions, Pfeffer and Salancik (1978) postulated a resource dependency theory that defined dependence based on an organization’s ability to: (1) control resources needed by others; and (2) reduce their dependencies on others for resources. Frazier and Summers (1984) describe the influence of channel partners on inter-firm strategies and how the initiator seeks the operational and marketing benefits available through the partner’s adoption of innovation. Net-dependence has been used to measure power-dependence in many studies (Pfeffer & Leong, 1977; Provan et al, 1980; Pfeffer, 1982; Provan & Skinner, 1989).

Stories in the trade press and empirical research suggest that socio-political forces play an important role in

IOS adoption (Nidumolu, 1989; Hart & Estrin, 1991; Pitts, 1991; Saunders & Clark, 1992; Iacovou et al, 1995; Reekers & Smithson, 1995). EDI implementation, historically, has followed a ‘hub’ and ‘spoke’ arrangement, where one firm (hub) initiates the implementation with its trading partners who form the spokes of the electronic linkages (Barber, 1991; Hwang et al, 1991; Pitts, 1991). The initiating firm may exploit either its powerdependence relationships with its trading partners or the conducive climate with its partners to establish electronic linkages with them (Vitale, 1986; Nidumolu, 1989; Bowersox, 1990; Pitts, 1991). In the early stages many hub firms provided technical support to induce their trading partners to adopt EDI and lock them in to a firm specific proprietary format. As the industry matured in the use of the technology, it evolved into a meshed network structure where each firm linked with multiple trading partners and communicated with standard protocols (e.g., ANSI X.12). Competitive pressures force many companies to adopt the technology or lose business if they are not part of the network (Robertson & Gatignon, 1986; Reich & Benbasat, 1990; Iacovou et al, 1995). Johnson et al (1992), in the first large scale study of firms in the transportation industry, found that competitive pressures were the most often mentioned reason for adopting EDI. These findings were reinforced by a later study (Williams, 1994).

## Research model and hypotheses

The research model in Figure 1 identifies the variables in the three broad categories – environmental, organizational, and innovation characteristics – and examines the nature of their influence on EDI adoption. A brief description of the model and the research variables, and justifications for the research hypotheses are provided below.

## Environmental characteristics

Since the adoption of EDI is a joint decision between two trading partners, environmental factors significantly influence EDI adoption. Various economic, social and political forces influence the inter-organizational relationships between the two partners and thereby the adoption decision (Hart & Estrin, 1991). Some of the environmental variables considered in EDI research have been market structure (Holland & Lockett, 1994; Klein, 1996), environmental complexity, uncertainty and dynamism (Reekers & Smithson, 1994; Holland & Lockett, 1994; Benaou & Venkatraman, 1996), competitive pressure (Iacovou et al, 1995), characteristics of interorganizational relationships such as power, dependence, trust, and climate (Clemons & Row, 1993; Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995; Benaou & Venkatraman, 1996; Reekers & Smithson, 1996; Cavaye, 1996). These variables have been drawn from different theoretical foundations in economics, strategy, marketing, organizational behaviour and IS, and have been used in different contexts to study different phenomena (adoption, diffusion, post-implementation impacts etc). We chose four variables to describe the environmental characteristics: competitive pressure, dependence, climate, and customer support. Our decision was based on a few considerations: the study focused on factors influencing EDI adoption, the development of the model was based on research in innovation adoption and marketing/ organizational behaviour, and the study was specific to one industry. Dependence and climate were found to be important in prior studies on inter-organizational relationships (Stern & Reve, 1980, Reve & Stern, 1986; Nidumolu, 1989; Hart & Estrin, 1991; Reekers & Smithson, 1994, 1996) while competitive pressure and customer support were found to be important in studies of adoption in marketing (Robertson & Gatignon, 1986) and in EDI (Pitts, 1991; Riggins & Mukhopadyay, 1994; Reekers & Smithson, 1994, 1996; Iacovou et al, 1995). A brief description of the impact of these variables on EDI adoption is described below.

Robertson and Gatignon (1986) identified competitive intensity as an important variable to influence adoption. Reasonable levels of competitiveness allow for some slack resources to try out the innovation and encourage the need to adopt new innovations to maintain the competitive advantage. While excessive competition may not provide firms adequate slack resources to try new innovations, in monopolistic situations innovativeness is stifled due to lack of incentives for change (Swan, 1970). In a competitive industry there is a constant need for firms to evaluate advances in technology and adopt them to gain competitive advantage or out of strategic necessity. In the context of EDI, many researchers have identified competitive pressure as an important determinant of EDI adoption and implementation (Reekers & Smithson, 1994; Iacovou et al, 1995; Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1996). In establishing an EDI link between two firms, typically, one firm initiates the action and uses either a coercive or support strategy to make the other firm adopt EDI (Hwang et al, 1991; Riggins & Mukhopadyay, 1994; Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995; Iacovou et al, 1995). While the benefits of EDI are apparent to the initiator, the partner firm may not be aware of the technology and its benefits, or derive the same level of benefits from the technology. Empirical studies have found that the initiator obtains more benefits from EDI than the follower (Riggins & Mukhopadyay, 1994). Reekers and Smithson (1994) claim that the gains are not equally distributed among manufacturers and suppliers in the automotive industry. However, many firms may have to adopt EDI out of competitive pressure or from fear of losing business. Many firms in the trucking industry had to adopt EDI due to competitive pressure (Johnson et al, 1992; Williams, 1994). Hence we propose that:

Hypothesis 1: Adopters will experience greater competitive pressure to adopt EDI than non-adopters.

It is well documented that large retail chains in the USA, such as Walmart, Sears and K-mart, have threatened to stop doing business with suppliers that do not have the necessary EDI link for transmission of purchase orders. Similarly, automobile manufacturers have insisted that their suppliers use EDI or lose business (Webster, 1995; Mukhopadyay et al, 1995; Reekers & Smithson, 1996). While the initiator can require partners who are very dependent on their business to adopt EDI it may not have the same force with partners that are not dependent. Williams (1994) found that the inter-firm dependence and the power of the initiating firm is an important determinant of EDI adoption in the trucking industry. Hence:

Hypothesis 2: Adopters will have greater net-dependence on their trading partner than non-adopters.

While this is the typical scenario in a buyer-initiated EDI operation with an uneven structural relationship, there have also been instances where firms have established electronic partnerships based on a long-standing conducive transaction climate to jointly derive the benefits of electronic integration (Bensaou, 1993; Reekers & Smithson, 1994; Holland, 1995; Meier, 1995; Fredrickson & Vilgon, 1996). Bensaou and Venkatraman (1996) highlight the importance of the fit between the trust in the partnership and the socio-political climate and the impact of fit on the performance of the inter-organizational system. Classic studies of IOS, primarily in supplier-initiated IOS such as American Hospital Supply (Vitale, 1986) and McKesson Drug Company (Clemons & Row, 1988), found EDI adoption to be based on the good relationship with their customers rather than power-dependence relationships. The benefits from electronic integration helped these firms gain a competitive advantage (Cash & Konsynski, 1985; Sokol, 1989; Venkatraman & Zaheer, 1990; Bowersox, 1990). Although a powerful trading partner may request an EDI link, the firm may agree or disagree based on a variety of criteria, including the transaction climate that exists between the two firms. Electronic business partnerships require a conducive transaction climate and a high level of trust between the participating firms since many of the normal manual controls may be absent in computerto-computer communication. Trust among the partners reduces the need for extensive control safeguards and paper trails that are normally absent in EDI linkages. For example, firms should reach a level of trust where the supplier is comfortable in automatically using a purchase order initiated from a customer’s computer as an input for its production planning without any manual intervention and with no formal paper document or voice confirmation of the order. The partners should also fee confident that they will be able to resolve amicably any problems that may arise from any errors. In advanced forms of EDI, access to firms’ data records, for instance inventory or shipment records, may be provided to the trading partner. This would require a firm to trust that its partners’ access to the company’s data will not be misused. When there is a balanced power relationship between two trading firms, trust becomes an important factor in establishing an IOS link. Felkner (1992) described the trust established in the transportation industry between Yellow Freight and Westinghouse Corporation for automatic payment without a paper-based invoice. Bowersox (1990) described the transaction climate that led to the formation of electronic business partnerships between 3M and Schneider National.

Hypothesis 3: Adopters will experience a better transaction climate with their trading partner than non-adopters

Incentives provided by suppliers to encourage adoption of innovation has been found to be positively associated with adoption of innovation (Gatignon & Robertson, 1989). The initiator can provide incentives and technical support to promote and facilitate the adoption of innovation (Saunders & Clark, 1992; Iacovou et al, 1995). Firms that would like to expand their EDI links quickly have to provide support for trading partners who may not have the expertise and assist them with implementation. It is in the interest of the initiating firm to provide support, as it gains significantly if the partners are able to quickly reach the level of expertise required. Firms sometimes have to market the concept of electronic integration to reluctant partners with very little knowledge of the technology and its benefits (Cavaye, 1996). There is also peer support to facilitate the adoption of the technology. Trade associations such as the American Trucking Association (ATA) present seminars and provide information booklets to encourage the adoption of EDI (ATA, 1994) leading to a larger critical mass that facilitates adoption of telecommunication-based technologies such as EDI (Rogers, 1995).

Hypothesis 4: Adopters will have greater customer support for EDI from their trading partners than non-adopters.

## Organizational characteristics

The importance of top management support and commitment for successful adoption of innovation is well documented in empirical studies (Baldridge & Burnham, 1975; Ettlie, 1986). Top management recognises strategic opportunities and provides long-term vision, attributes that are critical for successful adoption of an innovation (Quinn, 1985). Top management support has been consistently found to be important for successful IS implementation (Lucas, 1978; Sanders & Courtney, 1985; Guimaraes et al, 1992). Top management support has been found to be even more critical in the context of EDI (Premkumar et al, 1994; Raymond & Bergeron, 1996), because unlike traditional internally-oriented IS, EDI is an inter-organizational system requiring the cooperation of trading partners with widely varying business interests. It is necessary to develop an electronic network with various trading partners to create a critical mass to facilitate adoption of network inter-dependent innovations (Rogers, 1995). The American Trucking Association’s Management Systems Council identifies senior management commitment as one of the key factors for successful adoption and implementation of EDI (ATA, 1994). Ferguson and Hill (1988) in their survey of EDI users found top management commitment to be a critical factor in facilitating EDI adoption.

Hypothesis 5: Adopters will have greater top management support for EDI than non-adopters

Researchers studying innovation have found championing the innovation is critical in overcoming resistance (Kimberly & Evanisko, 1981; Rogers, 1995).

Champions are described as highly enthusiastic persons, who are committed to the innovation. They promote the innovation within the organization, create an awareness and favourable impression of the innovation among the users, and ensure adequate resources for its implementation. Champions become more important when it is necessary to convince trading partners, who are external to the organization. Rogers (1995) found this factor to be so important as to classify it as a separate category. In telecommunications-related implementation, Runge (1985) found project champions were critical for successful implementation. Empirical studies on interorganizational systems have also found champions to be a significant variable to explain successful adoption and implementation (Reich & Benbasat, 1990; Grover, 1993). Williams (1994) found product champions to be an important factor to facilitate EDI adoption in the transportation industry.

Hypothesis 6: Adopters will have more support from a product champion than non-adopters.

Researchers have found organizational size to be an important variable in adoption (Lehman, 1985; Runge, 1985; Ettlie, 1986; Delone, 1988; Grover, 1993). Larger organizations have a greater ability to mobilise the resources required for adopting innovations. However, smaller organizations are found to be more creative and open to new innovative ideas that can be stifled in large organizations. Empirical research suggests that lack of technical knowledge and resources hinder IT use in small firms (Cragg & King, 1993), and therefore smaller firms are less likely to adopt EDI (Saunders & Clark, 1992; Bouchard, 1993; Reekers & Smithson, 1996). Iacovou et al (1995) also found that EDI adoption exists more in the larger rather than the smaller firms. Since EDI requires a sizeable level of investment, larger firms may adopt it more easily than smaller organizations.

Hypothesis 7: Adopters will be larger in size compared to non-adopters.

## Innovation characteristics

Studies examining the influence of a variety of innovation characteristics on the adoption of innovation have been very popular in this research stream (Rogers, 1995). Unfortunately, the results of these studies have been inconsistent and no clear pattern has emerged. Tornatzky and Klein (1982) conducted a meta-analysis of research in the area and found that of the 25 innovation variables studied by researchers only three were consistently related to adoption. They are: relative advantage, compatibility and complexity. While most studies have examined the impact of these variables on individual adoption based on the individual’s own initiative, its influence on the organizational adoption decision is not well studied. A few studies in IOS and EDI adoption (Grover, 1993; Premkumar et al, 1994) have examined these variables in the context of organizational adoption and found some support for these variables. Recent studies on IOS and EDI provide evidence to suggest that cost is also an important variable (Ferguson & Hill, 1989; Saunders & Clark, 1992; Mukhopadyay et al, 1995). In order to explore further the influence of these variables in the context of organizational adoption of EDI, we decided to include them in the research model.

Relative advantage is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the product/process it supersedes. In an organizational adoption decision the perception of positive benefits of innovation provides the economic and political legitimacy to the adoption decision. A perception of relative advantage for the innovation over existing or alternate products/processes has been found to be positively related to adoption (Tornatzky & Klein, 1982; Rogers, 1995). Ettlie and Vellenga (1979) found it to be an important variable in explaining adoption of transportation related innovations. EDI offers several benefits to organizations (Banerjee & Goldhar, 1994; Mukhopadhyay, 1993; Swatman & Swatman, 1991). Traditional transaction cost theory (Williamson, 1975; Malone et al, 1987) also indicates that EDI reduces transaction costs and should therefore favour adoption by both parties. Although Saunders and Clark (1992) did not find relative advantage to be an important variable to explain EDI adoption, several other researchers have found empirical support for this variable (O’Callaghan, 1992; Grover, 1993; Premkumar et al, 1994; Iacovou et al, 1995).

Hypothesis 8: Adopters will perceive greater relative advantage of EDI than non-adopters.

Compatibility is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being consistent with existing values, past experiences, and the needs of potential adopters. Any new innovation brings changes to existing work practices, and most adopters resist change. Adoption of EDI could lead to significant changes in work procedures as it replaces many of the manual paper-based work procedures with electronic means, and may involve reengineering of the processes of entire departments and possible job loss/relocation (Ferguson & Hill, 1989; Swatman et al, 1993; Clark & Stoddard, 1996). The more the innovation is perceived to be consistent with existing procedures, beliefs, and value systems, the greater the chances are for its adoption (Rogers, 1995). Prior empirical studies have found significant support for a positive relationship between compatibility and innovation adoption (Ettlie & Vellenga, 1979; Kimberly and Evanisko, 1981; Tornatzky & Klein, 1982; Cooper & Zmud, 1990). Studies of EDI/IOS have also found some support for the variable (Premkumar et al, 1994; O’Callaghan et al, 1992; Grover, 1993; Cox & Ghoneim, 1996).

Hypothesis 9: Adopters will perceive EDI technology to be more compatible compared to non-adopters.

Complexity, determined by the degree to which an innovation is perceived to be relatively difficult to understand and use, has been found to be an important determinant of innovation adoption (Rogers, 1995). If adequate technical skills to use the innovation are lacking in the organization, then the organization will perceive the technology to be too complex and therefore may not adopt it. Studies on innovation have found this to be an important variable (Tornatzky & Klein, 1982; Cooper & Zmud, 1990), but the results have been mixed in the context of EDI/IOS (Grover, 1993; Premkumar et al, 1994). The relatively small size of firms in the transportation industry may make complexity a critical variable, since smaller firms are associated with lower levels of technical know-how (Cragg & King, 1993) and therefore could perceive EDI technology to be complex, thereby inhibiting its adoption.

Hypothesis 10: Adopters will perceive EDI to be less complex than non-adopters.

Cost has been found to be a significant inhibitor to EDI adoption (Saunders & Clark, 1992; Bouchard, 1993; Cavaye, 1996; Cox and Ghoneim, 1996). The cost to install the necessary hardware/software infrastructure and establish electronic linkage with trading partners can be substantial. Additionally, there are significant costs associated with training and re-engineering the work practices to ensure successful implementation (Barber, 1992; Clark & Stoddard, 1996). The cost of integrating the EDI systems with internal IS can also cause concern and inhibit EDI adoption. A survey of potential adopters (IS managers) found cost to be a major inhibiting factor to EDI adoption (Ferguson & Hill, 1989). This could be a significant variable in the transportation industry as firms are traditionally small, and the cost of adoption is a major factor in the decision making process (Cragg & King, 1993).

Hypothesis 11: Adopters will perceive EDI to provide more benefits compared to costs than non-adopters.

## Research methodology

A field survey approach was considered appropriate for data collection to ensure greater external validity and generalisability of the results. A survey instrument was developed that measured the various constructs. Wherever possible, multiple items were used for measuring the constructs and prior operationalisations used in other empirical studies were adapted to suit this research context. Most of the items were measured using a sevenpoint Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The innovation attributes of complexity, compatibility, cost, and relative advantage were measured using items that were adapted from earlier studies on innovation adoption (Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Grover, 1993) and modified to suit the EDI context (Premkumar et al, 1994). The two organizational variables, top management support and champion, were measured using three indicator items and a single item, respectively. Organizational size was measured as the natural logarithm of the sales revenue. Based on guidelines provided by Reve and Stern (1986), five items were used to assess the transaction climate. Net dependence was measured, using items suggested by Stern and El-Ansary (1982), as the difference between the respondent’s dependence on its trading partner and the trading partner’s dependence on the respondent. A single dichotomous item was used to determine if the respondent had adopted EDI.

The detailed questionnaire measuring these variables was pilot tested with four transportation companies in the trucking industry. During pilot testing we realized that a single questionnaire may not be appropriate for both adopters and non-adopters as the wording of some items and the tense were creating problems for nonadopters. Hence, we created two versions with suitable modification of wording and tense to make them appropriate for each group. We posted the survey to the chief executive of these companies and asked them to complete the first part and then forward it to the person in the firm who is most knowledgeable about EDI implementation. We expected that using a key-informant who was responsible for EDI adoption would ensure accurate and high quality response.

The data for the study were collected from a large scale field survey of 950 Class I & II motor-transport firms in the USA (i.e. firms with at least \$1 million annual sales revenue). The names and addresses were obtained from a national directory of transport firms. Three weeks after the initial survey was posted, a reminder card was sent to the respondents. A total of 181 responses were received for a response rate of 19%.

This becomes necessary if shippers have intermodal shipments or when carriers use other carriers for some of their shipments to gain logistical efficiencies. The average initial investment for EDI is around \$15 000. There is a wide variation in investment with a maximum of \$150 000. While some firms have a simple PC program to upload and download EDI data without any integration with their internal systems, other firms have very sophisticated EDI systems that are totally integrated with all their internal operations and support a wide variety of transaction sets. Also, firms that use third-party vendors for their EDI operations do not have as large an investment since the vendor makes the investment, but would incur greater operations cost as it would reflect the investment made by the vendor.

## Measurement properties

The measurement instrument was tested for various validity and reliability properties (Churchill, 1979). Validity assesses the degree to which an instrument accurately measures the constructs under investigation, and reliability measures the stability of the scale. Construct validity is normally evaluated using three forms of validity: content, convergent, and discriminant validity. Content validity assesses if all the dimensions of the construct are being measured. It is established through the process used in identifying the items and refining them through the process of pre-testing and pilot testing. In our study we used items that were validated in earlier studies and also refined some constructs through pilot testing to make it specific to this industry. The elaborate process of identifying and refining the items provides adequate confidence in the content validity of the measurements.

Convergent validity assesses the extent to which indicator items measuring a construct converge together and measure a single construct and is also referred to as unidimensionality (Gerbing & Anderson, 1988). Discriminant validity is the degree to which items of theoretically distinct concepts are unique from each other (Churchill, 1979) and is normally established through factor analysis (Nunnally, 1978). A factor analysis of all the multiindicator items of all the constructs reveals if the items load on the predetermined theoretically defined constructs and if there is any cross-loading of items on other constructs. It also helps to determine if theoretically distinct concepts are indeed separate variables in a measurement context.

Principal component factor analysis was used for testing convergent and discriminant validity. It tests if the theorised items converge together on appropriate constructs and discriminate across multiple constructs with minimal cross-loading among factors. The standard criteria of eigen values greater than 1.0, factor loadings greater than 0.3, and a well explained factor structure were used in the analysis (Zeller & Carmines, 1980). The results are shown in Table 2.

The results reveal that all the variables, except complexity and cost, exhibit adequate validity. The three items measuring cost and the two items measuring complexity loaded on a single factor. To determine if they are separate constructs we examined both the theoretical conceptualisation as well as the empirical validation. Past research has clearly differentiated between these two constructs and our theoretical conceptualisation has followed those definitions. On the empirical side, the operationalisation of the construct is based on prior studies that have established the convergent and discriminant validity of these two constructs (Premkumar et al, 1994). Since the primary objective of factor analysis is to identify the minimum number of factors, there is a tendency for constructs with few items (two or three) to load on other factors in a factor analysis of a large number of items (33 items). To check if they are distinct variables, we ran a separate factor analysis of the items measuring just those two variables. Also, another factor analysis of the items measuring all the variables in the innovation characteristics category of the research model, to which these two belong, was performed to verify that they are independent constructs. The results of both analyses revealed that they loaded on two separate factors confirming that they are two independent constructs. These results give us confidence to consider cost and complexity as two separate variables. All the items exhibited adequate discriminant validity since no significant crossloading of items among factors was noticed.

## Results

Discriminant analysis was found to be the most appropriate statistical technique to test the research model since the dependent variable is a binary variable and the independent variables are continuous. Discriminant analysis develops a discriminant function of independent variables that best discriminates the two groups formed by the dependent variable. The selection criteria of minimising Wilk’s Lambda and a tolerance level of 0.001 was used to generate the discriminant function to discriminate between adopters and non-adopters. The results of the analysis are presented in Table 3. The values of Wilk’s Lambda, Chi-Square value, and significance are provided.

The results indicate that the model is significant at P , 0.001. While the discriminant coefficient specifies the variables used in the discriminant function, discriminant loading provides the simple linear correlation between each predictor variable and the extracted discriminant function. Variables with discriminant loadings above 0.3 are normally considered as significant variables. Four variables – competitive pressure, top management support, organizational size, and customer support – were found to be significant variables to discriminate adopters from non-adopters. The mean, standard deviation, and univariate F statistics testing for equality of group means are also provided for each variable for the two groups. It can be clearly seen that the differences between the two groups are significant for these four variables.

Another test in discriminant analysis is to examine the classificatory ability of the discriminant function, i.e., the ability to classify the cases using the discriminant function into the correct group. The classification results are provided in Table 3. The overall classificatory ability is quite high (81.88%), but it should be noted that the values could be slightly inflated due to classifying the same sample on which the discriminant function was developed. It is necessary to compare the classificatory ability of this model with a chance model (Hair et al, 1992). The chance accuracy is determined by the formula $p ^ { 2 } + ( 1 - p ) ^ { 2 } .$ , where $\cdot _ { p } ,$ is the proportion of the sample in the first group. If group sizes were equal, ‘p’ would be 0.5 and the classificatory ability would be 50%. In our case $\cdot _ { p } ,$ was 0.487 and therefore accuracy of the chance model would be 50%, which is much less than our discriminant model (81.88%). A t-test on the classification accuracy of the two models found the accuracy to be significantly different for the two models (t = 8.42, $P < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ .

## Discussion and implications

The findings of the study are discussed under three separate categories: environmental, organizational, and technology variables.

The results clearly indicate that external environmental forces were predominantly instrumental in motivating firms to adopt EDI. Business competitive pressure, driven by customers’ demands and competitors’ actions, seems to be the primary factor to influence many firms to adopt EDI. Most of the firms have initiated EDI in a reactive mode based on external customer requirements rather than on a proactive mode to gain an edge in the market. This is understandable in the trucking industry where, typically, the carrier firm is much smaller than the shipper and often depends on a few large shippers for a large proportion of its business. These results indicate that non-adopters have not faced adequate competitive pressures to adopt EDI. Perhaps, their customers/shippers have yet to adopt EDI and are therefore not pressurising them. In an open-ended survey question on the factors that would influence the respondent to adopt EDI, the non-adopters suggested customer pressure as a primary factor that would influence their EDI adoption decision. This suggests that some carriers are subject to greater competitive pressure than others. As reported in earlier studies, some industries such as the automotive industry have been more aggressive in implementing EDI and used competitive pressure as the primary strategy for inducing their partners to adopt EDI (Webster, 1995). Hence, carriers servicing those industries are more likely to feel the competitive pressure to adopt EDI compared to carriers in other industries. Unfortunately, we did not collect information on the customers of the respondent to determine if this proposition would be supported.

The next most important factor is customer support for adopting the technology. Small firms are often intimidated by new technology and look for external support to implement it. The trucking industry is characterised by a large number of relatively small firms with limited IS expertise. Shippers have two strategies to make their carriers adopt EDI: a coercive strategy of threatening to reduce business if they do not adopt EDI, or a support strategy of helping the carriers adopt EDI by providing training programmes, software, and other incentives. While competitive pressures reflects the coercive strategy, customer support reflect the support strategy. It seems that shippers adopt both strategies to induce their carriers to adopt EDI. In the initial stages many firms had proprietary formats and therefore required extensive customer support and training to enable their trading partners to adopt EDI. Big companies in the retail industry, such as JCPenney and Sears, had their own proprietary EDI format and therefore had to train their partners. Also, many of the early initiators were in the ‘bleeding edge’ of the technology and also had to provide training and support for their partners, as external vendor support was minimal. In recent years, the growth of third-party vendors with more comprehensive support and the development of standard protocols (ANSI X.12) have to some extent reduced the need for extensive customer support. The industry association (American Trucking Association) and standards body Transportation Data Coordination Council (TDCC) have also provided support in terms of seminars, guidelines, and books to help firms adopt EDI (ATA, 1994). The results are consistent with many of the studies in the marketing literature related to interaction among manufacturers, wholesalers and retailers. The manufacturing firms engage in both support and coercive strategies to meet their business objectives; they use support strategies in terms of vertical interactions in the area of joint advertising, promotion, product development, market research and so on to increase sales; they use coercive strategies in terms of delivery schedules, price breaks, supply volumes etc, to achieve their business objectives. Iacovou et al (1995) suggest the use of both strategies in differing proportions depending on the firm the initiator is dealing with.

The other two environmental factors, climate and netdependence, were not found to be significant. Although adopters perceived a more conducive climate than nonadopters the difference was not significant. It is not clear why the net-dependence variable was not significant, especially since a majority of the respondents adopted EDI in response to a partner’s demands and normally carrier’s dependence on the shipper is viewed as higher than the shipper’s dependence on the carrier. A possible explanation is that competitive pressure overrides all other factors and therefore the primary reason for not adopting EDI is lack of competitive pressure. Hence, even though the non-adopters may be as dependent on their shippers as adopters, there is no competitive pressure from their shippers or competitors to adopt EDI. Another explanation for the lack of significance of the two variables could be that the carriers feel that the shippers are as dependent on them as they are dependent on the shippers, and that perception may have also contributed to lack of significance.

Among the organizational factors, top management support and organizational size were found to be important. This result is consistent with most studies of the organizational adoption of IT. Different firms react to similar environmental forces in different ways. While some firms sieze the opportunity and try to leverage it for competitive advantage, others languish and are passed over by their competitors. Top management’s futuristic vision plays a critical role in determining a firm’s response. Implementing EDI requires a significant investment and is a strategic decision for many companies since it requires interaction with their trading partners at the highest level. If top management’s commitment is lacking, it would be difficult to obtain adequate resources for adopting the technology.

Organizational size was also found to be an important factor in EDI adoption. Bigger trucking firms seem more likely to adopt EDI. The resources required to implement EDI may favour bigger firms over smaller ones. Another reason could be that bigger shippers (who are most probably early EDI adopters) are more likely to have business relationships with bigger carriers, and therefore these big carriers are subject to greater competitive pressures to implement EDI. Studies of IT adoption have found that smaller firms are slower in the adoption of new technologies. Larger trucking firms, such as Schneider and Yellow Freight, have made significant investments in IT to improve their efficiency and customer service and thereby maintain their market share.

It is surprising that none of the innovation attributes were found to be important in our study. This result, on the surface, seems to be inconsistent with the traditional innovation adoption literature. However, a more detailed analysis reveals an important difference between traditional innovation adoption literature and EDI adoption. In traditional adoption studies the emphasis is on the innovativeness of the adopters where the decision to adopt is based on the adopter’s own initiative, while in EDI adoption is influenced more by external competitive pressure that mandates the use of the innovation. In such a context, the influence of innovation attributes takes a secondary role in the larger context of business survival. Hence, environmental factors take precedence over organizational and innovation factors. However, it should be noted that relative advantage was significantly higher for adopters than non-adopters. This is consistent with the findings of other research which observed that external competitive pressure is the predominant factor to influence EDI adoption and its influence suppresses the impact of other factors. More than 70% of respondents in a recent survey have indicated that they adopted EDI due to competitive pressure (Iacovou et al, 1995).

Product champions, normally an important organizational factor to influence innovation adoption, was not found to be significant in this study. Perhaps, this could also be attributed to the environmental forces exerting overwhelming influence on the adoption of the EDI technology. Product champions have traditionally been found to be important for organizations that proactively adopt new innovations, where the champion’s role in creating an awareness of the innovation becomes critical. For an adopter reacting to external pressures, the firm has a clear choice to either adopt the innovation or lose business. In such a scenario the role of the champion is less significant.

In summary, the results of the data analysis indicate that four factors are important in discriminating adopters from non-adopters. They are: competitive pressure, customer support, organizational size, and top management support.

## Implications for practitioners

The implications of the findings are discussed below for two categories of firms: those initiating EDI with their partners, and non-adopters of EDI.

If a firm is planning to initiate EDI, the study findings suggest important strategies for successful implementation. An EDI network includes both large and small trading partners and it is advantageous to link with many of the firm’s trading partners in the EDI network to get maximum benefits from the technology. Since the study found that smaller firms are more likely to be non-adopters it is worthwhile to focus more efforts on these firms. While the larger trading partners may already have the technology, the smaller partners need more hand holding, as they are less likely to be aware of the technology or its benefits. Firms have to use both support and coercive strategies to ensure successful EDI adoption as both customer support and competitive pressure were found to be critical factors.

The initiator could use a variety of support strategies including marketing the technology and its benefits, assistance in the assessment of their needs, formulation of implementation strategies, training and education on EDI, technical assistance in implementation, and financial support for acquiring the technology. As a first step in the support strategy the initiator needs to market the technology and its benefits to its partners to enable them to appreciate the need for the technology. This step helps the non-adopter reach the ‘awareness’ stage in the adoption/diffusion life cycle. Then, the initiator has to follow up with additional support strategies to graduate the non-adopter from ‘awareness’ stage to ‘initiation and then to the ‘adoption’ stage. In the ‘initiation’ stage they could share their EDI expertise with their trading partners. They need to create a desire in the trading partner for EDI and help them perceive the relative advantage of the technology. They should try to convince the partner’s top management of the benefits of EDI, since the study found that their commitment to EDI is critica to the successful adoption of the technology. Then the initiator could help in the assessment of the partner’s internal needs in terms of hardware, software, and organizational processes to support EDI adoption and subsequent diffusion. Many initiators find that a major roadblock to expanding transaction sets and extensively using EDI is due to a partner’s failure to integrate EDI in their internal operations. Many small firms use EDI as a stand-alone message receipt and delivery system without any integration with their internal system. Very soon they get disenchanted with the technology as it does not provide adequate benefits and perhaps even increases their workload. Hence, it is to the advantage of the initiator to assist in the assessment of a partner’s requirements and formulate a strategy for adoption and diffusion to ensure that its own plans for expanding EDI usage does not run into roadblocks with its trading partners. Providing education and training is perhaps the most effective support strategy since it generates interna awareness, essential for continued EDI diffusion. Another strategy is to provide the necessary financial and technical resources to ‘jump-start’ the implementation process. However, it could be a costly strategy if there are many trading partners.

Firms also need to use coercive strategies in certain situations. For trading partners who are reluctant to adopt EDI, strategies such as creation of competitive pressure and threat of loss of business could be used to nudge them to comply. It is disadvantageous for the initiating firm to have both manual and EDI systems operating in parallel and therefore it should use all available strategies to convert their trading partners to EDI. Many firms have reduced their number of trading partners to ensure that all of them are EDI capable. A variety of coercive strategies in terms of reduction in the business volume, price and delivery incentives, additional information availability etc, have been used to induce partners to adopt EDI. However, the impact of these strategies on long-term business relationships have to be evaluated carefully.

The study findings also provide insights for nonadopters. EDI is fast becoming a defacto technology for inter-organizational communication and carriers that have not adopted EDI may be losing their competitive advantage. Although currently their customers may not want EDI, in order to stay competitive in the marketplace it may be necessary for firms to adopt it. Also, it is likely that very soon most shippers would use EDI. Hence, it would be useful for non-adopters to explore the EDI plans of their shippers to avoid any surprises. This would give them sufficient lead time to understand and learn how to use the technology. Another important factor is top management’s vision and support. In small firms the senior management may not be well informed on new technologies and therefore risk becoming late adopters and losing some of their competitive edge. Senior management in smaller firms are often so engrossed in daily operations and putting out fires that they may not recognise the need or find time to evaluate new technology alternatives. However, they need to consciously try to allocate time to evaluate these new technologies and develop a vision on the use of IT in their operations, in order to remain competitive. Many studies of IT adoption have found top management support to be an important factor. Industry associations and interest groups that have a vested interest in the adoption of these technologies could play an active role and inform this target group of the benefits of the innovation and provide assistance for implementation.

## Implications for researchers

The study has many useful findings that provide directions for future research. An interesting outcome of this study was the importance of two environment variables in explaining the adoption of EDI. Neither of these variables are included in a recent model of innovation adoption (Rogers, 1995). An important variable in the Roger’s model that may explain the reason for this anomaly is the type of innovation decision – optional, collective, and authority. Most studies of innovation and innovativeness of adopters are based on the voluntary adoption of innovation. However, when environmental forces such as competitive pressures force a firm to adopt an innovation, it is not purely optional. It seems that the basic model needs to be expanded to include the effect of environmental forces on the adoption decision, particularly in the context of inter-organizational systems where these forces play a very significant role.

EDI, like other communication technologies, requires the creation of a critical mass (Rogers, 1995) for the adoption to rapidly take off in the population. The innovation is useful only if the adopters use it to interact among themselves. As the number of adopters increases there are greater opportunities to interact, which creates the incentives for the non-adopters to use the new innovation. The applicability of the traditional ‘S’ curve in this context, where inter-dependency is a major factor influencing adoption, has to be examined.

We realised, a little too late in the data analysis, that we also need to identify the industry serviced by the respondent. While, initially, we assumed that all our respondents are in the trucking industry and therefore should have similar environmental forces acting on them, we realised that they operate in different industries and the characteristics at the customers’ end plays a role in EDI adoption. Since different industries are at different levels of EDI use, firms servicing different industries experience widely varying levels of competitive pressure to adopt EDI.

The research model we developed identifies a few important variables. There are other variables, identified by other studies, that could be included in the model. Future research could include these variables and develop a more comprehensive model. However, it should be noted that parsimony should not be sacrificed for comprehensiveness. Also, future research could test the current model in other industries since the data for this study were collected only from the trucking industry.

As EDI becomes more prevalent it would be interesting to examine the extent to which EDI is used in organizations and the benefits derived from using it. Are there differences in EDI use by firms that adopt EDI based on partner’s demand than those firms that adopt it on their own initiative? What are the barriers to expanding the use of the technology?

## Conclusion

This study examined the impact of various environmental, organizational, and innovation factors on the adoption of innovation in the context of EDI. Based on research in IT innovation adoption and organizational theory/marketing a research model was developed. The model was empirically validated using data from a large scale field survey of 950 firms in the trucking industry. A total of 181 firms responded with near equal numbers of adopters and non-adopters. Data analysis of their responses indicates that four factors are important in discriminating adopters from non-adopters. They are: competitive pressure, customer support, organizational size, and top management support. The results indicate that in the context of EDI, external environmental factors have a greater influence on EDI adoption compared to innovation factors that have been found to be more important in earlier studies of innovation adoption.
