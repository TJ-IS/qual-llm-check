# Requirements capture using SODA

J Bryant

School of Business & Management, Sheffield Hallam University, Pond Street, Sheffield, S1 IWB, UK

This paper demonstrates the use of an established management science framework for system requirements elicitation within a broad groupware development project. It explains how this framework, strategic options development and analysis (SODA) was used, and illustrates the outcomes produced within a demonstrator setting in a health authority that formed part of the project. The approach is examined and evaluated as an element within the overall system development process.

## Introduction

This paper describes how a structured methodology was used to support requirements elicitation in the development of a major information technology (IT) system prototype. Specifically, it describes how an approach informed by the strategic options development and analysis (SODA) methodology (Eden & Ackermann, 1993), was used to elicit executives’ personal perspectives upon, and to generate creative debate about, the sort of IT system needed to support an organization’s mission. The work produced some quite precise targets that indicated functional requirements for the subsequent software design. The application was carried out in a health authority as part of the UK Government’s Department of Trade and Industry’s £13 m computer-supported co-operative work (CSCW) programme, and this backdrop will now be sketched.

The creation of an environment which will enable, support and promote co-operative working across all the core areas of an organization’s business is an ambitious but practically important target for those working on commercial CSCW applications. The present project, which had just this aim, brought together five development partners and two academic institutions, supported by user organizations who hosted demonstrator applications: overall it involved about 40 man-years effort and cost nearly £1.4 m. The key objective of the project was therefore to understand and demonstrate how CSCW could be positioned within an enterprise IT framework, and to create a modular, integrated environment to support enterprise-wide CSCW. The development work was expected to raise awareness of the organizational and environmental as well as the technical issues involved. It was not within the scope of the project fully to implement organization-wide IT solutions within either demonstrator, though it was intended that the potential for and obstacles to achieving this would be clearly delineated.

In the next section of this paper, some background is given to the particular demonstrator which was the location of the work described. The initial project work which led up to the SODA application is also outlined. The following sections move on to explain the various stages of the SODA process as implemented within the project context: this culminated in a workshop session which generated outputs that guided subsequent development effort. The succeeding stages of work on the demonstrator are beyond the scope of this paper, and they are reported elsewhere (Steele et al, 1995). However, it does seem relevant to provide here some views of the contribution of the SODA work, and some reflections on the problems in interfacing with elements of the project that were encountered: this is the subject of the final section of the paper.

## Demonstrator focus

The demonstrator site was a UK health authority. Until recently, Area Health Authorities (AHA) have acted as purchasers of health care from hospitals and community health services (eg, psychiatric care, family planning and health visitors) for the people resident in their geographical catchment areas, and for this purpose received an allocation of government funds. In a similar way the provision of primary health care (ie, family doctors, dentists, pharmacists, etc) was the responsibility of the Family Health Services Authorities (FHSA). At the time of the study, the management of these two activities was being brought together. The new bodies—simply termed health authorities (HA)—had the opportunity to rethink their mode of operation, and so it was opportune for the demonstrator to consider the sort of information system which might be appropriate to the merged organization.

The concerns of those operating within a HA are immensely varied. While many strategic concerns now centre upon the regular process of contract negotiation with health service providers—an annual block contract with a hospital, expressed in terms of patient care episodes, would be used, for example, to provide for the needs of the population in each specialty (eg, ophthalmology, urology)—operational matters also make heavy demands upon information services. These can range from the requirement to provide regular or ad hoc statistical reports on activities, to the need to issue highprofile public statements indicating the authority’s stance on topical issues, or to respond to the handling of particular cases or situations. The service purchaser–provider relationship in particular involves close collaborative working, and is an obvious locus of relevance for emerging CSCW technologies. However, more routine processes requiring or benefitting from the contribution of specialists across the organization (and outside) are also commonplace. Thus, general practitioners may be in direct electronic communication with a FHSA, and may be consulted regularly on certain topics. The success of these processes is often a matter of some importance, given the rather public stage on which a HA has to play.

It was necessary to select a specific business process within which the CSCW application would initially be developed. While there were many candidate processes, the significant changes taking place at the time of the discussions—included the linking of the HA and the FHSA mentioned above and the imminent appointment of a new managerial team, as well as major governmentdirected changes in the health service supplier–provider relationship—led to the choice of a relatively uncontroversial, yet CSCW-rich activity as the setting for the prototype.

The chosen focus was the authority’s handling of Health Service Circulars. That is, the way in which circulars (ie, directives, requests, consultations and notifications) emanating from the Department of Health were treated, and the responses which were made. While this process might appear unexciting—and indeed the processing of many circulars was quite straightforward, requiring simply the noting of changes in procedures, or the submission of statistical returns—a significant minority of circulars raised important matters of strategy, which required careful internal discussion before a response could be given. Furthermore, there was a strong argument for taking a more pro-active stance towards Department of Health requirements, and for making a more active contribution to the debates on ‘live’ issues which precede policy changes. Generally too there was a desire to act from a coherent strategic base rather than responding piecemeal to successive department demands. In addition the handling of circulars mirrored the processing of many other documents, involving interaction and debate between senior HA staff, with contributions coming in from others within the organization. Finally, there was also the opportunity of building upon experience gained by an earlier AHA project at the demonstrator site that investigated document flow.

## Approach

Work on the HA demonstrator began at the very outset of the present project. Through a number of informal meetings between managers at the HA and project team members from the IT developers, an appreciation of the work of the authority and of the potential of the technologies which would feed into the intended system was gained; valuable team-building was also achieved.

The view was taken that an effective virtual environment for collaborative working must enhance the abilities of users to deliver value in striving towards organizational goals: and that its performance would be measured in terms of how it empowered and supported users, rather than (purely) in terms of its ability to handle information or process data. Furthermore it was essential that the design of such an environment should neither be constrained by existing work practices, nor by the technical capabilities of immediately available tools. Lastly, while the system should deliver in a manner which meshed with the culture and ethos of the user community, its implementation might involve new ways of working. In other words, the design process would need to be predominantly concerned with creating ways of facilitating effective action and interaction—as advocated in business process re-engineering (BPR) (Hammer & Champy, 1993)—rather than with replicating the arbitrary processes which had evolved historically.

Traditional approaches to information systems development (eg, SSADM (Cutts, 1991)) work to a specification of requirements from, on the one hand an analysis of the current system and how it works, and on the other an assessment of the deficiencies of the current system and any new demands which must be met, to suggest a logical view of the ‘ideal’ required system. The implications that ‘requirements’ are known (and just have to be recorded), objective (and look similar to all potential users), absolute (and are relevant to any business process), public (and cover only ‘legitimate’ organizational needs), and implementable (and the design needed to meet them is evident), can readily be challenged!

The corollary of the design stance adopted was that requirements definition had to acknowledge as central the HA’s mission to achieve good health for citizens, through purchasing on their behalf the health care that they require, and by promoting healthy lifestyles. Now subsidiary business goals and associated critical success factors define the agendas for groups and individuals both wholly within and across the boundary of the authority. Respective examples of these encountered in the study were, the need to have accurate activity information from service providers (vital for the Finance

Director), and the need to manage the contracting cycle (essential for the cross-organizational Acute Core Group). While it was expected that these lower-level goals would provide the raw data for business process identification, what was crucial was to see these in the context of the HA’s over-arching task. It was principally because of this desire to create a coherent rationality (Eden & Ackermann, 1993), linking the organization’s mission and strategic objectives to contributory actions that the SODA approach (Eden, 1989) was adopted.

The demonstrator project involved capturing the distributed wisdom of those presently involved in one way or another in handling Department of Health Circulars, looking at this process within the context of overall business objectives. Accordingly it was necessary to talk with those involved in the handling and use of circulars to establish not only what they did, but more fundamentally, why they did what they did with circulars. This involved exposing and clarifying the work objectives of individuals and the teams in which they operated and identifying factors that inhibited effective action, as well as confirming the more mundane aspects of document handling procedures.

What was needed was to trap the complex, idiosyncratic views of experienced participants in the HA, to understand how they perceived the relevance of circulars both for the roles they separately fulfilled, and for the wider aims and mission of the authority. For instance, a given circular could have very diffferent meanings and implications for different participants: for a director it might symptomatise government pressure on the HA, for a general manager it might demand effecting an operational change, for a librarian it might represent an item to be archived. The SODA technology provides a ‘facilitative device’ (in the form, for example, of formal ‘maps’ of peoples’ thinking) which supports and better enables the negotiation of a collective vision.

The overall intention of the SODA-based phase of the project was that through interviews with representative participants in the process, their views would be exposed, and through consolidation of these diverse accounts, working targets for process redesign would be created. A workshop, bringing together all those who had been interviewed, would be the means of achieving consensus on focus and targets, and of enhancing group commitment to the project. Implemented in this way, the SODA methodology was seen as an effective means of complementing the existing understanding of the physical and functional processes by which circulars were handled, since it would mean eliciting peoples’ perceptions of the strengths and weaknesses of this process, and drawing out the criteria by which they individually rated its success.

There were other, less obvious benefits for the project from adopting the SODA approach. Most importantly, the emphasis in SODA on stimulating a social process in creating a group model, was just what was required: firstly, because a high level of commitment to the project from across the HA was seen as essential for its eventual success; secondly, because the project would thereby play an important role in facilitating the ongoing change processes within the newly-formed HA. More obviously the mere fact of the SODA interviews would alert participating staff to the project, and such information as the interviewers might provide about the proposed groupware would help to inform them of the contribution which it could make to the HA. This was seen as helping to overcome a key problem which has been quite widely recognised in introducing and exploiting new technologies: making potential users aware of the opportunities which the technologies may offer. For example, in one of the most influential studies of groupware implementation (Orlikowski, 1992) it was concluded that ‘in the absence of mental models that stressed its collaborative nature, groupware was interpreted in terms of familiar personal, stand-alone technologies such as spreadsheets’. In other words, being unaware of the potential of CSCW systems, the intended users would not be able to appreciate the new ways of working which might be possible.

## Requirements capture

## Background

The HA response to circulars, is a time-constrained and necessary process, which for each circular requires the involvement of a different set of roles within the organization: tracking the progress or history of such responses is also considered important. However, the progress of a circular is not necessarily straightforward and senior staff may need to know at short notice the progress of a particular document: potentially it may also involve accessing minutes of meetings, memoranda, and other internal and external communications relating to the topic area, and having the means of intervening in this process by providing additional input or by redirecting it to other staff for further action.

Earlier work carried out in the Document Flow Project (Parsons, 1992) had already established the current means by which circulars are handled. A summary of the process is shown in Figure 1. While some incoming circulars may be copied for rapid executive action, the majority are handled using internal mail and a distribution list to ensure that appropriate staff take necessary action; copies are also archived for later reference. Although this procedure worked adequately, there were often delays—the photocopying of circulars presented a particular bottleneck—and inefficiencies (eg, multiple copying; non-receipt; time lags in secondary or tertiary circulation) and the system did not provide any means for ascertaining or supporting the responses of those who took consequent action.

## Interviews

Arrangements were put in hand for relevant staff at the AHA and FHSA to be interviewed, and initially six senior staff were seen. Elsewhere (eg, Norland, 1992) it has been noted that the composition of the group participating in pilot implementations of groupware is critical to eventual success. The members should be keen to carry out their collaborative task in the most effective manner; they should be actual users, not ‘technology voyeurs playing with the latest toy’; they should collectively be held in esteem by others; and the process in which they are engaged should be a core one in a main business area. All these requirements appeared to be satisfied in the present project.

Each interview was conducted in the respondent’s own office at the authority. There were two interviewers, but their roles in the process were not differentiated. All interviews began with a short introduction to the Integrated Cooperative Workspace (ICW) project, before opening out discussion to the topic of health circulars. Where there was time within the 45 min allowed in each case, the respondent was guided to comment on other collaborative processes with which he/she was engaged at the HA.

The interviews were not tape recorded. Notes were taken as rough cognitive maps by the two interviewers— this procedure is well described by Eden and Simpson (1989)—allowing the interview to develop in a fairly ad hoc manner, but also recording issues on which to prompt the interviewee for more detail. An example of a section of one of these maps is shown in Figure 2. This shows the logical skeleton of the argument made by an interviewee as to why deadlines get missed. Arrows link constructs and each arrow represents a personal theory or belief about the causality of the situation under discussion. For example, this interviewee thinks that the prevalence of new staff means that there is general ‘uncertainty as to what matters’, and this in turn contributes to deadlines being missed. The two maps from each interview were subsequently combined using the Graphics COPE (GC) software (Strathclyde University, 1994), when this became available locally, into a single cognitive map representing each session. Because of the tight overall project timescale, it proved infeasible to arrange a second, confirmatory, round of interviews with these respondents.

The products of the interviews (see below) were promising, but it was felt that it would be valuable to meet with more staff who had to act on circulars; many of those seen initially had roles around the dissemination and information management process, rather than being prime respondents. Accordingly six further interviews were arranged and held. In all, the respondents ranged through the then chief executive and some of his directors, to library staff and administrators concerned with the distribution of circulars. A half-day workshop, described later, was held as soon as feasible after the last of the interview series.

## Analysis

The twelve maps each contained an average of around 30 or so distinct constructs. This is fairly typical for a first interview of this length (Eden, 1985). A short analysis of ‘head’ and ‘tail’ constructs—these were those lying at either ends of chains of argument, the former often representing aims/objectives, the latter means/actions—carried out for each map, highlighted some personal goals and not-goals (Eden & Bryson, 1993) around health circulars. However, a full analysis of this sort was not carried out for two reasons: first, as the maps resulted from single interviews without feedback, they were not sufficiently logically coherent for this form of analysis reliably to generate goals and notgoals; second, by the time that the GC software was available, it was too close to the workshop for such analysis to have additional value.

The twelve maps were reviewed together and a number of common themes were identified across subsets of them. These themes and their sources are shown in Figure 3. ## Workshop

The workshop was an ‘out-of-the-office’ half-day event, attended by eleven of the twelve interviewees. This was the first time that they had come together in the context of the project, so the session began with a short general briefing on the CSCW project and its implications for the HA. The health circulars application was then introduced, and the process to date described. A plenary discussion gave an opportunity to modify and confirm the choice of focus as:

‘Department of Health edicts sent to all authorities (and the subsequent cascade)

but this was unanimously agreed.

It was explained that the aim of the workshop was to work from the rich data that the participants had themselves provided through the interviews, to identify a structured set of targets which should shape the software system design. It was agreed to use the themes that had been identified to organize this task, and three subgroups were set up, each to look at three different themes. The intention was for each group to develop through discussion the initial list of goals and not-goals relating to their particular topic. This work was supported and occasionally prompted by the project workers, but in the main the groups self-organized and ran effectively, drawing extensively upon the materials from the cognitive maps, but elaborating their ideas as necessary through debate. The outcome was an enhanced version of the original issue sheets. However, and most important, the sub-group development work meant that this material was now fully ‘owned’ by these participants, and the plenary discussion which followed was notable for the degree to which people could contribute from the perspective of ‘their’ given themes, towards a more rounded view of the whole process.

The process achieved the identification of critical issues in the handling of health circulars, and generated a wide range of ‘hard’ and ‘soft’ targets to form the basis of the next, process modelling, stage of the application. It also appeared to have generated a good degree of commitment from those so far involved, to the project as a whole, and a willingness to contribute to and take part in the work ahead.

## The targets

Following the workshop, the emergent targets were further refined. The ideas from the various sub-groups were brought together and organized hierarchically under broad headings—‘Top level’, ‘Activities’, ‘Information’, ‘People & Roles’ and ‘General’—which meshed with the process redesign framework which was to be used in the next phase of the demonstrator to shape the health circulars management process. An excerpt from the tabular structure which held these organized targets is shown in Figure 6, with the workshop targets in the left-most column. The three columns to the right contain features which are essential for harmonising the redesigned process with organizational and individual objectives. Thus the ‘Functional Requirements’ guide the system architecture, ‘Metrics’ provide indicators which can be used to assess the success in achieving any target, and ‘Evaluation methods’ indicate how such assessment might be carried out. These columns were constructed jointly by the SODA project workers and the IT developers who were to undertake the subsequent process modelling work as the next part of the demonstrator workpackage.

## Discussion

The use of SODA as a software development tool is unusual. This immediately poses a number of questions which will be addressed in this section. Firstly, in information systems terms, what was SODA used to do; and how does it stand in relation to other approaches normally used for such purposes? Secondly, in process terms, how easy was it to integrate SODA within the overall project; and how genuine were the links with other parts of the development cycle. Thirdly, how practical is it to advocate SODA as an element within a broader CSCW system development method; and what payoffs may accrue from its use?

Wilson (1990) provides a useful summary of the various approaches which have been used for information requirements analysis. He classifies these along two dimensions: one representing an emphasis upon either data or information (defined as ‘data plus the meaning ascribed to it’); the other representing the use of a language of verbs (activities) or of nouns (entities). These give rise to four categories of approaches. Thus an approach concerned with business activities would line in the information-verbs cell, while one focusing upon computer file structures would be classified as about datanouns. Seen within this classification, the work with SODA clearly lies within the information-verbs area, for as used in this project it was concerned with establishing the information required to support key business processes. Other parts of the overall CSCW project methodology, such as the use of role interaction models (RIM) and enhanced entity-relationship modelling (EER) complemented SODA and addressed the informationnoun and data categories respectively.

The use of SODA in this project is thus similar to, for example, the so-called primary task analysis (Wilson, 1990) used in the soft systems methodology. In the latter, a primary task system is developed to represent an area of wholehearted consensus between the perspectives of relevant observers. It can be argued that creating this sort of consensus within the present project was one purpose of the SODA work, and especially of the workshop session and its follow-up target-setting. However, the aggregated map produced went beyond narrow consensus to bring in some highly individual perspectives on the health circular process. Furthermore this map was one which by the end of the workshop the participants regarded as ‘their’ view of the process: there was a real sense of shared ownership. The fact that so many issues around health circulars had been acknowledged and included, allowed people to relax a little in the knowledge that (to them) significant matters would not get forgotten; at the same time there was an efficient way of organizing and navigating the complexity of the situation. It could have been beneficial to have worked interactively with the group map during the workshop, using a PC and linked projector, but even had this been feasible (which it was not at the time) the intrusion of this technology would have changed the structure and process of the session: on balance the sub-group working made a good use of the limited time available.

The overall SODA process delivered rather more than structuring support. As noted above, the interviews provided a two-way conduit to alert staff to the CSCW project and to encourage them to help shape its design. The SODA workshop represented an initial strong bringing together of users and developers within the HA demonstrator. It was a great success in this respect. Indeed even now, seen from some considerable time afterwards, it is regarded by those who were in the project team as a pivotal event in ‘gelling’ the development process. Another positive outcome was the raising of awareness across all participants of others’ views and requirements both of the narrow health circular process, and also of the wider strategic implications of the proposed groupware.

All of this contrasts even more strongly with conventional practice in requirements definition (The HCI Service, 1991) where it is still bizarrely (to the management scientist) commonplace for user involvement to be regarded as an optional bonus—‘it would be nice to have some’—rather than an absolute essential. To their credit, there are some methods which build from direct enquiry about user needs: for instance, contextual inquiry (Holtzblatt et al, 1991) involves designers observing users at work and talking through with them possible solutions, prior to analysis by the design team alone; the PAS Toolset (Galer et al, 1991) involves identifying stakeholders and their job goals and then capturing information about their tasks to create the product requirements. But there is far more work based upon such general techniques as task analysis and computer aided software engineering (CASE) tools which tend to demote the user’s role to that of laboratory object, rather than active subject. It is this comparison which more strikingly emphasises the benefits of using an involving, creative and user-centred process like SODA.

Moving on to consider the second issue—the integration of SODA within the CSCW project—the outcome is less impressive, though probably for reasons local to the project, rather than general to the approach. The overall thrust the CSCW approach developed in, and subsequently advocated as a result of the project (Elliot & Steele, 1995) has been fairly and plausibly presented (Osguthorpe et al, 1996), and does indeed include SODA as part of this methodology. While this is a tribute to the way in which the SODA elements ‘won over’ initially sceptical information science professionals, it is likely that this success was more to do with the second (social process) role of the work as to the first (substantive goal-setting) aspects. The apparent seamlessness of the links between the SODA work and later process modelling was not quite so apparent at the time that the project was undertaken; nor were the effects of the disciplinary differences between the IT specialists and the management science professionals insignificant.

It had been anticipated from the outset that SODA could make a worthwhile contribution to what was referred to as the ‘target setting’ phase of the overall project: indeed its use has been suggested in the original proposal for funding. However, when the demonstrator project got underway, the relevance of the approach was rightly subjected to quite severe scrutiny. It was only as a result of some fairly tough negotiation within the project team, and with a high degree of scepticism from those espousing conventional BPR methods—to the extent that SODA might reveal ‘critical success factors’ it eventually was grudgingly accepted by them—that the work described here was initiated. Indeed the tight timescales reported above were due in part from pressure to proceed to these other more familiar types of work without delay. Fortunately the managers of the overall project team were at this stage much preoccupied by the need to get the development partners working together effectively, and as the SODA work at least represented tangible progress on one demonstrator (the other was deliberately lagged by several months) co-operation was encouraged.

While the initial SODA interviews proceeded to programme, the majority of the project team were discussing issues about the hardware platform and communications protocols for the eventual deliverables. Accordingly, the running of the workshop at a critical time in the overall project development had an impact on the demonstrator which probably exaggerates its contribution to the software development and the later process modelling. The bridge between the immediate workshop outputs and the target structure (like Figure 6) was also problematic. There is certainly room here for further work to establish a better way to use the sorts of output which SODA can produce in process modelling.

An obvious further benefit of using SODA had been seen at the inception of the project as to provide a basis for system evaluation. It was unfortunate that as the project unfolded it became apparent that there would not be a further opportunity to revisit the outcomes of the SODA sessions, as had originally been hoped. This was because the project as a whole was unable to deliver a full prototype system with which authority users could have realistic experience of handling health circulars. This use of SODA for evaluating the final demonstrator product would have been a valuable means of benchmarking achievement against aspiration.

Turning now to the third issue—the choice of specific approach—mentioned at the beginning of this section, Wilson’s advice (1990) that it is wise for an analyst to ‘choose a methodology which ‘works’ for him’ is echoed by many others (eg, Cropper, 1990). SODA was used in the reported project because someone was available (the author) who had some experience of using what was seen as a potentially appropriate approach. There is no doubt that ‘target-setting’ work could have been tackled using a different approach (eg, soft systems methodology), but the outcomes would also have been different. It would thus be wrong to advocate too strongly the necessary conjunction of the methods used in this project: rather it can be reported that they worked well in this case by delivering what the project demanded of them. However, there is little doubt that the particular strengths of SODA (eg, its concern with the meaning of situations for individual participants; its emphasis upon and proffered models for explicit negotiation of opportunities and actions; and the structured social process which it offers) made a distinctive and valued contribution to the development programme. To this extent at least, it has much promise, and for this reason it is planned to test it further in future work (Osguthorpe et al, 1996).
