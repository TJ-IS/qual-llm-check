# An extension of the tri-core model of information systems innovation: strategic and technological moderators

V Grover

Management Science Department, College of Business Administration, University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

There has been significant empirical work on information systems (IS) innovation. With the advent of a myriad of new and diverse technologies being introduced into contemporary organizations this work is only going to increase in its importance. Unfortunately, results of prior empirical work have been fragmented. Many of these studies treat IS innovations as homogeneous entities and do not effectively integrate notions of IS innovation with general organizational innovation. Swanson (1994) proposes a tri-core model and theory of IS innovation that can help integrate the isolated studies in this area, and can form the foundation on which future study of IS innovation can be based. This paper, in the spirit of a cumulative research tradition, presents and builds on Swanson’s framework by incorporating contingencies that might affect the model. Specifically, notions of strategy (strategic push) and technology impact are discussed in the context of the framework. We hope that continued inquiry into this area will facilitate formulation of rich theory that will enable focused inquiry on IS innovation.

## Introduction

The study of information systems (IS) innovation is important. Re-engineering and other structural changes in organizational elements are prominent in todays’s business. Also, with corresponding prominence is the rapid adoption and diffusion of remarkable new computing and communications technologies that are often instrumental in enabling these structural changes. The flexibility offered by malleable processing power, storage, and networking is very different from the days of the centralised, inflexible mainframe. Therefore, there is tremendous opportunity for organizations to leverage these information technologies in a manner that enhances organizational effectiveness. To do so, the study of adoption and diffusion of technology-based innovations is critical if organizations are to respond to time-bound opportunities.

Unfortunately, the work to date on IS innovation, although significant in quantity, has been somewhat isolated and fragmented. More often than not, IS innovations (e.g., EDI, databases, CASE, EIS, etc) are studied as homogeneous innovations that are affected by a standard set of factors. Damanpour (1991) in a metaanalysis of the innovation literature describes the organizational factors commonly studied. He concludes that consideration of different innovation types, different organizational types, and the consideration of individual, organizational, and environmental variables can alleviate the instability of innovation research results.

Swanson (1994), recently proposed a model for IS innovation in an organizational context. We view this as an important development for a number of reasons. First, it is critical that we as IS researchers attempt to build a cumulative research tradition by integrating diverse sets of results. A framework that attempts to do so can not only provide a fundamental basis for evaluating prior results and progress, but can guide future work in a structured and more cohesive fashion. Second, the uncoordinated study of IS is of particular concern, given the pervasiveness and integrative nature of information technologies. Third, very few studies look at the relationship between IS innovation and business innovation. In an environment where information technology (IT) is pushing the path for organizations to reinvent the way they do business, such relationships are critical. Therefore, bridging the gap between the IS context and the general organizational context can serve to facilitate more holistic understanding of IS innovation. Finally, the diversity of today’s information technologies challenges us to go beyond treatment of IS innovations as homogeneous, with a common set of constructs affecting their propensity to be adopted.

The number of meta-analyses in general innovation are all motivated by the need for building a cumulative body of research and subsequently theory (e.g., Tornatzky & Klein, 1982; Damanpour, 1987, 1991; Kwon & Zmud, 1987). This study focuses on this objective by examining the Swanson model and providing guidance on possible avenues for building and expanding the model. Specifically, the role of strategy in innovation and the nature of technology is recognised and proposed as an avenue of research in re-evaluating Swanson’s work. The paper is organized into four major sections. First, the Swanson model is briefly presented in light of prior work on innovation. Second, propositions based on the model are presented and discussed. Third, six specific modifications of the model are discussed and presented. Finally, conclusions and avenues for future research are presented.

## The dual and tri-core models of innovation

The study of innovation is multi-disciplinary, spanning various fields in the social sciences. In general, researchers make distinctions between the adoption and diffusion of innovation (Damanpour, 1991). Adoption culminates in the decision to invest resources into a new technology, structure, or programme. Diffusion refers to the spread of an innovation among other members of a social system (Rogers, 1983). Therefore, diffusion would include widespread implementation of the innovation. Rogers (1983) discusses adoption as including knowledge acquisition, learning, and the decision to adopt. He describes the “earliness of adoption” as a key characteristic of innovativeness. Cooper and Zmud (1990) describe IT adoption stages as initiation (organizational need and/or technological push) and the adoption decision, while subsequent phases are described as adaptation, acceptance and routinisation.

Regardless of the specific stage model used, most innovation work examines factors that influence outcomes at different stages of the process, with the majority of empirical work concentrating on the adoption stage. The general thesis for organizational innovation has been that organic organizational structures (e.g., less centralised, less formalised, more specialised), more slack resources, and open communication channels (e.g., more integrated and interdependent organizational entities) leads to greater innovativeness and adoption behaviour (Kwon & Zmud, 1987). However, in many of the studies, the results have been inconsistent.

Some researchers attempt to explain inconsistencies by examining different types of innovations. Distinctions have been made between product and process innovation (e.g., Ettlie, 1983), radical and incremental innovations (e.g., Dewar & Dutton, 1986) and technical and administrative innovations (e.g., Daft, 1978). The most relevant to this study is the latter, which has been referred to as the dual core model of innovation—distinguishing between innovations that focus on the technical core and those that focus on the administrative core. Technical innovations relate to the technical system of the organization or the primary work activity. These may or may not involve technology (e.g., a new service or product). Administrative innovations relate to the social system of rules, roles, procedures and structures in the organization (e.g., a new way to carry out a decision). Daft (1978)

concluded that in all likelihood the organic structural conditions that facilitate innovation in the technical cores are the opposite of those required for the administrative core. Damanpour and Evan (1984) in their study of public libraries concluded that while most innovation studies focus on technical innovations, a combination of changes in the social and technical systems helps performance. Further evidence of these distinctions were found in a study by Damanpour (1987).

Swanson (1994) argues that the dual core model, while inadequate for the study of IS innovation, can be expanded to form a broader framework to incorporate the integrated nature of IS. This is desirable he argues, since most studies of IS innovation have not distinguished themselves from studies of general organizational innovation. IS spans both the technical and administrative cores and is therefore unlikely to be characteristic of innovation local to either. Further, IS involves communication technology and the ability to share information resources in both the technical and administrative cores. These effects can permeate beyond the IS unit facilitating innovation in the broader business unit. Therefore, Swanson proposes adding a third, the functional IS core, to the dual core model to link the technical and administrative cores.

The tri-core model proposed by Swanson is illustrated in Figure 1.<sup>1</sup> The central cylinder of the figure represents the IS products and services provided, typically by the core IS functionality that might have centralised or distributed responsibility within the organization. The model distinguishes three fundamental types of IS innovations (Types 1, 2 and 3) within the organization.

Type 1 innovation is closest to the functional core involving the IS products themselves. These innovations could either be a new IS administrative arrangement that supports IS work (type 1a) or an innovation that focuses directly on the technical IS task (type 1b). A type 1a innovation (IS administrative process innovation) is the concept of the chief information officer (Grover et al, 1993). This role offers a new form of governance and administration in IS units. Swanson describes the departmentalisation of the software maintenance function as a type 1a innovation. Type 1b innovation deals with the IS task itself where ‘the nature of IS work is changed’. Innovations that can be considered as type 1b are computer aided software engineering (CASE) and object oriented programming systems (OOPS). Both are currently being touted as transforming the development function and are integral to changing IS work.

Type 2 and 3 innovations extend IS innovation beyond the confines of the IS function into the general organizational environment. Both type 2 and type 3 innovations involve application of new IS products and services to the administrative core (or support services) of the business (type 2) or the core business technology (type 3). The administrative core of the business provides the administrative processes and infrastructure that supports the business and its products and services. Examples of type 2 given by Swanson include financial accounting systems, payroll systems and executive information systems (EIS).<sup>2</sup> Other type 2 innovations could include teleconferencing, electronic mail, and decision support systems. All of these affect the administrative core by facilitating decision processes or accelerate administrative processes.

Type 3 innovations integrate IS products and services into the core work processes (type 3a), organizational products and services (type 3b) and external relationships (type 3c) of the business. CAD/CAM systems (the major component of CIM systems mentioned by Swanson) which are integral to the core design of products illustrate a type 3a innovation. Many of the so called ‘strategic systems’ (Johnston & Carrico, 1988) that involved enhancement of product/service using IT (e.g., airline reservation systems) typify type 3b innovation, while EDI and other interorganizational systems which facilitate the coordination between customers and suppliers represent type 3c innovations. Swanson suggests that it is useful to distinguish between IS innovations in this way, where each IS innovation could comprise of IT and/or work organizational features. Therefore rather than treating IS innovation as a generic entity (as in much of the prior work) facilitated by a common set of organizational factors, the facilitation of each type of IS innovation might be distinct. Innovations central to IS functionality (type 1) might have professional and resource-based motivators while innovations central to business functionality (i.e., type 3) might have organizational and strategic motivators.

However, the unique integrated nature of IS makes it necessary to recognise the relationships between the three types of innovations thereby bridging the gap between the IS function and the general organization context (which is ignored on the earlier dual core model). Therefore, Swanson recognises weak order effects emanating from the IS functional core toward the business core (i.e., type 1 to type 3) and strong order effects emanating the other way (i.e., type 3 to type 1). In other words, IS process innovation (the adoption of CASE) might have some effect on the core and administrative aspects of the business but they will not compel business innovation. However, business innovation (adoption of a CAD/CAM system) since it is integral to the core line function or products of the business, might compel changes and innovation in the administrative structure of the business or the IS function itself. Implicit in the discussion of the model is the notion of business value, which ultimately justifies any innovation.

## Model propositions

A diverse set of IS innovations have been studied. These include administrative electronic data processing (EDP) among hospitals (Moch & Morse, 1977), distributed data management systems (Gorden & Gorden, 1992), materials requirements planning (MRP) systems (Cooper & Zmud, 1990), spreadsheet software (Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990) and interorganizational systems (Grover, 1993). These studies typically consider antecedents to a single IS innovation. Different variables affecting adoption cannot be compared across different IS innovations, and rarely is the impact beyond adoption on the broader organizational context (i.e., business impact) evaluated. By distinguishing among IS innovation types, Swanson proposes that the factors influencing each innovation are not homogeneous and can be expected to be different. By understanding these differences, organizations can be in a better position to evaluate how to improve IS innovativeness (presumably a desirable characteristic) for different types of IS innovations.

The postulated theory can be interpreted as having three key aspects. First, the theory presumes that innovations originating from the IS function have a duality in that they are influenced by both the needs of the host organization and the IS profession. Second, the theory recognises that IS innovations steming from the IS function will be influenced by the more immediate IS environment, while those away from the IS core are subject to organizational influences. Finally, the theory presumes IS innovations evolve over time toward more business rather than technical functionality. These three aspects synthesise Swanson’s work and can form the basis for broad testable propositions based on the theory. These are elaborated below.

Type 1 innovations involve technical and administrative changes in the IS subunit. Regardless of whether this unit is centralised or diffused within the host organization, it represents the repository of expertise required to build and administer the innovation. Most IS units seek to balance their need for professional affiliation with its business (host organization) affiliation. A professionally oriented IS unit seeks to focus on IS expertise, professional standards, and state-of-the-art technology. Business oriented units tend to focus on the source of its resources, the business it serves, by emphasising services to the host organization rather than the IS profession. Therefore, it can be argued that tight coupling of the IS unit to the profession would facilitate greater innovation in the IS core (Type 1 innovation). Swanson argues that type 2 innovations are also closely tied to the IS core and internal IS work processes than type 3 innovations in that they support the information needs of business administration. Often these information needs require functional or informational expertise which could require certain types of type 1 innovation or provide the seed for their subsequent origination. Type 3 innovations on the other hand emphasise the line functions and products of the business. Organizations that recognise the strategic role of the IS unit, will be more likely to support IS innovation that is critical to the business core (type 3). Therefore, the proposition can be stated as follows:

Proposition 1: Professional orientation of the IS unit wil influence (facilitate) innovations closer to the IS core (types 1 and 2), while business orientation or recognition of the importance of IS to the business will influence type 3 innovation.

The second proposition also relates to the two units involved, the IS unit and the broader organizational unit. Rather than treat the innovative characteristics of both units as invariably identical, there is a need to recognise that there might be differences that could affect the nature of IS innovation in organizations. The IS unit for instance, could be characterised by open communication channels, high diversity levels, larger size, slack resources, decentralised decision making, etc, all of which are acknowledged characteristics of innovative structures. The organizational unit may or may not have a corresponding structure, although the convergence of innovative propensity is clearly higher when the IS unit is structurally diffused rather than centralised. Therefore, in general, innovativeness depends on the relevant unit in which ‘learning’ and subsequent innovation occurs.

Proposition 2: Type 1 innovation in the IS core is more dependent on innovative IS unit structures, while innovation in the organizational unit (types 2 and 3) is more dependent on host organization innovative structures.

It should be emphasized that the variables mentioned above are not new. For instance size has been studied in prior empirical work (e.g., Moch & Morse, 1977; Kimberly & Evanisko, 1981; Zmud, 1984) as has slack (e.g., Rosner, 1968; Damanpour, 1987), the diversity of technological knowledge (e.g., Kimberly & Evanisko, 1981), and professionalism (Daft, 1978; Zmud, 1984). However, Swanson’s theory integrates these variables into a coherent framework that can form the basis for studying IS innovation with the broader organizational context.

The third proposition deals with the evolution of IS innovation. The general thesis is that as IS innovation continues to evolve over time, new work organizational features will be instituted to take advantage of the technology (Damanpour & Evan, 1984). Adoption and use of the technology’s features make the need for such work features apparent. Further, the business functionality of the innovation will be increasingly emphasised rather than the technical functionality, as its advocates push for more widespread organizational adoption. In other words, there is a natural migration of an IS innovation from its technical orientation to a more organizational orientation. As discussed by Swanson, the ‘business rationale supporting adoption should become better articulated and IS products and services should incorporate more substantial business functionality’ (p 1084). In some cases, business processes will change in order to leverage the IS innovation as we are seeing currently with the re-engineering phenomenon (Davenport, 1993). But ultimately the sustainability and diffusion of an IS innovation cannot be based on its technical functionality, and it will continue to evolve with additional extensions and enhancements, oriented toward business functionality. The evolution will be rapid for type 3 innovations which are germane to the business core, than type 1 innovations where professional rationale may sustain adoption for a longer period.

Proposition 3: IS innovations will evolve toward greater business functionality through enhancements or a change in associated work processes. The cycle will be more rapid in the case of type 3 innovation, followed by type 2 and 1.

In sum, Swanson’s model distinguishes between three types of IS innovation, proposes different antecedents to each type, recognises the duality of the IS unit, and emphasises the importance of business functionality in their evolution. Below, we discuss the model in a more critical light, suggesting avenues for extension and further work in this important area.

## Model extensions

It is our belief that the model originates from the fundamental aspects of the innovation dynamic. In doing so it fails to recognise that there might be certain conditions within which that dynamic does not hold. Two contingencies are proposed. The first includes cases where organizational directives or mandates predominate causing rapid intervention, structural changes and innovation. Such organizational imperatives, which are reflective of the intent of the organization and its decision-makers, can affect various facets of the tri-core model. We generically refer to this as the ‘strategic imperative’ as contrasted with the ‘innovation dynamic’ emphasised by Swanson. The second, includes characteristics of the technology that are subsumed within the IS. These technologies exhibit a high variance in their degree of complexity and sophistication. Therefore, the dynamics involved in their adoption could be different and could have significant implications for the model. Based on these two sets of contingencies, we offer some possible avenues for refining the model.

## The strategic and innovation imperatives

The innovation dynamic presumes that IS innovation stems from size, diversity, flexibility and integration, characteristics of organic structures. Such structures preserve uncertainty as the major source of creativity in the organization and institutional leadership is critical in creating a context that fosters innovation. These characteristics, as discussed by Swanson, are reinforced by the professional affiliation present in the IS functional core and business or strategic rationales as we move toward the administrative and technical cores of the host organization. Strong (type 3 to type 1 innovation) and weak order effects (type 1 to type 3 innovation) can stem from the innovation, where the strong order effects are dominated by business rationale forcing innovation in administrative and other supporting structures. Work process innovation succeeds technological innovation leading to greater business rationale and productivity over time.

Much of the empirical work in innovation and the tricore model is based on the innovation dynamic. However, there is a major caveat associated with this dynamic that must be recognised. The dynamic implicitly presumes an ‘innovation imperative’, which implies that the innovation will stem from organizational contexts conducive to innovative behaviour. The alternative imperative that could intervene and modify the innovation dynamic is the ‘strategic imperative’. Here organizations innovate because of a domination of strategy that might preclude any favourable contextual conditions. As discussed by Van De Ven (1986), “managing diversity requires framing ideas and problems so that they can be approached through experimentation and selection. (In contrast) order results from imposing standards and a concept of strategy on the organization” (p 603).

While Swanson recognises the strategic importance of IS to be critical to type 3 innovation, the model may benefit from further refinement. As such, strategic importance is reflective of the innovation dynamic as it provides a favorable context for IS innovation to occur. A ‘strategic imperative’ on the other hand is directly concerned with the content of strategy, which often includes the means by which an organization chooses to compete in the marketplace. For type 3 innovations this might force a fast sequencing in the order of strategy, structure and work processes. For instance, an interorganizational system implemented for competitive purposes to build in customer switching costs might require rapid intervention of strategy, change in customer-facing structures and processes to leverage the innovation and successfully implement the strategy. Contrast this with the MRP system discussed by Swanson, where the sequence is opposite, i.e., the innovation was brought in primarily to improve work processes and subsequently over time it evolved through affecting the administrative structures and became recognised as a strategic system. The innovation dynamic would hold true much stronger for the latter case than the former. A strategy imperative as opposed to an innovation imperative could affect the model in two ways by (a) reducing the potential impact of variables involved in the innovation dynamic (propositions 1 and 2), and (b) compressing the time cycle in order to rapidly enhance business effectiveness through the innovation (proposition 3).

Innovation is widely accepted as “the early adoption of an idea by one of a set of organizations with similar goals” (Becker & Whisler, 1967). The two contrasting forces described (the innovation dynamic and the strategy imperative) represent alternate forms of innovation and both are recognised in the innovation literature. However, significantly more emphasis is placed on the ‘innovation dynamic’ as the force creating innovativeness as a systemic organizational property within the context of some generic strategy. Table 2 highlights the distinctions between these approaches. The strategic imperative is usually driven by a market-based strategy (e.g., respond to a competitive threat or differentiating a product or service). This strategy permeates down the organizational structure and creates the impetus for inno vation that is output oriented (e.g., productivity, profitability). The velocity or rate of change is usually high due to the need for responsiveness. The strategic impetus usually creates changes in structure and work process to support the strategy (e.g., in the EDI system discussed earlier). The strategy is directed at a particular innovation or encourages a set of innovations to support strategic objectives. In contrast, the innovation dynamic fosters innovation at the sub-unit level by creating the context for an innovative process. Institutional mechanisms (e.g., communications networks, formal linking mechanisms, incentives) can nurture the diversity and integration necessary for innovativeness. Change is usually slower and more recursive in an innovative culture. It is usually undirected, which leads to the problem of managing part-whole relationships or effective coupling of sub-unit innovation (Peters & Waterman, 1982; Van De Ven, 1986). Process change or technology based change usually precedes structural or strategic change, depending on the strategic nature of the innovation and its effective management. The contextual variables in the innovation dynamic tend to be more consistent across organizational contexts as can be seen from the consist ency of results for innovation adoption (Kwon & Zmud, 1987).

It should be pointed out however, that these two forms of innovation are not necessarily mutually exclusive as illustrated in Table 3. The dimensions of the 2 × 2 grid represent the variance in effects of the two ‘forces’. Arguably the best context for innovation would be in Quadrant I or focused innovation, where both the innovation dynamic and strategy play strong roles. The interaction effect of these two forces could lead to directed innovation that is effectively communicated, nurtured and sustained through the innovation dynamic. Thus, while the modes might be independent of each other, their impact on innovation is not. This type of context will have more secondary innovation, decentralised responsibility and adaptation of innovation to changing environments. The relationship between strategy, structure, process and technology will be more iterative than linear.

Extension 1: In terms of Swanson’s model, we propose that such organizations will have strong order effects due to strategy and weak order effects due to diffusion of innovation from the sub-unit (IS) to the organization. Strategy could be directed at type 1, 2 or 3 innovation, although the business impact of type 3 innovation will be stronger. The strong and weak order effects nurture a more pervasive and directed impact for IS in lieu of organizational needs.

In Quadrant II, forced innovation occurs where the effects of the strategy imperative dominate those of the innovation dynamic. Such organizations might have strong dependence on planning systems, although often such systems institutionalise the process of planning rather than push innovation. Such centralised imperatives (depending on the scope of change) might require effective change management in order to alleviate resistance to innovation. Innovations are usually implemented through directive with linear changes in structure and processes, unless repeated strategic imperatives force further change.

Extension 2: Strong order effects of strategy for organizations in this quadrant will possibly force type 3 innovation, and subsequent type 2 and type 1 innovation. Diffusion or weak order effects will not be nurtured unless preceded by a strategic imperative.

In Quadrant 3, grass roots innovation, sub-unit innovation is encouraged by formal and informal institutional structures. These dynamics could be different at the subunit level and the organizational level, encouraging different levels of innovation.

Extension 3: Swanson’s model holds best in this quadrant. Type 1 innovation could be fostered in the functional core differently from type 2 and type 3 innovation, which could be fostered independently. Weak order or diffusion effects could foster IS innovation outside the functional core. Strong order effects propelled by strategy content are not significant, however, the importance of IS to the business could nurture type 3 innovation.

Quadrant 4, haphazard innovation, occurs randomly when opportunities (or problems) and solutions happen to intersect in the organization or its sub-units. No institutional mechanism or strategy brings about the innovation.

In conclusion it should be emphasised that pure modes of innovation are unlikely. Table 3 describes four scenarios in which technology adoption, in part, could be based on the degree to which the strategy imperative and the innovation dynamic prevail in an organizational context. If the objective of theory is to understand IS innovation in an organizational context, both forces must be explicitly recognised. In other words, strategy as a potent force should be examined in the study of IS innovation since it can intervene with the innovation dynamic and compress the adoption cycle. This is particularly true in the context of today’s information technologies and systems, many of which are enabling fundamental organizational change. Any disparity in empirical results should attempt to account for the strategic imperative. In other words, the early adoption of innovative IS (particularly systems that have an organizational impact outside the IS function) such as teleconferencing or expert systems might be instigated due to strategic imperatives that reinforce or counteract the innovation dynamic. Such imperatives reflect the contingencies in the organization and must be accounted for when considering any model for IS innovation.

## Technological impacts

Another related caveat is the nature of information technology itself. There are a large number of complex technologies being introduced and the interrelationships between these technologies are increasing. While IT by itself is neutral, the scope of impact could differ across technologies. Some technologies relate to individuals or groups while others are inherently infrastructural (e.g., organizational networks). These infrastructural technologies by their inherent nature can facilitate organizational integration, require widespread participation (i.e., a critical mass) in order to be effective and can often be applied to change the fundamental nature of a business process, usually transcending the various cores discussed by Swanson. For instance, large-scale networks and work flow systems may readily be applied to change the basic ways work is conducted or decisions are made. Shared resource systems can allow sequential processes to be restructured into parallel processes (Teng et al, 1994). Electronic platforms change the structural relationships between buyers and sellers in the marketplace. Infrastructural technologies might intervene with the innovation dynamic, indirectly through strategy (since they are usually more elaborate organizational systems) or directly through more immediate change of structural aspects of the organization.

Extension 4: Integrative technologies do not easily classify themselves as type 1, 2 or 3 innovation. They often serve to integrate the three types of innovation having strong and weak order effects through their integrative nature. They might also be a precursor for further type 1, 2 or 3 IS innovation. As such, the dynamic involved in their adoption might be different, involving aspects of the innovation dynamic, the strategic imperative and other considerations such as organizational infrastructure and integrative needs.

A second intervening technological factor is the sophistication of the IT involved. This can be interpreted in a manner consistent with notions of technological complexity discussed in prior innovation work (Tornatzky & Klein, 1982; Grover, 1993). This refers to the extent expertise is required to implement and use the IT. Technologies that have higher sophistication often require expertise of the functional IS core. This core offers the repository of (professional) skills required to adopt the innovation. Therefore, IS innovation within the technical core (type 1) could be stimulated directly based on technological sophistication of the IT involved, or indirectly (i.e., through type 2 or type 3 innovations) where strong order effects create the need to modify IS administrative or work processes. High business value could accelerate strong order effects of a technologically sophisticated innovation in the business core (type 3) and the weak order effects of an innovation in the technical core (type 1).

Extension 5: The underlying technological sophistication of the IS innovation could have a profound impact on the IS functional core. Sophisticated IT could influence the IS administrative and work processes (e.g., require specialists) and could accelerate strong order effects for innovations implemented in the business core (types 2 and 3).

Another intervening factor in this model is the halflife of the IT curve and subsequently most IS innovation. While Swanson suggests that IS innovation continues to evolve with technology, this evolution is now more frequently being interrupted by discontinuous change. This raises three issues: (a) how are changes in base technologies or new ways of integrating existing technologies reflected in the innovation dynamic? Hypothesis related to structural effects fostering innovation might break down beyond evolutionary cycles. For instance, a PC ten years ago is very different from the client-server architecture in which it is configured today; (b) older technologies like transactional databases might reflect innovative dynamics at the time of early adoption. However, as cost/performance ratios decline, the adoption decision might not reflect innovativeness and the duration of the early adoption time frame could be very small compared to the later adoption time frame. This would dilute the effects of the innovation dynamic; (c) in an era of omnipresent technology, early adopters are risk takers and might undertake substantial changes in work processes to leverage technology.

Extension 6: Later adopters might ride the technology learning curve and reach business functionality faster. Therefore, type 2 and 3 innovation might be reflective of the domination of late adopters that leveraged work of pioneers rather than any true innovation dynamic. All these factors could reduce the effects of the model.

## Discussion

Existing research on IS innovation typically studies single innovations in isolation and does not effectively integrate notions of IS innovation with organizational innovation. The Swanson model provides a useful typology of IS innovation that can form the foundation for innovation theory in this important area. We believe that the model is significant in its ability to distinguish IS innovations and their antecedents and bridge the relationship between IS innovation and general organizational innovation. Three broad propositions were presented to summarise the model. Six ‘extensions’ or further considerations were discussed in an attempt to provide guidance on how the model can be expanded into a more generalisable and testable theory for IS innovation. Particularly, explicit consideration of the strategic imperative and technological impacts can enable greater explained variance in any testing of the model.<sup>3</sup>

In summary, the extensions described above alert us to the presence of ‘moderating’ variables that could affect the three propositions discussed earlier. Moderating variables intervene and affect the strength of relationships. These moderators were framed in the above as contingencies that influence applicability of Swanson’s model. Two major sets of contingencies were considered. The first raises the possibility of strategy and the strategic imperative (i.e., what is the organization trying to do and how powerful is the strategic push throughout the organization?) as the dominant antecedent to IS innovation. Four scenarios contrast the domination of the strategic imperative and the innovation dynamic (presumed by Swanson). In these scenarios strategy forces can dominate through strong order effects, and limit the impacts of the innovation forces (described in Proposition 2). In contrast, the innovation dynamic and Swanson’s model would hold true in organizations where technology is not always implemented by fiat.

The second set of moderators raises contingencies regarding technology that could affect the model. Integrative technologies can encompass type 1, 2, and 3 innovation, and may involve consideration of organizational infrastructure and integrative needs that go beyond the innovation and strategic dynamic. Similarly, technologies that are highly sophisticated can have direct implications for type 1 innovation regardless of other factors. Furthermore, firms that ride the technology learning curve could reach business functionality faster and adopt more type 2 and 3 innovations. This copycat syndrome allows innovation as a result of learning in the marketplace and adopting at an opportune time, rather than factors in the innovation, and strategic dynamic.

Therefore, while the tri-core model offers a powerful foundation for integrating and building research in IS innovation, it is incomplete. Strategic and technological contingencies could moderate the model’s influences, and must be recognised, and systematically studied in order that we evolve toward a more comprehensive model for IS innovation. In addition, some variables like industry sector, competitiveness, industry concentration, etc (Gatignon & Robertson, 1989) have been examined in innovation, and can also contribute to explaining noise in the model either directly or as precursors to strategy. In summary, this article makes the case for more complete consideration of variables to frame the basic typology provided by Swanson within a contingent context, and to provide an effective basis for structuring this disparate area.

## Conclusion

With the substantial recent interest in business process re-engineering, many organizations are restructuring their inter-functional processes. As a strategy-initiated imperative involving integrative and sophisticated information technologies, often with a cost motif of leveraging investments in information technology, rapid and substantial IS innovation is occurring. The innovation dynamic implicit in the Swanson model (and in much of the prior innovation work) needs to be adapted to accommodate these changes. We believe that recognition of strategy as representative of the organizational intent to change in a certain way is important in any theoretical model explaining IS innovation. While this paper has raised more questions than answers and many of the propositions need to be further examined, we believe that continued debate is necessary to develop viable theory. This would indicate the need to integrate substantive concepts from innovation, organizational learning (Argyris & Schon, 1978) strategic management and organizational change (Lant & Mezias, 1992) in order to provide a more complete model of IS innovation. For instance, further examination of innovation–decision predictors, particularly those that are context dependent (e.g., compatibility) is warranted (Meyer & Goes, 1988).

Also, the influence of external factors (e.g., vendors) on IS adoption would be useful, especially in an environment where decision authority for IS resources is increasingly decentralised. Another avenue is to reexamine the origins of the dual core model, which was based on socio-technical change theory (Passmore et al, 1982), and integrate it with innovation theory. Yet another is to examine taxonomies proposed in re-engineering as they relate to the model. For instance Venkatraman’s (1991) five levels of process redesign (local, infrastructural, process, scope, network) might exhibit parallels with movement from the inner core to the business core of the model.
