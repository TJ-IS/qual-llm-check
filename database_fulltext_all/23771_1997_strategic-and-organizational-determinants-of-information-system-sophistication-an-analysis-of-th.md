# Strategic and organizational determinants of information system sophistication: an analysis of the uptake of database marketing in the financial services industry

K Fletcher<sup>1</sup> and G Wright<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, University of East Anglia, Norwich, Norfolk NR4 7TJ; <sup>2</sup>School of Economics and Business Studies, University of Leeds, Leeds LS2 9JT, UK

This paper investigates strategic and organizational determinants of adoption/non-adoption of strategic marketing information systems that differ in degree of sophistication. We identify potentially relevant variables and relate them to the use of database marketing (DBM) systems. Using an obtained sample of 49% of all major banks, building societies and insurance companies in the UK, we compare users and non-users and, within the users, we identify intercorrelations with those variables linked to degree of sophistication.

Adoption of DBM systems is closely associated with the organization placing a greater strategic importance on direct marketing decisions, strategic integration of information technology (IT) and marketing, the possession of a large direct marketing grouping and a strong marketing and information orientation. These factors, coupled with a greater degree of organizational slack and weaker organization control of resources and more decisive decision making, which separate adopting organizations from non-adopters.

Once adoption has occurred, greater DBM sophistication is closely linked to the power of the direct marketing function, the importance placed on the strategic integration of IT and marketing, the size of the direct marketing grouping, and the organization’s overall information orientation.

## Introduction

There have been many authors who have argued that competitive advantage can be gained through the strategic application of information technology (IT). Porter and Millar (1985) suggested that the competitive forces in an industry could be substantially changed by IT and that analysis of the value chain, both of the vertical and horizontal linkages in the marketing system and also within the organization itself, could show strategic opportunities. Parsons (1983) also saw IT as a competitive weapon with impact at the industry level, firm level and strategy level. Wiseman and MacMillan (1984) took a market oriented approach to IT deployment and argued for strategic targets (suppliers, customers and competitors) and strategic thrusts (differentiation, cost, innovation), in either defensive or offensive mode. McFarlan (1983, 1984) posited that as organization’s accepted arguments to use IT strategically then IT would move from a back-office supporting role to a front-office position. He presented two dimensions (the strategic impact of (a) existing operating systems and (b) application development portfolio) by which companies can position themselves depending upon the degree and nature of their IT use. This view is complemented by a Price Waterhouse report (1989/1990) which predicted that, by 1995, 50% of the benefits from IT investment would be in front office applications compared to 34% in 1985. Price Waterhouse argued that the majority of these new applications would be to support the marketing function.

However, in the 1980’s an increasing amount of evidence was published which concluded the move to strategic use of IT would not be as easy or as successful as the early writers had implied. For example, Shultz and Dewar (1984) argued that the technological challenge to marketing management was to adjust IT systems to a changing marketplace by adapting existing organization structures, processes and skills. Other authors have also reported that marketing managers did not find it easy to make strategic use of information systems (IS) (Fletcher, 1983; Oasis, 1989) partly due to inadequacies of extant IT systems when matched with the special needs of marketing strategy, but also because of a lack of strategic focus in companies and conflicts between IT managers and marketing managers.

It is perhaps not surprising that IT began to be seen as a competitive burden (Warner, 1987), or that the claims of gaining competitive advantage should be criticised as dogma (Benjamin et al, 1990). Indeed, the cycle of competitive attack and counter-attack (Wiseman, 1988) meant that immediate competitive advantage was not sustainable. A succession of major failures of IT (Caulkin, 1989), allied to claims that the productivity gains from computerisation were ‘puny’ (Bowen, 1986; Business Week, 1993), meant that a re-assessment of the situation was due. The overall conclusion has been that adoption of IT is, in itself, not sufficient for competitive advantage if the organizational and competitive context within which it is implemented is unsuitable. ‘Business process redesign’ have become the new keywords. Here, business strategy and IT strategy are integrated, IS became customer-based, and management and planning processes are changed to accommodate this reorientation (Watkins, 1992).

However, the lack of systematic empirical research in the use of IT for competitive advantage has been stressed by King and his co-authors (King et al, 1989) who noted:

Since 1990, several studies have redressed the balance. For example, IT management sophistication was conceptualised and empirically measured by Raymond and Pare (1992) and was later related to organizational structure and performance (Raymond et al, 1995). Boynton et al (1994) studied the degree of IT use and its relationship with ‘absorptive capacity’, which they theorised was dependent upon IT knowledge, process effectiveness, and IT management climate. They found that managerial knowledge of IT was critical for bringing about high use of IT within business units and allowing an organization to move beyond the more obvious applications to more strategic uses. Sabherwal and Vijayasarthy (1994), who also noted the lack of empirical research, found that an organization’s IS maturity (often taken as a surrogate for sophistication) did not seem to enhance adoption of telecommunication links. However, information intensity in the product and value chain were positively associated with the nature and degree of organizational use of telecommunications—as expected.

Additionally, Sabherwal and Kirs (1994) argued that the effective use and sophistication of IT is achieved by matching organizational capabilities and market context rather than by an uncritical adoption and development of systems. Indeed, Ramaswani et al (1992) have argued that sophisticated IT management is strategically orientated and integrated with an organization’s competitive strategy.

The importance of contextual factors on IT use and sophistication has thus been strongly supported empirically. The present study continues and develops this approach by studying the effects of selected strategic and organization variables on the use and sophistication of IT.

Our study focuses on a single information intensive industry, financial services, and a particular marketing application of IT, database marketing (DBM). Intuitively, it would seem that respondents in information intensive industries would have more fully thought through their opinions on enabling factors in the deployment of IT for strategic advantage (cf Sabherwal & Vijayasarathy, 1994). As in the current study, Sabherwal et al (1995) selected an information intensive industry to increase the likelihood that the issues addressed were important to the respondents. It has been argued that focusing on a single industry, within a common environment, controls for external influences (Child and Smith, 1987) and enhances internal validity.

DBM, a specialised use of IT was chosen for the focus of our study since it has both tactical and strategic applications, requires a re-focusing of the organization, and is of particular interest to a number of industries, particularly financial services. The methodology section gives the definition of DBM used in this study. The UK financial services market was deregulated in 1986, leading to a transformation in its activities. As the previously separate sectors of banking, insurance and building societies began to compete for customers and entered each others’ markets, the resulting customer focus frequently required changes to organizational structures. New businesses were also created, as with First Direct and Direct Line insurance, (subsidiaries of Midland Bank and Royal Bank of Scotland, respectively) to manage the new customer-orientated software and relationships.

By focusing on a particular technology (DBM) and a particular industry (financial services) internal validity of the study is enhanced.

## Research model

The research reported in this paper measures a number of organizational and strategic variables and relates them to the use of strategic IS in marketing. Our focus is on understanding the way in which internal strategic and ‘people’ issues relate to IS sophistication and applications. Given such an understanding, we aim to identify those factors of crucial importance in the development of sophisticated IT applications.

Overall, our research takes the view that the overall strategic approach of the organization will interact with the organization’s structural characteristics which, together, will facilitate or hinder the decision to adopt an innovation such as strategic IT. Numerous authors have commented on the way that IT can give competitive advantage when used strategically, and Shaw and Stone (1988) and Fletcher et al (1990) have argued that DBM can play a similar strategic role.

It has been argued that the capability of firms to enter the strategic IT phase will depend on three elements; their existing level of IT operations (Gibson & Nolan, 1974; Nolan, 1979; McFarlan & McKenny, 1983), and the strategic and organizational context within which decisions are made and implemented. The strategic context relates to a number of variables which reflect the ability of the firm to think and act strategically. The organizational context relates to these factors which will inhibit or support such a strategic orientation and reflects the past experiences and organizational learning that has taken place, as well as the structures created to manage operations. It includes organizational culture and decision making modes, and the social interactions within the organizational structure and with the outside world.

In this paper, the strategic context variables studied were the strategic importance of IT, marketing and direct marketing decisions, investment appraisal methods and suitability, strategic integration, and the nature of the competitive environment. The organizational context variables studied were size, organizational culture (particularly relating to IT and marketing), internal networks, degree of organizational control and slack, the power of direct marketing, and the nature of organizational decision making. Our analysis of the relevance of these variables to the successful implementation of IT at a strategic level is detailed in the next section. A subsequent section details, (1) how measurement of these variables was operationalised in questionnaire form, and (2) the nature of our sample of the UK financial services sector.

## Strategic context variables

## Strategic importance and integration

The successful strategic application of IT requires not only a recognition of the strategic importance of certain functional decisions but also the integration of business and IT strategy and a common understanding of aim, objectives and needs from users and suppliers of the IT system, (Abernathy & Clark, 1985). Thus, rather than strategy being imposed on functional areas these areas should themselves be involved in the strategic planning process and so, in the context of DBM applications, marketing and IT investment decisions should be linked and integrated.

There is an increasing amount of debate about the desirability of linking business and IT strategy (Price Waterhouse, 1989/1990). Goldsmith (1991) argues that IT strategy is simply business strategy with an information hat on, and that information strategies need to be developed in the same process and at the same time as the business strategy, if competitive advantage is to be secured from IT systems. While this may be true, the work of Watkins (1992) shows that, in the finance industry, this is far from being the norm.

For firms with a high degree of marketing and IT sophistication (treated as an organizational variable in this study, see later), the linkages between the two areas will be clear to the firm and DBM usage is likely to be well advanced. If deficiencies exist in either of these two areas of expertise then the stage of IT development is likely to be less advanced. Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) introduce the concepts of differentiation and integration in studying organizational effectiveness and argue that, as an organization grows it differentiates into specialisms which then have to be re-integrated back into the total organization to ensure sub-units do not have conflicting aims and objectives. This can be seen in the growth of specialist direct marketing departments which are often separate from their ‘parent’ marketing department. Shaw and Stone (1988) suggest that in the final stage of DBM development (which, they argue, has not yet been reached by any organization), the direct marketing department will become the parent and be in charge of all IT driven marketing communications and activities and subsume the traditional marketing department. The integration of marketing and IT personnel into ‘strategic activities and decisions, and the integration of marketing and IT investment decision making, is thus likely to increase the probability that an IT-based marketing strategy, such as DBM, is implemented and that sophisticated systems will be developed.

Appendix 1 details our measures of: (1) the strategic importance of marketing; (2) the strategic importance of direct marketing; and (3) the strategic integration of IT and marketing. Cronbach Alphas (conducted in-sample) were 0.64, 0.83 and 0.71 for the three scales, respectively.

## Investment appraisal

The difficulty of justifying strategic IT spend, as compared to operational IT spend, has increasingly become an issue as boards of directors have been asked to make large investments with little clear benefit (Price Waterhouse, 1992/1993). It has been argued that the focus of investment appraisal has moved from efficiency, to effectiveness, and now to strategic advantage (David Silk cited in Price Waterhouse, 1989/90, p 23).

In the past, the nature of investment criteria used to evaluate projects has focused on efficiency and cost reduction and ‘hard’ operational, rather than ‘soft’ strategic benefits have dominated. The difficulty of measuring the benefits has had its impact on levels of risk accepted. Traditional cost benefit analysis is no longer adequate for most IS applications that are innovative or produce or enhance revenue. Parker and Densch (1988) suggest that new criteria are needed to justify the long term investments. They suggest a mix of traditional cost benefit analysis and more qualitative approaches. It is possible that the type of investment appraisal mechanism is linked to the adoption of DBM, which creates the proposition that the more traditional the investment appraisal method the less likely that firms will invest in IT for strategic advantage. Appendix 1 details our measure of investment appraisal. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha for this measure was 0.68.

## Competitive environment

Attitudes towards the competitive environment may reduce and delay investment in strategic IT applications. Within the context of business markets, O’Neil et al (1973) report findings that early adopters are more likely to be: (1) in industries with a high rate of technical change; (2) operating in industries facing intense competition; and (3) be firms which view the innovation as presenting both a relative advantage and a degree of compatibility with previously adopted products (see also Robertson & Gatignon, 1986).

In general, there continues to be a lack of empirical evidence on the influences of adopter industry characteristics on early or late adoption of innovations. One study (Bamossy & Eenennaam, 1988) studied this area using an IT innovation, the use of personal computers. They argued that both current levels of competitive intensity, and expectations of future levels of competition were important to adoption decisions. It seems likely, therefore, that a measure of perception of adopter industry competitive intensity will be a relevant predictor variable of DBM adoption and sophistication. Appendix 1 details our measure of perception of the competitive environment. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.77.

## Organizational context variables

## Size and organizational innovativeness

The size of an organization has consistently been found to be positively related to innovativeness (cf Rogers, 1983). It is easily measured and is probably a surrogate measure of other variables, such as total resources, organizational structure, etc.

Nooteboom (1989) points out that studies of the effect of firm size on the adoption of process innovations, such as strategic IT applications, have not been extensively studied. However, David (1983) and Davies (1979) proposed models which identified firm size as a critical determinant of adoption. While it was initially thought that small firms were more innovative, they argued that only larger firms had the necessary resources to be innovative. Nooteboom suggested that the generation of fundamentally new ‘high technologies’ which are state of the art and have large scale applications are the province of large firms, while novel adoption and combinations of product, service and market are the province of smaller firms. He argued that the reason for this is that the former innovations require large teams of highly specialised labour and large capital outlays, which only large firms can sustain, while the latter innovations require close interaction with customers, flexibility and unorthodox applications which is more likely to be the case with small firms. According to Nooteboom, small firms are systematically lagging behind in the adoption of process innovations developed by other firms, although it is not clear why.

In the case of DBM, the technology of the financial services industry is mainframe-based and thus the disruption and cost of implementing DBM is greater than if a PC application could be adopted. This leads to the proposition that the larger the organization, the greater the likelihood of adoption of DBM. In our study, we measured the size of the marketing, direct marketing and IT departments.

## Organization culture

Piercy (1985) has long argued that there is a divergence between the prescriptive literature on marketing planning and actual practice, and that it can be explained by an analysis of the corporate environment for marketing management. He argued that variables such as organization structure, the configuration of corporate decision making processes, patterns of managerial behaviour and the impact of corporate culture, are relevant variables.

Culture can be seen as a system of shared values (what is important) and beliefs (how things work) that interact with a company’s personnel, organizational structures and control systems to produce behavioural norms (the way we do things here) (Uttal, 1983). If a discrepancy exists between the strategy decided upon and corporate culture then implementation problems may arise (Kono, 1990). Firms will normally have a dominant culture, but alternative subcultures will exist. The individual will have little power to control culture (Green, 1988) and decisions on strategies must therefore take note of the organizational culture (Parasuraman & Deshpande, 1984). Kanter (1983, 1990) has argued that there is no single type of organizational culture which supports the implementation of innovations, and that pragmatic decisions have to be made on this basis of ‘what will work for us’.

The culture of the organization is thus a major factor that could impact upon adoption and sophistication of IT innovations. For the purpose of this study, we investigate the degree of marketing and information orientation, since previous work in this area (Fletcher et al, 1990, 1992), has theorised these two aspects of organizational culture as being important prerequisites to the adoption and implementation of DBM.

A marketing orientation is based in both values and beliefs and, as Kotler (1992) argued, must be a part of the wider organizational culture if marketing personnel are to implement innovatory procedures and plans. Thus the existence of a marketing specialism within an organization and its integration into other activities is not on its own sufficient to ensure the organization is market led. Indeed, some organizations may not have a specialist marketing department but still fully accept the ‘customer orientation’ which diffuses through the organization and affects all procedures and controls. It seemed intuitively reasonable that the greater the marketing orientation of a firm then the more likely a need for DBM will be recognised and the greater the likelihood of adoption. Our measure of marketing orientation is given in Appendix 1. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.86.

We define information orientation as the recognition of the importance of customer and marketing information to the strategic activities of the firm and the existence of user-friendly infrastructures to support the collection, processing and dissemination of such information. Our conceptualisation thus has similarities with the concept of IT maturity. A high degree of information orientation thus represents the progress of the IT function into the strategic IT era (Sabherwal & Vijayasarathy, 1994) and as having the orientation necessary to support a strategic marketing application such as DBM. Our measure of information orientation is also detailed in Appendix 1. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.87.

Information is an essential ingredient to strategic planning, and can be obtained from both internal and external sources. Many authors have commented on the variability of information usage by firms and have related this to the lack of sophistication of IS. Often, IS within organizations tend to be accounting systems with a historical, internal focus. Development of strategically-oriented IS is an evolutionary process (Gibson & Nolan, 1974) and the further along this experience curve a firm is the more likely the firm will have the climate, skills, technology and information flows to support DBM.

Some firms may be sophisticated in their use of information, but not use it for strategic or marketing purposes. For these firms the necessary marketing sophistication is lacking. Other firms may have a high marketing orientation, but not have experience in the enabling IT hardware and software. The study of these two elements and their relationship with the adoption and sophistication of DBM is thus an important addition to our understanding of the use of customer focused strategic IS.

## Internal networks

Rogers (1983) refers to interconnectedness as being positively associated with organizational innovativeness. Interconnectedness is the degree to which the units in the organization are linked by interpersonal networks, allowing new ideas to flow more easily and rapidly. The existence of formal and informal internal networks is likely to be positively related to adoption of strategic IT interventions such as DBM. Our measure of internal networking is detailed in Appendix 1. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.63.

## Organizational slack

Organizational slack is a term first coined by Cyert and March (1963) and can be defined as the degree to which uncommitted resources are available to an organization. Whether these uncommitted resources are made available will presumably depend on the outcome of other influences and decisions, however the existence of organizational slack may be an enabling condition for the adoption of DBM. Our measure of organizational slack is given in Appendix 1. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.66

## Organizational control

The level of control placed on IT developments was suggested by Nolan (1979) as being an important part of the organizational environment. In a control environment, financial and performance management systems are used to ensure that DP activities are effective and efficient. Whereas, in the slack environment, sophisticated controls are absent and more resources are available than are strictly necessary to get the job done. Nolan has argued that the balance of these two variables will relate to the stage of organizational learning that the organization has reached with regard to its IT development. The strategic use of IT is likely to relate not only to resources relating to computing facilities but also on marketing’s ability to gather and electronically store data in a customer information file. Both of these enabling conditions may be subject to a lesser or greater degree of organizational control. Our measure of organizational control is detailed in Appendix 1. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.85.

## Direct marketing power

Power is an extremely difficult concept to define or operationalise, although it is becoming increasingly important in the area of relationship marketing. In an organizational context, it relates to the degree of which a person or department controls important and relevant resources or functions. A powerful direct marketing department will want control over marketing activities, marketing information, and the marketing software necessary to run direct marketing campaigns. A lack of such control will hinder the growth of relevant applications, will constrain strategic applications which cross functional boundaries, and suggest that a move towards DBM activities is unlikely.

This analysis leads to the proposition that the greater the existing power of the direct marketing department the greater the likelihood that they will be able to promote the adoption of DBM and influence its growth. Our measure of direct marketing power is detailed in Appendix 1. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.85.

## Incrementalism in decision making

Many organizations have to make a decision whether to make a major ‘revolutionary’ jump to an advanced stage of IT sophistication, or whether the same goal should be achieved in an ‘evolutionary’ way by a series of smaller, incremental steps. The first approach carries the greatest risk, but also the greatest rewards from gaining ‘firstmover’ advantage. The second, incremental approach means that the organization can learn as it proceeds, consolidating existing decisions before moving on. However, such an organization may be left behind by more dynamic competitors. The way in which an organization approaches strategic decisions is thus fundamental to its strategic approach. Intuitively, organizations which are relatively less incremental in their decision making may adopt strategic technologies, such as DBM, earlier and to a relatively more sophisticated level. Our measure of incrementalism in decision making is detailed in Appendix 1. The in-sample Cronbach Alpha was 0.68.

## Methodology

To reduce the influence of industry variables, one industry sector, the UK financial services market was chosen to investigate the strategic and organizational determinants of IS sophistication. This industry is noted both for its high degree of change since its deregulation in 1987 and its high interest in the use of strategic IT in marketing and sales. The sampling frame was named IT and marketing directors (or their equivalent) in the major banks, building societies, insurance companies in the UK financial services market. This consisted of the largest 28 banks (by net interest income and other operating income), the largest 44 building societies (by total assets) and the largest 108 life and general insurance companies (by gross premium income). Two mailings of a 12-page questionnaire and covering letter received a usable response rate of 109 (30% of respondents), almost equally representing IT and marketing responsibility, covering 86 companies (48% of firms). For industrial surveys of this type, this is considered to be a medium to good response rate. For those instances where both the IT and marketing director of the same firm responded, we analysed the questionnaires for differences in perceptions/attitudes. Previous studies had noted a gulf between the attitudes and beliefs of IT personnel and other functional managers (Oasis, 1989). Our analysis found a surprising agreement of views with no important differences reaching significance. The results reported here are therefore taken in aggregate. The sample response rate was compared to industry structure and was found to be representative of the industry. The questionnaire was designed such that respondents did not need to have IT-based marketing systems to answer the questions.

## Measurement of DBM usage and sophistication

Our major dependent variable in the current study was whether or not the respondent’s organization had a DBM system. This was evaluated by asking the respondent two linked questions:

(1) Direct marketing has been defined as an interactive system of marketing, based on a mailing list, which uses one or more marketing media to effect a measurable response and/or transaction at any location. Do you use direct marketing?

(2) Database marketing stores this response, and adds other customer information (eg, lifestyles transaction history etc) on an electronic database memory and uses it as a basis for longer term customer loyalty programmes, to facilitate future contacts, and to enable planning of all marketing.

Do you have a database marketing system? YES. . . . . NO. . . . .

If yes, what is the level of sophistication of your present system?

Low Level 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 High Level

Fifty-two per cent of respondents claimed to have a DBM system, (54% of companies) with a mean sophistication level of 3.4 (standard deviation 1.8) on the sevenpoint scale. Elsewhere in the questionnaire we asked three further questions that we felt to be additional, less judgmental, measures of DBM sophistication. There were: (12) a count of the number of types of results of campaigns that are measured; (2) a count of the amount of customer information held in the customer information file; and (3) a count of the degree to which the ‘virtuous circle’ of planning, implementation and control (Shaw, 1991, p 77) was complete. The exact questions that we utilised are given as the first three questions in Appendix 2. Table 1 shows the intercorrelations between these four measures. All correlations are positive and significant at the 5% (two-tailed) level. The next stage in our analysis was to normalise each of the four measures (as they are of different kinds, one being on a sevenpoint scale and the rest being counts) and finally compute an individual mean of the normalised measures and use that as our measure of DBM sophistication in our subsequent analysis.

## Findings

Differences between users and non-users of DBM in terms of our measures of strategic and organizational context.

Tables 2 and 3 present the mean scores on each of our strategic and organizational context variables for our 57 non-users and 52 users of DBM, and reports differences between the means using one-tailed tests of significance.

Inspection of Table 2 shows that, in general, while the strategic importance of marketing is perceived to be high, the perceived strategic importance of direct marketing is relatively low. (Recall that the maximum score on both scales is 21). The degree of strategic integration of IT and marketing is also perceived to be relatively high (maximum score 63). However, perceptions of the adequacy of investment appraisal methods were around the mid-point of the scale that we utilised. Importantly, the competitive environment in which our sample of financial services organizations are operating was perceived to be intense (maximum score 14).

## Regression analysis on DBM sophistication

Identification of those strategic and organizational context variables relating to DBM application sophistication are of importance since they differentiate the characteristics of those organizations obtaining the strategic benefits which justify the high investment and disruption caused by the adoption of DBM. For this reason, our next analysis used all of our strategic and organizational context variables in stepwise linear regression in order to investigate the importance of these variables as predictors of our composite measure of DBM sophistication (described earlier). It may be that our predictor variables are more or less closely associated with degree of sophistication of DBM systems, once adopted, than the decision to adopt, per se.

Table 4 presents the standardised beta-coefficients for the variables that entered a stepwise regression equation. The overall adjusted R<sup>2</sup> for this equation was 0.43 which indicates that 43% of the variation in our composite measure of DBM sophistication is explained by the variations in our four predictor variables: information orientation, DBM power, size of direct marketing, and strategic integration of IT and marketing.

Since none of the intercorrelations within our total set of predictor variables was above 0.53 and only three were above 0.4, we conclude that multicollinearity is not a problem with the current data set (cf Berry & Feldman, 1985).

## Discussion

This research extends previous research by empirically measuring a number of strategic and organizational variables (which have seldom been included in a single research study), and testing the relative importance of these variables to the adoption and sophistication of an IT application. A number of strategic and organizational variables separated adopter firms from non-adopter firms, but three out of five of our strategic context predictor variables did not differentiate adopters and nonadopters. Conversely, seven of the ten organizational context variables successfully differentiated between adopters and non-adopters. When related to sophistication, a reduced, and different, subset of variables were important—confirming the conceptual difference of adoption and implementation. Overall, organizational variables seem to be driving the adoption and sophistication of this strategic IT intervention, rather than strategic imperatives.

Despite the generally higher rating given by our respondents to the strategic importance of marketing compared to the strategic importance of direct marketing, it was only the latter which differentiated respondents. This finding supports the notion that direct marketing is a distinctive subset of marketing.

The strategic importance of marketing to an organization’s success seems to be widely accepted in the financial services industry, to the point where this no longer differentiates organizations. The perception of a low strategic importance for direct marketing, although separating users and non-users, suggests that most organizations do not, as yet, appreciate the opportunities that direct marketing holds for long term competitive advantage, suggesting a more limited tactical role is envisaged. If direct marketing personnel are not intimately involved in strategic decisions then this separation indicates the strategic role of DBM may not be fully understood. This inference is supported by the work of Fletcher and Wright (1995) who documented the relatively low sophistication of DBM in the financial services industry. As the sophistication of DBM applications develop, then it is likely that the strategic importance of direct marketing decisions will become increasingly recognised, and the strategic importance of direct marketing could then be expected to outgrow that of its parent function.

The separation of IT planning and strategic planning has been recognised as a major hindrance to the successful use of IT for competitive advantage in the finance sector. This is because, as discussed earlier, the degree of collaboration between functional areas and IT is an indicator of the ability of the organization to move from an administrative focus to a strategic focus. A measure of an organization’s strategic sophistication, and its ability to capitalise on IT opportunities, is thus the degree to which functional and IT areas are linked. Overall, the information intensive nature of the industry would justify a high degree of integration, and the recent changes in market and competitive context would encourage an increased focus on marketing. We theorised that DBM is a relevant response to these competitive pressures and a rational development of marketing practices. Our findings show that marketing and IT had a high degree of strategic integration, differentiated adopters from non-adopters, and also was one of the key factors related to sophistication. Our respondents are therefore well placed to capitalise on their IT investment, with an awareness of the criticality of IT developments and applications for customer focused activities, given our other finding of the strategic importance of marketing decisions.

We argued that an organization which is aware of the changing nature of competition, and the use of IT by competitors, is more likely to be an innovator or early adopter than a laggard, and is thus more likely to gain competitive advantage from its IT investment. The competitive environment of the industry was seen as being extremely intensive by most respondents, and likely to become more so, but our proposition that this would differentiate between adopters and non-adopters was not supported. The extreme skewness of attitudes on this measure may explain the lack of obtained significance.

The transfer of IT investments from back-office to front-office applications, because of this increased competition and the increased customer focus, has moved the focus of investment appraisal from efficiency, to effectiveness, and now strategic advantage, suggesting, we argued, that traditional cost-benefit appraisal methods are no longer adequate for strategic IS applications. The finding that degree of satisfaction with traditional costbenefit appraisal methods did not differentiate adopters from non-adopters suggests that, in general, organizations are likely to miss many strategic opportunities if this degree of satisfaction is indicative of a focus on short term tangible benefits rather than long term advantage. It may be that the intense competitive environment is encouraging a follow-my-leader attitude (cf Abrahamson, 1975), where decisions to adopt are reactive rather than strategic. Nevertheless, adopter firms do place more strategic importance on direct marketing decisions and integration of IT and marketing than non-adopters.

Overall, the organizational context seems of greater importance in the decision to adopt than the strategic context. Our findings confirm that size is an important variable related to adoption but in a more complex way than some previous authors have suggested. Whilst the size of the marketing and direct marketing department differentiates adopters, it is only the size of direct marketing which relates to sophistication. This is despite the, generally, relatively low strategic importance of direct marketing compared to marketing. We infer that ‘size’ is important but in terms of the size of individual departments or sections directly related to the strategic application or innovation under consideration. Once adoption takes place, the key to increasing sophistication of applications may be to expand the size of the relevant subsection, in this case direct marketing, rather than rely on the overall size of the organization. The relationship of size and power is also important and it should not be assumed that a direct correlation exists. Despite its generally relatively small size, the power of direct marketing to control key elements of DBM interventions is an important element in developing sophisticated systems.

Organizations adopting DBM gave the marketing function a higher degree of uncommitted resources (relating to computing facilities and information) than non-adopters, and a lower degree of organizational control (even though they perceived no difference in strategic importance). Nolan (1979) suggests that an imbalance of high control and low slack at the early stages of organizational learning can impede the use of IT. Our finding of high slack and low control for DBM users, compared to non-users, characterises firms in stage two of Nolan’s six stages, placing them before the critical cusp when applications become strategic. This balance encourages innovation and widespread usage of the technology but, Nolan argues, create problems for the future.

A high degree of information sophistication characterises the progression of the IT function into the strategic era, and this is necessary to support a strategic marketing application such as DBM. The information intensive nature of the financial services industry may imply that financial organizations have progressed further in their development of IS than in their strategic thinking. The high sophistication means they are better able to divert resources to marketing applications, as we predicted, and are more likely to realise the value of information. The high degree of marketing orientation also supports our view that as organizations attempt to make the customer the centre of the organization’s thinking and activity, then implementation of the marketing concept is required. The previous conceptualisation, that information orientation and marketing orientation are necessary for the successful implementation of DBM, is therefore supported, but sophistication in the industry seems to be driven by the level of IT orientation rather than marketing orientation.

The final organizational context hypothesis supported was to do with incremental decision making. A slow, evolutionary approach to DBM does not seem to characterise adopters. The degree of communication and the co-ordination an incremental approach allows does not seem to be required, and the willingness for organizations to make step changes in their IT investment decisions would seem to be a factor in the early adoption of DBM.

The remaining two organizational variables that we investigated: the existence of internal networks, and direct marketing power, did not separate adopters from non-adopters. However, direct marketing power was one of the key drivers of sophistication. Internal networking allows new ideas to flow easily and rapidly, both formally and informally. The high level of strategic integration of marketing and IT, discussed earlier, may mean that networking channels are not critical for achieving common understanding between functional areas since marketing and IT are already sufficiently integrated in both adopting and non-adopting organizations.

An alternative explanation, given the importance of the strategic context variables of (1) strategic importance of direct marketing decisions and (2) size of direct marketing department, is that direct marketing is in general isolated from other departments and is pushing the innovation through from a position of power. However, this inference is not supported by our finding that scores on our variable, ‘direct marketing power’ does not differentiate between users and non-users of DBM. Conversely, our regression analysis identifies direct marketing power as one of just four predictor variables of sophistication

Firms wishing to adopt a strategic IT intervention for marketing need to consider the strategic role of direct marketing and ensure that IT and marketing (as the ‘mother’ department) are integrated in their strategic decision making. The organizational underpinning which is necessary to support this seems to be that of diverting resources to the relevant area, both in terms of staff resources in building a direct marketing department and, particularly, access and control of computing resources. It may be better to make a definite and complete move to DBM, rather than by preceding by incremental changes in direct marketing practise, since competitive intensity will place second-mover firms at a disadvantage. This supportive organizational context extends to organizational culture, in that firms that adopt DBM are more likely to be customer-oriented, have user-friendly IS, and value information.

Once adoption has occurred, the innovation may continue to be used and developed, left in its initial state, or fail to provide the expected benefits and subsequently rejected. The sophistication of a DBM system is thus conceptually distinct from simple adoption. To exploit the full benefits, and justify the high costs of a mainframe-based DBM system the applications must move beyond simple direct marketing applications. Such sophistication requires the development of extensive customer information files as well as the development of various marketing skills and control mechanisms. This allows longer-term integrated marketing and campaign planning, such as is necessary for relationship building, rather than simply mailing list management. Overall, our measure of DBM sophistication indicated that average levels of sophistication in the financial services industry were generally low (see also Fletcher & Wright, 1995). Taken together, these findings suggest that the present lack of differentiation on our strategic context variables may be due to the tactical use of current systems.

Finally, we note that our research studied sophistication, not effectiveness or success of the DBM intervention. Our present findings indicate that, in general, adopting organizations may not be obtaining the strategic advantages possible with DBM and that systems are instead mainly tactical in their focus. As organizations move towards the integrated and coordinated marketing and communication strategies required by sophisticated DBM, then the nature of applications and customer focus required will force a participatory and flexible approach, crossing functional barriers.

The continuous interplay between stragic imperatives and organization form will require a regularly changing set of processes and procedures, with the constant and key element being a focus on the customer-supported by constant flow of relevant customer information to inform decision makers and support activities.

## About the authors

Keith Fletcher is Professor of Management and Dean of the School of Management at the University of East Anglia. He has a wide range of teaching experience at all levels, and has a special research interest in IT in marketing, having recently concentrated on the development of Database Marketing in the UK. He has published numerous articles on the use and implementation of IT in marketing, and has recently published the second edition of his popular textbook on IT and Marketing Management.

George Wright is Professor of Management Decision Strategy at the Leeds University Business School, University of Leeds, UK.
