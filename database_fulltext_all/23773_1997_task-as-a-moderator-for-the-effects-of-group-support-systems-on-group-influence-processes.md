# Task as a moderator for the effects of group support systems on group influence processes

W Huang and KK Wei

Department of Information Systems & Computer Science, National University of Singapore, Lower Kent Ridge Road, Singapore 119260

This research studied the interacting effects of group support systems (GSS) and task type on small groups. It focused on group influence processes rather than group outcomes, which has been largely neglected in GSS research literature. The results of this research indicated that GSS significantly affected group influence processes: GSS reduced the dominance significance (i.e., the influence dominance) among group members; and it increased the amount of influence behaviour and resulted in a less even influence distribution in intellective task groups, but not in decision-making task groups. Therefore, the findings suggest that task type may play an important role in moderating the GSS effects. An important implication of the findings is that organizational GSS users should be careful when they adopt and use GSS to support a variety of tasks. They should carefully examine the characteristics of the various tasks to be performed, and then choose suitable GSS structures to support these tasks.

## Introduction

The post-industrial societies of the next century are characterised by ever increasing knowledge, complexity, and turbulence (Huber, 1984), and thus decision making in organizations is frequently beyond an individual’s capability. Group decision making is thus essential to organizations, but unfortunately, group work is often unproductive (Jessup & Valacich, 1993). A group support system (GSS) is suggested as a solution to this important problem, which aims to increase the effectiveness and efficiency of group work.

Nunamaker et al (1991) proposed a general group research model, as shown in Figure 1. According to this model, the effects of GSS use in a group are contingent upon a myriad of group, task, context, and technology factors that differ from one situation to another. These four factors influence the group process, which in turn affects the group outcome. This model recognises the importance of group process to group outcome. Group process is central to a group (McGrath, 1984). However, most prior GSS research examined the effects of GSS on group outcome (e.g., Kraemer & Pinsonneault, 1990; Olson et al, 1993) while treating the group process as a black box (Zigurs et al, 1988; Sambamurthy & Poole, 1992). Further, some previous empirical studies reported that GSS improved group performance (e.g., Gallupe et al, 1988; Jarvenpaa, 1988; Sharda et al, 1988; Easton et al, 1989; Bui & Sivasankaran, 1990; Nunamaker et al, 1991) while other studies indicated otherwise (e.g., Steeb & Johnston, 1981; Gallupe, 1990; Gallupe & McKeen, 1990; George et al, 1990). Although explanations of this inconsistency of research findings have been offered (e.g., Dennis et al, 1991), the issue has remained one of the main concerns in the GSS field (e.g., Benbasat et al, 1993; Dennis & Gallupe, 1993). More research focusing on group process, rather than on group outcome, may provide additional insights into this inconsistency.

Further, many GSS researchers have used one single task type to study various group issues such as group size and group decision quality. But relatively few researchers have used groups to study task types. Hence, the task issue has been inadequately studied, which deserves more research attention (Mennecke & Wheeler, 1993). Prior research indicated that task type often contributed up to fifty percent of variations in group performance (Poole et al, 1985) and GSS use may be more useful for high complexity tasks than for low complexity tasks (Gallupe et al, 1988; Dennis et al, 1996). Therefore, this research, focusing on group processes and task type, addresses the issue of how GSS interact with task type to influence group processes.

The remaining parts of this paper are organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the relevant literature that forms the conceptual foundation for this research; Section 3 defines the dependent variables and formulates the research hypotheses; Section 4 describes the research methodology; Section 5 reports the experimental results; Section 6 presents the discussion and implications of the research findings.

## Literature review on GSS and task type

The literature review of GSS research in this study covered the research conducted in the past two decades. It is impossible to list all the prior GSS research papers here because there are more than 250 related papers in the literature. The selected papers shown in Table 1 reflect the prior GSS research in terms of the research focus and the task types studied, which is in line with the thorough task review conducted by Mennecke and Wheeler (1993).

Two conclusions can be drawn from Table 1. First, most prior GSS research focused on group outcomes and only a few papers (i.e., the research numbers 1, 15, 17, and 21 of Table 1) studied group processes. Second, most research did not study the effects of task type on groups. Only paper numbers 16 and 18 had task type as an independent variable in their studies. As a result, more research on group processes and task type is still necessary.

Further, as shown in Table 1, the task types that have been frequently studied are creativity, intellective, and decision-making tasks. Prior studies were generally conducted in different research contexts (e.g., dispersed setting vs face-to-face setting), groups (e.g., student groups vs real groups in organizations), GSS technologies (e.g., brainstorming vs stakeholder analysis vs structured meeting agenda), and task types (e.g., creativity vs decisionmaking task). As a result, it is difficult to compare these prior findings and thereby draw a general conclusion concerning GSS use (Dennis et al, 1991). However, the moderating effect of task type on GSS use can be, in part, reflected in the following research findings (see Table 1):

1. GSS tended to increase group performance such as group decision quality in an intellective task (Jarvenpaa et al, 1988; McLeod & Liker, 1992), but not in a decision-making task (George et al, 1990; McLeod & Liker, 1992; Tan et al, 1994).

2. GSS tended to result in a more even influence distribution among group members in a decision-making task (Zigurs et al, 1988), but not in an intellective task (Watson et al, 1988).

3. The effect of using a GSS was overwhelmed by the impact of the task-related information possessed by group members prior to the group activity (Clapper & Massey, 1995).

The above moderating effects of task type have been rarely specifically studied in the GSS literature, especially from the perspective of group process. This study therefore addresses these possible moderating effects of task type on GSS use in group process.

Hence, task type appears to be an important factor in GSS use. Conceptual research indicates that task characteristics determine the requirement and type of information that must be exchanged by a group (Poole, 1978). Task type and its characteristics exert a lot of influence on group decision process and outcome (Poole et al, 1985). Moreover, task type determines the influencemode in group processes, i.e., an intellective task results in the dominant informational influence in group processes whereas a decision-making task results in the dominant normative influence in group processes (e.g., Kaplan & Miller 1987; Kaplan et al, 1994). However, task type has been inadequately studied in the GSS literature (McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994). A good task classification system is necessary for a systematic study of task type because there are simply too many different tasks carried out by modern organizations.

Prior researchers in social psychology have proposed a number of task classification schemes (e.g., Hackman, 1968; Shaw, 1973; Laughlin, 1980; McGrath, 1984). A task refers to a set of problems and issues confronting a decision-making group which aims to seek a solution agreeable to the group members (Hare, 1976). Shaw (1973) studied 104 tasks using factor analysis, and proposed six empirically derived task dimensions: difficulty, solution multiplicity, co-operation requirements, intellectual–manipulative requirements, population familiarity, and intrinsic interest. Hackman (1969) synthesised and discussed the four task description frameworks of task qua task, task as behaviour requirement, task as behaviour description, and task as ability requirement. He suggested that the definitions and/or designs of tasks should be based on the task as behaviour requirement. Wood (1986) focused on task complexity and differentiated three types of task complexity: component complexity, coordinative complexity, and dynamic complexity. He argued that these three different task complexities formed the basis for differentiating tasks.

McGrath (1984) summarised the work of prior researchers, and developed a group task circumplex that consists of four quadrants and classifies tasks into eight task types (see Figure 2). These eight categories are mutually exclusive (i.e., a task fits in one and only one category) and collectively exhaustive (i.e., all tasks fit in these eight categories). Each category is logically related to the categories adjacent to it. Further, the circumplex differentiates task types along two other dimensions: task types can be separated into those eliciting conflicts and those requiring co-operation; and can be classified as conceptual or behavioural in nature (see Figure 2). This task circumplex has been widely used in GSS research (e.g., DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987; Zigurs et al, 1988; Nunamaker et al, 1989; Mennecke & Wheeler, 1993). Our study will also adopt this task circumplex as a basis for systematic research on task type. In this way, prior and future studies can be compared, and the related findings can be accumulated.

Mennecke and Wheeler (1993) proposed some guidelines for choosing suitable tasks for specific studies: “When a task manipulation is used in order to observe resultant behaviours, the tasks will often be chosen on the basis of the behaviours that the task is expected to elicit rather than on the basis of specific task components” (p 74). According to Table 1, the creativity task, intellective task, and decision-making task are three task types commonly used in GSS research literature. In this study, the resultant behaviours are expected to reflect the moderating effects of task type on group processes. An intellective task and a decision-making task can generate the two different influence-modes (or influence behaviours) in group processes (i.e., informational influence vs normative influence) (e.g., Kaplan & Miller, 1987). Therefore, these two tasks are likely to result in different group processes, thus eliciting the moderating effects of task type on group processes. As a result, these two task types which are located in quadrant 2 of Figure 2 are chosen for this study. In general, a correct answer exists for an intellective task and the task solution depends on factual information exchange. A decisionmaking task, however, does not have a demonstrably correct solution and the task solution depends on members’ preferences and values (McGrath, 1984).

## Research hypotheses

DeSanctis (1988) argues that a GSS can affect group performance by altering a group’s information exchange process. A GSS generally possesses many structures to alter the information exchange process and facilitate group work. The structures here refer to the rules and resources (including software tools) that are used in group interactions (Poole & DeSanctis, 1990). The five basic and commonly used structures (Bostrom & Anson, 1992) are: (1) anonymity (group members know the content of an expressed idea but do not know the owner of the idea); (2) simultaneity (group members can input their ideas simultaneously via keyboards); (3) electronic recording and display (all exchanged messages captured and saved in group memory or group databases can be retrieved and displayed on computer screens anytime in group meetings); (4) structured interaction process (e.g., an electronic meeting agenda for a group); (5) enhanced information processing (e.g., data processing or electronic voting). A GSS with these structures provides an equal opportunity for members to participate in group activities, enables all members to work simultaneously, and discourages behaviour that can negatively impact meeting productivity (Nunamaker et al, 1991). Consequently, a GSS is likely to influence group processes profoundly.

In group processes, members interact with each other and thus exert some form of influence (e.g., Kaplan et al, 1994). Hence, it is natural to study group processes from an influence perspective. Further, group researchers have shown their long-standing interests in the influence perspective (Zigurs et al, 1988), and in fact, a practical approach to study group processes is to examine them as interpersonal influence processes (Stasser et al, 1989). From this influence perspective, group members exert influences on each other through mutual interactions and communications. Because some members can be better at verbal expressions and communications than others, or some members may possess higher social status (e.g., higher positions or older ages) than others, the intensity of such influences exerted by each member may be different. Consequently, the influences exerted by group members may not be uniformly distributed. Some members could be more influential than others in group processes, leading to dominance in a group. These three aspects of the group influence processes can be described using three dependent variables, i.e., amount of influence behaviour to reflect the intensity of the influences, influence distribution to represent the distribution of the influences exerted by group members, and dominance significance to describe the dominant behaviour of the most dominant member within a group.

The amount of influence behaviour is defined as those actions that attempt to affect or determine the course of group behaviour, and the influence distribution is defined as the variance of the amount of influence behaviour in a group (Zigurs et al, 1988). The dominance significance is defined as the ratio comparing the most dominant member’s amount of influence behaviour over the average of other members’ amount of influence behaviour (Lim et al, 1994).

## The amount of influence behaviours

The solution of an intellective task depends on factual information exchanges among group members in order to find out the correct answer (McGrath, 1984). The GSS’s simultaneity enables members to give their opinions simultaneously. Further, the GSS anonymity enables group members to give their opinions freely through the electronic communication channel with less fear of social offensiveness and status differences (e.g., Nunamaker et al, 1991; Rao & Jarvenpaa, 1991). All these are likely to result in more factual information exchanges, thus leading to more influence behaviour in GSS groups.

H1 The amount of influence behaviour will be greater in GSS groups than in unsupported groups, in an intellective task.

In a decision-making task, there is no correct answer. The task requires the exchange of different values and preferences among group members (McGrath, 1984). These different personal values and preferences with social cues can often be better clarified and exchanged in face-to-face communication than in GSS electronic communication, because in GSS electronic communication, only textual messages can be transmitted and many social cues are reduced or even eliminated (e.g., Johansen et al, 1979; Sproull & Kiesler, 1986). Consequently, the (influence) behaviour that attempts to increase the understanding of members’ different values and preferences could be dampened by a GSS. On the other hand, the GSS anonymity and simultaneity could encourage more open communication and uninhibited influence behaviour among group members. As a result, influence behaviours can be increased. In sum, it is difficult to predict the net effects of GSS on the amount of influence behaviour. Further, relevant research findings in the literature could not be found as a basis for a logical judgment of the net effects. It is thus hypothesised that:

H2 The amount of influence behaviour will not be greater in GSS groups than in unsupported groups, in a decisionmaking task.

## The influence distribution and dominance significance

In an intellective task, a correct answer or solution to the task exists. Because different people normally possess different cognitive abilities, it is likely that some group members are more knowledgeable about the task solution than others. Although the GSS simultaneity provides an equal communication channel for every group member, these knowledgeable members are likely to contribute more to the group discussion because: (1) they possess more related information and knowledge about the task solution; and (2) they do not need to worry about the problem of ‘air-time allocation’ existing in face-toface discussion, where the air-time for speaking must be allocated among group members and the members have to take turns to speak (Nunamaker et al, 1991). The more contributions from these knowledgeable members are likely to result in a less even distribution of influence behaviour. Similarly, the most knowledgeable member in GSS groups is likely to become the most dominant member in group discussion and communication, which leads to a greater dominance significance.

H3 The influence distribution will be less even in GSS groups than in unsupported groups, in an intellective task.

H4 The dominance significance will be greater in GSS groups than in unsupported groups, in an intellective task.

In a decision-making task, no correct answer exists. The final group decision on the task solution mainly relies on the exchanges of members’ different preferences and values (McGrath, 1984). GSS simultaneity provides an open and equal access to the electronic communication channel for all group members. Moreover, although GSS can dampen social cues (e.g., Sproull & Kiesler, 1986) and thereby dampen the exchange of preferences and values, this dampening effect would be equally applied to every member in a group. Consequently, the influence behaviour exhibited through this channel may be more equally distributed among group members. Similarly, the influence behaviour from the most dominant member is likely to be dampened by the GSS. As a result, the dominance significance may be relatively dampened in GSS groups.

H5 The influence distribution will be more even in GSS groups than in unsupported groups, in a decision-making task.

H6 The dominance significance will be less in GSS groups than in unsupported groups, in a decision-making task.

## Research methodology

## Interaction analysis method

Interaction analysis for capturing group process interactions has become increasingly important in GSS research (e.g., DeSanctis et al, 1987; Sambamurthy & Poole, 1992). Many schemes for analysing and coding group processes are available in group research literature. Because this study adopted two dependent variables (i.e., the amount of influence behaviour and influence distribution) From Zigurs et al (1988), and the dominance significance can be computed from the dependent variable of the amount of influence behaviour, Putnam’s interaction analysis method and coding scheme (1981) modified by Zigurs et al (1988) was chosen. Putnam’s coding scheme captures the procedural messages in group interactions which occupy a substantial portion of group talking time, perform vital meta-message functions, and serve as indices of leadership emergence and decision-making processes (Putnam, 1981). The first five categories of this coding scheme which form the basis of measuring influence behaviour are: initiation messages, goal-oriented messages, integrative messages, implementation messages, and process messages.

## Measurement of dependent variables

Influence behaviour was measured by viewing videotapes (for unsupported groups and GSS groups) and by examining computer log files (for GSS groups). Each verbal act emitted by a group member was coded (Zigurs et al, 1988) by using Putnam’s coding scheme (1981). Here, as in Zigurs et al’s study (1988), there is no attempt to conduct an in-depth analysis of non-verbal influence behaviour such as eye gaze, hand gesture, facial expression, and so on. The amount of influence behaviour in a group was the sum of the coded scores of all group members. Two coders worked together on the coding scheme initially until they achieved mutual reliability. Then, they finished coding all the remaining videotapes and computer log files separately. The overall inter-rater reliability was 90%. More detailed description of Putnam’s coding scheme and coding procedure can be found in Zigurs et al (1988).

## Tasks

The two tasks adopted for this study were an intellective task and a decision-making task. The intellective task (an international studies programme task) was adopted from Zigurs et al (1988). This task asked group members to score a list of competing applicants based on six attributes. The six attributes were gender, expectations for social success, self concept, expectations for independence, attitudes about pre-marital sex, and prior travel abroad.

The decision-making task (a personal trust foundation task) was adopted from Watson et al (1988). This task asked group members to allocate funds to a list of competing projects based on their personal values. The projects were based on the personality components scheme described by Spranger (1928) who proposed that the six basic interests or motives in personality were theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, and religious.

## Experimental design and setting

The experiment in this study was a two-by-two factorial design. The two independent variables were task type and support level. The two support levels varied were GSS support and no support, and the two task types varied were an intellective task and a decision-making task. The dependent variables were the amount of influence behaviour, influence distribution, and dominance significance. A small group size of five was chosen for this study. A larger group size, or more accurately, a logically larger group size, may result in more contrasting research findings for GSS use in groups (e.g., Nunamaker et al 1989; Dennis et al, 1991). But the subjects in this experiment were chosen from the students at the same department in the same university. Hence, even if we chose a larger group size in this study, “a physically large group from a common culture . . . may have a high degree of overlapping domain knowledge that results in the group being logically small” (Nunamaker et al, 1989; p 147). The physically large student groups normally have a high degree of overlapping domain knowledge, and thus could result in the final research findings being less contrasting. Further, the main focuses of this study are on task type and group processes, rather than group size. Thus, a small group size was considered as suitable for the current research.

The group decision support system used in this experiment was SAMM (Software Aided Meeting Management system) which was developed by the University of Minnesota and run on an AT&T 3B2-4000 minicomputer under the Unix operating system (Unix System 5, Release 3.1.1). Each GSS group was connected by five private terminals (televideo model 970) to communicate with each other. For unsupported groups, members had a normal face-to-face discussion without any technological facility.

A total of 160 first year students from a large university participated in the experiment. They were randomly assigned to thirty-two groups. The average age of the chosen students was twenty and nearly half of them were male. Every subject was given a course credit for participating in the experiment. None of the subjects had ever used any GSS systems before the experiment.

## Experimental procedure

The experimental steps for the two tasks are: (1) for both tasks, group members were asked to do warm-up tasks; (2) for intellective task groups, individual members were asked to learn the task criteria; (3) for both tasks, individual members were asked to perform the tasks before the meeting; (4) for both tasks, members in GSS groups were asked to learn the structured meeting agenda and the operations of the SAMM system; (5) for both tasks, groups were asked to perform the tasks. The meeting sessions were recorded using a video camera.

## Research results

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for statistical tests. There are three advantages of using the ANOVA test. First, an ANOVA test can detect the main effects of the task type and support level for this study. Second, it can detect the interaction effects between the task type and support level. Third, it can increase the power of a statistical test (Emory & Cooper, 1991). If a significant interaction effect was detected, an analysis of simple effect was performed to reveal the nature of the effect (Keppel, 1991). A five percent level of significance was adopted for all tests.

## The amount of influence behaviour

Table 2 shows the details of the ANOVA test on the amount of influence behaviour. The ‘DF’ records an associated degrees of freedom for each source of variation. The ‘SS’ records an associated sum of squared distances for each source of variation. The ‘F ratio’ is the model mean square divided by the error mean square, where the mean square is a SS divided by its associated degrees of freedom. The ‘Pr.F’ is the probability of obtaining (by chance alone) an F value greater than the one calculated if, in reality, fitting group means accounts for no more variation than using the overall response mean. The observed significance probabilities of 0.05 or less are often considered as the evidence that an ANOVA model fits the data, which is marked using an asterisk as shown in Table 2. This table shows that support level and task type had no significant main effects on the amount of influence behaviour. However, there was a significant interaction effect between the support level and task type. Since the interaction effect was detected, an analysis of simple effect (Keppel, 1991) was used to analyse the interaction.

The analysis of simple effect showed that in the intellective task, the amount of influence behaviour was significantly greater in GSS groups than in unsupported groups (F = 6.4579, P = 0.0235\*). Hence, H1 was supported. In a decision-making task, no significant difference between GSS groups and unsupported groups was found (F = 0.5666, P = 0.4641). In other words, the amount of influence behaviour was not greater in GSS groups than in unsupported groups, namely, H2 was supported too.

## Discussion and implications

GSS increased the amount of influence behaviour for an intellective task (H1 was supported) but not for a decision-making task (H2 was supported). In fact, in the decision-making task, GSS dampened the amount of influence behaviour because the average score of the amount of influence behaviour in unsupported groups (40.70) was greater than the one in GSS groups (33.53). Hence, the effects of GSS on the amount of influence behaviour differed for the two different tasks. Hence, task type appeared to be a moderator for the impacts of GSS on the amount of influence behaviour.

Further, GSS resulted in a less even influence distribution in the intellective task (H3 was supported). Although H5 was not supported, the average score of the influence distribution in GSS groups (39.47) was less than the one in unsupported groups (56.98). This means that in the decision-making task, GSS appeared to promote a more even distribution of influence behaviour in a group. In sum, GSS had different effects on the influence distribution for the two different tasks. Again, this finding supports the conclusion that task type is likely to act as a moderator for the impacts of GSS.

The insignificant findings of H5 and 5% level may be explained by the sample size of 16, which may not be big enough to detect a significant effect. The power of the test (Cohen, 1976) was as low as 0.28 and the least significant number was 31. Hence, the sample size should be increased (at least up to 31) in future studies in order to detect any significant effect of H5.

For the dominance significance, the effect of GSS seemed to be stronger than the moderating effect of task type, i.e., GSS decreased the dominance significance in group processes regardless of the two task types (see Table 4). Since the dominance significance reflects the influence behaviour of the most dominant member in a group, one implication from this finding is that GSS tends to dampen the emergence of new leadership in a group, thus encouraging more democratic and open discussions in group processes. However, this finding might also result in the worry of some leaders in organizations that their authority would be eroded by adopting and using a GSS. If this worry exists, the adoption of GSS systems in an organization could be affected. But such worry is not supported empirically. In fact, prior research on leadership shows that for groups with formally elected or assigned leader(s), GSS does not affect or dampen the leader’s influence behaviour in group processes (Lim et al, 1994). In other words, GSS does not dampen the existing leader’s influence behaviour in group processes. Our research finding showed that the use of GSS could dampen the emergence of new leader(s) in a group. Consequenctly, GSS use could enhance the existing leader’s authority rather than reduce his/her authority. Further, our research finding should also be a desirable effect for some groups such as selfdirecting groups because the emergence of new leader(s) can be dampened by GSS use. Therefore, the current research finding about the relationship between GSS use and emerging leadership may contribute to the accumulated knowledge of group leadership.

Nevertheless, it is necessary to be cautious about applying the findings of this research to GSS practices. First, as an experimental research, the external validity of this study is relatively low although the internal validity is high. For example, the subjects chosen for this experiment were university students, who may be different from real group members in organizations in terms of personality, working experience, and group history. Hence, any generalisation of our laboratory research findings to organizations should be made cautiously and should take the above differences into consideration. However, real group members in organizations are generally less cooperative in laboratory experiments than student group members. It may be impossible to ask the real group members to fit into artificially designed experimental settings (they may simply resist). Additionally, it is normally much more difficult and expensive to get access to real group members in organizations. Consequently, most researchers in organizational science and social psychology still choose university students as subjects for their studies.

Second, the effects of GSS here actually refer to the effects of specific GSS structures used in this study, i.e., the five basic GSS structures of anonymity, simultaneity, electronic meeting agenda, electronic recording and display, and electronic voting mentioned in Section 3. There are other GSS structures such as brainstorming and stakeholder analysis, which were not used in this study. Thus, our findings should be confined to the GSS structures used rather than to GSS as a whole. Third, our research is not a longitudinal study. The interacting effects of GSS and task type found in this study may not remain the same over time. Hence, the research findings may not always be applicable to groups which use a GSS for a relatively long time. Future research can address this issue to examine the effects of the time dimension on group processes.

## Conclusion

This research focused on group processes rather than group outcomes. The results presented an interesting picture about the interacting effects of GSS and task type on group processes. GSS can significantly affect group influence processes: it can be used to dampen the emergence of new leader(s) in a normal or self-directing group, and the final group decision could thereby be reached in a more democratic way. Moreover, GSS has the potential to increase group influence behaviour (and members’ participation as well) for an intellective task (a generally beneficial GSS effect), but not for a decision-making task.

Further, the two different tasks in this study resulted in some different GSS effects even when using the same GSS system (SAMM). This finding suggests that task type may play an important role in moderating the GSS effects. An implication of this finding is that organizational GSS users should be careful when they adopt and use GSS to support a variety of tasks. They should carefully examine the characteristics of the various tasks to be performed, and then choose the suitable GSS structures for supporting their specific tasks. For example, to perform an intellective task, they can choose the five commonly used GSS structures to support their group processes. But to perform a decision-making task, they cannot choose the same structures again because these five GSS structures may have some beneficial effects for an intellective task (i.e., increasing group influence behaviour thus members’ participation in group discussion), but not for a decision-making task. Therefore, it is important to study the possible fit between specific GSS structures and task types in the future. This possible fit is in line with the match between task type and group process supported by GSS, suggested by Dennis et al (1996). So far, little research has been specifically conducted in this area.
