# On the classification of telework

J Lindstrom, A Moberg and B Rapp

Economic Information Systems, Department of Computer and Information Science, University of Linkoping, S-581 83 Linkoping, Sweden

Information and communication technology (ICT) provides organizations with a whole range of new possibilities for performing work and structuring the organization. The technical, and to a certain extent the organizational development, has been rapid and there has not been the time for any consensus on concepts to evolve. This article is concentrated to a limited part of the area of organizations and ICT, telework and concepts related to telework. It is argued that many of these concepts—notably telework—have been used by so many authors in so many different ways that they have lost their specifity. They are defined in so many ways in different studies that they in each instance need to be further defined to clarify exactly what is being studied. The aim of the article is therefore not to propose new definitions but to show instead how it is possible to discriminate among telework situations by using dimensions, along which new ways of doing work can be further specified. We distinguish among forms of organizations such as telecommuting, multiflex, mobile work, satellite office, and virtual organization. We also discuss concepts regarding work places, such as neighbourhood work centres, resort offices, satellite work centres and mobile workplaces. We discriminate between the telework situations by discussing dimensions for forms of organization and workplaces, respectively.

## Background

Information and communication technology (ICT) provides organizations with a whole new type of flexibility as regards where, when and how work is performed, which gives rise to a number of new organizational forms and new ways of performing work. When doing research in this area, one is often frustrated by the many concepts used without clear definitions. And when there are definitions, authors often define the same concept in different ways and they use different concepts for the same complex phenomena. This confusion regarding concepts makes it difficult to do research and compare results from different studies.

This article is concentrated on telework and concepts related to telework. We have been working in this area for a number of years and felt an increasing urge to do something about the lack of unity when it comes to concepts used.

We argue that many of these concepts—notably telework—have been used by so many authors in so many ways that they have lost their specifity. They are defined in so many ways in different studies that in each instance they need to be further defined to clarify exactly what it is that is being studied. That this is a complex area can be illustrated by, for example, this definition of telework:

[. . .] the use of information technology (IT) to enable people to work at a distance from their employers, generally at home. This development, [is] variously known as “telework”, “telecommuting”, “electronic homework”, “the electronic cottage”, “networking”, “distance work” and “flexiplace” [. . .] (Huws, 1991, p 20)

We believe that the many ways in which these concepts already have been used makes it impossible to propose one definition and get general agreement about it. We also believe that depending on the context it is natural that different aspects are highlighted and that different definitions exist. The aim of this article is therefore not to propose new definitions but to show instead how it is possible to discriminate between telework situations by using dimensions, along which new ways of doing work can be further specified. We propose a number of such dimensions that we believe could be used by researchers and authors in the area of telework to clarify what circumstances each author refer to. Other dimensions than those we propose might of course exist.

In section 2 we present some concepts in the area of telework. The descriptions have been made in order to identify various dimensions along which the concepts can be classified and analysed. We do not limit the discussion to concepts related to telework, but also include some traditional organizational concepts in order to be able to emphasise the characteristics which are associated with telework.

In section 3 some of the concepts have been selected for a more detailed discussion. A number of dimensions are presented according to which the concepts are illustrated.

Section 4 concludes the article with a discussion.

## Some concepts

As stated above, we feel that there is much confusion regarding concepts related to telework. In this section we will illustrate this by discussing a large number of such concepts. We begin with a discussion of the overall concept of telework. Subsequently we discuss concepts which mainly denote forms of organizations and workplaces. This grouping is a first step to structure the large variety of concepts related to telework. A further discussion regarding structuring and classification of these concepts is to be found in section 3.

## Telework

There is no unambiguous definition of the concept of telework. It is a broad-ranging concept which can take on several different meanings. One very wide definition is given by Huws et al:

We define telework as work the location of which is independent of the location of the employer or contractor and can be changed according to the wishes of the individual teleworker and/or the organisation for which he or she is working. (1990, p 10)

Olson provides a different and not so wide definition:

In general the term telework is used to refer to organizational work performed outside of the normal organizational confines of space and time, augmented by computer and communications technology. The work is not necessarily performed in the home. (1988, p 77)

This is a much more demanding definition in that it states that computer and communication technology should be used. Further, the definition seems to require that the person doing telework is an employee of the organization for which she or he works. In the definition from Huws et al above, nothing is said about the use of technology and it is explicitly stated that the person performing telework might be both an employee and a contractor. It is also interesting to note that Olson emphasizes that telework is not necessarily performed in the home. Apparently, this refers to a popular discussion about work at home which is one form of telework but definitely not the only one.

That telework refers to a large number of different ways of performing work is stated in one definition from the EU:

[. . .] the term ‘telework’ does not have a narrow technical definition. It covers a range of new ways of working, using telecommunications as a tool and, for at least part of the time, outside a traditional office environment. (European Commission DG XIII-B, 1996, p 11)

In this EU definition, one can note what is said about the place of work. It is stated that at least part of the time the work is performed outside a traditional office environment. Compare this to Olson’s definition above, where it was stated that telework could be performed outside the home. They both say that telework can be performed in almost any environment, but one of the definitions marks against what seems to be a popular belief about telework as work at home while the other one marks against telework as something entirely performed in an office. Maybe this difference reflects a difference in the picture given of telework in the US and Europe respectively.

One final example of a telework definition is from Nilles who says that telework is:

ANY form of substitution of information technologies (such as telecommunications and computers) for work-related travel. (1994, p xix)

As the two definitions from Olson and the EU presented above, this definition requires that technology is used for something to be called telework. It is worth noting that Nilles’ definition also puts use of technology up against travelling. None of the definitions above covers this dimension. Strictly, this definition implies that substitutions of electronic meetings and teleconferences for travelling to meetings are included.

Thus we see that the concept of telework can be given a variety of meanings. It might or might not require use of technology, organizational affiliation, a certain work place and so on. This inconsistency in definition among authors makes it impossible to use the word telework without further clarification. We believe much could be gained if these clarifications touched upon the same dimensions. Examples of such dimensions will be discussed in section 3. We refrain from arguing for or against any of the definitions above and will for the rest of this paper treat telework as a very wide and vague concept. In the remaining parts of section 2 we will list a number of telework-related concepts to demonstrate the large number of concepts, the inconsistency in definitions, and the existing confusion.

## Forms of organization

In this section we discuss a number of concepts which particularly denote different organizational forms. The concepts are discussed in terms of what is characteristic for the form, which distance relationship is intended and also the role of the ICT. The distance relationship applies to various forms of collaboration.

The first nine concepts are all associated with telework. We then discuss a number of concepts not normally associated with telework but still related to them.

## Telecommuting

A concept closely related to telework is telecommuting. As to the relation between the two concepts, telecommuting can be said to refer to a limited part of the wider concept of telework (Handy and Mokhtarian, 1995). One definition of telecommuting is the following:

The people who are telecommuters are employees of organizations (and thus not self-employed), and routinely spend between two and four days a week working away from the office. (Gordon, 1988, p 113)

This definition is very wide in the sense that nothing is said about the use of technology. Further, nothing is said about where the work is to be performed, even if Gordon in the sentence following this definition notes that the home is most common.

Other definitions point to the use of ICT as an important feature of telecommuting. One example of this is the following definition:

[Telecommuting is] the use of computer and communications technology to transport work to the worker as a substitute for physical transportation of the worker to the location of the work. (Olson, 1988, p 81)

Definitions very similar to this are common, see for example Nilles (1994) and Moktharian and Salomon (1994).

The definition from Olson above states that technology is required for something to be called telecommuting. However, it does not say anything about the place where the work is to be carried out. Other definitions include this dimension as well. One example is Kugelmass:

When an employee combines flexiplace, flextime and electronic communication, the result is telecommuting. Telecommuting refers to transferring work from the office to the home or to remote work centers. (1995, p 20)

This is a definition that leans on other concepts (flexiplace and flextime). Flextime is an established concept and flexiplace will be treated further below. It is worth noting that what Kugelmass here calls telecommuting also has appeared under the name of multiflex. This concept is treated in the section below.

In telecommuting situations the distance relationship is between the employee and the normal workplace but the role of ICT is not clear. It is, however, often assumed that ICT is used.

## Multiflex

Multiflex is, compared to telecommuting, a rather new concept which originated in some Swedish companies in the early 1990s. Multiflex is a form of work where an individual who is employed at a company can, within certain limits, choose both when and where work is to be performed (Moberg, 1993; Larsen, 1996). It is implicitly assumed that the individual has access to certain ICTtools. The individual has, therefore, the possibility of performing his or her work at various places. Often the individual’s home is one of the workplaces. Others include office hotels or neighbourhood work centres. Work can also be performed on trains, at hotels etc when travelling. A certain systematics in the way of working and a certain normalisation of where and when work is to be performed are required to fulfil the criteria for multiflex. It is not sufficient to work sporadically at a workplace other than the normal one to claim that multiflex is in force.

Juridically it is a question of an employment contract and perhaps it is just this that differentiates the multiflex individual from the hired individual consultant and/or a person contracted to perform a particular project.

What we have called multiflex has also appeared in the literature under other names: for example, flexible work arrangements (Olson, cited in Huws et al, 1990) or flexwork (Qvortup, 1993; Lindstrom & Rapp, 1996).

The relationship intended here is between individuals who, due to their affiliation to their organization or division, have the need for a relatively high level of cooperation. ICT is used mainly by individuals at a distance from their normal workplaces to be able to communicate with other group members in order to participate in the work.

## Mobile work

Mobile work involves the situation where an individual’s normal work can be performed when travelling. It is important to differentiate mobile work on the one hand and mobile workers on the other. Mobile work is performed when normal work is executed during travel at an ‘office-like’ workplace, while a mobile worker travels because his or her duties so require, e.g., a travelling salesperson who is visiting customers. If this person can, when travelling, perform some of his or her duties which otherwise would have been performed in an office, then this person is a mobile worker performing mobile work.

Furthermore, mobile work can be seen as a part of multiflex. To be able to call the form of work multiflex, a certain freedom with respect to time and a certain systematics in where the mobile work is performed are required.

In this case the distance is between the individual and a normal workplace, where the work would have been performed if it had not been done while travelling, with the help of ICT. ICT is almost part of the definition of this form of work, as it is the technology which enables the performance of work which otherwise would have been done at the normal workplace, while travelling.

## Flexiplace

Flexiplace is a form of work where an individual who is employed at a company can, within certain limits, choose the place where work is to be performed (Nelton, 1993; Kugelmass, 1995). These workplaces include the individual’s home, neighbourhood work centres and telecottages. In contrast to multiflex the individual cannot choose when work is to be performed.

The distance relationship here is between individuals in a company who, due to their affiliation to the organization or division, require a relatively high level of cooperation. ICT is used mainly so that the individual, who is distanced from his or her normal office or from colleagues in a work group, has access to information and the opportunity to communicate with other group members as a part of normal working duties.

## Work at home

Work at home is a concept we use to describe the form of work where an individual who is employed at a company has his or her workplace at home and does not have access to a workplace at the company. Our definition means that the individual works at home 100% of his or her working time, which differentiates this form of work from multiflex. Some definitions of work at home used in the literature do not require that the individual works at home all the time (see, for example, Olson, 1988). With this view, work at home is almost the same as telecommuting.

A self-employed person often has his or her workplace in or adjacent to the home. What differentiates work at home from self-employment is mainly the organizational affiliation.

The distance in this case is between the employee’s home—where the work is performed—and the normal workplace where work traditionally would have been performed. Many people work at home without the support of ICT. For the term to be of relevance for this discussion, however, ICT is a prerequisite in that the work performed at home is carried out using a computer and telecommunication technology.

## Satellite office

A satellite office is a form of organization where part of a division within a company is located at a different geographical site from the rest of the division (Moberg, 1993). This concept is not to be confused with a satellite work centre (see below). Generally the work performed is the same at the satellite office as in the rest of the division. With respect to the common affiliation to the division, dependency between the units is quite high. The geographically separated units have a common manager. In this form individual employees cannot choose where they perform their work. The satellite office is their normal workplace.

The difference from a branch (see below), which can also be included in the above definition, is that the satellite office is not solely aimed at a local market (cf. a local branch of a bank). The satellite office may perform central duties for the head office.

From the description of satellite offices above, it is evident that collaboration at a distance is between various groups within the same organizational unit. The mutual affiliation with the division causes a certain dependence and thereby the need for co-ordination of business activities. ICT plays an important role in co-ordinating the activities of the divided division described above. Despite the mutual affiliation with the division, using ICT, e.g., common databases and tools for communication between people, the satellite office can be located at a different geographical site from the division.

## Detached unit

A detached unit is a form of organization where a whole division has been geographically separated from the rest of the company, e.g., relocated in a different place from where the company carries out its main functions (the concept is discussed in Poignant & Rapp, 1993, for Swedish circumstances). Juridically the division is part of the company, which differs from the case of a subsidiary company. A detached unit may be given a high degree of independence with a high level of control over profits. An extension of this idea is the hiving off of a division and letting it form a company. A detached unit is a concept which is often associated with telework. Collaboration with and dependence upon other units within the company are greatest at managerial level. Individuals working at the detached unit are not able to decide themselves where they are to perform their work. The detached unit is their normal workplace. In the literature about telework the concept of a satellite office often refers to detached units. We think there are important differences between these two concepts. For instance, dependence on the rest of the company, particularly the division it belongs to, is greater for the satellite office than for the detached unit.

In this form, distance involves the co-operation between divisions within a company. ICT means that divisions within a company do not have to be located geographically close to each other. As access to common databases is available, information pertinent to the task in hand can be reached regardless of the unit’s location. The role of technology for communication between people is, however, not so important here as it is in the case of a satellite office, as the dependence between smaller groups within the same division is greater than the dependence between different divisions within an organization.

## Meetings/co-operation at a distance

Meetings at a distance are not an organizational form, but a central phenomenon providing a basis for telework. ICT allows freedom in time and space. The technology can in some cases replace travel in connection with meetings where the actors are geographically separated. Instead of meeting personally, the telephone, groupware applications or video, for instance, are used. This applies to all types of meetings: within the division, within the company, between units/subsidiaries, between different companies, with customers, suppliers and others. Apart from the geographical distance, collaboration can take place over time zones. For example work can be performed 24 hours a day for certain development projects (such as products and campaigns) in order to cut development times or gain access to certain competence or a certain market, for example. ICT-tools of great importance are CAD/CAM, communication tools and groupware applications.

In these cases the organizational forms can vary; for example, meetings and collaboration at a distance are important features of many of the other concepts we discuss.

Cooperation in this case may be between individuals in different companies and also between individuals within a geographically divided company. Due to access to common, electronically stored information, groupware applications and new applications for communication, individuals or groups can collaborate in a way which was not previously possible. Instead of travelling, ICT can be used for collaboration and also produce a different work content than previously.

## Virtual/imaginary organizations

The concept virtual (imaginary) organization<sup>1</sup> (Davidow & Malone, 1992; Hedberg et al, 1997) is difficult to define and is a rather unclear concept ranging over a wide area which in turn embraces many other concepts. A brief definition is given in Hedberg et al (1997):

The perspective of the Imaginary Organization refers to a system in which assets, processes, and actors critical to the “focal” enterprise exist and function both inside and outside the limits of the enterprise’s conventional “landscape” for med by its legal structure, its accounting, its organigrams, and the language otherwise used to describe the enterprise.

The concept lays strong emphasis on core competence and relationships among many small and highly specialised organizations. Furthermore, the concept of process is significant. It also involves a new way of viewing business where it is seen more as project co-operation between customers and suppliers than as something which is run on certain premises by a certain number of employees.

In virtual organizations the distance relationship involves co-operation over geographical distance among independent companies, i.e., co-operation is at company level. That ICT plays a central role is implicit in the term virtual. Because the various companies can have access to common, electronically stored information, i.e., have a share in a common information system, company limits can become less pronounced and new forms of collaboration may arise. It is also of importance that the technology make new forms of communication possible and thus facilitates human co-operation over geographical distances.

## Related concepts not normally associated with telework

In section 2, we have so far dealt with a number of concepts normally related to telework. There are, however, a number of concepts that have similarities with the telework concept but that are not normally associated with telework. One reason for this is that they do not imply the use of ICT to the same extent as the concepts discussed so far. For some of these concepts, another reason is that they in a sense belong to an age when ICT was not an issue. Below, a few of these concepts will be briefly discussed in order to show similarities with some of the concepts of telework and thereby illustrate difficulties in using vague definitions of telework. The concepts are (i) network (ii) franchising, (iii) outsourcing, (iv) branch, (v) subsidiaries and finally (vi) selfemployed.

## Network

A concept which has been in use for some time to describe the increasingly more important emphasis on solidarity, forming relationships and the ability of companies to collaborate is networking (Scott Morton, 1991). Miles and Snow (1986) have used the term dynamic network to describe a form of organization which developed during the 1980s. It is characterised particularly by an increasing number of joint ventures, industrial partnerships and subcontracting arrangements. According to Nohria and Eccles (1992) two conceptions of the term network can be distinguished. One use of network refers to new modes of organization that are made possible by advances in information and telecommunications technologies. The other use of the term network refers to a new ideal type of organization that is characterised by relations that are based on neither hierarchical authority nor market transactions.

The concept of network implies that there are several independent organizations which are co-operating with each other. Co-operation is performed at the organizational level. Such co-operation is performed over a geographical distance as these organizations are usually not located at the same site.

ICT does not have to play a decisive role in connection with industrial networks, but in practice it is often of importance in enabling data transfer or human communication over geographical distances in a way which was not possible before.

## Franchising

Franchising is a form of collaboration which implies that someone who has a successful business concept for marketing goods and services allows others, for a fee, to apply it in their business activities.<sup>2</sup> A franchise contract is a contract between two independent parties who remain independent on both the legal and economic planes. The contract defines both parties’ rights and obligations.

Franchising has many similarities to virtual organizations. They include close co-operation between legally separate actors who seem, when viewed externally, to be affiliated to the same organization. In a virtual organization, however, the long-term relations do not have such a high level of formality as those in a franchise chain. Furthermore, a company which is part of a franchise chain has the same name as the ‘main company’, while companies which ‘are part of’ a virtual organization are more independent, have their own names and can belong to several virtual organizations at the same time.

In franchise organizations the distance relationship involves co-operation over geographical distance between companies, i.e., collaboration is at company level. Because different companies can have access to common, electronically stored information, i.e., have a share in a common information system, close collaboration can take place between independent companies. Viewed externally, they give the impression of belonging to the same organization.

## Outsourcing

Outsourcing involves actors outside the company being commissioned to perform certain functions that were previously performed by the company. The reasons for outsourcing are largely the same as for creating virtual organizations, i.e. to gain access to equipment, services, trademarks, etc., without owning the company. One could perhaps claim that the virtual organization is what you get if you combine and extrapolate a number of reasons for outsourcing (Purchasing, 1994). Reasons of this kind include the desire to focus on core competence, the desire to quickly incorporate and take advantage of such knowledge that is not directly a part of one’s own core competence, an attempt to reduce indirect costs (i.e., particularly costs which are not salaries), wanting to change one’s cost structure in the direction of more variable costs and fewer fixed costs, and wanting to reduce lead times in product development and production. Perhaps one could say that outsourcing is regulated by formal, long-term agreements more often than virtual organizations. Outsourcing can rather be seen as a strategy than an organizational form.

Outsourcing, like the three previous concepts of network, virtual organization and franchising, is based on the distance relationship of collaboration over geographical distance between independent companies.

Because different companies can have access to a common, electronically stored information, i.e., have a share in a common information system, a company can choose to collaborate closely with a company instead of owning it.

## Branch

Branches and detached units share many similarities. A branch is a unit which is part of a larger organization, often with a certain amount of autonomy. In contrast to a detached unit, a branch works in an external market. A detached unit may act as a head office function. For those working there the branch is the main workplace, with management and colleagues.

As the branch is a local office for an organization which is mainly located at another site, there is a geographical distance between them. The branch, however, is not a concept normally associated with telework.

ICT can play a role in communication between the head office and the branch, but is not a prerequisite for the organizational form of a branch. The branch, a concept which has been in use for a relatively long time to refer to units within an organization, is thus not normally associated with ICT.

## Subsidiaries

A subsidiary is a long-established organizational form where one independent juridical unit has a decisive influence over another through ownership. It is a traditional form and is mentioned here in order to distinguish it mainly from the detached unit.

There need not be any geographical distance between a subsidiary and its mother company, though this is often the case. ICT does not really play a role in establishing a subsidiary. However, the technology increases the mother company’s prospects of controlling and quickly being able to find, for example, financial data at a geographically remote subsidiary.

## Self-employed

By self-employed we mean a small independent company with one or a few employees. A self-employed person can work for one or more organizations, in more or less collaboration and with more or less use of ICT in his or her work. The workplace of the self-employed is similar to the case of work at home in those cases where the self-employed perform their work in or in connection to their own home.

This is a question of geographical distance between different juridical units, i.e., between the individual selfemployed person and the organization he or she currently works together with. The term self-employed person is usually not associated with telework.

As in other cases we have discussed, ICT is not necessary when applied to the self-employed. What the technology has to offer, though, can influence the way they co-operate with other actors.

## Workplaces

In the section ‘Forms of organization’ we discussed telework-related concepts connected to organizational forms. Here we discuss concepts denoting different types of working places. These concepts are sometimes used as examples of forms of telework, which can be rather confusing. All of the concepts discussed with exception of the last two are specifically associated with telework.

## Neighbourhood work centres

The concept neighbourhood work centre (or local telework centre) applies to a workplace where employees from several companies work in communal premises (Nilles, 1994; Kugelmass, 1995). The workplace is located in the vicinity of a residential area with the aim of reducing the travelling distance to and from work. Another aim is to create a working environment with social contact for the individual. Normally the individual has access to a workplace at his or her company. A neighbourhood work centre can be a workplace for flexiplace and multiflex forms of work. The concept neighbourhood work centre is discussed by several authors; see, for example, Olson (cited in Huws et al, 1990). In Sweden, the concept appeared already in 1985, see Engstrom et al (1985) or Ranhagen (1985).

## Telecottage

A telecottage is, according to Qvortup (cited in Holloway, 1994):

a manned local center that provides computer equipment and communication equipment for local residents in sparsely populated or rural areas or in poor suburbs. This equipment is available for everyone in the area. A telecottage helps private people gain access to telephones, telelearning, data courses and meeting facilities. A telecottage also helps small companies gain access to business information, office equipment, educational opportunities, etc.

In those cases where the telecottage also offers office space, it has the same function as a neighbourhood work centre.

## Resort office

A resort office is a kind of neighbourhood work centre where office space and the opportunity to use ICT is available at holiday resorts. A resort office thus offers an alternative workplace in connection with holidays. The concept is discussed in, for instance, Gann (1991) and Kugelmass (1995).

## Satellite work centre

A satellite work centre (or satellite centre) is a workplace which a company has located at an appropriate commuter distance for a large number of employees (Olson, cited in Huws et al, 1990; Nilles, 1994; Kugelmass, 1995). It often involves a whole division or parts of a division which is located somewhere else and thus the concept should signify that the centre is the employees only—or at least main—workplace. A satellite work centre can be a workplace for the forms of organization named satellite office and detached unit.

## Mobile workplace

Mobile workplaces are of different kinds. The work can be performed at different locations, such as hotel rooms, or on trains or flights when travelling. Private cars, too, can be equipped with various office equipment such as telephone, computer, fax and printer. A mobile workplace is a workplace for mobile work and can be a workplace for people whose workform is multiflex.

## The home

Here the home signifies a workplace for individuals employed by an organization. They perform a larger or smaller part of their work for this organization at home. Home can also be the workplace for the self-employed. The home is often the workplace for telecommuters.

## Main workplace

A main workplace is a workplace which an employee has access to at the company he or she is employed at and at which one traditionally spends or would normally spend most of one’s working time.

## Flexible office

Office premises can be arranged in different ways. A workplace may be arranged as a ‘traditional’ office environment where for the most part each has a workplace of their own, often in a room of their own. A workplace may also be arranged as a large open office with individual areas sectioned off. Such a workplace may be termed flexible office. Similar ideas are discussed by Becker and Steele (1995). A flexible office means primarily that employees do not have their own rooms, but rather sit together in a larger office space which can be partitioned in various ways. One aim is to group people who work closely together. In some cases each person has his or her own desk. In other cases employees choose where they want to work on different occasions (nonterritorial office). Kugelmass states that:

[the] very newest remote work structure is not a distant building, but desk space that is not assigned to any single individual; instead it is allocated (sometimes by reservation) to whomever wants it for a short period. (1995, p 26)

The office arrangement aims to support co-operation and is often used to strengthen a project/team-oriented organization. In some cases flexible offices are combined with the opportunity for the individual to choose a workplace other than the office (see the flexiplace and multiflex forms or work above).

## Classifying telework situations

In the previous section we presented a large number of concepts used when discussing telework. All the concepts can be differentiated completely or partly by the fact that they include, for instance, various features of ICT, treat different forms of distance relationships and are applicable at different levels of analysis. In the manner we presented the concepts, i.e., a classification of organizational forms and workplaces, we have already provided a certain structure. In this section we continue with this structuring on the basis of various dimensions along which the forms and workplaces can be classified. We do this under the headings of ‘Level of analysis’, ‘Dimensions for organizational forms’ and ‘Dimensions for workplaces’. To illustrate the use of these dimensions, we discuss some of the concepts presented in section 2.

## Level of analysis

In this section we present dimensions for classifying the organizational forms previously discussed under ‘Forms of organization’. The forms have a lot in common, but there are also major differences. One such differentiating feature is the level of analysis applied. As can be seen from the descriptions of the distance relationship for the concepts discussed in the section ‘Forms of organization’, certain forms are relevant on the individual level in that they mainly involve the individuals’ way of working and how they experience various working circumstances. Other forms have to do with the company and groupings within it, while still others deal with relations between companies.

An illustration of a classification according to level of analysis is presented in Table 1. The illustration is made with the concepts discussed previously. Several of these concepts involve more than one level of analysis. Where the concepts are discussed on more than one level of analysis, the concepts involve an organizational form which mainly deals with one level, but which also has effects on the next higher level. As mentioned, meetings/co-operation at a distance form a separate case. The concept can be used on all levels of analysis and it lacks precision. It will, therefore, not be treated further here.

The concept virtual organization in Table 1 involve such an aggregate level that it cannot be analysed in the same terms as the other organizational forms of work. These other forms, however, can be represented ‘within this aggregated concept. We will therefore not discuss the concept virtual organization further.

## Dimensions for organizational forms

Certain mutual properties hold for the remaining concepts. They are relatively new concepts; it is natural to associate them with the use of ICT and they mainly involve the analysis levels of individual and group. We consider these concepts to be the core of the concept of telework. In what follows we identify certain dimensions which can be used to discriminate between the concepts, see Table 2. We will discuss six dimensions that we think are relevant for the discussion of telework-related forms of organization since they represent important differences between these. The dimensions should be seen as examples. In other studies the list could be longer or shorter depending on the organizational form described or the aim of the description.

The first distinction is whether the individual has just one, fixed workplace or whether there are several workplaces to alternate between. The fixed workplace might be either the office, the home, or some other type of distant workplace.

Some of the forms includes work while travelling and this is a second dimension.

The individual’s working hours can be either fixed or flexible. By flexible we mean that the individual has a major say at which times during the day the work is performed, i.e., not ordinary flextime.

Another flexibility in time concerns the individuals possibility to choose how much time should be spent at a distance and also when this work should be performed. This is our fourth dimension.

A fifth dimension along which the various concepts differ is the individual’s physical distance from his or her nearest superior. The different forms of work or organization can be characterised by the fact that an individual’s workplace is always at the same place as his or her supervisor, is never in the same place, or he or she works at the same place as the supervisor for shorter or longer periods. The latter two cases—where the individual is always or sometimes at a distance from the supervisor—may be a result of the working circumstances of either the supervisor or of the employee. However, in Table 2 we refer to the individuals’ distance to a supervisor who normally works at his or her main workplace. This also applies to the following discussion.

A last dimension which can be used to discriminate between the concepts is the individual’s physical distance to all or several colleagues at the individual’s own division. For satellite offices the alternative ‘sometimes means that the individual is always partly distanced from his or her own division, i.e., that part that resides at the main office, while at the same time the individual is never distanced from another part of the division, i.e., the group at the satellite office.

The six dimensions presented in Table 2 can be combined in various ways. To illustrate the principle of using dimensions, we use a number of concepts discussed in section 2. Note that since we have refrained from choosing one specific definition for any of the concepts discussed earlier, the classification made in the table should be seen as an example. Other authors might classify the forms in a different way.

For example, the working form of work at home means that the employee only has access to one workplace and thus cannot choose where the work is to be performed.

On the other hand in certain cases the individual can choose his or her working hours. The flexiplace form of work means that the individual can perform his or her work at several different locations, though within fixed time limits concerning how much time should be spent at a distance and perhaps also when this work should be performed. In multiflex and to a high degree in mobile work and in telecommuting the individual can choose both where the work is performed and when. In mobile work the work is performed while travelling and the workplaces can be trains, cars, hotels etc. The multiflex form of work also, to some extent, includes work while travelling.

Work at home also means that both the manager and colleagues are always at a geographical distance from the individual working at home. The detached unit form of work means, for an individual working there, that the manager’s and colleagues’ workplaces are always in the same place as the individual’s. On the other hand, satellite offices mean that the manager and certain colleagues at the division are at a geographical distance. As the telecommuting, multiflex, flexiplace and mobile work forms intend that the individual chooses his or her workplace, this means that the manager and colleagues at the division are sometimes at a geographical distance.

## Dimensions for workplaces

Here we present a number of dimensions that can be used for discriminating between different concepts regarding workplaces. The dimensions are listed in Table 3. As for the dimensions regarding organizational forms discussed above, the classification made here should be seen as an example. Other authors might classify the same concepts differently. For our illustration we use the concepts we presented in ‘Workplaces’.

Some of these workplaces are more permanent in character than others and can be normal places of work. The individual uses some workplaces as a complement to other places of work; for example a workplace in the home is usually combined with a workplace at the company. Several of the workplaces are chosen by the individual because they are in the vicinity of the home or to avoid a certain amount of travelling. Some workplaces are of such a character that they are usually only temporary set-ups for the individual. The workplaces mentioned in Table 3 also differ with regard to proximity to colleagues and to opportunities for social contact, either with people from their own company or with others. Another difference concerns who is responsible for the workplace, the company where the individual is employed or some other body (several companies together or a third party).

The dimensions given in Table 3 can be combined in different ways as to the workplace. To illustrate this we use the concepts previously discussed in ‘Workplaces’.

For example, the home can be the normal workplace but it can also be used as an alternate workplace for an individual that has his or her normal workplace somewhere else. Neighbourhood work centres, telecottages, resort offices, and mobile work places are on the other hand normally only used as alternate workplaces.

Some of the workplaces have as an important feature that they are located close to the homes of the individuals working there. Examples, besides the home, are neighbourhood work centres, telecottages, and satellite work centres. The reasons for using these workplaces are often to avoid certain trips. The same reason often motivates work while travelling (mobile work places). A resort office is an office that is used in combination with vacations. By having an office nearby one does not have to travel to the normal office for doing occasional work.

Work at home or at a mobile workplace means that the individual is often alone at the workplace. In a normal office or at a satellite work centre, social contact and the opportunity to share ideas with colleagues are possible. At neighbourhood work centres, resort offices or telecottages there will, mostly, be no colleagues, but there is the opportunity of social contact with other people working there.

Equipment at a workplace in the individual’s home is often paid for by the individual’s company, but sometimes by the individual himself. The satellite work centre can house several workplaces which are all paid for by one and the same company. The neighbourhood work centre is a workplace where several companies contribute to the equipment but it could also be a workplace where it is possible to rent a place from a third party. In a similar way it is a third party that rents out workplaces in resort offices or telecottages. The workplaces for ‘mobile work’ may be owned by the company or be made available by others.

## Discussion

The aim of this article has been to describe a number of concepts used in the area of telework and to show how it is possible to use certain dimensions to discriminate between such concepts. In section 2 we discussed the concept of telework and presented a large number of other concepts related to telework. In section 3 we showed that it is possible to discriminate between the concepts by using a number of dimensions such as form versus place, and level of analysis. Under the headings of form and place we discriminated further between the concepts by using dimensions such as the individual’s freedom in the choice of workplace and working hours for organizational form, and closeness to the home and proximity to colleagues for workplaces.

Such discrimination is important from both an academic and a practical perspective. Seen from an academic viewpoint it is important to use an as exact concept as possible when various phenomena are discussed. Without a clear frame of reference unnecessary confusion and imprecision may arise around what is actually meant, and occurrences and effects cannot be discussed in an appropriate manner. This has been clear in Sweden where a number of statistical investigations regarding telework has shown very differing results. The confusion caused by this may be traced back to different investigations using different definitions of the same concepts.

From a practical point of view a classification which is as precise as possible is of great importance for how easy it is to identify and judge potential effects of a particular form of work or organizational form.

From the discussion above, both with regard to the form of work and the workplace, it is evident that prerequisites for telework differ in many ways. There can be major differences between different ‘telework situations’. Many factors come into play to determine whether a ‘telework form’ is successful or not and what should be taken into consideration in various situations to make any possible improvements.

One example of an important question is the individual’s freedom to choose when and/or where he or she is to work. The more freedom the individual has to choose when and where his or her distance work is to be performed, the greater the opportunity to adapt the working situation to work requirements. Furthermore, the more co-operation with colleagues or the manager that takes place at a geographical distance, the greater is the risk that problems can arise. This increases demands that more effort must be made to inform and communicate, for example by using better ICT-tools, routines or other activities.

Another factor to be taken into consideration is the individual’s need for some form of social contact in connection with work when working at a distance over a longer period of time. In certain situations it is important to be near at least some colleagues to discuss problems or exchange ideas. In other cases such contact need not be with colleagues; it may suffice to meet other people at coffee breaks and suchlike so as not to feel isolated. Instead of having the workplace in the home, an individual can, for example, have his workplace at a neighbourhood working centre.

Yet another dimension has to do with the individual’s or group’s distance from the manager. This dimension emphasises how teleworking individuals or groups can be managed or co-ordinated. This is probably a transition to a form of management more geared to output-oriented control than before. Selecting those variables which are to form the basis for management and how they are to be matched against each other must be determined in each individual case.

In choosing which form of work is suitable in the various cases, the actual work duties to be performed play a significant role. It is the duties the work form is to be applied to which determine how the work or organization form functions. A person with duties requiring frequent and close personal contact with colleagues should not work at home full-time. It is difficult to say anything in general on which duties are suitable for the various forms of work or organization. The importance of the duties for the chosen work form has been discussed by several authors; see, for instance, Huws et al (1990), or Lindstrom and Rapp (1996).

Work duties are also linked to another dimension which is significant for the effectiveness of the chosen form, viz. the need for learning. There are duties of such a nature which require continuous learning, a type of learning where it is important that the individual spends time in an environment with others doing similar jobs. In this way each individual is given continuous opportunities to learn not only from their own experience but also from that of others.

As can be seen from this discussion, there are a number of interesting and important questions for future work in the telework area. We have shown that there are many factors at various levels that must be considered, such as management, type of duties, social contact and learning. It is a difficult task for an organization to decide on a certain telework form. It can be seen as a large jigsaw puzzle where a set of different variables must be considered simultaneously.

In future studies, we believe that it is important not to add to an already existing confusion by using new definitions of the concepts. Instead, we feel that the right way to go is to, in each instance, classify the studied phenomena according to a number of relevant dimensions. It has not been the aim of this article to present a definitive list of such dimensions but rather to propagate for the principle. It is also worth noticing that other authors might classify the forms in a different way. The concepts have originated from different purposes of use and therefore they might have different meanings for different people. We believe that more concepts as well as more—and possibly better—dimensions will appear in the future. It is an important feature of the ‘dimensions principle’ proposed here, that it is able to adapt to such changes.
