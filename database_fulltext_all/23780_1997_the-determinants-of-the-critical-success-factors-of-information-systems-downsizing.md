# The determinants of the critical success factors of information systems downsizing

GJ Udo<sup>1</sup> and RC Kick<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Center of Excellence Information Systems, College of Business, Tennessee State University, Nashville, TN 37203; <sup>2</sup>Department of Decision Sciences & Management, College of Business Administration, Tennessee Technological University, Cookeville, TN 38505, USA

Competitive advantage in the information age is based, in large part, on a firm’s capability to acquire and use quality information as well as effective and efficient services, with its information technology resources at the least possible cost. One possible strategy for improving the cost-performance ratio of information technology resources is known as downsizing. The downsizing effort is a strategic move that is believed by many organizations to be capable of yielding significant benefits. It can also be a costly endeavour which may leave a firm worse off. Therefore, careful planning and control must be exercised if information systems downsizing is to succeed. This paper reports the results of a study whose purpose is to identify and explain the critical success factors for a downsizing effort. Results of the study show that information systems downsizing may produce benefits such as improved information systems, improved organizational structure, higher productivity, and lower cost. The results also indicate that downsizing success is dependent upon complex linkages of communications, action plans, needs for downsizing as perceived by employees, and the specific method of downsizing used.

## Introduction

The information age is proving to be a double-edged sword for businesses. On the one side the variety and power of information technology (IT) resources (hardware, software, data communications, personnel) have been an enabling factor for sustained growth and profitability for some. On the other side there has been an increasing dependency on IT not only for growth, but for survival. In short, cost-effective deployment and usage of IT resources has become a strategic success factor for business firms (Saarinen & Vepsalainen, 1994). Firms, at a time of growing global competition and scarce economic resources, are striving to create efficient organizational structures and cost-effective (highly productive) information system (IS) functions (Earl, 1996). An IS function is defined as the total resources (measured by total costs) a firm commits to providing information for decision-making and IT-based services. Traditionally, firms have utilised centralised IS departments to provide the bulk of IT-based support. In the last decade there has been a movement to end-user computing where the centralised IS department has shared responsibility with end-users for providing information and services. Contracting with a third party for the production of specific IT support is a third method that firms may use for IS services.

As technology increasingly impacts the ability to compete in world markets, the streamlining of corporate organizational structures, with the goal of improved productivity of human resources, have become a major strategy for survival and growth (Earl, 1996; Saarinen & Vepsalainen, 1994). The strategic move of realigning the IS function with a firm is often a major component of wider corporate streamlining (downsizing). Downsizing of the IS function can be viewed within the context of firm downsizing in which multiple functional areas are affected, or as a strategy in which only the IS function is involved. It is apparent, then, that IS downsizing, not being an end in itself, is a part of the broader context of firm downsizing.

The potential benefits of downsizing have motivated many firms to jump into the process without sufficient analysis of the real cost of downsizing and the suitability of their situation to the process (Hylas et al, 1989; Isabella, 1989; Sabrell, 1990). Several companies have approached downsizing without thinking through all the consequences. For example, First American Bank’s Chairman Smith announced that his bank would save \$10 million and 150 positions over ten years by farming out major computing operations to an IBM subsidiary, without stating how much such an action would cost the bank or how well the bank would be served (Cusack, 1991). Metropolitan Life Insurance Company decided to halt its IS downsizing after realizing, half-way through the process, that it involved much more than technology issues and that the company needed to give it more careful consideration before proceeding with the process (Cusack, 1991). In effect, IS downsizing often requires more adjustments in the organizational structure than firms anticipate or prepare for at the outset (Huff, 1989; Davis & Hamilton, 1993). Thus, one important ingredient to successful downsizing is a thorough analysis of the total impact of the process before taking any action (Isabella, 1989; Neilson, 1990).

Some firms do not systematically cost-justify and evaluate the process of downsizing so it is not clear whether firms actually experience the potential advantages of downsizing. Case studies found in the literature are mixed concerning the experiences of IS downsized firms (Doll & Doll, 1990, 1992; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993; Cross, 1995). Some have reported substantial savings while others show inconsequential or no gains. It seems some firms simply transfer costs from one location or unit to another without any real cost savings.

Apparently not every company that downsizes its IS department benefits from the process (Sabrell, 1990). Some of the reported benefits might have come on an exploded scale (Keily, 1992); that is, the benefits might not have been as immense as reported, if a proper costbenefit analysis was done. Cameron (1994) rightly points out that “downsizing may be the most common, albeit under-researched, practice in the current world of business”. She adds that “empirical examinations of the different causes, results, and dynamics of downsizing remain at a low level”. Though Cameron’s (1994) observation is about organizational downsizing in general, it is equally true about IS downsizing in particular. Since IS downsizing has such a strategic impact on companies, empirical studies should be conducted to determine the factors driving a successful implementation.

The focus of downsizing in this paper is the IS function within the broader context of the company as a whole and with respect to the IS function: the process or means of attempting to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of IT resources owned or contracted for by the company, with the goal of improving the firm’s strategic position. The purpose of the study presented in this paper is to determine the factors leading to successful IS downsizing in the United States through a survey of companies which have implemented IS downsizing. The specific issues examined are:

assessment of the real (experienced) benefits and pitfalls of IS downsizing;

identification of factors that determine the successful implementation of IS downsizing;

I evaluation of the effective downsizing methods; and

development and evaluation of a framework of IS downsizing critical success factors.

## Theory and conceptual framework

The potential advantages of downsizing are impressive to corporations which are under great pressure to compete effectively. The potential advantages of downsizing include: (Huff, 1989; Neilson, 1990; Cross, 1995).

I cost savings;

I business integration or closer ties between functional areas and information systems;

I increased system flexibility or options;

streamlined responsibilities and decreased bureaucracy;

I improved user productivity;

I more user-friendly interfaces;

I maintaining consistency with the firm’s strategy;

I reallocating resources;

I simplifying management processes;

I refocusing the firm on its core business; and

I more effective and satisfied workforce.

Dearden (1987) enumerates the benefits as follows: lower costs, superior products, sense of ownership and responsibility to the end-user, reduced response time, and increased user initiatives. For the purpose of this study, the benefits of IS downsizing reported in the literature can be grouped as follows:

(a) improved information systems, including increased system flexibility and more user-friendly interfaces;

(b) improved organization, including business integration or closer ties between functional areas and IS, streamlined responsibilities and decreased bureaucracy, maintaining consistency with the firm’s strategy, reallocation of resources, simplifying management processes, and refocusing the firm on its core business;

(c) increased productivity, including improved user productivity, more effective and satisfied workforce, superior products, reduced response time, and increased user initiatives;

(d) cost savings.

To identify the critical success factors for IS downsizing implementation, it is helpful to apply the principles and concepts of project and change management as IS downsizing implementation is a type of project that significantly affects both the organizational processes and the people. Dauphinais and Pederson (1995) narrate fifteen guiding principles for change management. They also postulate that effective communications, a good and comprehensive plan, and widespread needs perception are essential for the successful implementation of change. According to Badiru (1988), communication is the prerequisite for employees’ cooperation in the successful implementation of any project. The specific details of communication should include the objective, scope, interactions among all parties involved, and the importance of the project (Imberman, 1989). Bell and Burnham (1991) state that communication should provide answers to the what, why, and when questions about the project. Other authors (Badiru, 1988; Imberman, 1989) maintain that the clearer and more detailed the communication at all levels in the organization, the higher the chance of success for the project. Commitment is a measure of how the employees perceive the involvement of the top management and significant others. Dauphinais and Pederson (1995) maintain that commitment is a result of communications (with five Cs) that are candid, contextual, constructive, consistent, and continuous. The common notion is that the greater the commitment, the better the chance of success for the project (Imberman, 1989). The coordination variable attempts to measure how well the efforts of groups and individuals were facilitated by the project team toward achieving the objectives of the project. A good coordination enhances harmonious and systematic integration of individual efforts and contribution (Badiru, 1988). Therefore, our first hypothesis (H1) states that the communication is a determinant of a successful IS downsizing project: one that leads to improved computer-based systems, improved organizational structure, increased productivity, and cost savings.

Dauphinais and Pederson (1995) maintain that the implementation plan must be well documented and detailed, covering all major actions required, including goals, systems, people, organizational culture and structure, and training. Bell and Burnham (1991) strongly urge that the plan should take into account all the steps needed to accomplish the change objectives, specifying everyone’s role, outcomes expected at each stage of the project, and the risks involved. Indeed, they maintain that a contingency plan should also be in place to take care of any unexpected events. Based on the guidelines provided by Dauphinais and Pederson (1995), together with Bell and Burnham (1991), an implementation plan is absolutely essential and should cover the goals, resource allocation, employee’s orientation, and organizational culture. Therefore, the second hypothesis (H2) is that an action plan is a determinant of a successful IS downsizing project.

Perception of need is another crucial element identified by authors (Bell & Burnham, 1991; Dauphinais & Pederson, 1995) for successful implementation of change. Dauphinais and Pederson (1995) suggest that the employees be made to confront the reality, that as new business models emerge, the company’s competitiveness is bound to falter and as such new measures have to be taken by the company. They also maintain that the organization has to build a powerful case for change, which implies “a reasoned yet powerfully persuasive justification for the changes targeted by your change effort”. This approach is capable of building a strong sense of urgency and action. According to Dauphinais and Pederson (1995), the need perception should be focused on the customer demands, survival of the organization, better working conditions for the workers, and of course, economic improvement for both the employees and the organization. Therefore, the third hypothesis (H3) is that the perceived need factor is a determinant of a successful IS downsizing project.

In addition to the three critical factors discussed above, the results of the pilot study conducted earlier by the authors (discussed later in the text) reveal that the downsizing method is a major potential determinant of IS downsizing success. According to Davis and Hamilton (1993), there are three dimensions to IS downsizing: the hardware, the software, and the IS functions. The hardware and software dimensions involve migration of the IS applications and the hardware/software from centralised mainframes to networks of smaller decentralised computers. The functional dimension of IS downsizing involves moving some or all IS responsibilities into the business units or to vendors (Melymuka, 1989). If some or all of the IS functions are moved to vendors, the organization is said to implement its IS downsizing process via outsourcing. The cheap and highly-skilled labour pool obtained abroad has led to a sudden increase in global outsourcing. The three major IS downsizing methods are: (1) reducing centralised IS department roles; (2) moving to end-user computing development (EUCD); and (3) contracting with vendors for IS services. Within the context of maintaining a centralised IS environment, a firm may reduce IS department roles and expenditures by reducing personnel and/or moving to a smaller platform. Moving to end-user computing involves shifting IS costs and responsibilities from the IS department to end-users. Contracting with vendors for IS services is normally done because end-users do not have the capability or vendors can provide the services at a lower cost. Therefore, the fourth hypothesis (H4) is that downsizing method is a determinant of a successful IS downsizing project.

In order to meet the requirements of path analysis for testing structural model, the following hypotheses will also be tested (Billings & Wroten, 1978):

H5: The improved information system has positive, direct and indirect effects on improved organization, increased productivity and cost savings.

H6: The improved organization has positive, direct and indirect effects on increased productivity and cost savings.

H7: Increased productivity has a positive, direct and indirect effect on cost savings.

Using the principles of project and change management and the results of the pilot study, a conceptual framework was developed to capture the critical success factors for the IS downsizing implementation success. ## Measures

## A. Independent variables

A search of the literature could not yield a previously tested and validated instrument with which to measure the study variables (Cameron, 1994), so an instrument was developed based on the principles of project and change management, results of pilot study, and facts highlighted in case studies (Doll & Doll, 1990, 1992; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993; Cross, 1995). All items were constructed with a five-point Lifetree-type scale. The responses were solely the perception of the respondents. The respondents were asked to, “Please indicate your agreement to the following factors that might have contributed to the successful implementation of information systems downsizing in your organization by circling the number that indicates the degree to which you agree the factors enhanced the success of the downsizing project in your firm”. In order to ascertain that the measures were assessing distinct constructs, an exploratory factor analysis was performed on the independent variables (using Varimax rotation) and the results revealed that the three factors were distinct. Since a total of 16 items of 98 cases were used for the factor analysis, the ratio is within the acceptance level for an exploratory analysis. Methods of downsizing were investigated as a fourth factor that may impact the success of IS downsizing implementation. The fourth factor was not included in the factor analysis since it was not measured with Lifetree-scale items. The four factors that constituted the independent variables are discussed below. Unless otherwise indicated, each scale was scored using a five-point response mode ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree.

Communication factor (eigenvalue of 2.97, alpha = 0.77) The communication factor consists of communication, commitment, and coordination variables. Each variable was defined with a single item. The efficacy of a singleitem measure is supported by the findings of many authors (Igbaria & Siegel, 1992). The respondents were asked to respond to the following:

I communications at all levels in the organization;

I strong commitment by every party to the project;

I sound coordination by the project team.

Action plan factor (eigenvalue of 3.11, alpha = 0.83) Effective planning has been considered by many as a key factor that determines the success of IS downsizing (Doll & Doll, 1992; Huff, 1989; Belohlav & LaVan, 1989). The five variables that comprise this category of factor were:

1. Clear downsizing goals. This indicates how well the goals of the downsizing process were stated by the top management and to what accuracy the employees understood the goals. Morris (1989) maintains that if the goals and objectives of corporate downsizing are not clearly stated and understood, rumours could start and reactions based on a third-party’s analysis could cause harm to the process. This variable was measured by asking the participants to respond to the following:

(a) clarity of downsizing goals and objectives;

(b) clear understanding of downsizing goals and objectives.

2. Resource allocation. This measures how well the participants perceived resources were appropriately allocated during the process. Kiely (1992) found that the costs involved in downsizing often prove to be higher than anticipated and that the goals of downsizing may not be attained if chief information officers (CIOs) do not believe that resources are appropriately allocated. This variable was measured with the following:

(a) optimal allocation of resources;

(b) good use of available resources.

3. Action plan. This measure indicates the quality and completeness of the action plan. Kiely (1992) and Rao (1992) maintain that the effectiveness and completeness (thoroughness) of a downsizing plan can affect its success. The respondents were asked to respond to:

(a) availability of plan of action;

(b) quality of action plan.

4. Employee orientation. This variable includes preparing the IS and the business unit staffs for the psychological and social impact of downsizing. It also includes training the affected employees on their new roles. According to Belohlav and LaVan (1989), employee orientation (training and development) must be considered first if corporate downsizing is to achieve its expected goals. This is equally true in the case of IS downsizing. The statements that respondents responded to were:

(a) sufficiently preparing the employees for the consequences of downsizing;

(b) quality of training during and after the process.

5. Cultural compatibility. This variable measures the degree to which the downsizing procedure is compatible with the organizational culture. The organization should clearly show this critical dimension in the action plan documentation rather than leave it for the individual worker to figure out. Dauphinais and Pederson (1995) maintain that in order to manage change successfully, the implementation plan must cover organizational culture issues. The respondents were asked to respond to the following:

(a) consistency of the downsizing approach with the firm’s culture;

(b) consistency of the downsizing approach with the firm’s strategy.

Perceived need factor (eigenvalue of 2.55, alpha = 0.69) Perceived need factor consists of the following variables: unit integration need, need for cooperation, and economic need. Perceived need factor attempts to measure how much the people believe the downsizing process to be necessary for their firm’s survival. Kiely (1992) maintains that if CIOs do not perceive downsizing as a necessary line of action for improving organizational performance, it could seriously affect the implementation success. Naturally, people work hard to achieve what they believe in. If top management succeeds in convincing the IS staff of the necessity of downsizing in terms of improving business units integration, improving the cooperation among employees, and enhancing the economic position of the company; and if the IS staff believe the same to be true, they will work hard to attain success. In other words, perceived need factor could affect how much of the benefits of downsizing is experienced by a firm. Accordingly, the participants were asked to rate to what extent they perceived the need for downsizing in the following categories:

I improving business unit integration;

I improving cooperation among employees;

I enhancing economic position of the firm.

## Downsizing Method Factor

The three main methods of downsizing are: (1) reduction of centralised IS department, (2) distributed computerbased systems and end-user computing development (EUCD), and (3) outsourcing which is contracting the IS services to a vendor. The respondents were asked to indicate which of the above methods were employed in their companies. The response options were anchored on a method-linked three-point scale as follows:

1. centralised IS reduction;

2. distributed IS/EUCD; and

3. outsourcing.

## B. Dependent variables

The success of any project can be measured by how much of the anticipated benefits are realised. Accordingly, the success of IS downsizing implementation can be measured by how much of its potential benefits are experienced by the companies. The benefits of IS downsizing reported in the literature can be grouped as follows: improved IS, improved organization, increased productivity, and cost savings. The distinction of these measures was verified with an exploratory factor analysis using 15 items of 98 cases. All items were constructed with a five-point Lifetree-type scale, ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. The responses were solely the perception of the respondents. The respondents were asked to, “Please indicate your agreement to the following tangible and intangible gains your firm might have realized from the downsizing process by circling the number that indicates the degree to which you agree the gains have been experienced in your firm.” The four success indicators are discussed below.

## Improved IS (eigenvalue of 3.23, alpha = 0.86)

This variable is often quoted as one of the benefits of downsizing. An improved IS is the one that is flexible, easy to use, easy to manage, user-friendly, protects against error, and requires less training time. Rao (1989) lists the following as some of the major purposes of IS downsizing: improving information flow, easier MIS management, and cost cutting. The participants were asked to respond to the following measures:

I increased system flexibility;

I more user-friendly interfaces;

I better access to data;

I improved network performance.

Improved organization (eigenvalue of 3.11, alpha = 0.82)

This is another often-quoted benefit of IS downsizing. Downsizing often integrates the IS functions into business units and corporate infrastructures since IS staff are required to learn about the business in addition to their IS functions. Since business unit people are actively involved in the design and development of the new information systems, there is a sense of involvement, commitment, ownership, and responsibility. The net effect of the experience of IS downsizing process is an improved organizational structure (King, 1983). Accordingly, the participants were asked to respond to the following measures:

I closer ties between end-users and IS department;

I streamlined responsibilities;

I shorter application development cycles;

I better organizational structure.

Increased productivity (eigenvalue of 3.01, alpha = 0.81) Some organizations have reported an increase in productivity as one of the benefits of IS downsizing (Udo & Kick, 1994). If the new system is more user-friendly, more flexible, and takes less time to learn; and if sense of responsibility, involvement, and ownership is boosted by downsizing, then productivity is bound to go up. The statements that measure this benefit were:

I enhancing business re-engineering (business process re-engineering);

I improved employee productivity;

I improved worker utilisation.

Cost savings (eigenvalue of 2.74, alpha = 0.76)

Cost savings has been reported as the foremost reason why organizations embark on IS downsizing (Hylas et al, 1989; Rao, 1992; Udo & Kick, 1994). The main costsaving areas are reduction in licensing fees, equipment, operations, administration, and maintenance. The respondents were asked to respond to the following measures:

I operation cost reduction or avoidance;

I maintenance cost reduction;

I administrative cost reduction;

I equipment cost reduction.

## C. Demographics

Participants were asked to indicate the type of industry to which their organization belongs. Industry type consisted of six levels: (1) manufacturing; (2) financial services; (3) transportation; (4) retailing; (5) construction; and (6) other.

Size of the company was measured by the number of full-time employees and previous year sales volume. IS budget and years of existence were measured at six distinct levels each.

Initiators of IS downsizing were grouped into five categories: (1) top management; (2) other levels of management; (3) workers; (4) vendors; and (5) others.

Downsizing duration consisted of five levels: (1) less than 6 months; (2) 1 to 5 years; (3) 5 to 10 years; (4) 10 to 20 years; and (5) over 20 years.

Organizational tenure was measured by the number of years an individual had been employed in the organization. Ranks of respondents consisted of: (1) top management; (2) middle management; (3) lower management; (4) staff/professional and (5) others. The demographic items were included in the background section of the survey instrument.

## Method of study

## Procedure

Since there were very few theoretical or empirical studies in existence, the authors conducted a pilot study to determine how much downsizing is obtained by firms and whether or not the firms experience the presumed benefits and pitfalls of IS downsizing. The pilot study involved a survey of 23 chief information officers (CIOs) of 100 of the top companies that were known as the most effective IS users. One of the main focuses of the pilot study was to find out from the top practitioners what actually caused them to downsize their IS departments and what costs and payoffs they experienced from the process. Based on the results of the pilot study and the written comments from the professionals, the authors developed an 80-item survey instrument for this study. Note that not all 80 items on the instrument related to the research issues of the present paper.

Participants in the main study were 450 CIOs (or their equivalent) from large US firms. To qualify for selection, a company had to be publicly owned, US-based, and be in the top half of its vertical industry group as listed in the annual ‘Fortune Industrial 500’ or ‘Fortune Service 500’. Overall scores were based on the sum of point totals of six criteria: IS budget, five-year profit average, current market value of major processors, percentage of budget spent on staff, percentage of budget spent on training, and ratio of personal computers and terminals to total employees.

One hundred and fifteen (115) instruments were returned. Seventeen (17) of the returned instruments were blank because IS downsizing did not take place in those organizations. Completed instruments used in the data analysis came from 98 participants which is a 22.6% response rate. Based on the size of the firms, IS budget, geographical location, number of employees and annual revenue of both the respondents and non-respondents, there was no significant difference between the two groups. The demographic characteristic of the respondents are provided on Table 1.

The respondents came from different industries: 38.8% from manufacturing, 15.3% from financial services, 13.3% from transportation, 8.8% from retailing, 7.1% from construction, and 15.3% from various other industries. Over 67% of the companies employed more than 1000 employees. Over 80% of the companies reported the previous year’s earnings of \$5 million or more. The IS current annual budget was over \$1 million for more than 62% of the participants. The IS downsizing initiators were senior management in 71.4% of the cases and other levels of management in 21.4% of the cases. The ranks of the respondents in their respective organizations were top management (65%) and middle management (32.7%).

The advantage of least-square multiple regression is its ability to recognise the two components of the model building, namely: the measurement model and the structural model. With this recognition the researchers can assess the measurement model before testing the structural model.

Path analysis was chosen in this study for two reasons: (1) the small sample size and (2) the study variables are well established in the literature. Other structural equation modelling techniques such as LISREL require a much larger sample size. The reason for the small sample size in this study is not farfetched. Most companies do not want to talk about their IS downsizing experience either because they see it as a strategic matter or because the experience is not positive. The first three variables in the study (communication, action, and perceived need) are well documented in the project and change management literature. The remainder of the study variables (methods of downsizing and the dependent variables) were initially tested in a pilot study and found to be significant. Fornell (1982) documented the advantages of path analysis over structural equation modelling techniques. Steps documented in (Billings & Wroten, 1978) were taken to ensure that the assumptions underlying the use of path analysis were not violated. The alpha coefficients indicated that the internal consistency reliability of all the multi-item scales were at acceptable levels. The alpha coefficients ranged from 0.69 to 0.86 while the correlation coefficients ranged from 0.012 to 0.561. This result is an indication that extreme multicolinearity (i.e., r’s .0.80) was absent. The Durbin-Watson ‘d’ statistics of the residuals of the endogenous variables were tested for autocorrelation using the Durbin-Watson (d) statistics, indicating the absence of correlated residuals. The d-statistics range from 1.42 to 2.09 with a mean of 1.88.

According to Billings and Wroten (1978), the use of the path analysis technique in testing a model invollves two kinds of analyses. The first analysis entails examining a full recursive model with a multiple regression analysis to evaluate significant direct effects of the predicting factors on each benefit. At the end of this analysis, the nonsignificant relationships are eliminated resulting in a trimmed model. In the first analysis, 98 cases were used to test the measurement model. The second analysis is known as the omitted-parameter test and entails using the eliminated relationships to test for the significance of the paths in the trimmed model. This is achieved by testing both the predicted and the nonpredicted direct paths of the model variables. At the end of the second analysis, the paths of the trimmed model are either confirmed or disconfirmed. The significance of the estimated path coefficients were based on t-statistics obtained from a jackknifing test (Tukey, 1958).

The omitted-parameter test was implemented with hierarchical multiple regression (Cohen & Cohen, 1983), which is capable of indicating the significance of the direct and indirect effects of the predicting factors on each benefit indicator. Cost savings was first regressed on communication before adding action plan in step 2, perceived need, downsizing method, improved IS, improved organization, increased productivity in steps 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7, respectively. Similarly, increased productivity was regressed on communication in the first step, adding action plan, perceived need, downsizing method, improved IS, improved organization in the second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth steps, respectively. Also, improved organization was regressed on communication, action plan, perceived need, downsizing method, and improved IS, adding one factor at a time in the order listed. Finally, improved IS was regressed on communication, action plan, and perceived need, adding one factor at a time in the order listed.

Each of the analyses provided information on the significance of the beta weight for the predicting factors. Based on the significance of the standardised (stable) beta weight, the corresponding hypothesis was either accepted or rejected. When the first predicting factor enters the regression model, the resulting beta weight is the total effect of that predicting factor on the benefit indicator, and when the last predicting factor enters the regression model, the final beta weight represents the direct effect of the variable. The difference between the total effect and the direct effect is the indirect effect of the predicting factor on the particular benefit indicator (Ross, 1975).

## Discussions and conclusions

The results of this study indicate that IS downsizing implementation has definite benefits that are the product of complex linkages among effective communication, effective action plan, perceived need for downsizing, and the specific methods used for downsizing. The real benefits of IS downsizing revealed in this study are: improved computer-based systems (in terms of ease of use, friendliness, flexibility, and suitability to local tasks), improved organization (in terms of flow of information, streamlined responsibilities, business unit integration, commitment, and involvement of employees), increased productivity and cost savings. These findings highlight the importance of a dynamic coordination, effective resource allocation, cooperating employees and the downsizing methods as the critical success factors for saving costs via IS downsizing. Coordination had direct and indirect effects on cost savings. This finding emphasises the importance of a well thought-out programme that leaves no stone ‘unturned’ in coordinating the efforts and skills of everyone in the organization during the downsizing process. One of the interesting findings of this study is that communication had adverse direct and indirect effects on cost savings. This is an indication that although effective communication may have positive impact on some benefits of downsizing, it could indeed cause the cost of operations to rise. The study also reveals that an improved IS and increased productivity have strong positive effects on cost savings. In effect, the results imply that downsizing may lead to a more effective use of a computer-based systems, which in turn, may lead to cost savings. Also, everything being equal, if a firm can keep its productivity level high, cost savings may accrue faster than if productivity is low. It is equally interesting to note that improved organization had direct and indirect effects on cost savings. This finding makes sense in that if a firm is well organized, cost can be minimised. The implication is that IS downsizing can result in a well-organized firm, in which every employee has a sense of responsibility, involvement, and commitment, and where information flow is timely and accurate resulting in significant cost savings.

In order to realise the benefit of increased productivity, the importance of resource allocation and the use of personal computers and local area networking is highlighted. The evidence for this conclusion is the fact that both variables have strong positive, direct effects on increased productivity. Distributed IS/EUCD downsizing method indicated both the direct and indirect effects on increased productivity. The results highlight the fact that those firms that experience improved IS also experience a rise in productivity level.

Improved IS had a direct effect on increased productivity. Since computer-based systems are tools in today’s firm, it makes sense that better tools resulting from IS downsizing can lead to a rise in productivity of the workforce. A downsized IS is likely to be more familiar environment, more user-friendly, and more flexible (Rao, 1992), and as such, the user can pick up speed on the required tasks. Another critical factor that can impact increased productivity, based on the findings of this study, is improved organization. The environment of a well-organized firm enhances productivity more than the environment of a disarrayed firm (Belohlav & LaVan, 1989). In essence, the benefit of increased productivity is linked to a good organization structure which is, in itself, a product of IS downsizing. This finding agrees with several other studies summarised by Brynjolfsson (1993). These studies conclude that large IS budget does not necessarily lead to higher productivity. The present findings may imply that since IS downsizing can lead to reduced IS budget, productivity is likely to increase.

The benefit of improved organization was found to be directly influenced by perceived need and distributed computing method of downsizing. The observation in this regard is that those participants who claimed that they strongly perceived the need for downsizing were those who reported experiencing the benefit of an improved organization. Similarly, those participants whose companies downsized to distributed IS/EUCD reported experiencing improved organization. Interestingly, the distributed IS/EUCD method of downsizing had a positive direct effect and a negative indirect effect on improved organization. This implies that although distributed computing is likely to lead to a better organizational structure, its indirect impact could militate against its direct impact thereby reducing its total impact. Communication, clear downsizing goals, and perceived need of business unit integration were also critical success factors for the benefit of improved organization because they showed significant indirect effects. These results confirm the concepts of project management (Badiru, 1988) and change management principles (Dauphinais & Pederson, 1995). It is interesting to note that realising the benefit of an improved IS function does not guarantee improved organization.

Furthermore, the results reveal that an improved IS function as a benefit from the downsizing process is directly influenced by effective communication, commitment from top management, a well thought-out action plan and downsizing via distributed IS/EUCD. An interesting result is the direct and indirect effects of the distributed IS/EUCD method of downsizing on an improved IS. This finding supports a practical notion that personal computers (PC) are easier to use and provide a more friendly environment than other computing platforms. In effect, if the organizational goal for downsizing is to obtain a more friendly computing environment and a better organizational structure, the organization should downsize to a distributed system and local area network with PC terminals. This conclusion is in accordance with the findings in the study of Ahituv and his collaborators (1989). The results also place emphases on communication and action plan in that both factors have significant direct and indirect effects on an improved IS. Communication is critical during the design and implementation phases of the new IS that results from the downsizing process. If a firm expects to experience an improved IS as a result of its downsizing process, it should involve and communicate effectively with the business unit persons who will be using the new systems. On an equal note, an effective action plan can spell out the expectations and requirements of the new IS that adequately prepare the users for the new computing environment (Bell & Burnham, 1991). Downsizing to a distributed system and end-user computing environment also affect the improved IS in an indirect fashion.

It is the authors’ hope that the findings of this study are meaningful and provide added insight into the network of factors that contribute to the successful implementation of IS downsizing. This study can be used as a starting point for the IS downsizing research since very few empirical studies exist in the literature. The limitations of this study are peculiar to survey studies. The measures are based on the subjective evaluation by the respondents. Further studies should compare ‘real measures’ with the ‘perceived measures’ in this study. While the linkages among the study variables are well evaluated on the proposed model, they do not prove causality (James, 1980). According to Shore and Martin (1989), alternative linkages may exist among the variables in the model. Further effort can entail a longitudinal study that may answer some crucial questions regarding causal relationships among the variables in this study. It is a real possibility that more complex relationships than we propose could exist among the model variables. In that case, other variables not included in this study may be necessary to understand why some companies are more successful in IS downsizing than others. In addition, the fact that some nonsignificant linkages were found for some of the variables implies that caution needs to be taken when interpreting the results. Until the downsizing strategy and procedure are better understood and documented, firms should proceed with caution when engaging in IS downsizing implementation.
