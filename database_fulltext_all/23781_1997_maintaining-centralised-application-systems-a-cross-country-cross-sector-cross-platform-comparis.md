# Maintaining centralised application systems: a cross-country, cross-sector, cross-platform comparison

W-G Tan<sup>1</sup> and GG Gable<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Institute of Systems Science, National University of Singapore, Kent Ridge, Singapore 119597; <sup>2</sup>Faculty of Information Technology, Queensland University of Technology, GPO Box 2434 Brisbane, QLD Australia 4001

Past studies of software maintenance issues have largely concentrated on the average North American firm. While they have made a substantial contribution to good information system management practice, it is believed that further segmentation of sample data and cross-country comparisons will help to identify patterns of behaviour more akin to many less average organizations in North America and elsewhere. This paper compares the Singapore maintenance scene with the reported North American experience. Comparisons are also made between: Government organizations, Singapore corporations and multinational corporations (MNCs); mainframe and minicomputer installations; and fourth-generation language (4GL) and non 4GL computer installations. Study findings, while in many cases were similar to earlier US studies, do show the importance of Singapore’s young application portfolio, the widespread usage of 4GLs and the severe maintenance personnel problems.

## Introduction

Software maintenance has often been cited as one of the critical issues confronting organizations with a large investment in computer systems (eg, Bunday, 1988; Neiderman et al, 1991). Researchers have attributed this view to two common concerns of managers who are responsible for the upkeep of the application portfolio. First, the task of maintaining application systems is much more complex than has been portrayed in the literature. This has led to problems of degenerating software quality, poor maintenance productivity, low programmer motivation and other management concerns. Second, the maintenance activity consumes a major portion of the information systems (IS) budget. Both Boehm (1981) and Martin and McClure (1983) estimated that software maintenance incurs as much as 75% of overall software lifecycle costs. Conte et al (1986) claim that maintenance accounts for over 60% of the total software engineering effort. IS researchers have aptly described the high cost of maintaining application systems as the ‘maintenance burden’.

The unit of analysis in past studies of software maintenance management issues has tended to be the average North American firm. While this past research has made a substantial contribution to good IS management practice, it is believed that further segmentation of sample data and cross-country comparisons will help to identify patterns of behaviour more akin to many less average organizations in North America and elsewhere. Questions of interest to maintenance managers thus include, “Do all computer using organizations face the same maintenance problems?” and “What contingencies or contextual factors influence these problems?”. The answers to these questions should interest not only maintenance managers outside North America but also IS planners at the corporate headquarters of multinational corporations (MNCs) who desire to understand better the maintenance environments of their subsidiaries and North American firms whose maintenance context more closely mirrors the circumstances of sample segments studied elsewhere.

The paper proceeds as follows. First, the study background is described. Second, the research methodology is related. Third, study results are presented. Fourth, implications of the study findings are explored. Lastly, several broad conclusions are drawn.

## Background of the study

## Singapore and its IT industry

Singapore is a city-state of 3 million people situated at the southern tip of the Malay peninsula along the narrow passageway between the Indian and Pacific Oceans. The republic has a thriving economy that, with rare exception, has seen 6–10% GNP growth per annum over the last two decades, yielding a 1994 GNP per capita of about US\$23,360 (World Bank Atlas, 1996). This rapid progress has recently prompted the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) to reclassify Singapore as an advanced developing country. The republic’s main economic activities include financial services, high value-added manufacturing, distribution, transportation, telecommunication, publishing, professional services and health care. In response to global competition for these activities and the rapid growth of the region, many MNCs have set up their manufacturing bases and operational headquarters in Singapore.

Singapore is one of the few countries in the world with an information technology (IT) strategy as the centrepiece of its overall economic planning, and the Singapore civil service is the most automated in the world (Singapore National Computer Board, 1992; Sisodia, 1992; Dedrick et al, 1995). With the availability of an excellent IT infrastructure, a pool of high quality IS professionals and attractive tax incentives for acquisition of hardware and software, MNCs and local corporations are also deploying computers in increasing numbers to augment their businesses (Gurbaxani et al, 1990). The Singapore Government encourages smaller firms to computerise through the provision of generous financial as well as technical assistance (Gable & Raman, 1992). According to a recent IT usage survey (Singapore National Computer Board, 1994), the overall level of computerisation in Singapore has jumped from just 13% in 1982 to 90% in 1994. This growth in IT usage is spectacular by any standard. The same survey further noted that companies in Singapore are also spending less on hardware and more on IT personnel and software, indicating that they have moved beyond the early stages of computerisation.

## Motivation for the study

Although computers have been deployed in Singapore since the 1960s, their numbers only saw a dramatic increase during the last 15 years, prompted by the large scale computerisation of the public sector (Singapore National Computer Board, 1994). According to a study by Rao et al (1987), Singapore IS organizations generally show little or no lag in the adoption of new technologies and Singapore IS managers may have greater freedom to adopt new technologies, as compared to their counterparts in the United States. Application systems in Singapore thus tend to be newer in terms of their underlying technology and the methodology employed in their development. Despite this apparent advantage, Singapore has a lower lever of organizational maturity in the management of these application systems. Moreover, although the majority of software developers and maintainers have received advanced software training from tertiary institutions, their average experience level is generally lower than that in the United States (Moo, 1993).

From the preceding, it is apparent that the background of computerisation in Singapore is quite different from that of North America. While it is important to take account of these differences when considering the validity of software maintenance research conducted elsewhere, it is also important to identify similarities. One cross-cultural study conducted in Singapore with this intention was undertaken by Couger-Zawacki and Associates (Couger, 1986) under the auspices of the Singapore National Computer Board. A part of that study looked at the attitudes of Singapore IS professionals toward maintenance work. The study findings suggest that, like their United States counterparts, Singapore IS professionals generally view maintenance work as relatively undesirable.

It is clear there is a need for further research aimed at identifying the specific issues faced by maintenance managers in Singapore. The extensive deployment of computers in industry and government and the rapidly growing portfolio of software applications on which Singapore is dependent, magnify the imperative.

## Previous empirical studies on software maintenance

In 1977 Lientz and Swanson (Lientz & Swanson, 1978, 1980, 1981; Lientz, 1983) addressed the fundamental question: what constitutes maintenance in the application software context? Their prior work and survey of 487 IS organizations in the United States eventually led to a useful classification of all changes performed on operational application software viz: (a) corrective maintenance: to correct faults; (b) adaptive maintenance: to adapt the product to a changed environment; and (c) perfective maintenance: to improve performance or other attributes. Although this classification has found acceptance among many researchers and practitioners and was adopted by IEEE as their definition of software maintenance (ANSI/IEEE Standards 610.12, 1990), its acceptance is not universal and the debate continues. Lientz and Swanson also reported how the IS organizations they surveyed had rated a list of 26 predefined problem areas. Although there were weaknesses in their methodology (Chapin, 1986), Lientz and Swanson’s work continues to be held in high regard in view of the comprehensiveness of the analysis and extensiveness of the study.

Other empirical studies that follow Lientz and Swanson have also contributed to our understanding of software maintenance (e.g., Guimaraes, 1983; Dean & McCune, 1983; Helms & Weiss, 1985; Chapin, 1986; Van Over & Weiss, 1988; Swanson & Beath, 1989; Dekleva, 1992a). Notably, Chapin solicited the views of 260 software maintenance managers and from his findings concluded that the two dominant problems in software maintenance are software characteristics (e.g. documentation) and personnel factors. He further suggested that the areas offering most potential for shortterm improvement are those associated with personnel factors. Dekleva carried out a survey among the attendees at the 1990 Software Maintenance Association Annual Meeting using an instrument that was adapted from the ones used by Chapin and Lientz and Swanson. He found trends which indicate that the importance and needs of software maintenance are now better recognised, particularly in relation to personnel, the software system itself and the maintenance environment.

About a decade after Lientz and Swanson had published their findings, Nosek and Palvia (1990) conducted a follow-on study employing the same research instrument and sample population. From their longitudinal data Nosek and Palvia conclude that, despite advances in the technology, software maintenance concerns in the United States have not changed much in the 1980s. In a departure from the traditional questionnaire approach, Abran and Nguyenkim (1993) shed further light on maintenance activities through a detailed case study.

Caudle et al (1991) studied key IS management issues in the public sector and reported that 70% of the respondents regarded productivity in software maintenance as one of the top ten issues. No studies, however, have been made to investigate further how this and other software maintenance problems in the public sector compare with those in the private sector. Similarly, although a number of researchers (e.g., Martin & McClure, 1983; Chapin, 1984) have drawn attention to the difficulties of maintaining application systems implemented in fourthgeneration languages (4GLs), there is a general lack of comparative data on 4GL vs non-4GL based software maintenance (Bennett, 1990). Furthermore, prior surveys, like those undertaken by Lientz and Swanson and Dekleva, have tended to present their results in terms of the average computer-using organization, largely ignoring the hardware platform used by the respondents.

## Methodology

## Objective of the study

The objective of the current study is to investigate prevailing software maintenance management practices and problems faced by Singapore maintenance managers and to compare Singapore’s software maintenance experience with previous United States findings. In addition, the study aims to identify any unique patterns of behaviour within the Singapore sample through partitioning the sample by industry sector (government vs Singapore corporations vs MNCs), hardware platform (mainframe vs minicomputer), and language type (4GL vs non-4GL).

The study concentrates on centralised application systems which, as opposed to those developed on personal computers and maintained by end-users themselves, reside mostly on mainframes and minicomputers in the custody of IS organizations. They are typically high volume transaction-processing applications and are often mission-critical.

Despite keen current interest in client/server technology, there does not appear to be a stampede to migrate centralised applications to this new environment (Computerworld, January 30, 1995; Datamation, July 1, 1995). In a recent Singapore survey it was found that most respondents are in no hurry to adopt the technology (Computerworld Singapore, March 10, 1995). Downsizing of large application programs to client/server systems is difficult (Sneed & Nyary, 1994). In fact, mainframes are projected to be in heavy use well beyond the year 2000 due to their declining costs, and perceived limitations and high development costs of client/server systems. Thus, the maintenance of centralised application systems is expected to remain a crucial management issue into the foreseeable future both in the United States and Singapore.

## Data collection

In order to provide a comparative basis for the study, the survey instrument used is a modified version of that originally employed by Lientz and Swanson (Lientz & Swanson, 1980), who also surveyed centralised application systems. Careful consideration was given to reported weaknesses in their research methodology. Unlike the Lientz and Swanson survey, respondents in the current study were required to describe their application portfolio rather than a single existing major application. It was felt that the data collected for an arbitrarily selected application would not realistically reflect the overall state of software maintenance practice nor the general problems respondents are confronted with. A further distinction of the current study is the surveying of maintenance managers, who are felt to be best placed to answer queries regarding software maintenance management practices and problems.

Part one of the questionnaire contains general questions on the respondent organization while part two surveys current software maintenance practices. In part three, Lientz and Swanson’s original 26 maintenance problems were posed to the respondents. Anticipating that these 26 problems may not exhaustively account for all concerns, provision was made in part three for respondents to indicate other problems they are experiencing.

## Sample population

Taking advantage of the close links between the Institute of Systems Science (ISS) at the National University of Singapore and the local IT industry, 263 people with key managerial appointments in centralised IS installations in government, local commercial, and MNC organizations were identified from the ISS contact database. ISS is a 15-year government-funded organization that has through its education, consultancy and research programmes played an important catalytic role in Singapore’s computerisation growth. Its contact database is believed to be broadly representative of the Singapore IS community as a whole.

A pretest of the survey instrument was conducted prior to the actual mailing. This was carried out with the assistance of three known subjects taken from the list of selected names, each representing an organization from the government, local commercial and MNC sector. Their feedback primarily influenced the presentation of the questionnaire.

Altogether, 88 completed questionnaires (88/263 × 100 = 34%) were returned from the initial and followup mailings. This is regarded as a satisfactory sample size given Singapore’s small base population as compared to the United States. Eight responses were unusable either because only packaged software was employed or the application software is maintained by the respondent’s corporate headquarters outside of Singapore. Eighty-three cases which also include the three pre-test cases, are analysed and described in the remainder of this paper.

With the exception of two anonymous returns, all other respondents indicated their personal details and desire to receive a copy of the survey results. It is observed that 15% of the respondents are senior corporate executives (e.g. CIO), 69% are IS executives and systems managers who have direct control over maintenance, while 15% gave analyst titles (e.g. project leader). Responses from these three groups for all interval data (e.g. the 26 problems) were compared using the t-test, revealing no significant differences at the 0.05 level thus suggesting that all respondents had a common level of awareness of maintenance management problems (and perhaps suggesting that responses from senior corporate executives were commissioned from their maintenance managers).

The distribution of respondents in terms of sector (i.e. public sector, Singapore corporations, MNCs) was statistically compared to that of the non-respondents. The calculated chi-square value did not indicate any significant difference at the 0.05 level, thus suggesting the sample is representative in this respect.

Table 3 further shows that 74% of mainframe-based applications are less than seven years old while the corresponding figure for minicomputer-based applications is 92%. In other words, applications in mainframe installations tend to be older than those in minicomputer installations. Furthermore, MNCs have more systems that are older than seven years (21%) than the public (14%) or local commercial sectors (15%). This is not surprising since MNCs in Singapore have been using computers since the 1960s while the public sector and Singapore corporations only began to computerise on a large scale in the early 1980s.

The prevalence of 4GLs in Singapore is evident from Table 3. The use of 4GLs is particularly widespread in the public sector (67%) and, to a lesser extent, among Singapore corporations (44%). Table 3 further indicates that 4GLs are equally popular among mainframe and minicomputer installations. COBOL ranks second in overall usage and appears to be the alternative implementation language to 4GLs for mainframe-based applications (47%). RPG, on the other hand, is principally associated with minicomputer installations (36%), especially those of MNCs (37%). The figures registered for the other third-generation programming languages i.e. BASIC and FORTRAN are relatively small.

## Allocation of maintenance effort

With the objective of identifying possibly unique maintenance practices associated with the use of 4GLs, computer installations with more than 50% of their code written in a 4GL were identified. Table 4 presents proportions of maintenance effort versus development effort by sector, installation type and language type (4GL vs non-4GL). No significant differences were found at the 0.05 level (ANOVA).

Table 5 indicates proportions of maintenance versus development effort in Singapore as compared to the Lientz and Swanson (1978) and Nosek and Palvia (1990) United States studies. From the table it is observed that results of the current study are similar to those of the Lientz and Swanson study. Since the Nosek and Palvia study reported a higher proportion of maintenance efforts than the Lientz and Swanson study, this may suggest that Singapore IS organizations lag behind their United States counterparts in their experience of the maintenance burden.

Table 6 shows how respondents are utilising their maintenance personnel in each of the maintenance activities indicated in the survey instrument as adapted from Lientz and Swanson’s original study.

Overall, the data demonstrate that most of the maintainers’ time is directed at providing enhancements for users (42%) (i.e. addition of new reports and functions) and updating business rules in the programmes in response to changing external business conditions (21%). The level of support for these user-initiated changes is comparable to that reported by researchers in the United States (e.g., Lientz & Swanson, 1980; Nosek & Palvia, 1990; Dekleva, 1992a). Emergency programme fixes account for only 12% of the total maintenance effort.

Comparison of Table 6 data with similar United States data proved to be less than straightforward as there is no universal agreement among researchers and practitioners as to the definition of maintenance activities. A recent study by Abran and Nguyenkim (1993) attempted to harmonise the maintenance data from previous work with their own using various assumptions. In the process, they restate the meanings of adaptive maintenance (to incorporate ‘enhancement for users’) and perfective maintenance (to incorporate ‘accommodation of changes to hardware and system software’). Table 7 is adapted from Abran and Nguyenkim (1993) and shows that the current study data is closer to the GUIDE (1985) results for corrective and adaptive maintenance while they are comparable with Lientz and Swanson’s data for perfective maintenance and user support.

## Software maintenance practices

Respondents were asked about their current maintenance practices in terms of how the maintenance function is organized, organizational controls instituted, and use of various maintenance techniques and automated tools. The main findings are summarised in Table 8.

Thirteen percent of respondents reported that their IS departments are organized into separate maintenance and development groups. This is about the same level as the Lientz and Swanson 1977 study but well below the 52% obtained by Dekleva (1992a) in his 1990 survey. In view of the different sample populations used by Lientz and Swanson and Dekleva, it is unclear whether the way software maintenance is organized in the United States has changed over time. Table 8 also shows that 45% of respondents assign new staff to the maintenance function and that this practice is more prevalent in the public sector (70%). Furthermore, 72% indicate they rotate staff between the maintenance and development functions.

Public sector organizations appear to rely more on formal audits (58%) but less on cost justification in order to regulate changes to their application portfolios. Apart from lesser dependence on formal audits, minicomputer installations exercise control over their maintenance activities in about the same way as mainframe installations. Generally, the level of use of organizational controls over maintenance in Singapore is comparable, and in some respects higher, than in the United States (Lientz & Swanson, 1980; Nosek & Palvia, 1990). Almost all respondents (98%) indicate they have implemented basic change request procedures. Swanson and Beath (1989) reported change request procedures to be the only common control technique among the United States organizations they studied.

Sixty-three percent of respondents indicate they employ structured design and walkthrough techniques in maintenance. In their study of software development methods in Singapore, Poo and Yap (1991) found that about 70% of the organizations they surveyed used at least one formal software development technique during the development process, e.g. JSD (Jackson System Development), SSADM (Structured Systems Analysis and Design Method). It is likely that such techniques are carried over into the maintenance stage. Table 8 further shows that 36% of respondents use the prototyping technique in maintenance, which is not surprising considering the widespread use of 4GLs in Singapore IS organizations and the relative effectiveness of the prototyping approach with these high level languages (Martin & McClure, 1983).

The survey results, however, suggest that respondents have not invested in automated tools as much as they have in structured and prototyping techniques. Overall, the percentage of respondents using testing tools is 29% while the corresponding figures for configuration management tools and program analysers are 15% and 4% respectively. Perhaps respondents have more testing tools because they are used in both development and maintenance while the other two tools are intended primarily for maintenance work. Testing tools are used more extensively in non-4GL installations (41%) than 4GL installations (17%). This is probably due to a general dearth of 4GL testing tools. Though not addressed in the survey, from discussions with maintenance managers, it would appear that fewer automated maintenance tools are generally available in Singapore and that those available are relatively less well supported than in the United States.

## Maintenance problem areas

Table 9 summarises the respondents’ ranking of the 26 maintenance problems originally used in Lientz and Swanson’s study (1980). The normal plots and Kolmogorov-Smirnov (Lilliefors) statistics (Norusis, 1992) show an approximately normal distribution for the 26 data sets. These maintenance problems were evaluated on a five-point Likert-like scale in which (1) represents ‘no problem’ and (5) ‘major problem’. The same table also lists the corresponding United States results (Lientz & Swanson, 1980; Nosek & Palvia, 1990). Although respondents were invited to state and indicate the severity of any other critical problems, very few actually did, suggesting that Lientz and Swanson’s original list remains representative for centralised application systems.

In view of the managerial positions of most of the respondents, it is not surprising that the leading maintenance problems found in the study are managerial in nature. In all three studies in Table 9 the two most severe problems are: user demand for enhancements (Q4) and competing demands for programmer time (Q8). Three of the four top problems in the current study reflect management’s view that they simply have inadequate maintenance staff: competing demands for programmer time (Q8), meeting scheduled commitments (Q24), and number of maintenance programmers available (Q7).

Thus the Singapore scores appear systematically higher than United States scores (Lientz & Swanson, 1978). Given the relatively youthful character of the Singapore application portfolio, it would seem unlikely that the maintenance problem in Singapore is radically more severe than in the United States. Neither is it felt that the systematically higher scores observed are due to a generally worsening maintenance situation worldwide. Other studies that follow on from Lientz and Swanson (e.g. Nosek & Palvia,1990; Dekleva, 1992b) do not suggest such a trend. A more plausible possibility is that there exist culturally-based psychometric differences between Singaporeans and North Americans, which are reflected in respondent perceptions of, and scoring of, the scales.

With the objective of netting out the systematic difference observed, the overall average of the 26 Lientz and Swanson scores (2.33) and the overall average of the 26 Singapore scores (2.84), were computed. Next, the difference between these two averages (0.51) was subtracted from each of the 26 Singapore scores, thereby effectively lowering the Singapore chart from Figure 1. The adjusted Singapore scores (these ‘netted out’ scores are equivalent to standardised scores but are more meaningful as they retain the original 1–5 scale) and the original Lientz and Swanson scores are plotted in question number sequence in Figure 2. The significance of differences between the two sets of scores were recomputed and are also reflected in Figure 2. After lowering the chart, nine significant differences are observed between the Singapore and Lientz and Swanson data. Many of the differences that existed before lowering the chart are gone (16), several have persisted (4), and several new differences have appeared (5). Relatively stronger observations can be made on those differences that have persisted.

Even after lowering the chart, Singapore scores on items Q1, Q5, Q14 and Q16 continue to be significantly higher than the Lientz and Swanson scores, suggesting that these differences are due not only to whatever systematic difference might exist, but also to differences that may be unique to these problems. It is noted that these four problems are ranked in the top half of the Singapore survey and the bottom half of the Lientz and Swanson survey. Interestingly, these four items all reflect maintenance personnel problems with turnover (Q1), skills (Q5), motivation (Q14) and productivity (Q16).

Sectorial, installation and language type differences Table 10 presents the results of an analysis of differences between mean scores for the 26 problems across sectors, installation types and language types.

Few significant differences were found between the sector mean scores. Management support for software maintenance (Q21) was scored relatively low in importance by all respondents, especially the public sector. It is further observed from Table 10 that the public sector has relatively higher scores for maintenance personnel problems (Q1, Q5, Q7, Q8, Q14).

Though no significant differences are observed in Table 10 between mainframe and minicomputer installations, it is interesting to note that minicomputer installations score 20 of the 26 problems as being relatively more severe than mainframe installations. ## Discussion of results

Results of the current study generally indicate that software maintenance problems in Singapore are broadly similar to those researchers have found in the United States. Nonetheless, several peculiarities were observed. Cross-sector and cross-platform analyses also suggest several differences. The main findings and analyses are discussed next.

## Similarities with the United States

## User demand for enhancements

The problem of high user demand for enhancements and extensions to existing application systems is as evident in the current study as in previous United States studies (e.g., Lientz & Swanson, 1980; Swanson & Beath, 1989). The high demand for enhancements is brought about by a rapidly changing business environment and changes in corporate and government policies. At the same time, the increasingly IT-literate local user community has become more demanding.

Accompanying this high demand is the inevitable growth in system size and complexity, which would have already been felt by some maintenance managers in Singapore, especially those in the older installations. System growth ultimately contributes to what Lientz and Swanson (1980) call the maintenance crunch, in which older systems require an increasing amount of upkeep while, at the same time, new systems compete intensely for the same resources. Despite high user demand, many IS organizations in Singapore may not have yet felt the maintenance crunch because of their younger application portfolios.

## Poor software quality

The concerns of Singapore IS managers with the adverse effects of poor software quality on maintenance costs are clearly shown in the results. Poor software quality is reflected in the prominence that Singapore IS organizations have attributed to problems with system documentation (rank 5), quality of the original programs (rank 8) and inadequacy of system design specification (rank 11). Such concerns have been raised in several United States studies including that by Chapin (1986) in which he found that nearly half of the reported maintenance problems were related to characteristics of the software-like inaccurate documentation and unstructured programs. The relative inexperience of Singapore maintenance personnel may have partly contributed to the problem. Given the youthfulness and rapid growth of the IT profession in Singapore, it is reasonable to assume that, on average, Singapore maintenance staff have fewer years of experience than their North American counterparts. Furthermore, the problem could be self-perpetuating as the poor state of documentation and programs frustrates the maintenance staff thereby causing them to leave. Subsequently, replacement personnel who lack familiarity with the systems make costly and error-prone changes. In contrast, hardware and system software reliability did not seem to be a significant area of concern in Singapore as it is in the United States (Swanson & Beath, 1989), possibly due to the newer technologies in use.

## Maintenance personnel issues

Though Singapore IS organizations are concerned with maintenance personnel problems, they do not appear to implement appropriate personnel practices to alleviate such problems. In the United States, according to Dekleva (1992b), software maintenance management problems correlate with personnel problems. In Singapore, the low strength of the maintenance team coupled with poor motivation, high turnover and relative inexperience were identified as important issues confronting IS organizations (Couger, 1986; Singapore National Computer Board, 1993). These problems may be exacerbated by the common practice of assigning new staff to maintenance and failing to rotate development and maintenance staff. Chapin (1986) also reported the problems of inadequate staff and inappropriate matching of the characteristics of the maintained application software with the personnel.

## Use of software techniques and tools

The survey results suggest there is substantial reliance on structured and prototyping techniques among the Singapore IS organizations surveyed. In contrast, automated software tools are used by only a minority of organizations to facilitate the maintenance task. The likely explanation for the low usage of such tools is that Singapore IS organizations place greater emphasis on building new systems and therefore tend to invest more in development rather than maintenance tools. It is also probable that these techniques and tools were first applied in development and later retained in the maintenance stage. Researchers have observed a similar level of impact of automated software tools on maintenance in the United States (e.g., Swanson & Beath, 1989, pp 104; Hanna, 1993). On the whole it appears that Singapore IS organizations do not lag behind their United States counterparts in adopting software maintenance techniques and tools.

## Singapore peculiarities

## Young application portfolio

The key distinguishing feature of the typical centralised applications portfolio in Singapore is its youth. This apparent youthfulness may encourage a false sense of complacency among Singapore IS organizations. McClure (1992, pp 146) has shown in one of her case studies that the youth of an application portfolio does not necessarily mean that its quality is high. A recent survey of IT usage in Singapore (Singapore National Computer Board, 1994) indicates that organizations of all sizes and industries are investing more in IT. Thus, although the average age of the typical application portfolio in Singapore will not increase significantly over the next few years, IS organizations nonetheless may find themselves paying more for maintenance. The average size of software applications is also growing rapidly as a result of continuing change requests from users. As applications grow bigger they become more complex, more expensive to maintain and the chance of introducing new errors increases.

## Widespread usage of 4GLs

The prevalence of 4GLs is a distinctive feature of the Singapore software scene. The widespread usage of 4GLs can be traced to their popularisation as productivity tools (Martin & McClure, 1983) during the early 1980s at a time when Singapore was starting to experience rapid computerisation in both industry and government. It would at first appear that 4GLs augur well for Singapore as they usually arouse expectations of greater ease of use, increased productivity in design and programming, and reduced maintenance costs. However, this is often not the case as warned by several researchers in the field (e.g., Chapin, 1984; Martin & McClure, 1983) and as suggested by the current study data. Another dimension of 4GL maintenance pertains to the personality characteristics and cognitive skills of 4GL programmers which according to Ketler and Smith (1992) are very different from those of 3GL programmers. Thus, Singapore maintenance managers not only grapple with technical problems unique to 4GLs, but also with matching attributes of programmers to the maintenance demands of the 4GL environment.

## Severe maintenance personnel problems

The skills, motivation and turnover of maintenance personnel are clearly major issues for Singapore maintenance managers, especially those in the public sector. According to a recent government survey (Singapore National Computer Board, 1993), IT vendors and IS organizations experienced a 14.6% staff turnover rate in 1992, down from 15.4% in the previous year. It is suspected that the turnover rate among IS organizations was much higher, as the same report cited knowledge of application environments and programming languages as the most sought after skills. The report states that the most common difficulties in recruiting IT professionals are their lack of experience and shortage of specific skills. It is likely that these maintenance personnel problems will persist as long as Singapore’s economy continues to boom and the pace of computerisation remains unrestrained.

## Different sectoral views of software maintenance

The study revealed that the three sectors hold different views of the software maintenance problem. The public sector is greatly disturbed by the motivation and turnover of their maintenance personnel whereas local commercial organizations attribute their maintenance woes to user demands. MNCs have concerns over the quality of their older application software. The public sector, in particular, has the greatest concern in view of the mass hiring of IS professionals into the civil service over the past ten years in order to meet the government’s accelerated pace of computerisation. The number of application systems developed for the Singapore Civil Service grew from 10 in 1983 to 558 in 1992 (Singapore National Computer Board Yearbook, 1992/93).

In the light of these findings, Singapore maintenance managers should realise that they need to assess their own environment to determine the best maintenance approach for their application portfolios. The maintenance priorities in an established MNC subsidiary may necessarily be quite different from those of the younger IS department in the public sector.

## Limitations of the study

There are a few caveats to this study. First, the survey results mainly represent the self-reported opinions of individuals from the participating organizations, nearly all of whom have managerial responsibility. While their views may differ from those of the maintenance teams, similarities observed between the current study and the Lientz and Swanson results suggest this is not the case. The current study concentrates on traditional centralised applications, which also prevailed at the time of Lientz and Swanson’s original study. However, unlike Lientz and Swanson, who asked their respondents to report on a single application, the current study queries the application portfolio. Furthermore, the Singapore sample consists of roughly equal numbers of public sector organizations, local corporations and MNCs whereas this sectoral balance may not have existed in the United States studies. Consequently, cross-country comparison of the data should be interpreted with some caution.

## Conclusions

This paper began with two questions addressing the extensibility of previous findings on software maintenance problems. The study results suggest that problems pertaining to centralised application systems in Singapore are broadly similar to what researchers have found in North America. Nonetheless, several peculiarities are observed and they are ascribed to factors like the preponderance of 4GL-based applications, relatively new applications, relatively new hardware and software environment, well educated and relatively young IS professional staff and severe maintenance personnel problem. Finer partitioning of the data by sector, hardware platform and software platform revealed other salient differences.

The study further indicates the existence of a systematic difference in the way that Singapore and North American respondents score the 26 pre-specified maintenance problems. This systematic difference was attributed to differing perceptions of, and scoring of, the questionnaire scales by respondents in the two countries. It underscores the importance of accounting for such possible psychometric differences between cultures in order to facilitate the extensibility of previous findings by North American researchers.

To the extent that observations made on the Singapore situation reflect similarities with other non-North American countries, the findings from the study have broader relevance. Also, there may exist organizations in North America and elsewhere that compare more closely to the Singapore model than to the general model suggested by prior North American research. These organizations, and MNCs with operations outside of North America, may find the study results revealing.
