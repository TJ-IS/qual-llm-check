# Use of methodologies: an empirical analysis of their impact on the economics of the development process

PD Chatzoglou

Department of Public and Business Administration, University of Cyprus, 75 Kallipoleos Str, PO Box 537, CY1678 Nicosia, Cyprus

Despite the increase in the number of commercially available development methodologies, there are still many projects where none is used. This problem can be attributed to the fact that many practitioners ignore the beneficial role of using such methodologies to both the quality of the end product and the economics of the development process. This paper, which is the second of a series, reports on a survey of information systems development practice carried out to provide evidence about the effect of using development methodologies on the economics of the development process. Data concerning 72 projects, obtained through a mail survey within the UK are analysed. The focus of the analysis is to provide evidence of how the use of methodologies affect economic parameters, such as time, cost, effort, number of people involved and outputs (in terms of requirements captured). The results show that the use of any methodology is generally proved to be a better option than using no methodology. However, the use of methodologies is not a panacea but specific methodologies can help towards achieving better results in specific areas.

## Introduction

Although the importance of employing a methodological approach during the system development process is widely documented (Finkelstein, 1990; Ashworth & Slater, 1993; McDermid, 1993), project developers must be very careful in deciding which methodology between hundreds (Bubenko, 1986) to use.

Many authors have suggested that there is no best methodology for all situations (Jackson & Keys, 1984; Avison & Wood-Harper, 1991; Avison & Taylor, 1997). It is also suggested that a methodology should be chosen depending on the particular characteristics of the system development project (Davis, 1982) or on certain variables in and around the problem situation (Tamai, 1993). In addition, all methodologies have some specific characteristics and attributes that make them suitable (or not suitable) for different type of projects.

Fenton et al (1994) suggest that rigorous experimentation is needed to evaluate methodologies and their effects on organizations, processes and products. However, in practice (Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996) developers usually choose specific methodologies for all sorts of reasons (availability, ease to use, found to work for other projects) except probably the right one (that is, suitability for the type of project they are currently working on). The results can be disastrous (Lindstrom, 1993).

This paper is the second of a series which present the findings of an in-depth survey of current practice associated with the requirements stage of a project. The main objective of the survey was to obtain information about the development process and the requirements capture and analysis (RCA) process from people who are involved in them, and who also look at them from different points of view (it covers 72 development projects across 54 different organizations within the UK). The respondents are experienced project managers, systems analysts and consultants.

In particular, our aim was not only to find out whether methodologies are used or not, but also to examine their effect on some important aspects of the development process. While the first paper of this series (Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996) was centred around the use of methodologies and project planning tools as part of the RCA process, this paper focuses on the effect of methodologies on the economics of the whole development process and the RCA process in particular. More specifically, the purpose was to examine:

1. Who uses a methodology and how often;

2. whether the use of methodologies plays an important role in the determination of the economics of system development, and what role they play; and

3. whether the use of different methodologies has different effects on system development.

The underlying conceptual model supporting this survey is presented in Figure 1. It assumes that there are some factors which affect developers’ decisions about the use of a methodology. The characteristics of this methodology will, in turn, affect the development process, its economics and the outputs of this process. It also assumes that all other parameters that affect the development process, its economics and its output (such as resources, management, team members, users etc) are constant. Although, this is a strong assumption, it can help us isolate the effect of the issue under examination as well as its implications.

There have been many attempts to classify methodologies (Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald, 1982; Maddison, 1983; Fitzgerald et al, 1985; Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988), only recently Avison and Taylor (1997) classified methodologies in terms of the characteristics of the project where they will be used (structure of problem situation, problem definition, clear requirements). On the other hand, this study focuses on the specific targets set by the project manager and examines the implications of his/her decision on the economics and the output of the development process. Constraints, such as availability, or commitment to a specific methodology (usually developed in-house), or resources or regulations (structured systems analysis and design methodology — SSADM, is the standard in the UK) are other important factors which affect managers’ choice of a methodology, but which are outside the scope of this study.

Some of the most important findings of this survey are as follows:

1. Use of a methodology or of a planning tool results in fewer iterations of the RCA process;

2. use of a tool for assessing the requirements gathered in the RCA process results in more iterations of this process;

3. managers are more confident about the quality of requirements gathered when a methodology is used; and

4. use of methodologies with different characteristics affect both the whole development process and RCA process in different ways.

The next section presents the research methodology used in conducting this survey, followed by the analysis of the results, which is divided into five main subsections. A classification of the data according to different criteria is presented in the first subsection. In the second, a brief description of the use of methodologies by various people for various projects is given, while in the third, the effect of methodologies on the number of iterations of the RCA process is examined. In the fourth, the effect of methodologies on the economics of the whole development and on the RCA process is analysed, and the relationship between the use of methodologies and the project managers’ confidence about the outputs of the RCA process are examined. Finally, the last section summarises the results and draws general conclusions.

## Research methodology

The data used for this study was obtained through a mail questionnaire survey. The target population included people who were involved in projects in the UK. After careful consideration of all the probability sampling methods, it was decided that the best one for this survey was the proportional stratification sampling method. It was selected for two main reasons. First, because it can provide a better representation of the population within the sample, and second, because the answers of different groups within the population can be separately examined and compared with each other. The sample population was divided into three different strata or groups according to the way people look at or deal with the development process. These three strata are: academics, software houses and consultancies and industry. Post-stratification strata were also created to assist in the analysis of the data according to systems with different characteristics.

Four sources were used to locate the survey population: (1) some editions of the ALVEY Programme (Annual Reports) were used to find individuals or companies that are involved in the development or the RCA process; (2) various magazines (Computing Decisions, The Software Users Year Book, etc) were used, and the names of some companies (software houses, consultants) were found. The criteria used in selecting these companies were their size (number of employees, sales), their products and their specialised services; (3) journals and books where the authors wrote about system development, and specifically the RCA process; and (4) a list of names of people involved in system development and who had participated in previous surveys conducted by the Computation Department of UMIST (University of Manchester Institute of Science and Technology).

Mail surveys are widely condemned for not being able to secure an adequate rate of response. However, in this case, it was selected because the sample population had a high educational level, and was likely to be very interested in the subject of the research. In such cases, mail surveys become more attractive than other survey methods and produce a better response rate (Fowler, 1984). This can probably explain the high response rate of this survey. More specifically, from the 185 questionnaires initially sent out 82 were completed and returned, but only 72 (38.92%) were finally included in the data set. The other 10 were discarded because of problems with the validity of the responses. All these projects had been completed recently.

The importance of the questionnaire was that both general information about the project development process, and specific information about the requirements capture process was gathered. However, not only factual data, but also respondents’ personal opinions about some aspects of the RCA process were elicited. Briefly, these questions referred to their experience, their position in the development team, characteristics of the projects, techniques, tools and methodologies used, and finally assessment of some factors that might be of importance.

All the respondents had to answer the same questions. This assumed that each question had the same meaning for all respondents and that differences in answers stemmed from differences among respondents.

## Analysis of the results

## Data classification

Analysis of the data suggests that the data set incorporates projects with different characteristics and it is fairly evenly distributed between the various groups. This diversification of the people participating and projects, as well as their experience (52% of them have participated in the development process of five to 15 projects, and 42% have participated in more than 15 projects) enhance the validity of the results since many characteristics of the projects are looked at from different points of view (Figure 2).

Looking at the size of the projects (in terms of the number of people involved, elapsed time and cost), it is apparent that although it varies from small to large, the majority of the projects can be classified as mediumsized (projects completed within 3 years, employing up to 30 people and with an overall cost of between £0.1– £0.5 million, but also allocating one to five personnel, more than 15% of the total elapsed time and 5–15% of their total cost to the RCA). Furthermore, in more than 71% of the projects more than 70% of the requirements captured were accommodated into the final system (Figure 3).

Finally, some additional tests (contingency and chisquare analysis) have shown that there is no conclusive evidence to suggest that there is any difference in the size of projects developed by different developers. On the other hand, some results highlight differences in the size of projects with other characteristics. More specifically, software projects are usually smaller than hardware or systems projects but they are more effective eliciting and implementing requirements. Similarly, bespoke projects are smaller than generic but more effective, and although projects with a well defined problem domain are smaller than projects with a moderately or poorly defined problem domain, they are more effective at gathering and implementing requirements.

## Confidence in requirements captured

According to Jordan and Macheski (1990), requirements are the features of the system components necessary to achieve the system objectives within constraints. Requirements are statements about data, procedures and people.

It is important to stress that when examining the requirements captured in the RCA process from a quantitative point of view, it was found that in 68% of the projects there were enough requirements captured in the RCA stage, while in 32% of the projects, the RCA process was completed without all the necessary requirements being gathered. Similarly, examining the requirements captured in the RCA process from a rather more qualitative point of view, there was a high degree of confidence (at the end of the RCA) among project managers that the requirements gathered were accurate and sufficient (very high 62%, moderate 31%, low 7%). These results do not imply that no omissions were found later in the development process, but rather that, roughly all functional and non-functional requirements (mandatory, desirable and optional) were identified.

In conclusion, it can be said that the outputs of the RCA process were usually satisfactory since, in almost 65% of the projects requirements that were both sufficient and adequate were gathered. However, the failure of the remaining 35% of the projects to capture the necessary requirements must not be ignored. They failed to capture the necessary requirements for the following reasons (the sum of percentages is higher than 100% because more than one option could be chosen):

1. Lack of time (61% of the projects);

2. poor access to information (51% of the projects);

3. insufficient manpower (22% of the projects); and

4. cost (19% of the projects).

No difference to the above results was observed when we controlled for specific developing organizations. More specifically, software houses, consultancies, industry and academics ranked the above mentioned four constraints in the same order.

These results are interesting since they suggest that usually, enough people and money are allocated in the requirements capture stage. On the other hand, the main problem appears to be limited time and poor access to information. They are asking for more time to allow the existing team to work better, to improve their communication with users and thus to discover the ‘hidden’ information required. This, in turn, can probably explain why, in 18% of the projects, just one iteration of the RCA process was performed. In addition, it was found that in 44% of the projects the original planned number of iterations had to change and more iterations were performed.

## Use of methodologies

Although, there have been many suggestions about the importance of employing a methodological approach during the system development process (Finkelstein, 1990; Ashworth & Slater, 1993; McDermid, 1993), in nearly half of the projects no methodology was used. It is suggested that this is because professionals have not found the methodologies available to be helpful (Tesch et al, 1995; Ward et al, 1996). It must be made clear here that in this study, reference to use of methodologies includes both use of specific methodologies and also use of techniques adopting a contingency-based strategy. Thus, use of methodology really means use of a methodological approach rather than simple use of methodologies (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1995). On the other hand, no use of methodologies imply that some non-related techniques are used in a random way, or there was no use of any methodology.

Table 1 shows that just 53% of the projects used a methodology in the development process and in almost all of them (46%) it covered the RCA process as well. However, it is interesting that 16% of the projects used a methodology in the RCA process but not in the other stages of the development process. In total, a methodology was used in the RCA process by 62% of the projects (46% + 16%), while 69% of the projects (53% + 16%) used a methodology in various stages of the development process. In other words, 31% of the projects did not use any methodology in any stage of the development process.

In contrast, it was reported that where no methodology was used, various non-related techniques and tools were employed. These findings support the findings of other similar surveys (Lubars et al, 1992; Thompson, 1993; Southwell, 1993; Hopker, 1994; Ward et al, 1996), but seems to contradict the results of Hardy et al (1994), where only 18% of the projects were found to use no methodology. Nevertheless, it is very encouraging that projects that use a methodology, choose such methodologies that cover the RCA process as well (Table 1).

The picture dramatically changes when projects are examined in groups according to the developer, project type and various internal characteristics. The obvious question that arises is ‘why do these differences exist?’ The answer is not easy and one can only speculate on the possible reasons:

1. The culture of the organization, the fact that people have different cognitive style and the problem situation (time scale and other constraints) may be important factors in influencing the methodology choice (Avison & Taylor, 1997).

2. Some people who work for industry may not be as well informed as the people from other development organizations. The fact that 75% of them use prototyping or in-house methodologies (Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996) partly supports the previous claim. A final aspect that was examined was the use of a planning and control model during the development and RCA process. It was found that while such a tool was used in two-thirds of the projects for planning and control of the later stages of system development, few of them cover the early stages as well. It emerged that less than one-third of the projects used such a tool during the RCA process.

Furthermore, it was found that in more than four-fifths of the projects an explicit review is used at the end of the RCA process, but in less than one-fifth of the projects a tool/model was employed to determine when enough information-requirements were gathered. Of course, it must be stressed, that many people expressed their concern about this, and also their doubts that any such tool could be designed. A respondent gave the following answer when asked to indicate the characteristics any such tool should have:

“If I could answer this question then RCA process would not be so much of a problem.”

They also felt that the RCA process is too informal and unstructured to be planned, never mind controlled.

## Use of methodologies and number of iterations of the RCA process

The notion of iteration is very well presented by Boehm’s Spiral Model for the systems development process (Boehm, 1988, 1990). In this study, an iteration of the RCA process can be defined as the repetition of all the phases (and activities within each phase) of the RCA process. The phases are considered to be: (1) gather information; (2) identify requirements; and (3) validate requirements. The examination of the relationship between the number of iterations and use (or not) of methodologies, techniques or tools during the RCA process has also given some important results (Table 2).

First, it was found that while 60% of the projects where a methodology was used during the RCA process, were completed using up to 2 iterations, 40% completed the process with more than 2 iterations. Where no methodology was used the findings are in reverse. This suggests that the use of a methodology (i.e. methodological approach) results in the need for fewer iterations, probably because requirements can be captured and analysed easier using a methodology.

A similar result was found by examining the impact of the use of a planning and control tool (P&C tool) during the RCA process on the number of iterations needed. It was found that in most projects that used a P&C tool, up to 2 iterations occurred. This may be either because planning is considered easier or more accurate when a P&C tool is used, or because developers do not want to deviate from their original plan even though they believe that their targets have not been achieved. This is especially true where organizational or departmental politics prevent them from admitting a mistake.

## Use of a methodology in the RCA process

This section examines only the projects where a methodology is used in the RCA process (Table 1: 45 (62%) projects). Let us assume now that a methodology is used and special emphasis is given to the RCA process. In this case, the relationship between economic parameters and the use of methodologies in the RCA process appears in Figure 6.

In detail, it was found that when a methodology is used in the RCA process, then less people are involved in the RCA process, but the total number of people involved in the whole development process is the same. A very interesting result is obtained by examining the relationship between use of methodologies in the RCA process and elapsed development time. It was found that when a methodology is used in the RCA process then, although the elapsed time of completing the RCA process is higher, the project’s total elapsed development time is lower.

Considering the effort in project development, it was found that with the use of a methodology in the RCA process less effort is required for the RCA process to be completed, but the total effort in project development is greater. Another important finding is that although projects using a methodology in the RCA usually have slightly higher cost than those not using a methodology, the total development cost is the same in both projects.

Finally, it was found that by using a methodology in the RCA process slightly more requirements are captured in that process. Similarly, the amount of requirements accommodated into the final system was found to be the same whether a methodology in the RCA process is used or not.

To conclude, the analysis in this and the previous section suggests that the use of methodologies appears to have positive implications in terms of the economic parameters of the development process. However, in order to benefit the most from the use of methodologies, emphasis must be placed on their use in the RCA process as well as in the later stages of the development process.

## Use of a specific group of methodologies

The third and final set of relationships that will be examined is between specific methodologies (group of methodologies) and T, E, C, P, and R. The purpose of carrying out this additional analysis is to examine if there is any difference in the results given by a specific group of methodologies.

It was found (Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996) that the methodologies mainly used by the projects of this survey were SSADM and various in-house methodologies not named, but also OOA (object oriented analysis), prototyping and SSM (soft system methodology) (Figure 7). However, some other methodologies were also used in one project only. These results support findings of other surveys such as Hardy et al (1994) who found that inhouse and SSADM were by far the most used methodologies, and that OOA was used by just 4% of the projects. Hopker (1994) also found that 11% of the projects he examined used SSADM and 6% of them used prototyping.

The above methodologies are classified into different groups. Although, methodologies can be classified in many different ways (Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald, 1982; Maddison, 1983; Fitzgerald et al, 1985; Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988; Avison & Taylor, 1997), there is no generally accepted classification method. Thus, to overcome this obstacle, a decision was reached not to group methodologies based on specific characteristics but rather based on one general criterion: whether they are considered to be structured or not. It is important to repeat once more that the purpose of this exercise is not to prove that one methodology is better than other methodologies, but to show that specific methodologies should be used to achieve specific targets.

All the structured methodologies were included in Group 1, in-house methodologies and prototyping were included in Group 2 and all other methodologies in Group 3. Group 4 (methodologies used in RCA process only) and Group 5 (all methodologies) were included for comparison reasons. They are classified as in Table 3.

To start with, it was found (Table 4) that projects using structured methodologies (Group 1) capture more requirements in the RCA process and accommodate more requirements into the final system compared to projects that do not use any methodology. Furthermore, they also need less time and effort to complete the whole development and RCA process, but with higher cost. They also require less people in the RCA process but the same number of people in the whole development process.

On the other hand, projects using prototyping or inhouse methodologies (Group 2) employ fewer people and are completed with less effort, but their completion elapsed time and requirements captured and accommodated into the final system are the same as in projects where no methodology is used. However, it seems that their main advantage is that, although the cost of the RCA process is the same, the overall development cost is lower.

It must be stressed that the purpose of this analysis is not to suggest that specific methodologies are superior or that they can always give better results, but quite the opposite, it is to stress that there is no methodology which is suitable for all projects.

## Which methodology to use?

First, let us stress that the use of any methodology is better than using no methodology. This is what the performance of methodologies in Group 4 and mainly Group 5 has indicated in all six scenarios examined in the last section.

However, the performance of the methodologies in Group 1, Group 2 and Group 3 has shown that although methodologies do not always improve the economics of the development process and the RCA process, it is better to use specific methodologies. In addition, the differences in the performance of the same methodology group in different scenarios suggest that what it is really important is to use the right methodology; the one which will maximise the possibility of accomplishing specific goals in specific projects.

Unfortunately, this is not what is happening in the real world. It was found (Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996) that many methodologies are used either because team members are familiar with a specific methodology, or because there is no other alternative. In some cases, methodologies were used because it was the policy of the development organization to use them, which in most cases were in-house methodologies. This can also partly explain why 35% of the projects (found in an earlier section) failed to capture the necessary requirements and the confidence of project managers was low.

A crucial question that arises is ‘if using a specific methodology is better than using any methodology and much better than using no methodology, can we choose a methodology from Group 3?’, and if yes, ‘what results should we expect?’

Although the performance of the methodologies in Group 3 was very bad in five out of the six scenarios, the answer is still ‘Yes’. Project managers can and must use these methodologies if they are appropriate for the specific project or the targets project managers have set.

Scenario 5 has purposely been designed to support this answer. In this scenario, the most important for the project manager is to accommodate in the final system as many requirements as possible (R: 3, 0, −3), at the least possible cost (C: 2, 0, −2), and within the least possible elapsed time (T: 1, 0, −1). They do not care about the economics of the RCA process or the effort and the number of people involved in the whole development process, and therefore they assign to them the value (0). Having this scenario in mind, and looking at both Table 5 and Figure 8, one realises that methodologies in Group 3 emerge as the most suitable methodologies for this scenario. This is evidence that specific methodologies should be used to achieve specific targets in specific projects. No methodology alone can be considered as a panacea for all projects.

## Quality of requirements gathered and use of methodologies

The last aspect that remains to be examined is the opinion of the participants in the development process about the results of this process in terms of requirements captured during the RCA process and the other stages of the development process.

Respondents were asked to answer two questions. The first was whether they believe that all the necessary information was gathered at the end of the RCA process, and the second question was about how confident they were that the information gathered was accurate and sufficient. Although, it is anticipated that their answers are highly subjective, they are a reliable indication about the effectiveness (or not) of the use of methodologies in the RCA and in the whole development process.

Examining the answers to the first question, it was found that, although the use of methodologies in the whole development process and in the RCA process in particular, improve the situation by capturing more information, this improvement is not significant.

On the other hand, when we turn the discussion to the quality aspect of the information gathered (by analysing the replies to the second question), the picture is completely different. It emerged that people are significantly more confident about the quality of the requirements captured when a methodology is used in the whole development process or even in the RCA process.

## Summary of findings and conclusions

## Summary of findings

Despite the huge increase in the number of commercially available development methodologies there are still many projects (31%) where no methodology is used in the development process or in the RCA process. The same applies for the use of a planning tool. The exception is that in the RCA process just a third of the projects use such a tool. Although, there is an explicit review at the end of the RCA process in almost all the projects, just 15% of them use a model to assess the information gathered.

Nevertheless, the use of methodologies and planning tools results in fewer iterations of the RCA process in most (60%) of the projects. On the other hand, the use of a model to assess the information gathered in the RCA process seems to have the opposite effect. More specifically, in 60% of the projects the use of such a model results in more iterations of the RCA process. Explicit reviews at the end of the RCA process do not affect the actual number of iterations of the RCA process.

A comparison between projects that use a methodology and projects where no methodology is used has shown that:

1. The use of a methodology in both the whole development process and the RCA process has the following effects on their economics:

(a) reduces the elapsed time, effort and cost of the development and RCA process; and slightly reduces the number of people involved in the development process;

(b) no effect on the number of people involved in the RCA process, the requirements accommodated into the final system, or the requirements captured in the RCA process.

2. The use of a methodology in the RCA process has the following effects:

(a) reduces the elapsed time of the development process; the effort of the RCA process; and the number of people involved in the RCA process and the whole development process;

(b) no effect on the cost of the development process or the requirements accommodated into the final system;

(c) increases the elapsed time of the RCA process, relatively increases the effort in the development process; slightly increases the cost of the RCA process; and slightly increases the requirements captured in the RCA process.

3. When structured methodologies (Group 1) are used then although cost increases, more requirements are captured and accommodated into the final system, with less effort, and in almost the same time and using the same number of people.

4. When prototyping or in-house methodologies are used the same amount of requirements are captured in the RCA and accommodated into the final system, in the same time, but with less effort, fewer people and less cost.

5. When other methodologies are used (Group 3) then although more people, effort and possibly time are required, more requirements are captured in the RCA process and accommodated into the final system, with relatively lower cost.

6. When a methodology is used only in the RCA process then although more time, effort, and requirements are captured in the RCA process, the time, cost, effort and people in the whole development process are fewer but the requirements accommodated into the final system are the same.

Finally, project managers are more confident about the quality of information when a methodology is used.

## Conclusions

The number of projects where a methodology is used cannot be considered satisfactory. Especially, in the cases of hardware projects, or projects with a well defined problem domain, developed by industry, and for use by the development organization, the number of projects that use a methodology is rather disappointing.

However, it is clear that the use of methodologies during the development process and during the RCA process, does effect economic parameters such as time, cost, effort and number of people involved. In the case of the RCA process, the use of methodologies and other planning tools also effects the total number of iterations of the RCA process.

Nevertheless, the effect on these parameters is different depending not only on which methodologies are used, but also on when these methodologies are used (which stages of the system life cycle they cover).

Therefore, project managers should not simply use a methodology, but they should choose a methodology which will be appropriate for the characteristics of the specific project and which will satisfy their resource limitations and simultaneously increase the number of requirements captured and accommodated into the final system.
