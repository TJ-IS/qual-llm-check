# IT outsourcing as strategic partnering: the case of the UK Inland Revenue

LP Willcocks<sup>1</sup> and T Kern<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Templeton College, University of Oxford, Oxford, OX1 5NY; <sup>2</sup>Christ Church, University of Oxford, Oxford, OX1 5NY, UK

The information systems (IS) literature, and the industry sectors confronted with information technology (IT) outsourcing, are beginning to perceive IT outsourcing as more than just a necessary organizational resource-acquisition venture. Instead organizations have begun to consider vendors as their partners, causing many companies to enter into more intricate deals that include both contractual and informal issues. In light of these developments, a growing concern with forming closer client–vendor relationships has evolved for both vendors and clients. This paper explores, in the context of a case study, three critical dimensions emerging from previous studies. These are: what is outsourced; the contract; and the relationship dimension. We also explore the process and management issues underlying these dimensions. The investigation is enabled through two analytical frameworks, one proven in previous research, one exploratory, but both specifically developed for IT outsourcing arrangements.

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) outsourcing is not a new concept, as it has been in existence in the form of application development contracts, in facilities management agreements and time sharing deals for several decades (Earl, 1991). Nevertheless, the rapid growth of outsourcing from a global market share of \$50 billion in 1994 to an estimated US\$70 billion by 1998, and US\$121 billion in the year 2000 (Blackall, 1995; IDC, 1996; Walker, 1996; Willcocks & Lacity, 1997), has ensured that it attains extensive worldwide business attention (see for example Applegate & Montealegre, 1991; Huber, 1993; Moad, 1993; Cross, 1995; Vowler, 1995; Bicknell, 1996). IT outsourcing is here defined as a decision taken by an organization to contract-out or sell the organization’s IT assets, people and/or activities to a third party vendor, who in return provides the services for a certain time period and monetary fee (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993).

This growth in significance of IT outsourcing has resulted in an increased concern with the management of an outsourcing venture, and in particular with the issue of risk mitigation. This has received considerable attention already in the two areas we will investigate in the case study, namely decision-making frameworks and contracts. But additionally we will argue that what critically demands managing, especially in the long-term ‘total’ outsourcing context, is the client–vendor relationship. As already discussed in various studies, IT outsourcing agreements result in inter-organizational relationships due to the dependency that arises (Cheon et al, 1995; McFarlan & Nolan, 1995; Kern &

Willcocks, 1996). Paradoxically though, with a few notable exceptions (Klepper, 1994, 1995; McFarlan & Nolan, 1995; Willcocks & Choi, 1995) the area in IT outsourcing that has received the least research attention so far is this relationship issue, and more precisely the characteristics that determine an outsourcing relationship. The importance of this research has been highlighted by EDS’s CoSourcing<sup></sup> (cooperative sourcing) framework, an early attempt at demarcating an outsourcing relationship (Alberthal, 1994). The case study offers an opportunity to investigate the content of a specific large-scale outsourcing arrangement, its contractual elements and how these interrelate and are influenced by client–vendor relationship over time.

The paper starts by outlining the genesis and detailing the frameworks to be utilised in analysing the case history. The decision-making framework was developed in the context of previous research (Willcocks et al, 1995b; Lacity et al, 1996). The additional, complementary ‘relationship’ framework, however, is derived from reviewing inter-organizational relationship theory, marketing theory, organization theory and the IS literature. Following the review, common elements are elicited which when combined, define the theoretical base of our relationship framework. We then detail and explore the case study in the light of these frameworks.

## Analytical frameworks

## Decision-making

In order to create frameworks against which the case experiences can be analysed in a structured manner, we refer initially to our earlier work in thirty private and public sector organizations showing decisions on six major factors having a primary influence on the effectiveness of subsequent IT sourcing arrangements (Willcocks et al, 1995b). These factors are:

Differentiator or commodity. An IT activity/service is a differentiator where it provides a basis for competitive advantage or, in the public sector, represents a leading competence advantage (Cronk & Sharp, 1997). A commodity activity does not distinguish the organization, and has to be done competently, and no more; an example, would be payroll.

Critical or useful. ‘Critical’ activities are integral to the organization’s achievement of goals and critical to its existing and future business direction. ‘Useful’ activities make incremental contributions but do not affect strategic direction or competitive positioning.

Degree of uncertainty — about future business environment and business needs and hence longer term IT needs.

Degree of technology maturity associated with the IT activity/service. Maturity is low when the technology is new and unstable, or where an existing technology is being used in a radically new application and/or where the organization has little inhouse experience in implementing the technology in the current application.

Level of IT integration. Highly integrated systems have complex and extensive interactions with other technical systems and interface in complex ways with multiple business users.

In-house capability relative to that of the market. This factor relates both to relative capability and also the in-house cost relative to what external suppliers will charge.

We found the lowest risk route to using the market was to outsource useful commodities in conditions of low uncertainty. Additionally on the technical front it was important to reduce risk by outsourcing discrete, as opposed to integrated systems, in situations of high technology maturity where the market could provide comparable service at a more efficient price. In practice there will be trade-offs between these factors. Additionally, we found the following questions needed to be answered positively if the outsourcing was to be effective:

Is there an economic rationale? This may not be straightforward. In the public sector, for example, outsourcing can be a way of avoiding capital expenditure and large outlays on IT updating that can hit annual budgets hard.

I Is there a low rate of technological change relevant to the content and length of the contract?

I Can the organization manage ownership issues around asset and people transfers?

I Is a suitable vendor available?

I Is there sufficient in-house management capability to make and deliver on the decision? One aspect of this may be the need to separate out IT planners/strategists from providers of IT services to ensure objective advice.

I Can the organization handle any significant human resource issues that will arise?

Clearly much depends on a tailored detailed contract and adequate evaluation systems in place to monitor vendor performance (Willcocks et al, 1996).

## Contract and relationships: towards a framework

Research into dyadic business-to-business and buyer– seller relationship formation, evolution, and structure, identified inter-organizational relationship (IOR) theory (White & Levine, 1961; Oliver, 1990; Van De Ven & Ring, 1994), marketing theory (Cunningham, 1980; Ford, 1980; Anderson & Narus, 1990), organization theory (Faulkner, 1995; Musgrave & Anniss, 1996) and IS literature (Henderson, 1990; Konsynski & McFarlan, 1990; Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1996), as the predominant areas for explanatory concepts and approaches. Detailed analysis of these literatures elicited a list of recurring key properties in an outsourcing relationship. In-depth analysis of the various approaches also revealed a set of common determinants underlying most interorganizational relationships and business relationships in general. Most approaches repeatedly stressed cooperation and intertwined economic/social exchange as pervading the relationship, heavily influenced by the level of commitment and trust (Thibaut & Kelley, 1959; Blau, 1964; Cook, 1977; Hakansson, 1982; Axelrod, 1984). Building these, it emerged, takes time and largely depends on personal experience, which comes about through repetitive cycles of exchanges (Hakansson, 1982). The exchanges are characterised by four main dimensions: (1) the parties to the exchange; (2) the kind and quantities exchanged; (3) the agreement underlying the exchange; and (4) the direction of the exchange (White & Levine, 1961).

Generally, following the interaction approach of Hakansson, we can infer that the more the parties interact and adapt to each other, the stronger the relationship will become. This cyclic process in the evolution of a relationship emphasises the role time plays in the development process (Cunningham, 1980; Van De Ven & Ring, 1994; Musgrave & Anniss, 1996). More specifically, exchanges of information and hence communication play the key role in building trust, while trust depends on the level of commitment invested by the individuals. Commitment to the relation then becomes evident through the investments of time and resources made.

From this review and our previous research into IT outsourcing we were able to develop an exploratory model designed to map the determinants of an IT outsourcing relationship (see Figure 1). Applicable to both vendor and client, the model elucidates those factors that characterise the nature of a sourcing relationship and the likely behavioural traits of the parties involved. The two level arrangement of the model emerges from the nature of an outsourcing venture, which can be characterised as consisting of a contractual level and a cooperative level between company A (vendor) and B (client).

## (1) Contractual level

The starting point of the model is the initial contract stage, which specifies in detail the exchanges of services and/or products, financial matters, assets and/or staff transfers, information exchanges and other formal issues (e.g. assessment and monitoring phases). The exchanges can be characterised by such dimensions as timeliness, value, regularity, quality and content, and are extensively detailed in the contract. They will also vary considerably in their degree of formality (ranging from formal financial exchanges to informal social adaptations). These continuing exchanges provide the medium through which firms can change the form of their relationship, i.e. from contractual to cooperative (Easton, 1992).

Macneil (1978) postulates that the agreement regulates the contractual relation along several key dimensions. The most important dimension is that relational exchanges tend to take place over time, so that each exchange must be assessed on its initiation and resulting effect. Furthermore: ‘Relational exchange participants can be expected to derive complex, personal, non-economic satisfactions and engage in social exchange’ with their partners (Dwyer et al, 1987). Macneil’s contractual dimensions are built into our framework (Figure 1) as they define the link between the fundamental contract and the normative development of an outsourcing relationship.

Realisation of the stipulated elements in the contract is largely dependent on the continuous exchange of information between the participants, as only through continual communication can either side fulfil its legal obligations, achieve expectations and satisfaction, and avoid conflicts. Thus flexibility at the contractual level is absolutely fundamental, since adjustments, changes and investments not foreseeable in the initial agreement have to be made to continue the evolution of the relationship. Since the initial agreement will, in general, be neither self-enforcing nor self-adjusting, there has to be an informal element of flexibility inherent in any relational exchange contract (Goldberg, 1980).

A contractual or legalistic aura pervades the formal level, as service level agreements, product provisions, asset and/or staff exchanges, payments, and other stipulated terms are met. In parallel, social adaptations will be initiated by both the client and vendor to smooth the outsourcing transition to a working relationship. Social adaptations are a vital process as they guarantee the integration of the formal contractual level with the informal cooperative factors. Social adaptation will evolve gradually as the two cultural distances between the participating organizations are moved closer (Ford, 1980). It is largely a task of developing cooperation, commitment, and trust. According to Forsgen et al (1995) adaptations take place in attitudes, rules, norms, knowledge and corporate strategies. These can be manifested most clearly where a common language is created between the parties. This is important, as the visible running of the operation can be integrated quite easily in a relationship, but, as we will find in the Inland Revenue case, the unwritten norms that are part of an organization can only be grasped through a phasing-in period or experience.

## (2) Cooperative level

The cooperative level depends on frequent and continuous exchanges of information; communication mechanisms underpin the majority of exchanges and processes occurring at this level. The communication mechanisms commonly employed in an outsourcing venture are daily interactions with the vendor, and possibly weekly, monthly or yearly meetings with the steering committee overseeing the whole venture (Cross, 1995; Willcocks et al, 1996). Formal communication in this context is characterised by hard facts such as technical, legal or commercial data, whereas informal communication is more likely to be personal, supportive or soft data (Hakansson & Snehota, 1995). As elucidated earlier, communication might lead to greater trust, and contrastingly, greater trustworthiness can cause improved formal and informal communication levels (Dwyer et al, 1987; Anderson & Narus, 1990). Therefore meaningful communication is a necessary antecedent to trust. Frequent communication may also help to avoid conflicts, facilitate solutions to problems, and reduce uncertainty levels (Aiken & Hage, 1968; Easton, 1992).

Information exchanges also assist vendors and clients with managing each others expectations by taking care that dissatisfaction is kept at a minimum (Lacity et al, 1994). Misalignment of ambitions and expectations is often found to be the root cause of problems (Vowler, 1996). To avoid such mishaps both parties need to develop mutual goals and/or objectives that guide the relationship. Mutuality will improve cooperation by sharing the risks and rewards of the venture equally (Shepherd, 1995). We thus infer that complementarity and commitment in outsourcing evolves naturally through the pursuit of success (Klepper, 1995; McFarlan & Nolan, 1995).

Both the client and vendor show their level of commitment through their investments of resources, knowledge, and time (Johanson, 1994). These investments are specific to the relationship, but typical returns include the rendering of current transactions, the accumulation of knowledge, and improved control. The knowledge acquired may, for example, cover the level of technical, administrative or logistical competence of the partner (Easton, 1992).

However, commitment and responsiveness of either party to the venture demand a process of social and cultural adaptation. Fitzgerald and Willcocks (1994) found that a degree of cultural understanding, an element of flexibility regarding the contract, and a notion of fair dealing has to exist in outsourcing relations. Problems in ventures tend to arise, though, where the parties involved do not share the same social and cultural traits/norms (Mills & Murgatroyd, 1991). These complex ‘rites and rituals of corporate life’ pose difficulties during change initiatives since changes to these values require time for adaptation (Deal & Kennedy, 1982). In various cases the differences in culture cause a level of anxiety in employees as both sides flex their power to remain in charge.

At the lowest level of formality individuals from the client and vendor side form social and personal bonds. A bond between two firms implies tying together relations between partners (Easton, 1992). Therefore, strong bonding is dependent upon the satisfaction of each partner. Hence, the development of the relationship depends on social and personal bonds, so much so that alleviation of conflicts, ensuring satisfaction, and continuing adaptation all depend to a certain extent on the closeness of the bonds between the individuals. Personal bonds established between individuals in the organizations further increase familiarity and trustworthiness. Moreover, in certain cases the strength of the personal and social bond transcends and even replaces the economic bond, thus determining the raison d’etre for the flourishing of the relationship.

## Working context — ‘atmosphere’

Atmosphere, as represented in Figure 1, both produces and is a product of exchanges at contractual and cooperative levels. A number of features can be specified. Commitment and trust are interdependent, as greater commitment leads to greater trust and vice versa. Commitment to the relationship is a clear signal that a party is serious about achieving success, and is willing to exert effort on behalf of the relationship (Mohr & Spekman, 1996). Commitment in an outsourcing relationship might be measurable by specific investments, such as the vendor’s allocation of specific people to the contract, the regularity with which the service team interacts with the client, the frequency with which the service team might change. Trust grows with commitment, and it starts with taking the risk to trust the other party. For Andersen and Narus (1990) and Mohr and Spekman (1996) trust stems from the belief that a party’s word is reliable and that it will fulfil its obligation stipulated in any agreement, by acting predictably and fairly. Fairness encompasses two key aspects: the perceived ‘feel-fair’ of the outcomes received, and the perceived fairness of the vendor process for managing the relationship (Kumar, 1996), the latter signalled perhaps by the number and quality of vendor staff committed to handling the relationship.

Once a party develops trust in the other, a pattern of commonality arises as both parties become increasingly ready to work cooperatively towards established objectives (Brunard & Kleiner, 1994). Regardless of the level of trust, there will always be areas of difference, as the two parties will inevitably have some separate goals specific to their interests (Kumar, 1996). Therefore trust as a construct in outsourcing relationships, as in most other business relationships, tends be less intensive and involve lower personal commitment, than interpersonal relations in many other contexts (Anderson & Narus,

1990). Related developments in relationships are cooperation and conflict. Cooperation according to Axelrod (1984) involves (a) avoiding unnecessary conflict by cooperating as long as the other party does; (b) avoiding provocation in the face of conflict; (c) practising ‘forgiveness’ after provocation; and (d) practising clarity of behaviour so that the parties can adapt to each other’s behaviour. Realistically, outsourcing relations will experience both cooperation and conflict. The degree of each is affected by a range of factors, to be explored in the case study. However one feature inherent in such relationships are power issues.

Power-play in outsourcing relations is mainly caused by dependency and leads to a power-control dilemma (Easton, 1992). Power-dependency becomes evident through the influence one party can exert over the other (Cunningham & Tynan, 1993). This power, though, is dependent on structural features and the interests of the parties in the exchange relationship. Generally, in largescale IT outsourcing, structural features influence power relations in different ways over time, one observed pattern being an asymmetric dependency in favour of the vendor once an organization has transferred a significant amount of assets and/or staff (Willcocks, 1994b; Willcocks & Choi, 1995).

The achievement of expectations, or more, in an outsourcing relationship leads to satisfaction defined by Anderson and Narus (1984) as ‘a positive affective state resulting from the appraisal of all aspects of a firm’s working relationship with another firm’. In theory pursuit of each other’s benefits increases perception of closeness and trust. The closeness and achievement of expectations afford a strong feeling of ‘chemistry’ and results in satisfaction with each other’s performance feeding back into developing trustworthiness over time and determining the overall successfulness of the relationship (Kumar, 1996).

Having elaborated the exploratory model, we now move on to outlining the research approach. This will be followed by an analysis of the case in the light of our the decision-making and contracts and relationships frameworks detailed above.

## Research approach

In order to investigate these issues and the applicability of the frameworks in a distinctive context we selected the Inland Revenue/EDS outsourcing arrangement, signed in May 1994. The case was chosen because: (1) it represents one of the few large-scale IT outsourcing deals in the UK and is commonly referred to in ‘strategic partnering’ terms by participants and observers alike; (2) it is a long-term, ten-year deal where risk mitigation through contractual and, especially, relationship means are widely regarded as critical issues; and (3) we could gain access to major participants and stakeholders throughout the 1993–1997 period thus allowing us to conduct a longitudinal study.

## Data collection

For the case we interviewed eight participants drawn from Inland Revenue senior managers with IT, general business or Board responsibilities, employee representatives, vendor management, and external consultants who assisted in pre-contract negotiations and in post-contract issues. Our own view, adopted in the research approach, is that gaining at least a triangulation of respondent viewpoints is important for establishing whether any basic, agreed patterns to events, motivations, influencing factors and outcomes emerge. This also allows the researcher to establish differences in interpretation by respondents and investigate possible explanations for those differences. All eight respondents were first interviewed in Autumn 1993. Subsequently respondents were interviewed between three and four times across the 1993–1997 research period. Interviews varied from 40 minutes to 120 minutes in length and were conducted using a semi-structured questionnaire with many openended questions. All interviewees were assured of anonymity so as to promote open discussions.

Interviews followed the same protocol, proceeding from an unstructured to a structured format. In the unstructured section interviewees were asked to describe their version of the outsourcing story. Where a respondent was being re-interviewed the unstructured section related to what had happened since the previous interview, their new version of the story, and their assessment of developments. All respondents were then asked semistructured questions designed to elicit information on specific issues missing from their previous accounts. These issues included: the scope of the outsourcing decision; its sponsors; pre-contract issues; assessment of previous and subsequent vendor performance; vendor strategy and mode of operation; management of the contract and emerging issues (for example levels of dispute, vendor staffing levels, retained in-house skills); development of the relationship (e.g. exchanges, development of trust/cooperation — delineated in the relationship framework — see above); and contextual factors influencing perceptions, behaviours and events. When participants expressed a viewpoint they were asked to provide specific supporting evidence, in the form of documentation, and in some cases further anecdotal, more detailed evidence. Interviews were then transcribed, and the text confirmed with the relevant respondents. Additionally we sought supporting documentation in order to construct the case histories. This included annual reports, internal financial documents, public reports by the National Audit Office, details/summaries of outsourcing contracts, and some internal memos and reports.

## Data analysis

By 1997 the transcribed interviews amounted to 380 single-spaced pages. We then developed a higher level of abstraction and interpretation by applying the precepts of intentional analysis to the transcripts (Sanders, 1982). The approach was also used in related work in which the UK Inland Revenue figured, and is described in more detail in Willcocks et al (1996). Briefly, however, Sanders prescribes four steps for interpreting transcribed interviews from an interpretivist perspective. Firstly, the researcher describes the facts of the phenomenon, with facts here understood as socially agreed realities, agreed that is amongst all respondents. In step two, researchers determine how participants ascribe meaning to reality by how participants perceive cause and affect. In step three, researchers identify themes across the interviews and, in step four, we assess our own views as to how the accumulated evidence can be interpreted, and whether specific patterns, themes and interpretations emerge from the rich data. This latter set of interpretations makes up the bulk of the case study. As Lincoln and Guba (1985), Craig Smith (1990) and Boland (1985) point out, the validity of interpretive analysis defies quantification. However, we have included excerpts from the transcribed interviews to help readers to judge for themselves the validity of our analysis. These sources and procedures allowed us to develop a qualitative, interpretative approach to case study construction (Walsham, 1995). So constructed, the case history will now be detailed in the next section.

## IT outsourcing at the Inland Revenue: case study

## Context and overview

In line with government policy in the early 1990s the Inland Revenue (IR), in common will all central government departments, sought to market test the costefficiency of 25% of all its activities against external suppliers by 1994. By mid-1992 the idea was to outsource IT operations on a five-to-ten year contract. At that time IT operations cost £100 million a year and involved 1200 staff. There were 2400 IT staff in all, 13 data centres, and an annual IT budget including capital investment of £250 million. By January 1993 three vendor consortia and three single bidders — EDS, Hoskyns and Sema Group — were pursuing the contract, planned to commence in 1994. It was clear that the contract needed a very big supplier. In the event, in November 1993 a £1 billion ten-year contract was awarded to US computer supplier EDS. At the time this represented the biggest outsourcing contract ever awarded in Europe. At this stage the estimated cost savings from handing over IT assets, staff and management of activities were £225 million over the life of the contract. EDS would pay the IR £70 million for its mainframe assets. IT staff would be transferred to EDS in two tranches: the computer operations staff at the commencement of the contract in mid-1994, and the remaining 800 key development people 18 months later in early 1996. Some 300 staff would stay in-house, while it was estimated that 200 would leave through natural wastage.

## Early days: 1993–1994

Early on there were some industrial relations difficulties over possibilities of job loss, and terms and conditions of transfer. In 1993 an internal job evaluation scheme seemed to threaten pay cuts before transfer to EDS. A further issue was whether staff would retain their public sector redundancy terms when transferring to EDS. April 1993 saw a one-day strike over these issues. Another bone of contention was who would pay for any staff redundancy. The Revenue wanted all staff who refused to relocate to EDS’s data centre or refused alternative offers of work to nevertheless receive redundancy payments. EDS wanted to levy extra fees of up to £50 million to cover these unanticipated payments. The Revenue argued that EDS won the bid on a set price. By the end of 1994 these issues were largely settled, with the Inland Revenue Staff Federation seemingly satisfied with the transfer arrangements (Pickard, 1994).

According to Geoff Bush, the IT Director in 1993 (and Director General in 1995), the rationale for total outsourcing was: to gain rapid access to new technologies; enhance the capability of IT to meet business needs, and especially to reduce development time; optimise IT staff career opportunities and achieve step improvements in efficiency, including cost efficiency. Senior managers also indicated that privatisation would increase flexibility within IT: in terms of pay, conditions and promotion policies, escaping public spending constraints, and also increasing the capability to respond to change by re-engineering activities or re-equipping staff as needed. It would also lead to the possibility of selling spare capacity — not previously an option in the public sector.

The IR had set up a contract management team, that varied in size between 6–20 people, and that worked together for two years on the tender, evaluation and negotiation processes leading up to contract signing in May 1994. The team was a mixed group of professionals with expertise drawn from legal, human resource, outsourcing, IT, civil service, property and contract management areas as needed. A senior IR manager worked full time at making the high level decisions, providing the resources, protecting the team and its progress, and managing relationships with other senior IR managers and government ministers. There was also administrative support to establish a critical audit trail. The person who was going to manage the contract for the IR also joined the team and took part in the 1994 negotiating phase. The role of the contract team was to make a market, that is attract bidders and vet the bids. Respondents stressed that how this was handled had implications for the relationship with the eventual vendor. As one respondent said:

People thinking of outsourcing rarely take into account the cost of actually responding to their requirements. If the potential customer is not thinking laterally, and does not see the issue, they are already doing something to the relation ship . . . because that cost has got to be recovered and there’s only one place it comes from . . . (but) if you are minimising the bid cost, you really are doing something really rather helpful to the potential deal down stream.

The team also used ex-vendor consultants to predict vendor costs and prices, and provide the information vendors would need to make a bid. This included, for example, details of current performance measurement, costs, budgets, human resource policies and pension arrangements. Coupling this with an analysis of the slack in the systems in terms of IT performance and cost also enabled the team to calculate a price at which a vendor could agree the deal while still making a reasonable profit. This again was felt to have relationship implications:

It’s terribly hard to get across this notion that supplier absolutely has to make a reasonable return if your objective is to have some kind of strategic relationship, and some real power being pushed into your business by the vendor to achieve your business objectives. (Outsourcing consultant)

It was widely recognised amongst the team that, with 134 risks of varying severity identified, risk mitigation was critical and depended upon the quality of what one respondent termed the ‘up-front thinking’. One element was tranching the contract, that is dividing the IT activity/asset transfers into four separate parts. This eventually was reduced to two, with the standard services and processing being transferred first, and the major development work transferred later, provided the first tranche was working. The service work was all carefully monitored for service quality and timeliness, and a built-in cost reduction was operationalised annually. The contract included penalty clauses which included financial remedies. For development and future work a charging approach was also developed that combined pricing certainty within flexibility in order to avoid price escalation typically found in long-term deals. This worked on the basis of a set work unit price with discounts offered where volumes of work were ordered sufficiently in advance. The contract also ensured continuity of service at the end of the contract in the event of a switch in supplier or a move back in-house. The contract also stipulated that the supplier could not bind the IR into a technology not available from a third party, and enabled a testing of prices in the market against any step shift in technology. Under the contract the IR could also market test any activity, with the possibility of another supplier taking over that activity if it could be done more cost-effectively.

In 1994 the retention and development of certain inhouse skills, involving about 29 people, was also designed to mitigate risk (see Figure 2 for an overview, developed in late 1995). The central group here was the contract management team, described as ‘the guardians of the contract’. Here the contract management processes were deliberately re-engineered at first to ensure tight control, that could be loosened subsequently. The leader of the six-person team had an understanding of IT at the IR, of the IR business together with experience of negotiation. The team also contained expertise in resource procurement, commercial accounting, productivity monitoring and planning, covering about fourteen different tasks in all. According to one IR respondent:

The 14 processes range from looking at the resources employed and delivery of service down to looking at the costs that the supplier is incurring for delivery of that service . . . are they being prudent or finding the more expensive stuff . . . basically at this level they are monitoring the performance, virtually against contract.

The IR also retained three people with technical expertise to look at the overall architecture of IR systems, understand the technical issues the vendor was grappling with, work with them at a high technical level and provide advice to IR business project directors. About twenty of the latter were also retained in-house to lead all projects and work with the relevant vendor project managers. Additionally there were several staff working in the IR business with what was called ‘business-related IT’ experience. These would work on feasibility studies with the vendor, on developing business requirements and in testing systems for business applicability (see Figure 2). Clearly all these retained staff would need to work closely with relevant vendor staff in order to perform their mutual roles. Additionally, external outsourcing consultants from the original contract team were regularly used to review progress and developments.

## Emerging issues: 1994–1995

Senior managers in EDS and the IR were aware of the high profile nature of what some of them termed the ‘strategic partnership’ between the two organizations, and were understandably anxious to make it work. At the same time, such a large contract within the context of inevitably changing government requirements and political timetables could hardly be expected to run smoothly. One area was pricing the contract. In practice the original savings on running the base service remained protected by contract. The IR was also running into problems with the development of its ‘Pay and File’ system, introduced in 1993 for handling Corporation Tax. The system collects billions of pounds annually in company taxes. Problems emerged with EDS’s handling of the system, though these stemmed mainly from inadequacies in what was handed over to EDS. Flaws in the system resulted in late or non-paying companies not being reported to senior IR management for follow-up action. Because of the staff transfers taking place, the IR found itself short of resources to develop the system. Subsequently self assessment for companies was postponed until 1999 to allow more preparation time for the corporation tax ‘Pay and File’ system.

After initial adjustment problems on both sides it also became clear in early 1995 that relationship management required more comprehensive attention. Over time, outsourcing induced the IR to add two new sets of skills to what they already had available in-house: contract and, subsequently, relationship management:

What we found was that you can get your screwdriver out when things go wrong at the contract level, that is monitoring service levels, understanding what the business needs and translating that into resource requirements. You can tune up the processes on these, but you need to go to a higher level if things are putting the overall goals and the relationship at risk. You absolutely need commercial skills and business skills to maintain the partnering and the relationship, and make sure you are getting leverage. There needs to be senior management commercial type skills sitting above the contract management team, holding on to the relationship. (Consultant)

The IR IT Director assumed responsibility for relationship management, initially spending most of his time on this set of issues (though this reduced to 30% by late 1996). Another IT manager was responsible fulltime for relationship management on a day-to-day basis. By 1995 the IR had developed its in-house IT skills and management capability to that illustrated in Figure 2.

For the vendor side, a relationship management role was already in place. A view on the qualities required in that post was expressed:

The most important quality for a relationship manager is to be in tune with customer needs and expectations. Understand what they are trying to do . . . what business impact they are trying to make by using technology. They are changing and they don’t always express it in terms of technology. The relationship manager must be the link between the business requirements, and the technology down the road. But you can’t change your technology overnight, the investments are too big, but you want to make sure you do it right. So it’s not a technical job . . . (Senior vendor manager)

Partnering operated at several levels. There was a sixmonthly board-to-board meeting between EDS and IR. There were joint partnership meetings every six weeks between the four senior IR and EDS managers directly responsible for the outsourcing deal. These picked up issues of concern and also set up a partnership development group staffed by a senior IR manager, a senior EDS manager and a consultant, their role being to identify future issues requiring special attention, that would not get dealt with through the normal contract management mechanisms. According to one member:

The three of us, it took ages, have developed a way of com municating and trusting. We orchestrate what needs to happen on both sides of the fence, having got access to and commitment from all of the people up the chain that matter.

One resulting innovation was a joint perceptions exercise between client and vendor managers that identified 68 different strands of activity, specifically designed to make the relationship work better, and needed to achieve the higher objectives of the EDS–IR deal. A client and vendor managerial pair was responsible for delivering results from each. Additionally:

Each activity was given an EDS owner and an Inland Revenue owner. Each was charged with setting out plans . . . and would report monthly to me and my EDS counterpart . . . settlement of the issue was included in their performance agreements and pay structure . . . noone could say that the reason an issue is not settled is because my counterpart won’t agree. (IT Director, Inland Revenue)

Clearly these mechanisms and processes were designed to enhance the working relationships, motivations and focus of both vendor and IR managers. It also enabled a focus on developing mutual understandings, perceptions and goals. The objectives here were to attain the wider objectives of both parties, not just those achievable at the contractual micro-level. In addition to these mechanisms and processes, a consensus amongst senior managers was developed around over a dozen guiding principles to regulate the relationship. According to one respondent, during 1995 these became a code of behaviour: “when people do not behave in line with them it’s not uncommon for them to be challenged”.

## The self assessment tax project

One of the objectives of relationship-building was to sharpen mutual responsiveness and access to resources. By mid-1995 a fundamental time-constrained development tended to be attracting the available resources. In 1995 the UK government announced its intention to save some 3000 jobs amongst tax staff by introducing a selfassessment tax scheme for taxpayers, to be operational from April 1997. When the IR first drew up its outsourcing plans it was not aware of this project, first announced in general terms by the Government in 1993. It represented probably the biggest single reform of UK tax administration for fifty years and was highly dependent on supporting information systems being in place. Not surprisingly, this systems development created considerable anxiety amongst EDS and IR senior IT staff. Neither wished to be seen to fail on such a high profile development project, now called the Computerised Environment for Self-Assessment (Cesa) project.

EDS believed that the system should be kept as simple as possible. Off-the-shelf packages could provide the capability to record who should receive the forms and the payment history of each taxpayer. The difficulty was that two systems would have to be maintained, because the new system would not interface immediately with the existing Cop/Coda systems holding all current records. Moreover, the new ‘simple’ system would not facilitate spot-checks on the accuracy of taxpayers’ returns. IR management therefore wanted a tailored package to meet its specific requirements but this greatly increased the risk of failing to deliver a robust system on time. Development was also shot through with uncertainties. The cost of the project was unknown as at mid-1995. An estimate that the system would have to handle 90 million transactions annually was not that dependable. EDS managers questioned whether the chosen technology was sufficiently expandable. Also the system — ICL VME mainframes linked to servers and thousands of terminals — was dependent on ICL Goldrush super-servers that had never before been used on such a large project. EDS also worried that the ICL equipment seemed to cost more than twice as much to buy and run as equivalent IBM hardware.

For EDS managers the project represented a high risk in terms of short timescales, technical complexity, and a major change in the way business would be conducted. Furthermore, the project was only to come under EDS control when the remaining 800 IR development staff were transferred to them in early 1996. This added a further risk, as senior managers in both organizations conceded, with management of the project being transferred in mid-flight to an outside supplier. By mid-1995 EDS managers were asking for control of the project to be handed over earlier. They felt that they could not guarantee delivery before they had control, and had arrived at agreement over the choice of systems. IR senior managers were willing to concede this control to maintain the ‘strategic’ relationship, but more importantly to give EDS an improved opportunity to deliver. Failure would reflect badly on both sets of managers, and also on the outsourcing contract as a whole. In these circumstances, it seemed likely that the cost of the project would be less of an issue than on-time delivery of a robust system. Additional resources would be needed, either from diverting staff from other IR projects, or buying in additional resources from EDS above the original contract price, though developments in partnering were making price and quality of resource uncontentious issues.

From mid-1995 to April 1997 the base service part of the IR-EDS contract was delivered largely unproblematically. The challenges were in the development work, particularly on the high profile Cesa project. In the event the 800 IR development staff were transferred to EDS six months earlier than the planned date of January 1996. Subsequently over 450 staff were working on the Cesa project full-time, and by mid-June 1996 the extra costs incurred for such work had largely used up a £20 million contingency fund set aside for the purpose. EDS reduced risks by abandoning some of the development work carried out previously by the IR, and went for less complex software for the first Cesa release due in November 1996. It also scaled down the use of what EDS respondents described as more risky object-oriented software. Instead, using client/server techniques, ICL mainframes would be used with Sequent servers to link with 10 000 local terminals.

In November 1996, EDS and IR planned to cut-over live data from the existing IDNS Cullinet-based database containing taxpayer records to the newly installed ICL mainframes with Sequent servers running Oracle. Release 1, management accounting based on ICL and Sofware AG’s Adabas, would also go live. Subsequently the plan was to introduce Release 2, the main tax self assessment system, in April 1997, with Release 3, the compliance system to enable tax staff to follow up queries on tax returns, going live in March 1998. In the buildup to the first Cesa release, trade union and IR respondents reported a range of teething problems. In a rehearsal in October of going live to 15 000 network users, 3000 could not log on. Some Phase 1 software was delivered behind schedule and had ‘bug’ problems. There were delays in developing compliance software for systems checks in Releases 1 and 2, though IR representatives suggested that such software was not needed until Release 3. Trade union representatives pointed to large-scale job cuts against a rising post and number of queries from taxpayers, and work being focused on processing tax returns rather than on training staff on compliance issues to ensure tax returns were accurate.

Despite these issues, on 4 November 1996 the first Cesa release went live to a restricted user group. All our respondents suggest that the emerging technical problems were minor, the following being a typical comment:

The system was available as planned to everyone who needed to work on it on the day. The number of users will increase as the work we need to do on it increases. This gradual build up has always been part of our plan. (Project manager)

This seemed to be a product of the amount of human resources put on to the project, coupled with the scaleddown, relative simplicity of the systems design.

At much the same time, it was announced that EDS was expected to receive £1.6 billion over the contract duration rather than the £1 billion announced in 1993. By January 1997, EDS was pursuing plans to combine the management of the computer functions of the IR with those of its Social Security division. (In the late 1990s EDS also had outsourcing contracts with the UK Department of Social Security to run its mainframes, Child Support Agency systems, systems development and strategic initiatives). The aim, according to both EDS and IR senior management respondents, was to maintain and improve quality of service to DSS and IR end-users while saving millions of pounds through economies of scale on machines, maintenance contracts and support staff. EDS planned one strategic business unit with IR and Social Security Divisions. The IR Division would be streamlined to include four main capabilities: a Partnership Operations Directorate to coordinate EDS services in IR and management relationships with IR customers; a Partnership Delivery Directorate for development, integration, delivery, enhancement and maintenance of IR systems, especially Cesa; a Partnership Development Directorate to develop new initiatives with IR; and a Programme Office Directorate to plan and manage processes for the IR Division. In April 1997 Release 2 of Cesa — the main tax self-assessment system — went live with few problems, as per schedule.

## Case analysis and discussion

## Decision-making and outcomes

The case demonstrates some early successes with ‘total or ‘strategic’ outsourcing, though a number of significant risks remained latent within the outsourcing arrangement over its first three years. These are summarised in Table 1 which compares the decision-making factors detailed in the earlier part of the paper against 1994 – 1997 events at the UK IR. In terms of our check questions a suitable and motivated vendor was found, no easy matter given the scale of the contract and the commitment it represented. Moreover, significant human resource issues arose with asset and people transfers, and these seemed to have been well managed. For the first few years, at least, there seemed to be sufficient in-house management capability retained or developed to deliver on the decision. One can begin to question the original economic rationale, though the real issue here is whether the IR could have carried out many of the projects, like Cesa, in the time frames required; also it is necessary to assess the economic rationale as part of multiple objectives, rather than in isolation.

In terms of our analytical framework, however, one interesting aspect of this case is that it involved total outsourcing of what generally had been recognised as an in-house IT function with a good record (Dyerson & Roper, 1990; Willcocks, 1994a) though that has not always been the retrospective view of users (Smith, 1996). In some respects, in-house capability relative to the market was high; for example, the Worthing data centre and in-house knowledge of IR functioning. In the light of rising costs over the 1994 –1996 period it is also by no means clear that in-house service costs were out of line with what the market would charge either. On the other hand, it was clear that the outsourcing evaluation enabled the identification of ways in which in-house IT could be run more efficiently. The two most economically viable options were to achieve these in-house, or point them out to the vendor and let this be reflected in the price charged. The IR chose the latter course of action. The reasons cited for outsourcing make sense but the overall mandate from government would seem to have been the dominant influence. An important driver was the desire for cost savings and the assumption that private sector suppliers could deliver IT more efficiently than public sector departments, even while making a profit. The twenty per cent cost savings regularly touted at the beginning of the project would seem to have been partly offset by the unanticipated £200 million extra cost incurred within a year of project commencement. When the self-assessment development project is then taken into account, it becomes clear that cost savings were, by 1996, becoming a much lower priority, though the original operational cost savings of £220 million were still protected by contract.

In the analysis it is useful to highlight three specific areas that deserve specific attention.

## (1) Political and technical uncertainties in the public sector

The case points to the political and technical uncertainties that can plague public sector projects. The government-mandated introduction of the self assessment scheme by April 1997 represents merely a largescale high profile example of the additional risks engendered when having to develop new systems during an outsourcing contract. Even a ‘strategic partnership’ arrangement seems to come under considerable strain in such circumstances as prevailing in the IR deal. In previous work we have pointed to examples of senior government ministers developing government policy without a real understanding of the IT implications, and the difficulties involved in providing information systems support (Willcocks, 1994a). In the IR case this factor would seem to have a bearing both on the government commitment to tight deadlines for the delivery of the self-assessment scheme, but also to its predilection for privatising IT services wholesale. In the IR case it may well have been looking for a domino effect with the Inland Revenue deal having managers and a supplier committed to making it a high profile success, so leading the way for other government departments, and perhaps also for local government and the NHS.

## (2) Risks

Some commentary on risks is provided in Table 1. In terms of our analytical framework, this total outsourcing contract would seem to emerge as fundamentally high risk. The case already demonstrates the considerable business and technological uncertainty prevailing in just the first three years of the project, and the political/governmental overlay of actions and concerns distinctive to the public sector would seem to make no promises for this decreasing over the contract’s lifetime. Moreover, in terms of outcome, there is little discrimination between whether IT/IS activities are strategic or useful, or represent leading competence advantages or are commodities. Treating IT/IS in these terms, as an undifferentiated commodity the preferred contractor or ‘strategic alliance’ route is the only remaining way of mitigating risk, even though, historically many longterm, large-scale single supplier contracts had produced disappointing results (Lacity & Willcocks, 1996; Strassmann, 1997).

Again a range of risks in the arrangements come to the fore on the technical front. It is clear that many IT/IS activities in the IR are highly integrated, and may become even more so if mooted plans to link activities with other government departments, particularly the Department of Social Security, are pursued. Moreover, IT activities also have multiple user and customer interfaces and in many respects go to the core of the Revenue’s operational functioning. Furthermore, the IR has outsourced a range of activities with low technological maturity, as shown notably in the ‘Pay and File’ and Cesa projects. In practice the risks associated with outsourcing such projects were exacerbated by having to pursue political timetables and deadlines and hand over projects to EDS while part way through the development process. The IR also handed over most technology to the vendor, though it contractually secured agreement that EDS would not bind IR into any technology that it could not get from a third party, and also secured technology transfer rights in the event of contract termination.

## (3) Asymmetries of dependence

The case does raise some concerns about asymmetries of dependence developing over time (Willcocks, 1994b). Contractually, if IR senior managers were unhappy with EDS performance, they could decide not to transfer the development staff in 1996. The contract was specifically set up to transfer staff over at different times in order to give IR some leverage over the supplier. However, one can already see that by mid-1995, the Inland Revenue could hardly switch supplier; it was already highly dependent on EDS. It also is clearly in the interest of the ‘strategic supplier’ to increase this dependence. One particularly worrying feature in other long term outsourcing deals we researched was the extent to which in-house technical capability became eroded over time (Willcocks, Lacity et al, 1995a; Willcocks et al, 1996). Some elements of this are in the present case, with inhouse project management and technical capability perhaps not meeting the high performance criteria stipulated in the research findings of Feeny and Willcocks (1997). It is possible for an in-house IT function to lose control of its IT destiny over time as a supplier actively penetrates the business as well as the technical management of the client organization. Such dependence on a single supplier also means that switching costs at any point would be very prohibitive.

## Contracts and relationships

The mitigation of such risks is mainly by contractual terms, including for example penalty clauses and exit arrangements and any risk reward incentives prevailing, and by the long term relationship and the mutual dependence and commitment such a relationship assumes. On the latter, there were indications by late 1996 of both parties consciously putting in place mechanisms for relationship development. Additionally, as noted above, the high profile nature of the contract and the high concern by EDS and IR managements, for their own reasons, to demonstrate the effectiveness of the arrangements also serves to mitigate risk somewhat.

## (1) The ‘contractual’ level

In terms of our second framework, it would seem that both parties were very secure on establishing the relationship at what we term the ‘contractual’ level (see Figure 1). The contract was highly detailed and designed to reduce negotiation on price to a minimum, not only at the bid stage but across the lifetime of the contract. After initial staff relations problems, assets and staff transfers were managed well by both vendor and client organization. Very tight performance assessment criteria and procedures were put in place and a well staffed contract management team monitored vendor performance closely and exchanged information on a regular basis with vendor counterparts. The vendor for its part, over the first two years, established suitable complementary management staffing arrangements, and service ethic, to deliver the contract. The informal social adaptations necessarily developed over time as a result of communication and contact. One comment here is that both vendor and client managers were used to operating at this contractual level, and developed ways of working at this level, but this could and did create modes of exchange that hindered further ‘cooperative’ developments. In terms of our Figure 1 framework the ‘atmosphere’ at the ‘contractual’ level, the context in which ‘contractual exchanges occurred, greatly influenced, but in time also became influenced by, developments at what we term the ‘cooperative’ level.

A further point emphasises the importance of operating at both contractual and cooperative levels. As one respondent said, in referring to the need to go beyond contract micro-management: ‘the management is to do with the totality of what you get for your money, not just at the micro-level’. As we have seen in this case, the IR and EDS managers operated closely on cost/performance and service levels and issues, eliciting business requirements and getting these translated into EDS objectives. But none of this focused on the larger picture, and over time none of it could be guaranteed to translate into the ‘totality’ of joint benefits intended from the overall outsourcing arrangement. Both client, and subsequently the vendor, came to realise that this required additional mechanisms and management effort, particularly in the area of relationship development and maintenance. The new 1997 EDS management arrangements to some extent were designed to respond to this perceived need.

## (2) The ‘cooperative’ level

In earlier research we found notions of partnering and relationships somewhat vaguely articulated by practitioners involved in outsourcing deals. Partnering was seen as involving some or most of: non-reliance on the contract as the basis of the relationship; mutual desire to work things out plus a ‘give and take’ philosophy; fair profit for the vendor; ability to work together at personal levels; cultural fit between the client and vendor organizations; good treatment of client’s transferred staff; and a perception that the vendor understands the client’s business and problems (Fitzgerald & Willcocks, 1994; Willcocks & Choi, 1995). In practice both client and vendor organizations found considerable difficulty in moving to the ‘cooperative’ level detailed in Figure 1; and this was the case even in self-styled strategic ‘partnerships’/‘alliances’.

What is interesting in the IR case is how ‘cooperative’ thinking occurred at the early bidding stage, with the intention at the IR of sending signals to potential ‘partners’ of the potential nature of the relationship. The signals involved trying to keep down the bid costs incurred by the vendor, and ensuring the vendor would make a reasonable profit. As one IR respondent said:

If you are minimising bid cost, and not driving incentives down for the supplier, you are doing something really rather helpful to the potential deal down stream . . . acting otherwise you can damage the relationship side irrevocably.

At the same time EDS itself was anxious to promote its self-perceived capability for partnering and ‘co-sourcing’. As David Thorpe (Managing Director, EDS UK) demonstrates, much of this used the language of trust, commitment and mutual objectives shown in our Figure 1 framework (Thorpe, 1996).

Once the deal became operational, however, given its complexity and size and the time pressures for delivery there was an understandable focus on the ‘contractual level. Initially neither vendor nor client put in place thorough additional formal mechanisms for development at the ‘cooperative’ level. Our respondents recognised that the development of the relationship was a function of time and interactivity, as the framework suggests. The main assumption in the first year would seem to have been that interactivity at the ‘contractual level’ would over time develop a shift in ‘atmosphere’, facilitating ‘cooperative’ behaviour. On this point the IR experience suggests several things about our Figure 1 framework. The first is that ‘contractual’ level patterns of behaviour and exchange on their own can actually hinder rather than support subsequent development of ‘cooperative’ patterns. One influencing factor here, as found at the IR, is the type of contract management culture and skills pu in place. The second is that the development of ‘cooperative’ level behaviour may require separate formal mechanisms and management arrangements, as depicted in Figure 1, but also specific management skills (see case detail above) in both client and vendor organizations and probably distinctive from those needed at the ‘contractual’ level.

Certainly a feature of the IR case is the move to relationship mechanisms and processes, essentially formal communications mechanisms, in order to facilitate the development of mutual objectives, social and cultural adaptations, and social and personal bonds. This was allied with partnering behaviour being included in performance measurement and reward systems of the relevant managers from both sides, and was intended to ensure that both IR and EDS staff invest their knowledge, time and resources into relationship issues. This investment then became translated, partly but significantly, into how the parties worked together on a range of issues, with the Cesa project being a primary example (see above). On this point the IR case is suggestive as to how ‘cooperative level’ behaviour can be triggered. The formal communications mechanisms depicted in Figure 1 would seem to be a critical factor, but also gaining vendor–client management commitment to mutual objectives by getting them to agree to joint activity, and building recognition of degree of achievement into the formal reward system. The problem then became one of sustaining developments at the ‘cooperative’ level. In addition to those operating at the ‘contractual’ level some further mechanisms and processes were needed. On the IR side this involved developing formal relationship management positions, but also using a small group, including outside consultants, to monitor and develop responses to issues as they emerged at both ‘contractual’ and ‘cooperative’ levels. For the vendor it meant involvement in the six-monthly Board-to-Board meetings, the six-weekly joint partnership meetings and in the partnership development group, some rethinking of management skills required, and to a degree, the 1997 re-organization of the EDS IR Division.

The development of these over time in the IR case would suggest that in terms of our framework, formal mechanisms and processes are a critical first enabler for cooperative developments. Furthermore the flow of the framework from ‘contractual’ to ‘cooperative’ levels, and in each case from formal to informal mechanisms and processes would also seem to be something more than a logical sequence underpinned by the academic literature. The IR case suggests that the flow may also be a necessary temporal sequencing in order to both trigger and sustain the ‘cooperative’ level patterns, exchanges and behaviours.

It is interesting to speculate why this may be the case. For example some of the literature on inter-organizational relations point to the importance of personal and social bonds in developing and sustaining relationships (McFarlan & Nolan, 1995; Blois, 1996). But do these precede or can they even replace formal mechanisms and processes as a driver at the ‘cooperative’ level? One issue may well be that formal mechanisms enable the larger targetting required to achieve significant changes in patterns of interaction and behaviour. Secondly, and related to creating the critical mass needed for change, they may well enable the better targetting of significant benefit areas and the creation of reward mechanisms more widely available organizationally. Thirdly, relationship development may well be a function of success, as much as a precursor. Certainly this was the thinking when formal processes were introduced at the IR:

We’ve targeted key individuals and functions and done some research in what their issues and problems and agenda are, and we’ve picked something important to them and applied partnership talk in a positive way. We are doing operational things and delivering tangible benefit so they can see partnership working. That’s time consuming and difficult but it’s working. (IR respondent)

A fourth area involves how trust, cooperation and commitment in business relations actually develop over time. The academic literature indicates that this will inevitably vary depending on the specific circumstances. Zaheer et al (1997) define trust as the expectation that an actor can be relied upon to fulfil obligations, will behave in a predictable manner and will act and negotiate fairly when the possibility of opportunism exists. In examining 107 inter-firm supplier–buyer relationships they found that inter-organizational trust was the overriding driver of exchange performance, negotiation and conflict, while interpersonal trust exerted little direct influence on these outcomes. Nevertheless: ‘interpersonal trust may also matter through its institutionalising effects on inter-organizational trust’. From this perspective, formal processes and mechanisms at the IR would develop inter-organizational trust, with interpersonal trust being a minor influencing factor. It may well be that there was an initial, tacit over-dependence in the IR case on the development and influence of interpersonal trust. This may well have something to do with the management culture in large vendors:

Some of the large vendors will not invest in anything much that it believes does not add to their bottom line. If you ask to put a robust programme office together, or feed your contract management systems with information some wil tend to resist . . . some have empowered their people and created remuneration arrangements based on certain characteristics. Broadly, their managers do what they want to do and this has advantages but can be unhelpful . . . you can get a reaction by changing their performance review but if they are empowered without a framework it’s difficult to get them to be consistent. (Consultant)

The significant individuals here are what Katz and Kahn (1978) have referred to as the ‘individual boundary spanners’ who are more closely involved in the interorganizational relationship than other members of the organization, and tend to interact with their counterparts to a greater extent (Friedman & Podolny, 1992). As the quote indicates, without formal mechanisms and processes and ‘a code of behaviour’ as developed eventually at the IR (see above) it is difficult to get empowered vendor managers to behave consistently. Yet on the definition cited above, consistency and predictability are fundamental elements in the formation and sustaining of trust. It is also doubtful if interpersonal trust would have contributed significantly to the development of interorganizational trust. Therefore at the IR, mechanisms and processes were put in place in order to attempt to develop interaction, demonstrate investment of time and resources, and achieve tangible results thus hopefully raising the levels of both inter-organizational and interpersonal trust.

A final component in our framework is that of power and dependence. We have touched on several aspects of structural dependency and power asymmetries in favour of the vendor developing over time in strategic partnering arrangements. But clearly much depends on the quality not just of the ‘contractual’ but also the ‘cooperative’ arrangements as they are developed over time. We have argued that the IR contractual arrangements seem to reduce risks significantly. But as one respondent commented, referring to the power aspects of the contract:

The contract is a bit like a nuclear deterrent. You need one and you have got to have a framework, but if you’ve got to use it you are probably in trouble. (IT Director, Inland Revenue)

But if, additionally, mutual objectives can be identified, and higher common business objectives can be pursued, then mutual dependency can develop that offsets somewhat the structural dependence over time. However, one cardinal point made on this by all respondents was that this relationship aspect required constant attention and day-to-day management at many different levels in both organizations. For strategic partnering in IT outsourcing arrangements this tends to underline the degree of formalisation needed at the ‘cooperative’ level as shown in Figure 1.

## Conclusion

The case study reveals a complicated set of issues being managed fairly effectively in a difficult public service context. The case raises the issue whether the UK public sector environment in the early and mid-1990s was actually appropriate for anything other than relatively short term contracts on a selective basis. At the IR it became clear that volatility in government policy added a high risk item to an already complicated large-scale outsourcing arrangement in ‘start-up’ mode. The fact that this cut across attempts to develop several major new systems using new technologies that the supplier as well as the in-house IT staff were not clear on only heightened the risk. However, the high profile nature of the deal, and the importance of the IR as the tax raising department for the government, probably served to mitigate many of the risks that would not be so easily offset in other strategic partnering cases.

In other strategic partnering cases we have seen asymmetries of dependence developing over time in favour of the vendor. This was not that apparent in the IR case, at least up to early 1997. A necessary protection is the identification and development of key in-house capabilities and skills to ensure the identification and delivery of business requirements, the ability to make technology work and the management of external supply (Feeny & Willcocks, 1997). There were indications that in the IR case these issues were understood and being acted upon.

One conclusion that must be drawn from the case is that, in strategic partnering for IT outsourcing, getting the ‘contractual’ level right is central to success but falls into the ‘necessary but not sufficient’ category. We developed and tested an exploratory framework for analysing the elements of a complementary, additional set of levels at which the vendor(s) and client organization need to interact. In applying it to the IR case we found the framework to have considerable applicability in terms of coverage of issues. The framework also enabled us to elicit the key areas that the parties themselves identified as necessary to act upon, and the innovative management approaches they adopted, in order to attempt to convert the rhetoric of strategic partnering into a day-to-day reality.
