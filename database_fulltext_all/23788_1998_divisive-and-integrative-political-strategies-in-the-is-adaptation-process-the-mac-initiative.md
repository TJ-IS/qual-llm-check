# Divisive and integrative political strategies in the IS adaptation process: the MAC Initiative

JAA Sillince<sup>1</sup> and S Mouakket<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Management School, Royal Holloway, University of London, Egham, Surrey, TW20 0EX, UK; <sup>2</sup>Management Department, Faculty of Economics, University of Aleppo, Aleppo, Syria

The MAC Initiative project is described as seen through the eyes of users at one university. These subjective interpretations are described in terms of five different assumptions about politics and power. We show that information system users and developers hold multiple perspectives of events and switch between them in order to empathise with others, and that it is this perspective switching which enables them to behave coherently. One advantage of this ability to switch perspectives is shown by the software developer’s abilit to switch from a ‘zero sum’ perspective (a divisive political strategy) to a ‘socially shaped’ perspective (an integrative political strategy) as a means of managing the adaptation process.

## Introduction

The process of software development takes place today in a highly political atmosphere in which various competing subunits of user organizations have markedly different ideas of what software they want. Capturing and investigating these is the aim of the interpretive approach (Walsham, 1993). The existence of competing subunits in user organizations is a source of power for software developers. This means that software developers can deal with subunits either by using divisive strategies, thereby weakening them politically and reducing their capacity to press home their demands, or by integrative strategies which they use to get several user groups to agree on a common set of requirements.

## Theory

We take a multi-theory view (Pettigrew, 1985; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991) in order to illuminate our area of interest from a number of viewpoints. Our theme is politics and power in information system (IS) customisation within organizations, which we argue can be examined using several complementary perspectives. We choose five such theoretical positions for our paper, although we do not claim complete coverage of the theoretical possibilities. First, these perspectives are described and then a case study is presented, which is analysed against the theoretical perspectives. All five positions represent relatively recent improvements over the functionalist position (Selznick, 1949; Parsons, 1951; Miller & Rice, 1967) which assumed a normative consensus and which was never able to account for people doing things against their will (Dahl, 1957) giving rise to criticisms of lack of realism (Pfeffer, 1981; Mintzberg, 1983).

## Zero sum power

The first theoretical position is that of the ‘zero sum view of power (Clegg, 1989; Fincham, 1992). Politics is seen as the process of conflict between individuals over an object. The object is either a reward, a punishment, a right, an identity, or expertise (French & Raven, 1959). The object possesses its own intrinsic power, because possession of the object increases the power of the possessor. Since only one party can possess an object, when one individual wins, others lose. The relevance to IS is whether workers lose when new IS are installed (a literature reviewed by Kling, 1980). Users and their managers vary in their degree of success in this contest depending on the situation studied. Newman and Noble (1990) concluded that users won, whereas Markus (1983) showed that a financial IS enabled control to be tightened in a divisionalised company, despite attempts by the divisions to sabotage the system. Shostak (1987) showed that air traffic controllers lost bargaining power when a new IS was installed. Because power has a locus— something to be won or lost (Pfeffer, 1981)—the theoretical viewpoint promotes discussion of IS’ effects on centralisation and decentralisation (Hill, 1981; Robey, 1981; Mosco, 1989). One candidate for a locus has been information (Hickson et al., 1971; Gottlieb & Borodin, 1973), which may be fought over and whose possession may give organizational power. One practical conclusion which has been drawn is that power in IS development should be more balanced between the various parties involved, and particularly that users should become more involved (Boland, 1979; Robey & Farrow, 1982; Ives & Olson, 1984; Friedman & Cornford, 1989).

The zero sum view has several weaknesses. It cannot account for people accepting IS when it leads them to lose power, failing to discriminate between altruistic and power-hungry motives (McClelland, 1970). More generally, people discover new ends or new means to old ends—Coombs (1992) found that doctors accepted new IS when they discovered that they could use them to improve patient care and to gain more resources to that end. Indeed, the balance of power in the zero sum view is always fragile since the rules of the game change so rapidly—Willcocks & Mason (1987) found that a new IS which threatened doctors’ privileged possession of expertise was eventually accepted by them when the IS began to become out of date.

## Processual power

The second theoretical position is that of processual power, which regards power as a relationship (Pettigrew, 1973; Orlikowski, 1989) involving an exchange of a desired object such as a reward, an identity, or expertise (French & Raven, 1959). Relevant concepts are the relations of reciprocity, dependence, exchange, commitment over time, and the effect of organizational structural variables such as complexity and heterogeneity (Pettigrew, 1973; Ranson et al, 1980; Clegg, 1981; Mintzberg, 1983). The possibility of exchange leads to a positive sum view of power (Fincham, 1992). The relevance of IS is that it provides a focus for negotiations, with the roles of technology supplier and user central to a processual account. These roles are simplistically described in the theory, and only accounted for at the technological level, with no inclusion of organizational or group dynamics. The parties are seen as equals, and so the perspective fails to account for inequalities of power, user resistance and mistrust (Knights & Murray, 1994). Despite this, the processual perspective has added to our knowledge of the management of conflict over IS. For example, investigation of user–analyst relationships has shown that participation of users in IS development can encourage conflict (Walsham, 1993) and that the balance of participation and direction can be altered by user–analyst interaction (Newman & Robey, 1992).

## Organizational power

The third theoretical approach is that of organizational power, which treats IS as having social and organizational as well as technical attributes. Examples include the ‘web’ model, a loosely structured model incorporating known organizational and group dynamic factors (Kling, 1987, 1991), the organizational process model (Cyert & March, 1963; Allison, 1971) and the negotiated order model (Strauss, 1963). System success depends not only on technical attributes of the IS but also on personal and social attributes (attitudes and understanding) of the individuals. The IS relevance is that organizational communication (Yates & Orlikowski, 1992), power shifts within the organization (Curtis et al, 1988; Orlikowski, 1989), training (Newman & Rosenberg, 1985), conflict resolution (Robey & Farrow, 1982; Robey et al, 1993), history of support (Pettigrew, 1973), organizational responsibility (Walsham, 1993; Boland & Day, 1989), accountability (Franz & Robey, 1984) and enabling of change (Dagwell & Weber, 1983) become important between business and IS staff. Orlikowski (1989) has provided one of the most exhaustive lists of organizational factors affecting IS development and implementation. The list includes division of labour, patterns of dependency, coalition formation within teams, acts of coercion and rebellion, displays of territoriality, resentment and stereotyping. Another variable affecting behaviour of system analysts is hierarchical position in the organization. Mouakket et al (1994) found that systems analysts give less time to lower ranking users. Despite its helpful effect on our understanding of IS development, the organizational power perspective assumes a rational view of interests (Newman & Noble, 1990), and forces a rigid separation of social and technical aspects of IS.

## Structurally constrained power

The fourth perspective is that of structural constraints on power, which concerns itself with how goals and assumptions of powerful or powerless individuals and groups function at the organizational level. These cognitions include ideologies of class (Willott, 1987) and organizational self-perceptions as hierarchies versus markets (Fincham, 1992). Class ideologies influence decisions about the use of IS for deskilling, intensifying and controlling labour (Braverman, 1974; Orlikowski, 1988, 1989) and reactions of resistance or resigned acceptance (Burawoy, 1979). Self-perceptions lead to reliance on market (deal-based) versus administrative (rule-based) mechanisms. Although the perspective deals with cognitions, it contains no theory of subjective meanings, and so is unable to deal with whether exploitation (a subjective definition) is taking place or not. The assumption that IS automatically leads to managerial control has been criticised (Child, 1985) as has the contention that IS leads to deskilling (Burawoy, 1979; Littler, 1982). Moreover, management is not omniscient (Knights & Willmott, 1990; Murray, 1989). Nor does IS have predictable or uniform effects on power distribution—a study of rail freight yards showed that although supervisors lost control of marshalling yards, they regained it at the area level by means of IS-supported management control systems (Dawson & McLoughlin, 1986). Structural constraints, whether objectively or subjectively defined, therefore have an ambiguous role in IS. IS can have an influence on deskilling or empowerment, or market or administrative systems, and on centralised or decentralised structures. The perspective is not able therefore to reason about the reciprocal influence of IS and organizational structures and behaviour on each other.

## Socially shaped power

The ‘social shaping’ perspective on power starts from the position that peoples’ cognitions about IS are socially constructed, so that even ‘technical’ attributes of IS are socially defined (Berger & Luckman, 1971). Social and political activity has the effect of illuminating what is taken for granted and what is problematic. Power is therefore the ability to change social definitions about things from being problematic (liable to being challenged by others) to being taken for granted (able to be accepted by others). One approach to the perspective, actor–network theory (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987) suggests a number of steps in this process—definition of problems, arousal of social interests, enrolment of agencies and actors, and mobilisation of network members. Network members define each other using texts, computers, knowledge, and money. Networks where agreement, acceptance and commitment are reached converge so that decisions become irreversible.

These considerations of power are now examined in the light of the case study. The institutional setting of the case study will be described next.

## Institutions

UK universities receive income both in the form of fees per student, and as block grants (related to a number of criteria) from central government. The UK universities divide roughly into two groups. The first group comprises the ‘new’ universities whose income depends upon the teaching of large numbers of undergraduate students. In such institutions the profit margin per student is small (fees are predetermined and hence uncontrollable). This means that reducing unit costs is vitally important. The second group, the so-called ‘old’ universities, receive larger central government grants, and so can afford to concentrate less on undergraduate teaching and more on postgraduate teaching and research, which provides an extra source of government income. Although the division into ‘new’ and ‘old’ universities is relatively fixed, the relative priority for funding between them, and that between teaching and research, depends upon periodic government reviews, one of which is a quinquennial Research Assessment Exercise (RAE).

As far as the substance of university activities of teaching and research is concerned, information management requirements are very small. Teaching mostly remains a face-to-face activity which makes relatively small information management demands (records of student assessments and exam results). On the other hand, university research is largely an individual activity for which information management requirements are satisfied merely by a personal computer with Internet and email access.

This means that universities perceive their information requirements principally as a means of relating to the environment (government and students) from which they receive their income. Information given to the government (cost and quality data) is seen as crucial. Equally important is data to manage student fee income. A third concern of growing importance, as modularisation enables course-switching by students, is information passed from one university to another. What is not seen as important is information to monitor and evaluate students. The strategic dimension of university information system requirements is therefore one of outward rather than inward focus. Nearly all sources of conflict and uncertainty for universities are located outside universities themselves. These include government policy on funding, student numbers, institutional rankings, and priorities between teaching and research. So universities’ information needs are largely uncontrollable.

Despite this outward orientation, university information sets and systems have in the past been relatively incompatible. Administrative computing in UK universities has depended upon local customisation of commercial systems as well as on internally developed software. These systems have mostly reflected the particular local institutional structures and information requirements, leading to a proliferation of systems containing noncomparable data in incompatible formats.

## MAC

In 1988 the UK University Grants Committee (UGC), which had responsibility for distributing government finance to all higher education institutions, decided to fund a Management and Administrative Computing (MAC) Initiative for developing management and administrative software for use in UK universities. Its objective was to make data from different universities comparable in order to help it in allocating resources between universities (Goddard, 1994). At that time there was little common software used, and considerable difficulties were expected in aligning the new system and each university’s existing procedures. The way in which that adaptation took place provides an interesting insight into the complexity and difficulty of the adaptation process, and the consummate political skills required. The aim was to encourage institutions to develop common administrative information systems and in particular to develop the capability to provide the UGC with information better suited to its role as resource allocator, and therefore to enable it to reward particular institutions which were performing well according to UGC criteria.

From the beginning the method chosen was the creation of common software managed as one large project. This choice was not an obvious one. There were other possibilities, in particular, the use of centrally-created specifications such as minimum data sets (descriptions of information which all institutions should ensure they collected and made available to the UGC). Such an approach would have avoided the need for one, large and risky software project designed to suit all institutions, and would still have provided comparable information to a central office.

This approach was adopted by both the UK police service (Sillince & Frost, 1995) and National Health Service (Sillince & Frost, 1994a, 1994b). These two government functions were much more complex than the university system and this fortunately preserved them from the simple-minded ‘one system’ approach of the MAC Initiative. Also, the centralising motive (to provide direction of teaching and research quality assessment) was much more evident in the case of universities. The police and health organizations were more independentminded, more suspicious of central government interference, more pluralistic and more overtly political (both in the sense of priority on the party-political agenda and in terms of formal political representation). Also, these two services required, for operational effectiveness, significant quantities of data to be seamlessly transferred between the many suborganizations around the country. In contrast, the universities did not need to swap much data between themselves.

## Northern University

Although the MAC Initiative serves nearly all major UK universities, only one has been studied as the user organization, and it will be referred to as Northern University. This university’s previous administrative system was a centralised one, controlled by users in the Administrative Central Office, enabling the academic departments little or no access. A number of systems had been developed centrally, including systems for finance, student records, personnel records, and planning. New applications were planned for room booking, research grant costing and postgraduate admissions. Users included three groups: a small group of top executives, the technical staff of the IS Department, and the clerical support staff at the Administrative Central Office.

By 1988 the UGC had decided on a plan of development, and offered all higher education institutions the opportunity to join the project with the initial expense borne by the UGC. Eventually nearly all major UK universities, including Northern University in July 1988, decided to opt into the collaboration.

Joining the MAC Initiative has a political dimension. [The senior administrative team at Northern University] felt that the UGC wanted Northern University’s co-operation, and we could not say no. (Academic Registrar)

‘Political’, to this informant, meant that there was a commitment to being supportive on the part of Northern University, rather than there being any genuine feeling that MAC would answer a particular need. His meaning should not be confused with the UGC’s hidden agenda and centralising bias.

One essential reason for joining the MAC Initiative was political. The [UGC] wanted the co-operation of all universities and Northern University felt pressured to join. (Director of IS)

## Methodology

The present study involved a longitudinal case study approach to investigate the work of systems analysts and users within Northern University. This necessitates an interpretive method (Walsham, 1993), which does not accept the everyday world at face value but rather attempts to make explicit how it is perceived by the actors. Consequently, this research aims to understand IS as social systems. Different groups such as analysts, users and managers may have different frames of meanings, depending upon their social positions, historical circumstances and interests. This kind of research facilitates an analysis of different actors, their interpretations of the new technology and their associated actions. Time is captured through the combination of retrospective and real time analysis. Putting the researcher in close touch with the users, the systems analysts and their total set of concerns, this type of study is appropriate for documenting these events and their effects on system development. It reveals how unexpected events outside the scope of system development and the interactions of the parties involved affect the process.

The researchers were not driven into the field with neatly labelled and rigidly constructed sets of hypotheses. Such an approach would have been inappropriate, given that little is known about the analyst’s work which forms the basis for the study. Relevant questions were formulated before the field work began and further areas of concern emerged during the process of the field work. The use of longitudinal case studies serves the following two important research objectives.

1. Improved measurement validity and alternative interpretations of events may be described in a more valid manner when data are collected as they occur. The reader should be able to follow the derivation of any evidence from the initial research question to the conclusion of the study (Yin, 1984). The chain of evidence improves data reliability. For example, with longitudinal research methods one can show: the extent of change in user perceptions during system development, the analyst’s response to these changes, and the effect of these two factors on the outcome.

2. Longitudinal study makes it easier to collect a variety of subjective interpretations from actors involved in the development process. The respondent’s interpretations of events may be collected as they occur, and thus reflect the meaning provided by the different actors as opposed to the researcher’s preconceived notions (Franz & Robey, 1984). Actors’ impressions are secured as the events unfold, rather than after the system is developed.

The adoption of a longitudinal case study approach entailed the use of several data collection methods. From the combination of such methods, a rich set of data surrounding the specific research issue was obtained, as well as capturing its contextual complexity (Kaplan & Duchon, 1988). Furthermore, the use of multiple data sources helped overcome the various threats to validity posed by each data-gathering instrument (Yin, 1984; Bonoma, 1985; Benbasat et al, 1987). Interviews and documents, as well as observation, were included for this reason. Data were collected from primary sources (e.g. users and systems analysts), and interview data were checked with documentary material. Interviews are normally the primary data source for interpretative case studies as they provide access to the interpretations of participants regarding actions and events (Walsham, 1995). Thus we used the principle of triangulation, described by Denzin (1978) as “the combination of methodologies in the study of the same phenomenon”. Triangulation may be used not only to examine the same phenomenon from multiple perspectives, but also to enrich our understanding by allowing new or deeper dimensions to emerge (Jick, 1979; Bonoma, 1985; Kaplan & Duchon, 1988; Stake, 1994). Its effectiveness rests on the premise that the weakness in each single method is compensated for by the counter-balancing strengths of another. Data collection was confined to semi-structured interviews with Northern University staff and to documentary records.

## Case study

The MAC Initiative Management Team was created in 1988 by the UGC to supervise the MAC software project. Each university had a representative on this body; for example, Northern University’s Academic Secretary was a member. A prominent firm of management consultants were appointed to undertake first stage, high level requirements analysis. They used joint application development to elicit a detailed view of information needs common to all universities. This involved representatives from five universities reading a ‘rough cut’ data model for different university functions such as finance and student records before each meeting. The management team were also given copies of each model and asked to pass their comments to the consultant. This process involved the participation of some 140 senior academic and technical staff from the five universities chosen, together with a team from the consultancy firm, plus representatives from the Department of Education and Science, and from University Statistical Records, a central archive organization. An information specification of current requirements, or blueprint, was produced in 1989. This was a general 21-page guide to the future system together with a timetable and explanation of the six proposed applications, plus a 250-page volume containing requirements lists, data model, data dictionary, data store, and entity cross-reference table.

There was also another process of ‘migration’, aimed at discovering how the requirements could be achieved. Universities were to be allowed to choose to which of several software ‘families’ (each ‘family’ was eventually identified with a software development company) they wished to belong. The UGC made additional funds available for each university to seek advice from an ‘independent’ consultant. Each consultant was supposed to study his university’s existing system and recommend a customisation plan based on his reading of the blueprint. This had to be summarised and passed back to the MAC Initiative Management Team together with summary forms describing key details of each university’s present system priorities and facilities. Therefore, although each university could choose its own consultant, the main direction was by means of the blueprint and the overall supervision was by the MAC Initiative Management Team. The ambivalence was intended, presumably, to give a sense of ‘ownership’ at the grassroots, while at the same time serving the UGC’s need for central control.

The Administrative Central Office of Northern University regarded the migration strategy exercise as both confusing and artificial. The process was derived from multi-attribute utility theory, a method for scoring attributes of alternatives in such a way as to guide eventual selection of one preferred option. The Director of Personnel said:

It was unrealistic, cumbersome, unclear, repetitive, and it forced me to use an artificial scientific method. First, I had to complete a matrix to determine the priorities of each requirement within each sub-system and also between subareas. I had to give a numbered value for each item, which was a really difficult task. . . You see, some requirements cannot be quantified. The matrix was supposed to assist us with the completion of special forms given to us. . .In addition, I was pressured to finish completing the forms in a specific time. I spent three full weekends trying to finish them.

When this migration strategy was completed in August 1989, the management team hired a National Computer Council consultant to study migration strategies. He agreed that universities should be grouped into ‘families’, but argued in favour of similar software systems rather than similar user requirements. This was based on the argument that most universities wanted fourth generation languages enabling the widespread use of software by relatively untrained administrative staff, so that the most logical grouping principle would be what software was available (rather than what was required). This meant that the eventual system would be chosen based on usability rather than functionality criteria. Understandably the technical staff objected to this. For example, Northern University’s Director of IS commented on the NCC consultant’s work:

His analysis was very general. He was mostly interested in finding a logical way for grouping universities.

Individual universities were advised by their consultant according to their existing hardware systems (something easy to uncover), rather than their requirements as users (a far more difficult thing to find out). In Northern University’s case, little direction was given by the consultant on data, processes, interface, and management reporting, and the need for greater decentralisation and user friendliness were ignored. Northern University’s Director of IS commented:

The consultant’s recommendation was based mainly on the idea that joining Powerhouse would allow Northern University to retain its existing hardware whilst enabling it to join other universities that have already upgraded. . .In short, this recommendation was too shallow. It did not reflect Northern University’s requirements.

Development of the system was to be done by four companies. However, the majority of universities chose the same software developer because of its large and sophisticated database (thus forming a development family) (Goddard, 1994). Northern University, after some months’ delay, decided to ignore its consultant’s advice and follow its IS Department’s advice and opt for this software developer too.

A contract was signed between the software developer and Delphic Ltd, an umbrella company set up to represent the universities’ interests in converting the blueprint into a data dictionary. This ignored the migration strategies of the individual universities for customising the blueprint, which turned out to have been futile. This was a major deficiency of the consultation process, because many individual universities’ requirements were overlooked in the broad-brush approach of the blueprint. For example, although Northern University considered postgraduate student records to be a priority, this requirement was not in the blueprint.

The system developer did not encourage user participation. As the design process continued, little information was transmitted by the developer back to the universities. The blueprint was the basis for user requirements, so that designers did not feel the need to consult users directly. The contract stated that delivery would be within three years, by the end of August 1993. By January 1993 the software developer announced that a number of sub-applications would form a ‘basket’ and these baskets would be delivered one after the other, rather than all at the same time. The contract was altered to give delivery in early 1996. The delays added to the lack of consultation in the minds of critics. The Higher Education Funding Council for England (HEFCE), the UGC’s successor body, commented:

Some of the apparent slippage results from over-optimistic expectations and promises made in the early stages of the project. These occurred partly from initial under-estimation of the complexity of the task and have fuelled some of the criticism levelled at the Initiative.

This frustration resulted in an independent body, the International Centre for Higher Education Management, being asked by the UGC to appoint a consultant to review the work by the software developer and consider the universities’ criticisms. However, ironically, the consultant appointed was from the original consultancy whose lack of consultation had produced much of the frustration.

## Finance application

The application group suggested several new requirements for finance in addition to those identified at the beginning of the design phase. Indeed, it produced three wish lists during the design phase, many requirements emerging after several testing sessions had been conducted. These new requirements resulted from external bodies such as HEFCE needing extra information on finance, the creation of new internal university departments such as an Internal Audit, and the modularisation of courses into 12-week semesters.

As an example, Northern’s Systems Accountant complained that the system was too complex, documentation was non-existent, screen handling was counter-intuitive, and there was no standardisation of key strokes. Of four testing sessions, two had failed to satisfy users. These were nominal account suspension (here there was a serious problem with security, and the quality of screen messages and screen navigation was poor) and transaction exception reporting (in which requirements had been completely misunderstood). Moreover, these testing sessions represented an incomplete test schedule.

Requirements continued to change, and users continued to be dissatisfied during tests. The software developer therefore decided that no more testing meetings were to be held and that a Beta system of the finance basket would be delivered to Northern University instead. A Beta system is a working version in which any bugs or other problems should be reported back to the developer for subsequent system upgrades. The Systems Accountant at Northern University commented:

Getting a Beta system means getting a system without the bugs being removed from it. Nobody knows what would be delivered and how correctness would be accomplished. The system is no longer to be delivered [in the way that was] originally decided.

After six months the Systems Accountant attended a final testing session of the whole finance application. Many bugs were discovered; certain sections worked in a different way from the way intended, the style of management information was poor, and some data presentations were misleading. Her comment was:

It did not go well. There were lots of problems and many complaints. Some bits of the system were missing, while other sections did not perform the work properly. I believe the main reason for this was that the software developer combined MAC Finance Application with its own Accounting System and produced a new system. This meant that the application group has no control over the development of the finance application as it did before.

Only half the finance application had been delivered to Northern University by July 1994. It was not efficient for document processing (e.g. ledger and invoicing), it was not user-friendly, and it was not good at answering queries or producing reports. These problems made the university start to consider withdrawing from the finance application and making separate arrangements outside of the MAC Initiative. In April 1995, Northern University went back to its original (pre-MAC Initiative) software supplier, and bought another finance application.

## Management information system (MIS)

A project manager had been appointed by Northern for the implementation of the MAC Initiative, responsible for overseeing the project timescales, the technical implementation process, and the tasks performed by different application groups within the university. This project manager represented Northern University on the MIS application group. During 1992, after several meetings of this group, the project manager became increasingly unhappy with its progress. By the summer of 1993, an acceptance test of what had already been tested failed, due to bugs and the system’s inability to meet user requirements. In April 1994 a test of about 70% of the MIS application was carried out, and the application group declared itself satisfied. An acceptance test for the whole MIS system was planned for 1995 but was cancelled along with any further testing.

Despite the application group declaring itself satisfied, the project manager at Northern thought it would eventually prove to be useless, since in the testing many reports had been cut out due to lack of funding—resources were instead directed toward a student application:

The application group, under pressure from the Delphic family, had to cut about 50% of the MIS application. The main type of reports which were cut were the financial ones, mainly because the financial application developed by the software developer was too flexible to be used. The reason behind this cut was to provide funding for the students application which was rewritten by the software developer. (Project Manager)

Toolsets intended for the MIS application were found to be unsuitable. As a result, the MIS application group, at Delphic’s request, chose to use only 30–50% of the MIS application produced by the software developer, and to seek an external commercial package for the rest of the work. Delphic identified the commercial package most suitable for the universities.

## Personnel

The Personnel application was not a first tier application, so that Northern University was not entitled to test it formally:

The Personnel system is being implemented and tested by other universities. We have no say about what we do and do not need. After looking at a prototype of part of the system for three months, I had several requirements. However, I got the message that it is too late to change anything now. I really do not see any scope for changes. I believe this prototype system will be the final system. It is what we will get at the end. . .Our existing system is very user friendly, whereas MAC Initiative is not at all. I had to go through three screens to get to a personnel record, while in the current system I need only go through one screen. Also the response time is long. I need about 15 seconds response time on MAC Initiative, while it takes me less than four seconds on the current system. (Director of Personnel)

However, Northern University came to accept the new system’s imperfections:

It is a far more comprehensive system than the one we already have. However, there are some requirements which we want but are not included. This is expected since MAC Initiative will cover only 80% of our requirements as included from the beginning in the Blueprint. We expected to cover the remaining 20% of our requirements in-house. (Senior Assistant Personnel Officer)

Some features were superfluous. These included the production of organization charts, and the religion of staff. More serious was the difficulty in correcting mistakes, data fragmentation onto too many screens (for example, six screens for inputting data), poor screen layout, not enough continuity of ‘house-style’ in different modules, user-unfriendliness, and the impossibility of grouping people according to common characteristics. A typical response by early 1995, after 70% of the personnel application had been delivered, was voiced by the Senior Assistant Personnel Officer:

It is very cumbersome to navigate through different screens. I believe that there would be no major advantage of MAC over the current system. It is not going to make my life any easier.

## Physical resources

The new physical resources system had an unsatisfactory room bookings and timetabling module—something the existing system had provided for adequately. This criticism, together with a recommendation that the software developer change the application before final delivery, was expressed in letters from the Building Systems Officer to the Deputy Registrar, who chaired Northern University’s MAC Implementation Group. An extract from the second letter is as follows:

The conclusion, shared by all, is that the system falls way short of the existing [. . .] system in meeting our requirements. Were the systems to be implemented in their present form, the room bookings section would be inoperable, and the property register would be operable only with extreme difficulty, and with considerable additional operator time and supplementary manual records necessary. It is essential that the systems are modified before they are implemented.

Eventually the Delphic family of universities decided to buy a separate package produced by a different developer for room booking and timetabling. The Senior Assistant Registrar also investigated the application and agreed with the complaints. Initially, however, Northern University’s IS Department did not take the problem seriously, assuming (incorrectly as it happened) that other modules to be delivered later would attend to these criticisms. One reason for this, was that no explanatory material was included with delivered applications, so that the only written information was that in the 1989 blueprint. This was becoming (in 1995) increasingly out of date. Moreover, users complained it was too technical, long and complicated. Despite this, its discussion of requirements was too general for users to find useful. This increased the surprise of users when they discovered that applications, when they were at last delivered, were not what they really wanted. For example, the blueprint requirement which misled the Building Systems Officer to think that room bookings would be available was:

Provide a record of usage (i.e. hours or days per year etc) of certain assets such as rooms. (Vol II, p 178)

However, this does not excuse the developers, since in many cases requirements were clearly stated but were not achieved.

## Students

The structure of the student system was unclear, because it was unfriendly, it was not possible to create management reports, and it was difficult to navigate through screens. Customisation was needed for more than 20% of the application, covering areas such as screen presentation and reports. Other universities had similar problems:

I believe the system was written by people unfamiliar with the education environment and the structure of the educational system. Also, they were very technical people, which accounted for the sophisticated terminology used in the software. In short, the disappointment in the quality of the software received coupled with the slippage in the time frame made me lose confidence in the system. (Assistant Registrar)

So although he was disappointed, the Assistant Registrar believed Northern University had no choice but to use it. The student application was very specialised and no alternative commercial package existed. Also the existing system was very deficient in this area; unlike the MAC system it did not enable modular course structures to be expressed.

## Academic departments

Commitment to MAC within academic departments was to be increased by inviting ‘key users’ to take part in further planning. However, this participation was superficial. The first occasion that departments were invited to participate was in March 1993, in a letter from the Academic Secretary to Heads of Department. The condescension is almost tangible. The software developer’s non-participative stance towards universities is mirrored by Northern University top executives’ stance towards academic departments:

Although MAC Initiative has been under way for some considerable time, it has not been appropriate to write to you earlier because information about the new system is only becoming available at a sufficient level of not-too-technical detail. (Academic Secretary)

The letter is dated five years after the start of MAC. The benefits are stated in top–down terms, ignoring any possibility that a new system might have potentially different benefits for academic departments:

It is important for users throughout Northern University to share ownership of future software systems, and to avoid creating or maintaining duplicate records: consistency, reliability, and the avoidance of duplication are the key benefits to be realised. (Academic Secretary)

There was a hierarchy of condescension and information flow, of the form: Software Developer—University—Academic Department. A further hierarchical quality was demonstrated in a complaint about lack of information. Here is a complaint from the Academic Registrar:

We are kept very much in the dark and there is a feeling of fear and frustration among us because we do not know what is going on. I currently represent Northern University and from the meetings I have attended so far I have received little information about the progress of MAC. (Academic Secretary).

The University was just as much to blame by not providing for any meaningful participation by academic departments. Here is a complaint from one department:

Without knowing exactly what the perceived needs of other departments are, it is hard to say at this stage whether our needs will be regarded as special. . . (Chairman, Management School)

In fact it was in April 1994 (by which time four out of six baskets had already been delivered to Northern University) that the Deputy Secretary announced a survey of academic departments’ facilities and needs. The justification for the survey was to minimise disruption to the adoption of the MAC system rather than to take account of any departmental requirements.

## Discussion

Prior to the MAC Initiative, universities had poor IS with insufficient management attention devoted to them. Although investment in IS had been relatively high, the problems of developing common data sets and systems went beyond any one university’s responsibility or interest. Moreover, there was no critical success factor which depended upon good IS. Universities did not need good systems in order to survive. Survival depended on other factors—political parties’ agendas, parental support, economic activity being the main ones. Therefore, in general, universities did not have good systems, staff, processes, or technology. This should be borne in mind as a background dooming factor when evaluating the success of MAC.

A consideration of the political nature of the process reveals the software developer’s use of ‘strategies of outflanking’ (Jermier et al, 1994, p 289) such as integration and division. Division or ‘distributive bargaining’, is the treatment of political entities as if they are playing a zero sum game. Integration, on the other hand, or ‘integrative bargaining’ is the treatment of political entities as if they were playing a positive sum game (Davies, 1986).

Political entities attempt to increase their power by division (of their opponents) or by integration (with their allies). An example of division is when an issue is stated in such a way that it divides the opposition into two mutually opposed camps, so that one’s own allies are left as the largest body of opinion. An example of integration is the building of a coalition. The MAC developer used both division and integration as powerenhancing methods. Therefore, despite evidence that managerial strategies in the form of document-based plans for new technology adoption are lacking (Rothwell, 1984; Marsden et al, 1985; Rose & Jones, 1985; Clegg & Kemp, 1986; Love & Walker, 1986; Shaw, 1986), the case shows that the politics of new technology adoption may be a very different matter and that political strategies are actively used.

## Division

The MAC developer’s approach to users was divisive to the extent that several different user groups were identified and dealt with separately. This involved the developer providing distinct things for each user group. The MAC Initiative was constrained by many elements of political uncertainty including government decisions about spending levels in higher education, whether to increase or decrease differences between ‘old’ researchbased and ‘new’ teaching-based institutions, and about how much government direction would occur in the future. The software developer had not only this uncertainty to contend with but also a university system which contained discrete parts, each with its own objectives. For example, at Northern University, there were four user groups—senior managers, the Administrative Central Office, the IS Department, and academic departments. Each group had its own interests, which was a source of weakness which the software developer played on in his dealings with Northern University.

The first user group to get involved, in 1988, was senior management, which wanted to get something for free, and also wanted to be included in important future government allocation decisions. The blueprint as a text represented an unwritten agreement between senior management and developers that such a set of requirements common to all UK universities was feasible. Initially favourably inclined, they became increasingly sceptical about the potential benefits of MAC to Northern University.

The IS Department made the critical decision to select the system developer in 1989. They accepted the blueprint as long as it was made relevant to Northern University by means of the migration strategy. They wanted maximum software functionality, and so were attracted by the size and sophistication of the software developer’s database. They became disillusioned when they discovered that the customisation implied in the migration strategy would not take place. They had sold the idea of customisation to the less enthusiastic senior management but they became compromised and thereafter had an air of resigned detachment about MAC.

The Administrative Central Office became involved between 1993 and 1995 as MAC applications were delivered and was involved in the testing process. Its wish was to have a useable system.

Finally, in 1995, end-users in academic departments became involved, but in a much less intensive or influential way. Getting academic departments involved had been devolved by the software developer onto Northern University itself and so the software developer had no direct dealings with academic departments. The patronising and undemocratic way that universities were treated by the software developer exactly mirrored the way that senior managers at Northern University treated their own academic departments.

There was therefore a clearly developed series of stages, beginning with the UGC as sponsor, then moving on to university senior managers, then to university IS departments, then to university central administrators, and finally to academic departments. The sheer size of the MAC Initiative required an orderly transition with users through the software development life cycle. Because user organizations comprise competing subunits, software developers can use them as a means of increasing their power. They can choose to deal with subunits separately, targeting benefits to the specific user group to much greater effect.

This compartmentalised approach is vulnerable to disruption when political conflict between subunits exists. Although there is no evidence of it in this case, Walsham (1993, p 83) and Knights and Murray (1994, p 137) have both observed the dislocating effects of political conflict (usually between departments of the same organization) on IS projects.

Another consequence of giving each user group a view of only one life cycle stage is that it becomes easier to make exaggerated claims about the eventual benefits of the system, and to hide the technical difficulties involved. Under such conditions, the initial sponsorship decision is made by someone within the user organization who promptly ceases to have any interest in the project. Oz (1994) found that one of the chief reasons for failure of the system he studied was misleading statements about the technical limitations of their system by the software professionals. Another project whose technical feasibility went unquestioned was TAURUS, the project to computerise share dealings at the London Stock Exchange. Estimated cost savings were initially given greater weight than technical feasibility, yet it was the latter which eventually caused the project’s abandonment (Currie, 1994).

## Integration

The main attempt to integrate opinion between the many participating universities was firstly the blueprint itself and secondly the migration strategy between the blueprint and the individual universities’ requirements. The process required users to score alternatives on various requirements to arrive at a ‘preferred’ alternative. Despite its mathematical and artificial character, noted by one participant above, the method had the advantage of neutrality. Users in the case study regarded the integration attempted as unsatisfactory, principally because it did not lead to customisation.

As mentioned above, political actors attempt to increase their power by division (of their opponents) or by integration (with their allies). In the MAC case, division and integration were both used. Why was this? Division was used in the part of the IS development process where the MAC developer wanted to keep users weak—the definition of problems and the arousal of social interests—in order to reduce the scale of demands (too many problems and too high a state of arousal would have been unmanageable). Integration was used in that part of the process where the MAC developer wanted consensus—the enrolment of agencies and actors and the mobilisation of support—in order to produce closure and decision. This suggests the following hypothesis:

Division and integration are derived from very different notions of power. Division is used when politics is regarded as a zero sum game of win or lose. Integration when it is chosen as a political strategy is not processual, nor is it a voluntary opting into a positive sum situation, but rather it is the manipulation of consensual-seeming language to generate closure, decision and finality. Integration increases the power of the language manipulator and is therefore more relevant to the socially shaped power perspective. Table 2 presents the evaluation of the case study material against the five power perceptives.

In terms of a view of the case as a game, the divisive strategy by the MAC developer is evidence of its zero sum power perspective, but this is contradicted by the fact that all universities could gain something from MAC, which is evidence of their positive sum, processual perspective. On the other hand an organizational power perspective reveals that MAC adoption was definitely part of a top–down, senior management policy within Northern. The socially shaped perspective shows that a change of definition occurred during the case, because whereas initially universities considered it more important to be independent from government control (so that lack of comparability of different universities’ data was not a problem), their wish to influence the central funding allocation decisions caused them to change this opinion. Thus we have taken four different views of games derived from the perspectives introduced above. These games are not inconsistent with each other. Although the MAC developer attempted to be divisive, universities themselves felt they were gaining. This led to the universities’ top managements supporting and pushing MAC within their institutions, and also led to a change of attitude by universities from an initially hostile to a friendly one.

In terms of reality construction, the zero sum perspective presents the UGC’s acquiring of knowledge as a divisive means of gaining power, but the processual perspective suggests that universities themselves constructed a positive sum reality based on access to a central government funding process. This is an example of the fact that it is possible for different actors in the same situation to have inconsistent cognitions for understandable reasons.

Metaphor is relevant in all perspectives. Independence of universities is the power ‘object’ in the zero sum perspective. The ability of the UGC to get universities to impart comparable information is the power ‘force’ in the processual perspective. The MAC developer’s network of relationships with user groups is the power ‘web’ in the organizational perspective. The UGC’s goal of directing universities is the reference for ‘control’ in the structurally constrained perspective. And the ‘subjective nature of power’ is the effect of participation in creating the universities’ illusion of having power in the socially shaped perspective.

The locus of power has a hierarchical interpretation in three of the perspectives. The organizational perspective suggests that the user groups at Northern University varied in power according to organizational structural variables such as differentiation (favouring top management) and expertise (favouring the IS Department). The structurally constrained power perspective suggests that several actors including the UGC, Northern University’s top management, and the MAC developer all determined some of the actions of others. The socially shaped power perspective suggests that hierarchical perceptions mirrored or reinforced one another, as the condescending treatment by the MAC developer of Northern University was mirrored in the way Northern University’s top management treated its acedemic departments.

The power ascribed to information, or the locus of knowledge, shows various interpretations. The UGC regarded information as a means of achieving its end of improving quality in universities. The organizational power perspective suggests that MAC was viewed by Northern University as making better use of information. The structurally constrained perspective suggests that information available to the MAC developer depended very much on the sequence in which he dealt with Northern University’s user groups, and so this sequence had an important effect on the power of user groups to influence the MAC developer. Eventually, however, Northern University staff realised that MAC was not automatically better than their own in-house systems.

Consideration of roles suggests that the UGC had many roles, that consultants had important boundaryspanning roles (bridging between the universities and the MAC developer) and that departmental key users had an important insider role.

The relevance to IS was interpreted differently according to the perspective adopted, revealing that IS was a prize (the universities would get a free system), a negotiating focus (attempts to alter the blueprint), an instrument (of speedup and format standardisation), and a closure mechanism (by using the blueprint and migration strategy to increase acceptance).

Evidence of power was manifested as conflict in the zero sum perspective (university staff felt resentment against the MAC developer), as individual influence in the processual power perspective (the top management’s decision to join MAC), as ideological influence in the organizational power perspective (academic departments did not make demands, considering themselves separate from the administrative system), and as closure by the socially shaped power perspective (the UGC getting the universities signed up for MAC).

On the eight dimensions of Table 2, the behaviour of the three main actors (Northern University, the UGC, and the MAC developer) can be understood in terms of many inconsistent interpretations (ranging from a minimum of two (reality construction) to five (metaphors and roles)). In the face of such multiple interpretations it is surprising how political events can be understood in any coherent way by different actors. One, potentially deterministic, approach to answering this question is to regard social and organizational structures as the result of conflicting political interests, giving rise to instabilities and inconsistent understandings of what those structures mean (Tickell & Peck, 1995). Within organizations, the problem is exemplified by reward systems which reward conflicting behaviours such as social work versus crime busting in police departments, and product versus function job descriptions in matrix organizations (Boettger & Greer, 1994).

Another less deterministic approach takes the view that multiple perspectives have cognitive advantages to political actors seeking optimal payoffs in the presence of competition and uncertainty. Taking multiple perspectives (enjoying the benefit of others’ perspectives) enables political actors to become aware of the effects of their activities on their interests (Orlie, 1994). For example, multiple perspectives generate politically feasible ways of resolving problems (Eden, 1994). We suggest that actors themselves carry multiple perspectives of events and switch between them in order to empathise with others, and it is this perspective switching which enables coherent behaviour. One advantage of this ability to switch perspectives is shown by the software developer’s ability to switch from the zero sum perspective (the divide and rule strategy—treating Northern’s top executives, the IS Department, and the Administrative Central Office separately, see Table 2, column 1, row 1) to the socially shaped perspective on power (the integration strategy—using the migration strategy as a means of persuading universities that their requirements were being met, see Table 2, last column, last row), as a means of managing the adaptation process. In the earlier stages this enabled the software developer to avoid a situation during requirements determination of a unified user organization making irresistible demands. In the later stages it also enabled the software developer to effect closure of the project by using devices which encouraged consensus.

Another example is provided by the way in which universities were aware of UGC zero sum motives (Table 2, column 1, row 2) and yet were also able to perceive advantages to themselves using the processual power perspective (Table 2, column 2, row 2).

A further example is provided by the way in which universities used zero sum arguments to see themselves as gainers (getting free information systems) but also developed the processual power perspective to see themselves as possible winners of an attempt to alter the blueprint (Table 2, columns 1 and 2, row 7).

## Conclusion

The practical implications of the study centre around the use of division and integration as political strategies by software developers. Division involves the separate treatment of user groups within the same user organization. Integration involves the making of hollow promises of adaptability of the global system to local needs. User organizations seeking to counter such strategies should create mechanisms for dealing with them. Division requires user organizations to create means of coordinating user groups. Integration requires means of ensuring in a written contract that customisability meets local needs and that migration to local requirements is sincere and effective.

The academic implication of the study is that often there is no one single interpretation because not only do multiple, conflicting interpretations exist but also because participants find it useful to employ different ones at different times. Moreover, although the five perspectives become progressively more explanatory (from zero sum through to social shaping) their usefulness as guides to action declines.
