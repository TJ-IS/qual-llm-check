# Self assessment as a means of measuring strategic and operational benefits from EDI: the development of a conceptual framework

C Fearon and G Philip

Management and Information Systems Division, Queen’s School of Management, The Queen’s University of Belfast, Belfast BT7 1NN, UK

This paper evaluates the benefit success associated with Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) from nine leading companies in Northern Ireland through a process of self assessment. The procedure involves a comparison of pre-implementation benefit expectations with post-implementation perceptions of realised benefits. Three ‘benefit states’ are used to denote the degree of the gap between expectations and perceptions of realised benefits. If a shortfall or negative gap is experienced throughout a company as a whole, then this benefit state is described as a ‘deficiency’. If there is no gap and a company receives what it expected then this benefit state is termed ‘neutrality’. If the perceived realised benefit, on the other hand, exceeded the original benefit expectations, then this benefit state is regarded as ‘efficiency’. Assessment results from a benefit rating instrument and personal interviews indicate that ‘deficiency’ is the benefit state which is prevalent for most companies. A ‘benefit success matrix’ is used to illustrate the relationship between: benefit states, implementation success and the implementation approach. The application of benefit states to the benefit success matrix can be used as a retrospective self evaluation tool for companies to consider the mix of factors required for achieving successful EDI development.

## Introduction

Whilst there has been case study research illustrating the long term strategic benefits of Electronic Data Interchange (EDI), many authors argue that these benefits have been traditionally very hard to measure, and the implications for their strategic use are rather unclear (Dearing, 1990; Holland et al, 1992). Furthermore, the introduction of EDI can be delayed because of the difficulties associated with justifying cost over achieving sometimes intangible benefits (Philip & Pedersen, 1997), and because of the opinion that it will be the facilitator or customer in the supply chain network that will receive most benefits (Scala & McGrath, 1993; Janssens & Cuyvers, 1993). For the prospective adopter, there is also an additional difficulty in finding a mechanism to assess the potential benefits of EDI before the actual implementation (Galliers et al, 1995).

Benefits from EDI have been classified as strategic and operational (Reekers & Smithson, 1994). The former relate to the development of corporate strategies through the building of external relationships with customers and competitors, the latter refer to improvements made to the internal functioning of the organization apparent in everyday activities (Benjamin et al, 1990; Banerjee &

Golhar, 1992; Reekers & Smithson, 1994). Johnston and Carrico’s three-stage model (1988) illustrates the evolutionary nature of benefit achievement from EDI for most companies. The first stage, ‘traditional’, is involved with technology used to support operations. The second stage, ‘evolving’ sees EDI supporting the business strategy, such as supporting a Just-In-Time manufacturing system, and the third ‘integrated’ stage notes how a business strategy would be totally aligned and heavily dependent on the use of EDI technology (Reekers & Smithson, 1994).

There is debate over the extent to which EDI is either: (i) the enabler of the major strategic benefit, ‘competitive advantage’ or; (ii) merely an industry norm which is necessary in today’s modern trading environment. (Bakos, 1991; Clemons & Row, 1991a; Booth & Philip, 1996). It has been argued that the competitive advantage potential of inter-organizational systems and EDI is related to the level of strategic benefits ensuing from: a reduction in cost associated with collapsing the value chain; and the ability to differentiate products and service capability (Porter & Millar, 1985; Fiedler et al, 1995).

In order to sustain these strategic benefits from EDI, it is advocated that current adversarial methods of competition need to be replaced in favour of longer term collaborative trading practices. It is suggested that these changes in the nature of competition will facilitate provision of benefits for all parties in the EDI trading relationship (Benjamin et al, 1990; Holland & Lockett, 1995; Riggins & Mukhopadhyay, 1994). The changes in the nature of competition are being driven by a need to organize sophisticated logistics chains in response to rapidly changing customer requirements (Garstone, 1995); and to create value added linkages among organizations in a complex global trading environment (Johnston & Lawrence, 1988; Trauth & Thomas, 1993). Unfortunately, there are severe limitations to the applicability of EDI and inter-firm integration in many instances, due to profound differences in the objectives of different parties in the supply chain that inhibit information sharing (Clemons & Row, 1993).

There are also conditions under which EDI enhances inter-firm partnerships, leading to long-term trading relationships that may confer competitive advantage (Bakos & Brynjolfsson, 1993; Clemons & Row, 1991b, 1992; Clemons et al, 1993). These include changing the internal processes of participating companies and progressing towards a fundamental rethink of how supply chain networks should be organised according to their underlying resource dependencies (Short & Venkatraman, 1992; Galliers et al, 1995; Reekers & Smithson, 1996). Nevertheless, critics would argue that despite the theory and speculation concerning benefits from EDI, there have been few benefit studies performed (Bergeron & Raymond, 1992). Therefore, claims of their existence should be treated with caution until further empirical research is performed (Reekers & Smithson, 1994). However, recent evidence from a sectoral analysis of UK industry (Cox & Ghoneim, 1996), found that integration of EDI systems with core business activities was a major factor in determining maximum benefit achievement and avoidance of implementation difficulties.

## Evaluating EDI benefit success: the need for a new perspective

Currently, there appears to be an absence of a rich, tactical understanding of how the theoretical benefits of strategic expectations from EDI can be fully operationalised (Massetti & Zmud, 1996). There is an assumption in the literature that ultimate EDI ‘benefit success’ always constitutes the achievement of a significant number of high realised strategic and operational benefits. However, this may not be the case for all companies. The problem with using high realised benefits as the sole measure of benefit success is that it ignores the benefit expectations companies had in mind before implementing EDI. A possible consequence within the EDI planning process is the absence of a mechanism for post-implementation evaluation and review of benefit success within each company (Galliers et al, 1995). Within the area of strategic information systems planning, few organizations have a complete management process to ensure proposed benefits will be realised. Most success criteria during postimplementation reviews are technical in nature, focusing on time and cost issues rather than assessing success or failure in achieving the desired benefit results (Ward et al, 1996).

The purpose of this paper is to argue that a comparison of post-implementation perceptions of realised benefits with pre-implementation benefit expectations can serve as a rough surrogate measure of evaluating EDI benefit success. ‘Benefit success’ can be regarded as a synergistic measure of: (a) the actual strategic and operational benefits a company has achieved; as well as (b) the ‘benefit state’ or extent of benefit planning gap experienced within each company. It is this study of ‘benefit states’ and their characteristics that the research paper will primarily focus upon. The ‘benefit state’ of a company can be defined as the extent of the planning gap between pre-implementation benefit expectations and post-implementation perceptions of realised benefits. If the benefit planning gap is negative then the company is described as experiencing a state of ‘deficiency (disconfirmation of expectations). If there is no benefit planning gap and a company realises the benefits it expected then this benefit state is termed ‘neutrality’ (confirmation of expectations). If the perceived realised benefit was greater than original benefit expectation, then this benefit state is regarded as one of ‘efficiency’ (exceeded expectations). These concepts and relationships are captured in Figures 1 and 2.

Within this framework, this paper addresses the following research questions:

1. Which benefit states are companies experiencing and why?

2. Have companies experienced problems with implementation success? If so, has this contributed to particular benefit states?

3. Which implementation methods were used to achieve benefit success?

4. Can the relationship between benefit states, implementation success, implementation methods and benefit success be conceptualised into an analytical framework?

These questions establish not only the benefit state each company was experiencing, but also elicit the reasons why participants believe this to be the case, including organizational and technical problems which affected benefit success. There is also an assessment of whether radical or incremental implementation approaches were favoured by the companies for achieving their benefit success. The final research question is addressed by conceptualising empirical and literature evidence in the form of a matrix, and evaluates how benefit states contribute holistically to our understanding of EDI development.

## Methodology

The sample for this study consisted of nine companies which were selected from Northern Ireland’s ‘Top 100 performing companies, as published in The Belfast Telegraph in January 1995. The selection criteria were similar to Cavaye and Cragg (1995), in that companies had to be profit oriented and systems had to be implemented over a given period of time. An accessible cross-industry sample was used to provide contrasting company profiles, in order to gain a broad explanatory insight into the factors contributing to benefit success. Both indigenous and multinationals were sampled to enable a study of SMEs (Small and Medium sized Enterprises) and large organizations so that the results would reflect the widest possible cross-section of EDI users. Informants were qualified to provide reliable responses because they were the most senior persons responsible for EDI in their organizations. In all cases, they provided demonstrable knowledge of technical and strategic issues concerning the implementation of EDI from its first adoption in their organization (See Table 1).

The collection of data employed two complementary methods: interviews and questionnaire. Initially, a series of nine semi-structured interviews were carried out with the aid of documented material. These interviews were designed to understand the reasons which contributed to benefit planning gaps. Personal interviewing is recognised as allowing a degree of flexibility and providing a richness of data for exploratory research (Babbie, 1992). The interview data was collected based on a qualitative inductive method recommended by Eisenhardt (1989) and focused on each of the research questions. In each interview, a reply to an individual question was probed further to elicit specific issues as the conversation progressed.

The information was then used in conjunction with a self assessment benefit rating instrument. The rating instrument was administered through a questionnaire, after the initial discourse had finished, so that the extent of the perceived benefit planning gap for individual benefits could be measured roughly. The questionnaire also collected background information about the organizations and the extent of EDI implementation. The data from the rating instrument is shown in the Appendix. To protect against bias, the self assessment data was interpreted and justified in the light of interviewee responses. This had the advantage of placing the benefit measurements from the self assessment ratings in context and increasing the instrumental validity of final results. Interviews, on average, lasted approximately one to one and a half hours and were supplemented with additional internal documents where appropriate. The transcripts of the interviews were analysed in the context of research questions. There is no attempt to provide statistical recognition to support the frameworks or generalisations made.

## Discussion of results

## Strategic and operational benefits

The strategic and operational benefits used for assessment are based on a review of the relevant EDI literature (Benjamin et al, 1990; Banerjee & Golhar, 1992; Reekers & Smithson, 1994). Each company was asked to assess their pre-implementation benefit expectations and post-implementation realised benefits under each of the eight items of strategic benefits, and ten items of operational benefits (see the Appendix for list of items). Depending on the nature of informants’ replies, each item was rated according to the following categories which depict the level of benefits ‘expected’ and benefits ‘realised’: None (N), Low (L), Medium (M), and High (H). Details of the results can be found in the Appendix. Generally, the ranking of the most important strategic benefits were as follows: improved company image; improved customer service; improved trading partnership, and increased competitive advantage. Similarly, components of the operational benefits received the following ranking: reduced paper documents; decreased administration costs and increased error reduction.

## Benefit states and the underlying reasons

As shown in Table 2 each company fell into one of the three benefit states and, in the following section we discuss the underlying reasons for experiencing the corresponding benefit states. Briefly, of the nine companies, only one (Company 5) was experiencing ‘efficiency’, three were in a state of ‘neutrality’ while the majority (five companies) were in the ‘deficiency’ state with their current implementations. Benefit states for ‘current implementations’ were derived for each company primarily from the self assessment data (see the Appendix), but also included interview evidence. Self assessment ratings gave an indication of the extent of benefit planning gap, but interview evidence helped place some of the readings in a better context. Past benefit states from ‘early implementations’ were interpreted solely from the informants’ interview responses. Although not immediately relevant, they serve to illustrate the migratory properties of benefit states over time, through subsequent EDI implementations.

## Reasons for neutrality

## Appropriate benefit targets

The IT manager for Company 1 noted that:

We didn’t aim too high, we got what we expected, especially with the reduction of paper documents and administration costs. . . The benefits we were looking for were: elimination of delays; decreased inventory costs; high reduction in paper documents and decreased administration costs.

No radical re-engineering was used in conjunction with EDI even though this was a factor recommended by consultants. The few targeted operational benefits that were initially expected had been achieved subsequently. This culminated in the cessation of orders being printed for manual processing and removed the need for clerical staff to be present in the computer rooms.

## Learning curve effect

Companies 6 and 7 believed that benefit deficiencies diminish over time and that a migration towards ‘neutrality’ occurs as experience with EDI increases through a learning curve (Figure 3). For example, a spokesperson for Company 7 noted that: “We are having teething problems at the moment but that will change over time”. The IT manager for Company 6 suggested that deficiency occurred only during early implementation and training. However, after a short time this dissipated as problems were “ironed out during the early years of operation”.

## Reasons for deficiency

## Dependency on third parties

For Company 2, the absence of the high expected level of operational benefits provoked a slight deficiency in the firm’s competitive advantage by slowing down the speed at which it can introduce new insurance products and amend the specification of existing products. The deficiency that occurred was blamed on unexpected discrepancies in the level of third-party network service quality; in terms of dealing with technical problems with EDI software, handling message standards and the inability to roll out new EDI insurance products quickly enough.

The company believed that the current deficiency was temporary and that all operational and strategic benefits will be fully realised in the near future through the advent of the ‘Polaris’ electronic trading initiative for the insurance industry.

## Loss of first mover advantage

For Company 3, deficiency was partly due to the process of the initial optimism from first mover advantages diminishing because of EDI becoming a strategic necessity and an industry norm.

## Artificially hyped benefit expectations

The IT manager in Company 4 stated the principal reason for deficiency as being:

Prior to installing EDI, you had all the expectations of what it could do for us. A lot of it came from the service provider who was very keen to point out the benefits of EDI, mainly to top executives of the company, who really did not have the technical knowledge to evaluate what they were being told and were really just going by the hype.

## Poor internal communication

The IT users in Company 4 were not happy because they were not consulted by management before EDI adoption. The IT department believed this decision affected the eventual benefit outcome for the worse.

## Poor alignment of EDI strategy

Company 4 placed great emphasis on devising a future ‘corporate strategy’ surrounding EDI. This would mean introducing centralised redesign of purchasing and billing as opposed to the ad hoc ‘local strategy’ currently in place for these functions at every one of the company’s sites.

## High environmental complexity

For Company 8, deficiency occurred because of the erratic nature of the re-ordering process within the fabrics industry. This meant that forecasting customer requirements was considered a very complex procedure to be managed effectively by EDI. Consequently, for Company 8 it was believed there would be difficulty in realising a large number of high operational benefits from EDI. Company 3 noted that frantic market conditions in the clothes manufacturing industry meant that creating stable ordering processes throughout the supply chain was difficult. In addition, trying to bring their own small retail outlets onto mainstream EDI would not be financially feasible. Hence, there was a prevailing belief that high strategic and operational benefits could never be achieved under such conditions.

## Reasons for efficiency

## Aggressive EDI proactivity

Company 5 originally experienced operational benefit deficiencies with EDI for a short time after their first system implementation, but they do not experience any now.

The reason for this apparent dichotomy according to the IT manager was that EDI had been originally viewed as “an expensive fax for orders”. He believed that “anyone who uses EDI just for printouts is not going to get any benefits from the order point of view”. In contrast, the company is now very proactive in recruiting new customers and suppliers via EDI.

In relation to EDI strategic planning, the comment was made:

We do see EDI as being a strategic thing that we need to do in the business. So we are trying to push it in every direction. . . Our business has changed and developed over the years and EDI has helped us to keep up with that.

## Partnership trading

It was the IT manager’s opinion (Company 5) that, “EDI should be both mutually beneficial to both the supplier and the customer, if it wasn’t, I don’t believe it would have progressed so much over the years”. Most of the strategic benefits are associated with attracting customers via EDI from the UK. In the future, the company expects to gain greater benefits by seeking additional EDI trading partners in Europe.

## Commitment to infrastructure investment

The system was considered very easy to use and technology investment was not an immediate problem. Rather, it was believed that the potential for business problems would occur “when trading partner demands suddenly increase exponentially” in a couple of years and the business had not planned for the change.

The company’s original problems (during early implementations) were due to a lack of technical skills and resources. While not immediately suffering from their effects now, they were still believed to represent an ever present danger.

## Benefit states and problems with implementation

Companies that experienced ‘deficiency’ also had problems with their implementation success. Conversely, the companies in a benefit state of ‘neutrality’ or ‘efficiency’ had no major problems with their implementation. The presence of implementation problems seem to be the major cause of ‘deficiency’. Existing literature categorises problems associated with EDI implementation according to whether they are organizational or technical in nature (Cox & Ghoneim, 1996).

Technical problems (Stafford-Jones, 1994; Galliers et al, 1995; Cox & Ghoneim, 1996).

Problems with the compatibility of multiple message standards, interconnectivity of modems were experienced by Company 3 and 6 which had a direct effect on their implementation success back in the late 1980s and early 1990s. These problems have dissipated as the technology progressed and the learning curve developed. An issue of greater significance, is the inability of companies to invest in strategies that facilitate internal diffusion through in-house integration of EDI messages (Company 9). Generally, integration and diffusion issues have had a major effect on the achievement of operational benefits. Company 8 provided an example of the trend towards seeking better system flexibility and internal diffusion with internal ‘databases’. It was already in the process of scrapping its legacy systems and investing in a networked PC-based solution which was believed to be the way forward for the company.

Organizational problems (Hansen & Hill, 1989; Emmelhainz, 1990; Scala & McGrath, 1993; Galliers et al, 1995)

These have an immediate impact on the realisation of operational benefits. For example, Companies 3, 4 and 9 still had staff sending faxes along with EDI messages for some time after EDI implementation because audit trails were partially eliminated. This problem has generally dissipated because trust in the new systems and procedures has increased over time through the provision of staff support and training. Other problems related to managerial attitudes towards EDI included: lack of top management support for EDI; poor EDI management education; and lack of awareness of strategic benefits (Companies 4 and 9).

The manner in which benefit states interact with other aspects of EDI implementation must also be considered an important aspect of evaluating benefit success. This type of investigation is necessary because it facilitates a comparison of how benefit success can be achieved for companies with contrasting buyer–seller exchange relationships (Cunningham & Tynan, 1993).

## Benefit success through ‘radical change’ or ‘incrementalism’

Venkatraman (1991) noted the importance of managers choosing an IT development path (or implementation approach) based on seeking cost efficiency (operational benefits) or enhancing capabilities (strategic and operational benefits). Therefore, it was no surprise that the implementation approaches varied among the participating companies in this research in accordance with their strategic objectives. The following discussion evaluates reasons for choosing development paths for EDI innovation based on two categories of implementation approach, ‘incrementalist’ and ‘radical change’ (Fiedler et al, 1995). These were applied to Venkatraman’s (1991) stage model of process innovation to incorporate major benefits associated with Drew (1994), as shown in Figure 4.

Most indigenous and multinational suppliers do not wholeheartedly believe in the feasibility of introducing radical change (as denoted by Davenport, 1993; Venkatraman, 1991, 1994) with major implementations which are associated with high benefit expectations (see Figure 4). The main reasons cited were the relatively high degree of cost, uncertainty and project risk involved (Farbey et al, 1995).

For example, Company 6 noted that they were testing the feasibility of redesigning their warehouse in order to achieve higher realised operational benefits. However, they had looked at this possibility before, only to reject it because of the cost and project risk involved. Company 5 also mentioned that it was willing to make major infrastructural changes spurred on by general management. Again, concern was expressed over the high amount of financial and internal resource commitment that would be required if and when exponential growth eventually takes place. Company 9 found that its poor EDI implementation success affected its plans for growth. It would try in the future to attract new customers trading via EDI but has no plans to invest in extra system functionality because of the project risk involved. Therefore, the smaller indigenous companies are taking a low risk and low cost approach to implementing EDI. This finding is similar to comments made by Sabherwal and Vijayasarathy (1994), in relation to medium-sized companies and their limited resource capability for developing inter-organizational systems.

This incrementalist view is possibly enhanced by the fact that multinational and indigenous suppliers believe that EDI is no longer a source of technological opportunity for competitive advantage. Rather, EDI is an interorganizational strategic necessity for suppliers in order to maintain interdependent partnership links with major customers as denoted by several researchers (Bakos, 1991; Keen, 1991; Webster, 1995; Booth & Philip, 1996). The ‘incrementalists’ appear to conform to Johnston and Carrico’s (1988) evolutionary method of achieving benefit success from EDI.

Other multinationals inclined towards a more radical development path. Company 4, a multinational supplier, attempted an EDI implementation that exhibited a high deficiency. One reason for this, according to their IT manager, was the company’s inability to push hard for a more extensive centralised redesign of their purchasing and billing strategy. The semi-radical or hybrid approach the company originally used failed because a more “corporate strategy” was required (Holland et al, 1992; Cox & Ghoneim, 1996) for better organizational control of an extensive implementation which stretched over a number of geographical site locations. ## Characteristics of the ‘benefit success’ matrix

The following evaluates the benefit success of the nine companies in the study by interpreting their ‘quadrant positions on the matrix based on the available evidence (Figure 5).

The matrix attempts to conceptualise empirical evidence for the relationships between benefit success, implementation success and implementation approach mentioned in the discussion. All companies using EDI are at some position on the matrix, depending on the alignment of EDI with their company’s strategic objectives (Cox & Ghoneim, 1996) and their attitude towards the significance of EDI as an enabling opportunity for competitive advantage from a network perspective (Cunningham & Tynan 1993; Fredriksson & Vilgon, 1996). The matrix itself is divided into left and right sides: the higher the position of the company on the left side (‘B’ quadrant) or the right side (‘C’ quadrant), then the better the company has been at achieving EDI benefit success.

## Right side of the matrix

The strategic objective sought from this side of the matrix is competitive advantage (upper right ‘C’ quadrant). This is very difficult to attain because of the high risk and high implementation success required from a single implementation. It is easy to understand why many companies would disregard this as a risky first time investment option when considering EDI adoption. This is similar to the ‘IS benefits evaluation ladder’ concept of Farbey et al (1995). This suggests that the later of the eight ‘rungs’ on the ladder reflect high potential degrees of business benefit enabled from the application of different hierarchies of information systems. However, the benefits associated with rungs 5, 6, 7 and 8 are at the expense of very high levels of management complexity, uncertainty and risk. Companies 2 and 7 typically reflect these characteristics which associated with the ‘C’ quadrant of the matrix. Although, not immediately apparent from Table 2, the goals of the insurance companies emerged from interviews as involving medium to long term competitive advantage, enabled through the use of electronic trading and EDI.

At present, the method of achieving this advantage is based on a higher degree of radical change through business network redesign. For example, the two insurance companies under study are participating members of a wider technological scramble for electronic trading enabled competitiveness, with the leaders eventually being able to control the bulk of the major insurance markets in Northern Ireland. The project risks involved with companies investing in EDI on the right side of the matrix are very high. If benefit deficiencies occur, they could be very severe, inevitably involving a loss of strategic advantage to competitors (resulting in a position in the lower right ‘D’ quadrant).

In a different industry context, this may have happened with multinational supplier Company 4. The company has had a poor implementation record, and was experiencing a benefit state of severe deficiency because of its failure to recognise the need to re-engineer its purchasing function and implement EDI under a corporate strategy (Holland et al, 1992). It attempted to combine a semi-radical and a low risk incremental approach to its first implementation of EDI, which in this instance proved to be a confused implementation approach.

It is also in the right side of the matrix that business network redesign and business scope redefinition are most likely to occur in association with direct migrations to Johnston and Carrico’s (1988) integrated stage of EDI maturity. The latter is likely to occur with insurance companies in the near future. This is evident from the changing nature of the business, enhanced value adding of insurance products/services and technologies which are re-inventing relationships with intermediaries in a bid for sustained competitiveness through closer binding of buyer–seller resources. These are similar to conditions cited for business scope redefinition by Drew (1994). Business network redesign could reflect the more ‘dynamic’ network perspective of some of the other companies as they evolve in the future (Fredriksson & Vilgon, 1996). For example, it could soon depict Company 5’s situation when exponential growth becomes a definite requirement for managing additional customers and suppliers in multiple buyer–seller trading relationships.

## Left side of the matrix

This depicts stage (lower left ‘A’ quadrant) and early stage two (upper left ‘B’ quadrant) in Johnston and Carrico’s evolutionary route to EDI maturity. In terms of benefit planning, the target ‘A’ quadrant would be characterised by low benefit expectations (mostly operational in nature) from each implementation (Company 8). Over time, this would typically involve a series of incremental improvements to the EDI infrastructure. The strategic risks are less in terms of losing competitive advantage, because it is not really an expected benefit anyway. Major strategic benefits are not expected, but operational benefits are. Therefore, deficiencies that occur would be in relation to operational benefits (Company 8; Company 9).

It is possible to reach the upper left quadrant, characterised by achieving strategic and operational benefits and facilitating a short term, medium competitive advantage. Reaching this quadrant could be possible if: (a) the implementation is highly successful and avoids major problems; (b) the system provides sufficient flexibility to cater for multiple trading partner requirements without major changes to internal business processes; (c) fragile first mover advantages occur because other companies have not yet realised the relative advantage of EDI as a technological opportunity (Company 5). However, first mover advantages dissipate after other companies jump on the bandwagon and the company’s position moves back towards the ‘A’ quadrant (Company 3).

## The line of benefit neutrality

This represents an ideal benefit state for an effective implementation approach. If a company plans well, then it should receive the benefit success it expects, taking into account feasible inter-organizational opportunities and internal resources it has available (Company 1; Company 6). If a company moves from left to right on the matrix (from incremental change to radical change), the planning process becomes more complex and, consequently, it becomes more difficult to achieve benefit success without some degree of benefit planning gap. Company 1, in the right upper part of ‘B’ quadrant is positioned on the line of ‘neutrality’. It is below the ‘point of intersection’, but cannot cost justify large scale re-engineering of internal processes associated with the right side of the matrix because the volume of business transactions with suppliers is too low to warrant major investment. This finding is similar to conclusions made by Reekers and Smithson (1996) who stated that benefits from raised profitability and enhanced competitiveness from EDI could only come through significant adaptation investments.

## Movement on the matrix

Movement from left to right is not pre-determined and many organizations may wish to stay on the left side of the matrix. The consequence is that they may never receive a high level of strategic benefit success that enables sustained competitive advantage.

## Conclusions

While this study was based on a sample of nine companies, it is possible to demonstrate the value of self assessment as a retrospective benefit evaluation method when applied to the benefit success matrix. Emmehainz (1990) noted that estimating the benefits of EDI is a very subjective exercise and these estimations are frequently challenged throughout the implementation, even more so than costs. The development of more holistic approaches is being called for across firms in order to understand the mix of factors required for successful EDI implementations (Fredriksson & Vilgon, 1996). There is an increased need for more appropriate benefit evaluation frameworks that make the vital link between strategy and implementation (Farbey et al, 1995). These are required because of the current poor quality of IT decision-making processes (Farbey et al, 1995) and an over-reliance on benefit frameworks which promote strategic awareness and understanding but do not relate to practice (Brady et al, 1992; Farbey et al, 1995). Therefore, it is hoped that the self assessment technique and application of the EDI benefit success matrix described here can be used by IT and business managers as a tool for retrospective evaluation of the benefits from EDI implementation and indeed all other information systems. Also, this framework can provide useful guidelines for those who are considering the introduction of EDI for the first time.
