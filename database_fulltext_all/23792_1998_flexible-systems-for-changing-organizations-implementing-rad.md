# Flexible systems for changing organizations: implementing RAD

T Jones<sup>1</sup> and SF King<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>KPMG, Leeds; <sup>2</sup>Leeds University Business School, ESS Building, University of Leeds, Leeds LS2 9JT, UK

Rapid application development (RAD) promises to deliver high quality applications quickly. But the approach relies on close partnerships between IT staff and business users which may not be easy to achieve in the current climate of rapidly fragmenting organizations and, like all major innovations, RAD implementation must be managed carefully over an extended time period in order to become fully instutionalised. In this paper RAD implementation at two large UK organizations is described. One has failed to institutionalise RAD, the other has succeeded. The key lessons from this work are that even basic RAD tools and techniques can deliver benefits; that RAD implementors need to be given time to learn through experience of use; that a permanent methods group can help to stimulate RAD learning and act as a linking mechanism between successive information system (IS) development projects; that a RAD framework should be prescriptive and that an education programme which explains both the role of the information technology (IT) specialist and the business user should be pursued vigorously.

## Introduction

Rapid application development (RAD), the delivery of high quality applications quickly, by highly committed small teams of users and information technology (IT) staff, is an issue of considerable interest to many organizations at present. The UK has pioneered the development and dissemination of RAD via the dynamic systems development method (DSDM) consortium of over 170 full-member organizations. DSDM attempts to do what its forerunner public-domain methodology, the Structured Systems Analysis and Design Methodology (SSADM) failed to do: deliver systems that the organization needs at the time when they are needed (Millington & Stapleton, 1995). Indeed, the traditional waterfall approach to IS development, with its emphasis on sequential activities, pre-defined detailed deliverables and a separation of users from IT specialists has been heavily criticised as delivering systems that are over budget, over schedule and not what the users need (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1995). Waterfall methods are often synonymous with large bureaucratic centralised IT departments (Galliers & Sutherland, 1991) and can exacerbate the ‘culture gap’ between IT staff and users (Grindley, 1995), whilst providing a ‘social defence’ for both sides via ‘a set of organizational rituals with the primary function of containing anxiety’ (Wastell, 1996, p 25). When combined with rapidly changing organizational and technological environments (Miles & Snow, 1986; Scott Morton, 1991; Baskerville & Smithson, 1995; Bettis & Hitt, 1995) the failings of the waterfall approach become a cause for concern and a stimulus for RAD.

Whilst RAD has been the subject of considerable practitioner interest for several years, there have been few detailed empirical studies of RAD implementation in the literature. This paper contributes a case study of RAD implementation in two large UK organizations, using a context-process model to explain why RAD appears to have been successfully institutionalised in one organization, but not in the other. The first part of the paper presents guidelines for RAD best practice and reviews some of the empirical research to date. This is followed by a detailed description of troubled RAD implementation at PowerCo. The third part of the paper compares the PowerCo experience with the best practice guidelines and with the experience of a successful user of RAD, The Insurance Business. In the final section, we step back from RAD practice and methodology as enacted in individual information system (IS) projects and examine the issues surrounding the institutionalisation of RAD in large organizations.

## What is RAD?

James Martin coined the phrase ‘rapid application development’ to describe the development of computer applications by small, close-knit teams of IT and user staff using advanced integrated computer-assisted software engineering (I-CASE) technology (Martin, 1991). According to Martin, a typical project should progress through four phases:

1. requirements planning;

2. user design;

3. construction;

## 4. cutover.

The first two phases are user-dominated with IT staff present to provide technical guidance. Users drive out their requirements in intensive workshops. IT staff use I-CASE tools to capture the requirements in the form of evolving prototypes showing the proposed user interface to the system. Each phase is ‘timeboxed’; i.e. a time limit is set and the required functionality prioritised. Functionality missing at the end of the timebox is postponed until the next iteration of the system. At the end of the user design phase, code is automatically generated from the high level design using the I-CASE toolset. Further refinement of the generated system takes place during the construction phase with the cutover phase seeing comprehensive testing, end-user training, ‘organizational changes’ and parallel running with the current system.

Small teams, close co-operation, high commitment, timeboxing and code generation are seen as essential for rapid development; with a key argument being that quality is also improved due to closer user involvement, bugfree code generation and the delivery of systems when they are needed and not ‘two years later’ when the business has most likely moved on. Costs fall because fewer staff are involved for a shorter period of time. Furthermore, RAD is not just appropriate for small systems; large systems can be built providing they are split into modules that can be delivered by small teams quickly. But RAD is not appropriate for software with minimal user interfacing requirements, and for the development of complex algorithms.

In contrast to Martin, the UK DSDM framework does not prescribe tools and techniques, but instead serves as a high-level project management framework for RAD. Version 2 of the DSDM method has nine guiding principles (DSDM, 1997):

1. Active user involvement.

2. DSDM teams must be empowered to make decisions.

3. The focus is on frequent delivery of products.

4. Fitness for business purpose is the essential criterion for acceptance of deliverables.

5. Iterative and incremental development is necessary to converge on an accurate business solution.

6. All changes during development are reversible.

7. Requirements are baselined at a high level.

8. Testing is integrated throughout the life cycle.

9. A collaborative and co-operative approach between all stakeholders is essential.

## RAD experience to date

There have been very few detailed studies of RAD practice. Published work indicates both successes and concerns.

## RAD benefits

Apart from Martin’s (1991) list of RAD benefits (presented earlier) further evidence of RAD ‘success’ includes the rapid growth of DSDM membership in the UK from some 300 full and associate members in 1995 to over 700 in 1996; and Jacques (1992) reports that research at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) has revealed productivity increases as great as 1200% over the traditional COBOL environment with corresponding savings in cost and coding errors together with a closer match to user requirements.

## RAD concerns

But RAD is not without its critics. Ljubic and Stefancic (1994) state that success with RAD depends not only on the capabilities of IS professionals, but also on the knowledge and commitment of the users. There is no doubt that RAD requires teamwork, but people tend not to know how to behave in teams or how to lead them. Ljubic and Stefancic identified the following problems which RAD teams may experience, and which influence RAD implementation success.

## 1. General problems:

• management doesn’t recognise the importance of IS to the organization;

• users don’t want to change their existing systems;

• the enterprise objectives and strategy are not identified, and therefore do not guide IS development.

## 2. Requirements and design problems:

• users are unable to identify and define their information needs;

• users cannot communicate with IS professionals;

• users fear involvement in RAD workshops;

• users lack time to commit to the project;

• IS developers are unfamiliar with the tools used for design.

3. Construction problems:

• user-initiated specifications are not highly regarded by IS professionals;

• modules delivered by separate RAD teams are often hard to integrate.

Furthermore, Daniels (1996) argues that RAD ignores the long-term, focusing on business functionality required at the time, with rigorous engineering postponed until later. This ‘build-and-hack’ culture encourages second-rate work as ‘prototypes only prove that you can build a prototype’. Carmel (1995), like Daniels, claims that RAD is a fad and is overhyped. The effect is that it is not understood and suffers from numerous definitional ambiguities; e.g. does RAD include design? Do RAD iterations evolve into a production system or is the prototype rewritten from scratch? Even if its definition is resolved, RAD will not work because its implementation typically fails one or more of the conditions necessary for success which include picking the right team, effective management and customer support and the use of a defined methodology. Finally, Reilly (1995) concludes that to be successful with RAD the software product can no longer be considered in isolation. RAD is a process whereby consideration of the users and their working environments becomes paramount.

## Research design

An opportunity to study RAD implementation in detail arose when the former employer of one of the authors commenced a RAD implementation programme in 1994. The company, a large recently-privatised UK electricity company, PowerCo, derived some benefits from RAD, but did not continue to use RAD after the initial IS development project (the names of the organizations described in this paper have been changed to provide anonymity). In order to provide a contrast to sharpen the findings, RAD implementation at a second large UK organization, a major insurance company, The Insurance Business (TIB), was studied subsequently. TIB has been featured prominently in the UK computing press as a RAD implementation success story.

## Research question

The study addresses the research question:

Why did RAD implementation fail at PowerCo, but succeed at TIB?

Yin (1994) suggests that the choice of research approach should be governed by the nature of the research questions. In particular, survey research is most appropriate when addressing who, what, where, how many and how much questions, whilst case study research is appropriate when addressing how and why questions. Case studies are useful for revealing the details of a phenomenon; in particular, the relationship between the phenomenon and its context. Therefore a case study approach has been adopted for this research, using a combination of interviews and document analysis.

## The PowerCo experience

In this section the RAD implementation process at PowerCo is described.

## The Pathway project

## Background

RAD implementation at PowerCo was undertaken as part of a major IS development for Operations Division: the Pathway project. Operations Division is responsible for the distribution of electricity to all customers within the company’s region. This includes the planning and installation of new supplies as well as maintenance of existing supplies. Both domestic and commercial markets are served. Existing information systems to support Operations Division’s activities were either manual or computer-based but not integrated and were becoming increasingly time-consuming to use. The Pathway IS was to support many of the division’s core activities via an integrated suite of modules. Figure 1 shows the flow of information between departments. The trigger for action is typically the receipt of a customer order into the Supplies department or a network failure raised by the Repairs department. The resulting purchase order will be serviced by either the Maintenance or the Projects department who will invoice the Finance department in order to claim the cost from Supplies or Repairs. Often work will be commissioned from another strategic business unit (SBU), Engineering Division, thus stimulating a flow of orders and invoices between the two SBUs.

Operations Division contacted the in-house IT department, ITUnit, to determine whether to purchase a package or whether to develop the suite in-house. A decision was made to develop in-house due to a belief that a package would be more costly and would not provide the functionality required. RAD, a new development approach for ITUnit, was tabled as one reason why inhouse development would be less costly than going to an outside supplier.

A second major issue was that ITUnit was, to an extent, fighting for its life. The unit had a history of project problems, like many other in-house IT units in large organizations, and was now subject to market pressures as PowerCo SBUs were allowed to shop outside for IT services. Indeed, two recent project failures had been investigated at board level. Comments from a customer contact survey included: “ITUnit has a bad image within the business, it must improve this image if it is to compete for services”; and “I have never come across a less efficient IT function.” Like the other divisions within PowerCo, ITUnit had recently been restructured with the loss of many jobs, creating a climate of mistrust between management and staff and inter-branch rivalry as each ITUnit branch became a profit centre.

Thirdly, the RAD approach represented a significant change in working practices from the traditional waterfall development methodology in place at ITUnit. The methodology was characterised by low levels of user involvement, physical separation of users and IT staff (ITUnit was on a separate site to the operating divisions), and a focus on ‘IT speak’ and detailed, jargon-ridden requirements specifications.

This was the context for RAD implementation. RAD was introduced by the Pathway IT project manager, who, like all ITUnit project managers, had considerable leeway in dictating the development approach to be used on his project. Unfortunately, he had no prior RAD experience and there was no attempt to provide a comprehensive education in the new approach for the team, or to develop a detailed framework specifying roles, responsibilities, techniques or deliverables. Team members were expected to look to the DSDM documentation for guidance, but this was high-level and non-prescriptive. In effect, RAD had been used as a bargaining device to ‘win’ the work for ITUnit.

## Development of Pathway

The life cycle of each of the four Pathway modules will now be described (Figure 1 shows the relationship between the modules).

## SupplyMod

The first module, SupplyMod, covered the provision and alteration of electricity supplies to all PowerCo customers. The module was developed to control this work, and to provide an interface to the associated business functions. Requirements analysis was undertaken by a team of two user representatives and two analyst/programmers via structured interviews with business users. The output, a requirements specification document, followed the same format as the SSADMstyle deliverables produced previously and included data flow diagrams, entity-relationship models, record layouts and requirements lists. The customer project manager was to state later that “too much detail was produced at too early a stage”, particularly since PowerCo was being ‘re-engineered’ at the time. The requirements were prioritised by Pathway managers without consulting the supervisors or users. Risk analysis was performed, but only informally and was not documented anywhere.

Module development was via evolutionary prototyping, with prototypes being developed by ITUnit developers, based on the specified requirements. These prototypes were released to the user representatives for validation of the screens and the underlying processes. It was felt by the customer project manager that experience was lacking with the prototyping process and that “the prototyping process was not initially pinned down, resulting in the process taking longer than expected.” Interfaces with other systems were only considered at a high level; prototype iterations concentrated mainly on the look and feel of the screens. Failure to consider the interfaces to other systems caused problems subsequently when they were found to be more complex than at first thought.

User testing was integrated into the process of prototype release. This meant that on release, business representatives not only verified the look and feel of the screens, but also tested the functionality. A number of changes were requested, but few bugs were found. However, the degree of change required unsettled the users.

The move to RAD required a greater level of commitment from the users. Furthermore, the continued use of SSADM-style deliverables perpetuated communication problems between developers and users, as identified by the customer project manager. In a post-implementation review, a user representative commented: “Run-time errors created a feeling of an unfinished application. This hampered the process as concerns existed as to whether the prototypes were fit for the purpose.” The term ‘runtime error’ was widely used by the users, but was often found to reflect inaccuracies in the prototype, such as missing fields or misunderstandings regarding processing of input, rather than ‘bugs’. More positively, the customer project manager thought that the IT project manager had done a very good job despite the lack of support from senior ITUnit management regarding gaining greater commitment and input from Engineering Division staff. The lead user representative felt that the IT staff were “an excellent crew” but suggested that they were sometimes “a little remote”. The IT staff involved found prototyping very beneficial but were critical of the sporadic user involvement due to the commitment of users only part-time.

## MaintMod

This module was developed to handle the contracted maintenance work. These contracts were negotiated with PowerCo and included both Operations and Engineering Divisions, with the work covering routine maintenance to the company’s infrastructure.

Requirements analysis for the project was performed primarily by an ITUnit developer and two user representatives; end-users were excluded from the early stages. This process again produced a traditional ITUnit format document which this time also included first-cut screen designs (on paper). Module development was undertaken by a second ITUnit developer. Restricted user input into the analysis activity meant that the size and scope of the module was not accurately defined, a matter requiring attention at development time. Prototypes were developed rapidly by the ITUnit developer and then released to user representatives for verification and validation. This process, with many iterations, led to the final screens being substantially different from those initially specified. During validation, any changes or errors were reported by the users to the main user representative, filtered, and relevant information relayed back to the ITUnit developer working across town, at the ITUnit site. The prototypes were initially released to the main user representative, who performed testing prior to their release to the ‘shop floor’ workers. A large amount of feedback was generated through the above verification process.

Inaccuracies in the prototype experienced during testing again reduced user confidence in the system, leading to a feeling that the system was unfinished. However, the prototyping approach increased user expectations that something was happening, helping to maintain support levels. Again, working relationships between IT staff and users were seen as ‘excellent’ by the user representatives, but the interfaces to Engineering Division were difficult to progress due to a lack of interest from Engineering Division staff.

## FinanceMod

The need for this module was identified late in the project as a result of excluding the Finance function of Operations Division from the initial analysis. This meant that Pathway had overlooked the need to cater for invoices and invoice processing.

Due to the late start, timescales were particularly tight for this module. Requirements analysis was performed quickly by two ITUnit developers, and two user representatives, using both individual and group interview techniques. The output from this process was an SSADM-style document consisting of a high level statement of user requirements and data and process models. The project sponsor identified the user representatives to be involved in the subsequent development of the prototype. However, the design was virtually complete before any demonstration to the user representatives. Despite this, the screens were generally accepted apart from a number of cosmetic changes. Testing was performed via the release of prototypes to the user representatives, who were responsible for verifying the prototype screens. Prior to the release of the prototypes, the ITUnit developers performed unit and system testing but were not aware of any plans which the user representatives had for the testing of the prototypes. ITUnit developers were not present during user testing sessions and only received requests for bug fixes or additional functionality via the user representatives.

The project sponsor had several observations after the development of the module. The requirement for invoicing had been missed initially, and it was felt that an experienced business analyst would have identified this need. Communication was poor between ITUnit and Engineering Division, and also between the FinanceMod and MaintMod teams within Operations Division. The resultant specification was in ‘IT speak’, meaning that users did not understand the proposals initially (although subsequent prototyping clarified matters considerably). The concerns of the sponsor and project manager represented a reduction in the level of support for Finance-Mod, and for RAD, at this stage; however, the module was implemented ‘within timescales’ and the financial requirements of Operations Division were ‘fully met’ subsequently (post-project interview with the user representative).

## RepairMod

This module was developed to handle the one-off repairs which become necessary on the PowerCo network infrastructure. Such one-off repairs are the result of incidents such as lightening strikes on lines or vandalism.

Requirements analysis was performed by an ITUnit developer and a user representative. The initial requirements were obtained using interview techniques, with the user representative providing the input. The ITUnit developer prototyped the initially identified screens back at the ITUnit offices, but on demonstrating the prototype to the users it was found that a number of functions had been overlooked. Therefore development became userdriven, involving a user team spending a period of time in a workshop environment brainstorming the requirements, which were then passed to the ITUnit developer for prototyping. Subsequent workshops involving IT and user representatives determined further requirements via a process of iterative prototyping. The team of user representatives demonstrated the module to other users who were not directly involved in the workshops. This was a conscious decision with the aim of reducing the threat of rejection. The user representatives received regular releases of the prototype, integrating the testing into the development life cycle. Faults and changes were recorded, allowing them to be addressed by the project team in further prototype releases.

As with MaintMod, the prototype’s inaccuracies reduced users’ confidence (“It felt like you were getting an unfinished application due to run-time errors”), but the existence of an understandable prototype and the use of workshops increased their expectations and satisfaction with the process, and with RAD (“In general the iterative development approach was very successful”; “Workshops were a particularly valuable technique”).

## Analysis of Pathway

A number of issues arise from the Pathway experience. The initial decision to replace the established waterfall IS development methodology with RAD was taken quickly by an individual IS project manager and was not a carefully planned, consensus-based organizational change. RAD was a bargaining device used to win business with the promise of not only rapid development, but also an increased likelihood of meeting specific user needs in contrast to the generic (and by implication inflexible?) ordering and invoicing packages available in the marketplace. The risks of bespoke development using a new methodology and new tools were clearly regarded as worth taking. To compound the risk, there was no formal training of any stakeholder group with respect to their role in RAD.

There were a large number of stakeholder groups: senior business management, senior ITUnit management, the IS project manager, the business unit managers for each module, the analysts and programmers, the user representatives and the users. Senior management involvement and support for Pathway appeared to be lacking, as evidenced by the problems the project team encountered in getting staff from the Engineering Division involved. There was no project steering committee to provide a forum for resolution of these difficulties. Nor were the risks formally documented and acted upon. ‘Rapid’ therefore also implied a haste to get on with development rather than lobby for senior management support and think through the appropriate highlevel supporting structures.

The various stakeholder groups were physically separated. This was most in evidence at the analyst/ programmer-user level where IT staff would leave their Greenlands Road offices to conduct interviews with user representatives at Smith Street, return to the offices to prepare (paper-based) specifications, revisit Smith Street for validation, return to Greenlands Road to develop first-cut prototypes, revisit Smith Street to demonstrate the prototypes . . . and so on. Yes, this was iterative development, but the time delays and physical barriers of waterfall development were still in place. It was not until the final module, RepairMod, that the users became sufficiently concerned and proactive to set up their own RAD workshops, with IT staff participation subsequently.

Furthermore, ‘IT speak’ remained. Specifications were created using data flow diagrams, entity-relationship models and long lists of jargon-laden requirements. Old methods die hard. Prototyping started once the specifications had been agreed and thus inherited the potential inaccuracies of the specification documents.

Prototyping initially disturbed the users. They welcomed the closer involvement and the tangible deliverables, but were not prepared for close inspection of halffinished systems. What had previously remained hidden behind closed doors at Greenlands Road was now on their desktops.

Finally, despite the preceding concerns, the working relationship between IT staff and users was generally very good.

## RAD at TIB

PowerCo is the focal case here. While lessons can be learned from a single case study, greater insight can usually be drawn from a cross-case comparison. Eisenhardt (1989) therefore suggests selecting extreme situations or polar types, such as a successful organization and an unsuccessful one in order to derive deeper insight. While PowerCo made some progress with RAD, a number of problems were encountered. In contrast, The Insurance Business (TIB) has been cited in the UK computing press as a RAD success story. We therefore contacted TIB to obtain information on their approach to RAD. In contrast to the detailed, longitudinal study of the Pathway project, the TIB data was collected via interviews with the Process Improvement Team manager and a TIB IT consultant, and via document analysis. Therefore the findings are presented in the form of an overview of generic RAD practice at TIB rather than as a description of the life cycle of a specific IS project.

## Background

Many insurance companies are chasing the same customers, and product and service innovations only confer temporary advantage in the marketplace. This is a ‘datedriven’ environment with launch dates dictated by marketing initiatives. Therefore the deadlines for the underlying systems have to be met. Like their PowerCo equivalent, ITUnit, TIB General Insurance Business Systems (TGIBS) used waterfall methods with little user involvement and also had a history of IS project problems. Unlike ITUnit, TGIBS have created a unit, the Process Improvement Team (PIT), tasked with improving the inhouse IS development process. PIT recognised that traditional methods, such as SSADM, failed to deliver complex systems on time and to budget and have developed a RAD framework to combat these problems.

## The TIB approach to RAD

Each RAD project is formally started off by the project sponsor who gives a presentation providing an overview of the problem area. The first step within the TIB RAD framework is to understand the current system and its problems, providing an opportunity to reflect on the system requirements. Workshops are then scheduled to obtain initial opinions and ideas for the problem area. The next step is to provide a more detailed picture of requirements. Using business objects to define the problem, propose solutions and give a rich picture of the system, the aim is to determine sufficient detail to estimate, plan and resource the project (see Figure 2).

The development phase is always started with a joint user-IT workshop. Led by the business sponsor, the scene for the project is set and the development launched. Roles and responsibilities are determined and the importance of the system and its scope are made clear to all. The software product is built using fixed time periods, called timeboxes. The system is presented to the user team at workshops called consumer tests where the aim is to define and prioritise changes and to schedule the next workshop. This process drives out all the changes before the end of the fourth cycle, allowing the system to move rapidly towards implementation. Acceptance testing begins once the first iteration is released to the business. Hence the testing of an iteratively built system is a continuous process beginning at the earliest possible point in the life cycle and continuing throughout the development of the various iterations.

PIT are proud of their RAD results. At the time of writing sixteen systems have been developed and eight are under development. Productivity is typically 40+ function points per man-month as opposed to an industry average of eight. These success stories have been widely publicised throughout TIB and have led to a high level of support for RAD. Furthermore, the RAD framework is constantly evolving as lessons are learnt from each successive IS development project.

## Comparing PowerCo and TIB

We return to the nine DSDM principles in order to compare the RAD practices of the two companies (Table 2).

Like PowerCo, TIB observe the DSDM principles 4, 5, 6 and 7 but have a more formalised project control structure with consumer test workshops, a four-iteration limit and workshops to gain consensus on the high-level business requirements. Principles 2, 3 and 8 are more fully supported at TIB where the active involvement of the business sponsor in assigning roles and responsibilities provides a clearer mandate for empowered collaboration, and where timeboxing helps to keep the project on course. Finally, principles 1 and 9, the active, widespread collaboration between all stakeholders are encouraged at TIB by business ownership of projects, extensive use of workshops and the use of business objects to facilitate shared understanding of the business issues at hand.

For PowerCo, the process of developing and implementing the Pathway IS was also a learning process in rapid application development. This is in accord with the views of the PIT manager at TIB who commented that RAD cannot be learnt a priori, it can only be understood through the experience of doing. PowerCo learnt a great deal about RAD through Pathway. Their initial approach was undoubtedly techno-centric and lacking in structure or skills; essentially, the Pathway IT project manager set out to win the work for ITUnit and used RAD as a bargaining device to argue that the inhouse bespoke development would deliver a system at lower cost and with a closer match to user needs than the packaged alternatives.

He was clearly a believer in RAD, but did not attempt to instigate either an educational programme or a formal RAD framework at that stage. Instead, his developers were told to meet the users, prototype, and take it from there. Many of the developers had a mainframe background and were used to the waterfall, SSADM-style development process in place at PowerCo at the time. They were new to RAD and new to client–server development. During the 20 months from the start of module SupplyMod to the end of module RepairMod, the development teams attempted to master Microsoft Access, prototyping and user involvement. That they succeeded in delivering all four modules and largely satisfying their user community is evidence that RAD had shown considerable merit as a development approach. The postproject interviews with all parties showed an almost uniform view that prototyping was useful despite the unfinished feel of early releases, and that working relationships between ITUnit staff and users were generally very good. The key missing elements were identified as timeboxing, workshops, co-location of teams, understandable high-level specification documents and management of the interfaces between the module teams and other functional areas, most notably Engineering Division.

## Institutionalising RAD

Sauer (1993) defines a failed IS project as one that is terminated prematurely through loss of support. This is in contrast to the narrower definitions of failure often encountered in the literature such as overrunning of budget or of schedule. The fact that Pathway is still in use at the time of writing, some three years after the start of the first module and some 16 months after the end of the last module, would appear to show that, in Sauer’s terms, Pathway has maintained support levels and can therefore be deemed a success. But the verdict on RAD is less positive. To date, RAD has not been adopted by ITUnit, despite the success of Pathway and the largely positive comments of the development teams.

Why was this so? A key factor was the rapidly changing organizational structure and power relations within PowerCo then and now. PowerCo had been privatised recently and was embracing market forces and restructuring rapidly in order to become more responsive to changes in its environment. The split into autonomous SBUs, such as Operations Division and Engineering Division, is one example of this; the increasing use of third-party IT consultants is another example. ITUnit no longer has a monopoly on system development within PowerCo, and the external suppliers have been steadily gaining ground. These suppliers bring with them their own IS development appproaches. Furthermore, ironically, bespoke development is giving way to package selection and tailoring, and whilst RAD can be used for this work, there is no experience of this within PowerCo. RAD, therefore, is associated with bespoke in-house development which is becoming a thing of the past at PowerCo.

Individuals also have a role to play in the institutionalisation of development approaches. The Pathway IT project manager introduced RAD thinking into PowerCo, and can be seen as a key supporter. Late in the project, he sent his staff on a DSDM training course in order to further develop their RAD skills. At about that time, ITUnit recruited an experienced RAD professional to further advance RAD practice within the company. The consultant went on to propose a RAD framework for PowerCo based on DSDM but prescribing tools and techniques in a similar way to the framework developed at TIB. The framework was never used, and both the IT project manager and the consultant left PowerCo shortly afterwards. With ITUnit losing internal market share to the external IT suppliers, there was inevitably a climate of uncertainty surrounding the futures of the in-house IT staff. Whilst the IT Director has publicly praised the RAD work and has sponsored new initiatives aimed at raising morale and delivering a better service to the customer, the reality is that capable IT staff can often find more attractive employment elsewhere, often with the external suppliers that are competing with ITUnit for PowerCo business.

Thus the combination of a failure to institutionalise RAD, the departure of key RAD advocates, the fragmentation of the business into many separate units and the demise of in-house bespoke development in the face of external suppliers and packages represents the end of an era at ITUnit and, for the time being, the end of RAD. Figure 3 presents these events in the form of a contextprocess model which draws upon Sauer’s work by viewing RAD implementation as a process which is both influenced by, and influences, its organizational context (Sauer, 1993). The former influence being strong, the latter weak.

Previous research on IS methodology implementation has shown that the change must be managed carefully and treated as a significant organizational innovation (Veryard, 1987; Rockart & Hofman, 1992; Kautz & McMaster, 1994). Rockart and Hofman use the MIT90s model to analyse methodology innovation (Figure 4). The elements of the model can be viewed as a set of levers which can be pulled in order to improve organizational performance. However, there are often subtle interconnections between the levers and several may have to be pulled in the same direction in order to succeed in change. Indeed, the over-reliance on technology change without consideration of the effects on the other elements is often cited as the principal cause of IS failure (Scott Morton, 1991). Returning to PowerCo, the emphasis on prototyping using Microsoft Access and developing client–server applications indicated a highlytechnological mindset driving RAD introduction. But, as shown in the DSDM principles, RAD also has a strong cultural dimension exemplified by the need for active user involvement and close teamworking. But at PowerCo there were significant structural problems, for example physical separation of IT staff from users and poor co-operation across SBU boundaries. There was little investment in training of individuals for their new roles as RAD team members. Furthermore, RAD was not a strategic innovation, but was instead a short-term gambit to win business for ITUnit. Certainly senior business and IT management were not actively involved in the introduction of RAD.

Looking at the external environment we see both drivers for RAD and further barriers to successful implementation. The drivers include the industry-wide move away from mainframe development towards PCs and client– server systems. Tools such as Access which facilitate rapid prototyping are now in widespread use. Other external drivers include the increasing availability of large-scale software packages for routine applications such as order fulfilment, and the burgeoning marketplace for IT services. Both developments put pressure on previously safe internal IT departments to react more rapidly and effectively to changing customer demands. External forces also had a role to play in inhibiting RAD uptake. The most obvious example of this being the lack of co-operation between the new, market-focused SBUs Engineering Division and Operations Division. Boundary-spanning information systems such as Pathway inevitably get caught in the crossfire when structural and cultural change of this magnitude takes place.

At TIB, RAD has clung on despite some ‘hard times according to the PIT manager. The PIT team has managed to survive for more than three years and, in doing so, has assisted in the development of 24 systems (16 complete, 8 in progress). At the time of writing, in Sauer’s terms, RAD has succeeded at TIB in that sufficient support has been maintained to ensure the longevity of the PIT organization. With the PIT in place, there is continuity between successive IS development projects. PIT acts as the keeper of the faith and maintains the RAD framework. It also provides RAD consultancy to projects that wish to use the approach, thus stimulating organizational learning. It may be expected that the longer PIT survives, and the more successful IS projects are completed, the more support for RAD will be institutionalised within TIB. Like PowerCo, TIB have recently restructured into SBUs, but RAD had already established a strong presence within the organization and has continued to be the approach of choice for the majority of in-house developments. Where external packages are used, the package vendor’s methodology is followed initially, but subsequent customisation is undertaken in-house by TGIBS staff using the TIB RAD methodology. Apart from package procurement, there is very little outsourcing of bespoke development. Figure 5 summarises the RAD uptake process at TIB.

## Conclusion

RAD promises flexible systems by bringing IT specialists and users together in a less bureaucratic and more intensive way of working. Changes in user requirements are communicated rapidly to their IT colleagues who, in turn, can update the systems rapidly and accurately. Flexible systems are important because many organizations are facing increasingly hostile business environments and are responding by adopting more flexible, market-orientated ways of working, and herein lies a paradox; for market-orientation is potentially both a stimulus for RAD and an obstacle. For PowerCo marketorientation entailed restructuring into SBUs and outsourcing some IT services. Both of these developments may challenge the central RAD tenets of active user involvement and close co-operation of all stakeholders, since previously informal internal boundaries are replaced by formal external boundaries and contracts. The longevity of RAD within an organization may well depend more upon how high-level issues, such as boundary negotiation, are addressed than on the appropriateness of any particular technology.

The evidence from these two case studies implies that RAD techniques are useful, even in a hostile climate,
