# An investigation of task-technology fit for managers in Greece and the US

TW Ferratt and GE Vlahos

Department of MIS and Decision Sciences, School of Business Administration, University of Dayton, Dayton, Ohio 45469- 2130, USA

Based on a task-technology fit (TTF) model, this study adds to our understanding of how computer-based information systems (CBIS) support managerial decision making. Based on four views of decision making and various classifications of CBIS, the findings indicate that both the Greek and US managers value CBIS most highly for resource allocation, evaluating alternatives, identifying problems, ranking alternatives, and short-term decision making. Few TTF differences were found between Greek and US managers. One difference, concerning the value of office information systems, may have arisen from technological differences; however, additional research is needed to understand if these TTF differences arise from technological or cultural differences. Two surprising hypotheses emerge from the findings to provide guidance for future research: (1) standard, fixed-schedule, fixed-format information reporting systems (IRS) provide greater value for managers than more flexible decision support systems (DSS), at least for some managerial decision-making activities, and (2) the amount of use of a CBIS is not directly related to its perceived value. Overall, the results indicate that the fit between managerial decision-making activities, and CBIS has considerable room for improvement.

## Introduction

In the past two decades considerable investments have been made in computer-based information systems (CBIS) to support managers. Our understanding of how CBIS support managers is based primarily on assumptions about the role of information technology in supporting managerial work rather than empirical evidence. This study uses a model of task-technology fit (see Figure 1 and Goodhue, 1995; Goodhue & Thompson, 1995) to guide an empirical investigation of two interrelated questions: (1) what managerial decision-making activities are best supported by existing CBIS, and (2) what types of existing CBIS best support managerial decisionmaking. These questions are two sides of the fit between task (i.e., managerial decision making) and technology (i.e., CBIS).

Previous studies have reported that managers from all levels and functional areas use CBIS to support their work (e.g., Rockart & Treacy, 1982; Lee, 1986; Batley & Mangos, 1988; Rockart & DeLong, 1988; Igbaria et al, 1989; Mawhinney & Lederer, 1990; Igbaria, 1992; Rahman & Abdul-Gader, 1993; Vlahos & Ferratt, 1995). These studies indicate that, at least implicitly, there is some degree of fit between managerial task and information technology, i.e., CBIS do provide some value for managers. This study uses an explicit measure of the value of CBIS in supporting decision-making activities as perceived by managers, i.e., user evaluation shown in Figure 1, as the measure of task-technology fit (TTF): the higher the perceived value, the better the fit. Goodhue (1995) used a similar measure for TTF. Through focusing on an empirically measurable value of CBIS, this study tests hypotheses based on the TTF model in Figure 1 as well as exploring empirically how CBIS support managerial work. To develop the specific elements for user evaluation, i.e., the measure of TTF within this managerial decision-making context, it is important to understand the two sides—task and technology—of this fit. We begin by reviewing a more complete conceptual description of managerial task, based on various models of decision-making, and information technology, based on various types of CBIS.

Literature review of key concepts in the TTF model: managerial task and CBIS

The concept of task in the TTF model refers broadly to “actions carried out by individuals%” (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995, p 216). In the context of this study the individuals are managers and their actions of interest are their decision-making activities. The concept of technology in the TTF model refers to “tools used by individuals in carrying out their tasks. In the context of information systems research, technology refers to computer systems (hardware, software, and data)%” (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995, p 216), in other words, CBIS. To examine the fit between managerial decision-making activities and CBIS, specific decision making activities and various types of CBIS that managers use to support these tasks must be identified.

To identify specific managerial decision-making activities that define the task in the TTF model, this study uses four different views of managerial decision making. Anthony’s (1965) view represents decision making relative to the three traditional management functions of strategic planning, management control, and operational control. Anthony’s model is inherent in Gorry and Scott Morton’s (1971) seminal framework on decision support systems. Mintzberg’s (1973) view represents decision making in terms of the four decision roles of entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator. Mintzberg’s description of management roles represents a break from the traditional description of managerial work presented in Anthony’s model and provides a distinctive decision-making view for this study. The third view represents an amalgam of writings on decision making (e.g., see Harrison, 1987). This broad view of managerial decision making includes identifying problems or issues, generating alternative courses of action, evaluating the outcomes of each alternative, ranking the alternatives and choosing one, and implementing the chosen alternative. The fourth view represents Isenberg’s (1984) and Rockart and DeLong’s (1988) notion that a manager uses a mental model of the organization as a basis for decision making and that a critical role of CBIS is to support the development and use of this mental model. These four decision-making models are not intended to encompass all the work that managers do; instead, they identify a variety of activities involved in decision making, thereby providing a basis for exploring the value of CBIS in supporting decision making, which is a major managerial task.

To identify specific types of CBIS that define technology in the TTF model, this study reviews several writings concerned with information systems that support managerial work, particularly decision making. As suggested by Rockart and DeLong (1988), managers need support for their traditional planning and controlling activities, which have been supported by fixed-schedule, standard information reporting systems (IRS) and more flexible decision support systems (DSS) (e.g., Gorry & Scott Morton, 1971; Alloway & Quillard, 1983; Udo & Guimaraes, 1994); more recently, executive information systems (EIS) (e.g., Watson & Frolick, 1993; Nandhakumar, 1996) have been especially developed for higher level managers. However, managers also need support not provided by these types of systems. Office information systems (OIS) provide more of the communication support associated with decision making through word processing and electronic mail. Vlahos and Ferratt (1995) in their study of managerial decision making use these four types of systems plus two others: transaction processing systems (TPS), which collect, process, and store data related to organizational operations, and artificial intelligence/expert systems (AI/ES), which attempt to simulate human intelligence or make available the knowledge of experts for decision making in specialised areas. Although additional types of CBIS could be included, these six describe a broad set of major types of CBIS used to support managerial decision making.

## Objectives of this research: hypothesis testing and exploratory analysis

Guided by the TTF model of Figure 1 and the review of key concepts from that model—specifically managerial task and CBIS—this study seeks to understand better how CBIS support managerial work. The specific objectives are as follows:

1. where appropriate, test TTF hypotheses about the value and use of various types of CBIS in supporting managerial decision making; and

2. where there is limited prior empirical evidence, explore the fit between CBIS and managerial decision-making activities.

## Specific TTF hypotheses

Looking at TTF from the technology side, one could ask which CBIS best support managerial decision making activities. The hypothesis below suggests an answer that is based on the literature that follows:

H1: DSS and EIS support managerial decision-making better than TPS and IRS.

The empirical work of Alloway and Quillard (1983) identified TPS as the dominant form of CBIS at that time, with 79% of the installed base. IRS, which they called monitoring and exception reporting systems, were based on TPS, and were the systems most frequently used by managers. However, they asked managers which systems were more appropriate for their important needs. The managers indicated that more flexible query and analysis systems, i.e., DSS, were more appropriate than monitoring or exception reporting systems, i.e., IRS. Indeed, the managers’ demand for new systems was shifting to more flexible inquiry and analysis systems rather than the fixed-schedule, standard monitoring and exception reporting systems. Alloway and Quillard called for a major shift in information systems resources to provide more inquiry and analysis systems for managers. Zmud (1986) argues that DSS technologies are important elements within EIS, but that EIS should go beyond retrieval and analysis of ‘hard’ information to include the capability to handle ‘soft’ information, particularly executive communication and thought support. The general orientation of these and other works (e.g., see those cited above in the literature review) is that DSS and EIS will provide a better fit for managerial decision making than will TPS and IRS.

The link between user evaluation and utilisation in the TTF model of Figure 1 suggests a second hypothesis:

H2: Managerial use of CBIS is related to user evaluation of CBIS, i.e, TTF.

As Goodhue and Thompson (1995, p 218) point out, “Utilization is the behavior of employing the technology in completing tasks.” According to their model, when fit is high, use will be high; when fit is low, use will be low. That is, in the context of this study, the more managers value CBIS, the more they will use CBIS. Measures of this variable have included hours or frequency of use, diversity of applications employed, or proportion of tasks for which an individual decided to use the system. Goodhue and Thompson (1995) found weak support for the link between user evaluation and utilisation in their study even though theories and research on attitude leading to behaviour provide the basis for this part of the TTF model. They suggest a need for further research.

As indicated in the TTF model of Figure 1, individual differences affect the fit between task and technology. Individual differences arise from a number of sources. Managers from different countries have a number of potentially powerful sources of differences. Given the opportunity to study managers from Greece and the US, this study examines the hypothesis below that is based on the literature that follows:

H3: User evaluation of CBIS is not the same for managers from different cultures.

Prior research has identified the importance of culture as a basis for differences in individuals as well as information systems (e.g., Hofstede, 1980; Ein-Dor et al, 1993; Watson & Brancheau, 1991). To examine individual differences affecting TTF, managers from two different cultures, Greek and US, are used. While the US and most industrialised and economically advanced western European countries have been using CBIS for the past two decades, smaller, less industrialised nations have just recently begun investing in CBIS for their corporations. It was impossible for such countries to computerise until microcomputers and microcomputer software made it economically feasible. Since this became possible, an increasing number of small and large organizations are acquiring powerful and costeffective microcomputers and software for their administrative tasks.

Differences between individuals in Greece and the US may arise not only from differences in culture and accessibility to the most advanced technology but also from differences in training. Because of Greece’s strategic geographic location in southeastern Europe and the Middle East and its full membership in the European Union (EU), many European and other international corporations have subsidiaries and regional headquarters in Greece. Although these corporations may bring with them the advanced CBIS of their parent organizations, they have a problem finding trained managers and personnel who know how to use it. A study by Doukidis et al (1989) found that training was a major problem and many of their respondents expressed dissatisfaction with the training and advice provided by vendors.

Hofstede’s (1980) work suggests that differences may occur on four dimensions of cultural value: uncertainty avoidance, individualism versus collectivism, power distance, and masculinity (assertiveness) vs femininity (nurturance). According to Hofstede, Greece has much greater uncertainty avoidance than the US. The US is much more focused on individualism, whereas Greece is much more focused on collectivism. Greece has greater power distance than the US, but the difference between the two countries is not as great as on the first two dimensions. The two countries are fairly similar in their scores on Hofstede’s masculinity index. These dimensions may be useful in explaining cultural differences in TTF that may arise.

All told, managers in Greece and the US have a number of bases for individual differences that could affect the fit between task and technology. However, prior research does not suggest specifically how those differences will affect the fit. Thus, it will be appropriate not only to test the general hypothesis noted above, but also to explore where the differences exist.

Looking at TTF from the task side, one could ask which managerial decision-making activities are best supported by CBIS. Given the limited amount of prior empirical work, it is inappropriate to propose hypotheses about which activities are better supported by CBIS; instead, it is more appropriate to conduct an exploratory analysis. However, it is appopriate to hypothesise that management level is an important source of individual variation in TTF, i.e., individual differences in TTF may arise from the different tasks that managers at different levels perform:

H4: User evaluation of CBIS is not the same for different management levels.

## Methodology

## Sampling procedure

Our study is based on a survey of managers of corporations in Greece and the US. The data for the study were gathered via survey questionnaires. A preliminary version of the questionnaire was reviewed by expert researchers in the area of CBIS. The questionnaire was also tested with samples of US managers. The comments and suggestions of the researchers and managers regarding the clarity, validity and consistency of the questions were incorporated into the final version of the questionnaire. As noted later, scales derived from the resulting questionnaire demonstrate good psychometric properties.

The survey instrument (see Appendix) was distributed to approximately 1000 managers from 200 middle-tolarge corporations in Greece as well as 1000 managers from 200 US corporations randomly selected from the Value Line Investment Survey. A covering letter (in Greek and English, respectively) explaining the purpose and importance of the survey was also included. The recipients were asked to select five managers who personally used information technology to respond to the survey. Fifty-five managers from twenty-nine organizations in the Greek sample responded. At least 41% of the respondents were employed by foreign corporations in Greece. Sixty-four managers from twenty-one organizations in the US sample responded. Table 1 shows the characteristics of these samples. Although both samples are predominantly male, the Greek sample is more so; on average, it is also younger and more highly educated than the US sample; the US sample has taken more computer courses than the Greek sample; and both are similarly distributed across first-line, middle, and top management levels. (See Appendix, question 7, for the survey item used to classify management level.)

## Measures of variables

The following variables from the TTF model of Figure 1 are measured in this study: user evaluation (as a measure of TTF), utilisation, and individual differences. User evaluation (or TTF) is measured in two ways: user evaluation of CBIS (regardless of type) in supporting decision-making activities and user evaluation of specific types of CBIS in supporting decision making (regardless of activity). The higher the user evaluation, the better the fit. As noted earlier, Goodhue (1995) used a similar measure for TTF. Utilisation is measured by self-reported hours of use. Individual differences are based on culture, as indicated by country of the manager (Greece vs US), and management level (top, middle, and first-line). ## User evaluation of CBIS in supporting decisionmaking activities

For this TTF measure managers were asked to rate the value of CBIS in supporting the decision-making activities represented by the domain of the four different views of decision making presented in the literature review. (See Appendix, questions 8–13). Individual survey items were developed to measure the value of CBIS for (1) Mintzberg’s (1973) decision roles—entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator, (2) specific decision steps—identifying problems or issues, generating alternative courses of action, evaluating the outcomes of each alternative, ranking the alternatives and choosing one, and implementing the chosen alternative, (3) Anthony’s (1965) specific decisionmaking tasks—long term (strategic), mid-term (tactical), and short term (operational) decisions, and (4) mental models, specifically the development and use of the manager’s mental model. Altogether, the survey includes fourteen items that focus on measuring TTF for specific decision making activities. In addition, a broad view of value was measured by an overall importance question.

A major constraint in designing measures was the length of the survey. To obtain managerial respondents the survey had to be relatively brief. As a result, multiitem measures of each of the views were not feasible; however, the fifteen items used to measure user evaluation of decision-making activities provided a pool of items for developing a psychometrically sound overall measure of user evaluation, i.e., overall TTF.

A five-item, unidimensional, overall measure of user evaluation was derived from the fifteen items above through factor and scale reliability analyses. Cronbach’s Alpha (coefficient of reliability) is 0.84 for the Greek sample and 0.82 for the US sample. The five-item measure includes the two items asking about the CBIS used to support long-term and mid-term decision making, the two items asking about the value of the CBIS relative to the mental model, and the overall importance question. These five items, after recoding to a common seven-point scale, were averaged for each respondent, yielding a valid, reliable measure of overall user evaluation, i.e., overall TTF.

## User evaluation of specific types of CBIS

For this TTF measure the survey presented two listings of types of CBIS included in the literature review presented earlier. (See questions 17 and 19). The first listing of types of CBIS classifies systems into the general categories of fixed and flexible systems. The second listing consists of the six commonly known types of systems identified earlier, e.g., information reporting system (IRS), decision support systems (DSS), and transaction processing systems (TPS). In each question respondents ranked the listed sources of CBI in order of importance for their decision making with (1) being the most important. (Ranks were recoded to yield the following weights, which were then averaged across respondents: 1 recoded to 7; 2 recoded to 5; 3 recoded to 3; 4 through 7 recoded to 1; and 0 (no rank) recoded to 0). Since the nature of this data is not interval, parametric statistics are not used for hypothesis testing, nor are coefficients of reliability computed. However, the consistency of the top ranked type of CBIS across the two measures is an indication of high reliability in these measures. Specifically, see top-ranked regular reports in the top part of Table 2 and the comparable top-ranked IRS in the second part of the table.

## Utilisation

Participants indicated their use in hours per week of various types of hardware. These times were combined to yield total hours of use. Similarly, the weekly times for software use were obtained. Another question asked respondents to indicate total hours of use per week. The average of these three totals provides a reliable measure of use. Cronbach’s Alpha (coefficient of reliability) is 0.91 for the Greek sample and 0.86 for the US sample.

## Results

Given the role of individual differences in the TTF model of Figure 1, the results are presented for each of the individual differences variables in this study. Thus, the first two hypotheses—(1) DSS and EIS support managerial decision making better than TPS and IRS, and (2) managerial use of CBIS is related to user evaluation of CBIS—are tested for each of the different levels of culture and management level. The exploration of managerial activities that are best supported by CBIS uses a similar approach. Finally, the third and fourth hypotheses on individual differences in TTF—user evaluation of CBIS is not the same for managers from different cultures or different management levels—are also tested.

## Testing the TTF hypotheses

H1: DSS and EIS support managerial decision making better than TPS and IRS.

Table 2 shows that regularly scheduled reports have the highest TTF for managers, regardless of individual differences. The relative importance of regular reports (when compared with other fixed and flexible systems) and IRS, which provide regularly scheduled reports, (when compared with other commonly known systems) is higher than any of the other types of CBIS for Greek and US managers as well as for all three management levels. This better fit of IRS with managerial decisionmaking activities is counter to the hypothesis. When comparing DSS to TPS, the results are mixed: for managers grouped by culture, there is not much difference between DSS and TPS, whereas for all managers grouped by level, top managers rate DSS higher than TPS, but first-line managers rate TPS higher than DSS. When comparing EIS to TPS, once again the results are mixed: for managers grouped by culture, the results are in the direction indicated by the hypothesis for the Greek managers but not for the US managers, whereas for managers grouped by level, top managers rate EIS higher than TPS, but first-line managers rate TPS higher than EIS. Altogether, inspection of these results indicates that this hypothesis is not supported. Implications are presented in the discussion of results.

## Individual differences in TTF: testing hypotheses 3 and 4

H3: User evaluation of CBIS is not the same for managers from different cultures.

In examining which managerial decision-making activities were best supported by CBIS, individual differences based on culture as indicated by country were statistically significant beyond the 0.05 level for two of the fourteen items used to assess TTF. User evaluation of CBIS in supporting decision making activities was different for Greek and US managers with regard to: 1. ranking and choosing an alternative, and

2. guiding decisions based on the mental model.

For both decision-making activities the Greek managers rated the value of CBIS higher, indicating a better TTF for the Greek managers relative to these aspects of the managerial task and the information technology supporting them.

In examining which CBIS best support managerial decision-making activities, individual differences based on culture were statistically significant beyond the 0.05 level for four of the nine specifically named types of CBIS used to assess TTF. The greatest individual differences in TTF between the Greek and US managers occur in their rankings of office information systems (OIS), executive information systems (EIS), information reporting systems (IRS), and on-line and interactive systems. The US managers rated OIS and the on-line, interactive systems as more important than the Greek managers. The Greek managers rated EIS and IRS as more important than the US managers.

H4: User evaluation of CBIS is not the same for different management levels.

In examining which managerial decision-making activities were best supported by CBIS, individual differences based on management level were statistically significant beyond the 0.05 level for two of the fourteen items used to assess TTF:

1. generating alternatives, and

2. implementing the chosen alternative.

For both of these decision-making activities first-line managers rated the value of CBIS highest, while middle managers rated the value lowest, indicating a better TTF for first-line managers relative to their performance of these aspects of managerial work and the information technology supporting them.

In examining which CBIS best support managerial decision-making activities, individual differences based on management level were statistically significant beyond the 0.05 level for only one of the nine specifically named types of CBIS used to assess TTF. The greatest individual difference in TTF across management levels occurs in their rankings of EIS. Top managers rated EIS significantly higher than middle and firstline managers.

In using a two factor general linear model to statistically analyse the effects of the two individual differences (culture and management level) on each of the twentythree TTF measures (fourteen decision-making activities and nine specifically named types of CBIS) shown in Tables 2 and 4, only one significant interaction exists between the two individual difference factors. Even though this single significant interaction could have occurred by chance, it is interesting to note that the interaction occurs with the resource allocator role. For Greek managers, top managers value CBIS the most (6.4) for this role, while middle managers value CBIS the least (4.4). For US managers, middle managers value CBIS the most (5.2), while first-line managers value CBIS the least (4.2).

Even though many similarities exist between managers from different cultures and levels, the overall results above suggest that hypotheses 3 and 4 are supported. TTF is not the same for Greek and US managers nor for managers at different levels. That is, the individual manager’s background makes a difference in the two sides of TTF, i.e., user evaluation of a CBIS for supporting decision-making activities and the decision-making activities best supported by CBIS. The discussion below examines alternative explanations or implications of these and the other findings above.

## Discussion of results

Proposed revisions to hypotheses 1 and 2

The empirical results suggest revising the first two hypotheses that guided this investigation since the results are counter to our understanding of the role of information technology in supporting managerial work as reflected in these hypotheses. In the first hypothesis, the traditional belief has been that more flexible DSS provide a better fit to management decision making than the standard, fixed-schedule, fixed-format IRS. In the second hypothesis, the traditional belief inherent in the model of TTF has been that managers will use more those CBIS that provide them with greater support. Our results suggest that it is appropriate to reformulate these hypotheses.

## Proposed revision to hypotheses 1: the value of IRS compared to DSS

Although Goodhue and Thompson’s (1995) TTF model does not specify which CBIS best support managerial tasks, it focuses attention on the task and technology variables that must be empirically examined to understand how information technology supports managerial tasks. The results of our empirical investigation suggest this revision to the first hypothesis:

IRS provide more valuable support than DSS for some kinds of managerial decision-making activities.

This revision of the first hypothesis is based on the results indicating that the relative importance of IRS is higher than all other types of systems. The exploratory results indicate which managerial decision-making activities are best supported by CBIS: resource allocation, identifying changes (entrepreneurial role) and problems, evaluating alternatives, and short-term decision making. However, the methodology does not necessarily support a conclusion that IRS best support these activities.

Future investigations of this hypothesis could be based on alternative explanations of why IRS provide more valuable support than DSS. For example, one explanation could be that IRS are better at supporting specific managerial decision-making activities that require mainly standard information, e.g., resource allocation, evaluating alternatives, identifying problems and changes needed, and short-term decision making. An alternative explanation could be that IRS take less of the manager’s time to use than DSS while still providing valuable information for these activities. A third explanation could be that IRS have evolved to where managers can access pre-specified queries or analyses that are likely to meet most information needs that arise for these activities.

Proposed revision to hypothesis 2: type vs. quantity of use

User evaluation of CBIS is not related to the amount of use.

Instead, user evaluation may be related to the type of use behaviour. This revised hypothesis arises from the mostly absent correlation between user evaluation and amount of use. It suggests a need to re-conceptualise the utilisation variable as well as the link between user evaluation and utilisation in the model of TTF. The amount of time a manager uses CBIS is not necessarily the concept of use that applies here. Furthermore, this study used overall measures of user evaluation and amount of use to yield results suggesting this hypothesis, not measures of use for specific types of decision making activities that could be matched with specific user evaluations for those activities. Examining the link between user evaluation and utilisation in a causal direction opposite that shown in Figure 1 helps illustrate the ideas suggested here.

Some managers may use CBIS a small amount but receive great value for that limited use. Consider a manager who performs a critical resource allocator role relatively infrequently but finds that a CBIS provides great support for that critical role. Other managers may use CBIS a large amount and also receive great value. For example, a manager may use CBIS frequently for supporting various decision-making activities (e.g., allocating resources, identifying problems, evaluating alternatives, and short-term decision making) and get great value from this use. Alternately, this type-rather-thanquantity-of-use hypothesis implies some managers may use CBIS a large amount but receive little value. For example, the manager who consistently spends a large amount of time working with spreadsheets and word processors to prepare data for presenting alternatives to others may receive relatively little value from this use.

Goodhue and Thompson (1995, p 218) recognise the difficulties associated with the concept of utilisation in their statement that “the construct is arguably not yet well understood.” Future researchers will need to articulate more clearly the concept of use and how to measure it. They may also want to formulate the relationship between user evaluation and utilisation as non-linear to reflect the kind of relationship described above.

## Implications from hypotheses 3 and 4: TTF and individual differences

It is interesting to speculate on the basis for individual differences in TTF. The differences could be culturally based. For example, the fit between managerial decisionmaking activities and CBIS could be different in Greece and the US because of differences in the way managers in these countries perform these activities or in how they perceive and interact with technology. If cultural differences are the basis, the implication is that researchers and managers need uniquely to understand the task of managers and how managers perceive and interact with technology for each different culture.

The basis for differences in fit could be the technology in use. For example, one country or one management level could have technology that better supports managerial decision-making activities. If different levels of technology are the basis of these TTF differences, the implication is that matching the technologies in the two countries or across the three management levels would eliminate differences. Eliminating TTF differences is conceptually much simpler if the basis is technological rather than cultural (e.g., in the case of Greek and US managers) or rooted in different kinds of managerial work (e.g., in the case of different management levels). Even so, the differences may be economically difficult to eliminate, particularly if the cost of providing the same technology to all cultures or all management levels is high.

Our study provides suggestive evidence that individual differences in TTF, at least to some extent, could be based on differences in technology. The percentage of US managers using PCs or workstations connected to a central computer system (64%) or local area network (36%) is much larger than the percentage of Greek managers connected to a central system (49%) or local area network (5%). In addition, the percentage of US managers using word processing (67%) and Email (59%) is much larger than the percentage of Greek managers using word processing (42%) and Email (9%). These differences in technology may help explain why on-line, interactive systems (that involve workstations connected to a central computer or a network) and OIS (that involve word processing and electronic mail) provide a better fit for US managers than Greek managers. These findings of technology rather than cultural differences are not dissimilar from the findings of Ein-Dor and Segev (1992). If these TTF differences are, indeed, based on technological rather than cultural differences, rapid changes in the costs and implementation of technology could reduce such differences.

Of course, the choice of technology to use could be culturally based. For example, managers in some cultures may prefer to communicate face-to-face rather than via technology; or perhaps they would prefer not to use a keyboard that is needed for a word processor or Email, particularly if keyboarding is perceived as associated with a lower status clerical position. The greater concern for collective good found in the greater collectivism of Greece may be related to a preference for a richer interpersonal exchange than is available through technology; similarly the greater power distance of Greece may apply to the preference to have clerical people perform the keyboarding that is associated with word processors and Email. These post hoc explanations could be the basis for future research.

Additional analysis of our data shows that the most commonly used type of software for the Greek managers is spreadsheet software. The difference in spreadsheet use between the Greek managers (4.1 hrs/wk) and US managers (1.7 hrs/wk) is statistically significant beyond the 0.05 level. The greater use of this technology may help explain why Greek managers rated the value of CBIS higher than US managers for the decision-making activity of ranking alternatives. Is the reason for this difference in use of spreadsheet technologically or culturally based? The distribution of managers across managerial levels indicates that Greek and US managers are fairly similar. Thus, the difference in use does not appear to be based on differences in the composition of the two samples. However, the emphasis on specific managerial decision-making activities could differ from one culture to another. For example, managers in some cultures could be more concerned about risks and therefore spend more time evaluating and ranking alternatives, or perhaps they are more likely to trust their own analyses rather than to delegate this type of analysis to non-managerial subordinates. Hofstede’s (1980) work suggests that a cultural explanation could apply here. On one of his key dimensions of culture, uncertainty avoidance, Greece ranked much higher than the US. Once again, this explanation could be the basis for future research.

## Summary and conclusion

A model of TTF was used to guide this empirical investigation of managerial use of CBIS. It led to two related questions: (1) what managerial decision-making activities are best supported by existing CBIS, and (2) what types of existing CBIS best support managerial decisionmaking activities. Based on the TTF model, these two questions, and a review of the literature to identify managerial decision-making activities and various types of CBIS, four hypotheses were developed and tested:

H1: DSS and EIS support managerial decision making better than TPS and IRS.

H2: Managerial use of CBIS is related to user evaluation of CBIS.

H3: User evaluation of CBIS is not the same for managers from different cultures.

H4: User evaluation of CBIS is not the same for different levels of managers.

In addition, given limited prior empirical work, an exploration of the value of CBIS in supporting managerial decision-making activities was also conducted.

Based on results from Greek and US managers as well as three management levels, two revised hypotheses have been suggested. One hypothesis, in providing an answer to the two major questions guiding this study, proposes that IRS are better at supporting some managerial work than DSS. A potential implication of this hypothesis, if supported by future research, is that investments directed at improving IRS will provide greater value than investments in DSS, particularly for specific managerial decision-making activities. The other revised hypothesis proposes that the amount that a CBIS is used is not directly related to the manager’s evaluation of the CBIS, thereby requiring reformulation of the relationship between user evaluation and utilisation in the model of TTF. Even though these hypotheses are based on both Greek and US samples as well as all three management levels, thereby transcending cultural and managerial differences, further research should be conducted to confirm our findings and improve our understanding of the types of CBIS that corporations should invest in to support managerial decision making. For example, studies based on observations or interviews, rather than survey questionnaires, should provide additional understanding.

Examination of the third hypothesis—that user evaluation of CBIS is not the same for managers from different cultures—led to support for this hypothesis. Even though some evidence suggests that TTF differences could be based on different technologies used in these countries, cultural differences, such as uncertainty avoidance, power distance, and collectivism vs individualism could also be explanatory. Explaining which cultural factors influence TTF would be a valuable contribution of future research.

The exploration of which specific decision-making activities are best supported by CBIS yielded similar results regardless of individual differences. In particular, resource allocation, evaluating alternatives, identifying problems and changes needed, and making short-term decisions had the best task-technology fit for all groups.

Stepping back from the specific results and reviewing the overall pattern of results suggests two additional conclusions. First, the overall level of TTF has considerable room for improvement. For US managers only one of fourteen managerial decision activities—identifying problems or issues—has a rating (5.3 or higher out of 7, or 76%) indicating that CBIS provide a 75% or better fit. For Greek managers this number is slightly higher at three of fourteen, with the three being evaluating the outcomes of each alternative, identifying problems or issues, and ranking the alternatives and choosing one. Top managers had five of fourteen—resource allocator, entrepreneur (identifying changes needed), identifying problems, evaluating alternatives, and ranking alternatives; middle managers had none; and first-line managers had five—identifying problems, generating alternatives, evaluating alternatives, ranking alternatives, and implementing a chosen alternative. Thus, much more work is needed to develop CBIS that strongly support managerial work.

Second, the number of similarities between different groups of managers (Greek and US as well as top, middle, and first-line) is greater than the number of differences. Even though individual differences to affect TTF, many managerial decision activities are supported by CBIS equally well for Greek and US managers (12 of 14) and different management levels (12 of 14). Likewise, several types of CBIS provide equally valuable support for both Greek and US managers (5 of 9) and different management levels (8 of 9). These similarities do not mean that differences can be ignored. However, they suggest that researchers and managers should find that their general understanding of managerial decision activities and the role of CBIS in supporting these activities will apply to a greater rather than a lesser degree at least across these two countries and management levels without a need to make major modifications based on cultural differences or management level.
