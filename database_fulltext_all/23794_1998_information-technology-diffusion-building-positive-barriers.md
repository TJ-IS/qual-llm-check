# Information technology diffusion: building positive barriers

R Baskerville<sup>1</sup> and J Pries-Heje<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30302-4015, USA; <sup>2</sup>Institute for Informatics and Management Accounting, Copenhagen Business School, Howitsvej 60, DK-2000 Frederiksberg, Denmark

This paper demonstrates how to build positive barriers to the diffusion of an information technology innovation. Positive barriers are desirable elements that disable the diffusion of an innovation to an undesired set of potential adopters. System developers are motivated in many ways to construct such positive barriers, for example, by security and privacy of proprietary innovations. This paper draws on the broad research that has illuminated the enabling factors in the diffusion of information technology, and shows how selected examples of these factors can also be applied in usefully constructing barriers against diffusion.

## Introduction

Diffusion of innovations (DoI) regards the process of adopting and implementing innovative products, such as newly developed information technologies. The purpose of this paper is to describe a reversed or inverted viewpoint of DoI in information technology (IT); that is, we ask, “What can stop an undesired DoI?” Most current research in this area views the issues from the position of how to promote or enable the DoI. Current work does not usually consider the application of these enabling concepts for the purposes of disabling DoI. From our reversed viewpoint, certain barriers to DoI are positive, or desirable in nature.

Why would we want to disable the diffusion of IT innovation? A number of reasons are described in more detail below, but examples include the retention of proprietary revenue-producing technology<sup>1</sup> such as design methodology, or privacy technology such as data encryption techniques. In consequence of this unusual application of DoI theory, this paper contributes an approach to devising sets of security safeguards that can be used to help protect inappropriate, proprietary or confidential innovations in IT from unplanned and undesired diffusion. Positive barriers to DoI represent a new theoretical basis (‘why’) and new implementation approach (‘how to’) for improvements in the security of IT innovations. The discussion also contributes an analysis of a previously unexplored risk category, viz., undesired DoI.

Traditionally information system (IS) research has focused on various means of legal protection to block undesired DoI. Copyright, for example, prevents copying of the expression of an idea, and patents give you the right to control a novel product or process. However, legal ways of blocking DoI are very expensive, these often taking a long time to set up, and the protection offered differs very much across the globe (for example the very different copyright laws in the US and Europe). Clearly some advancement beyond purely legal protection is needed.

Aside from its direct usefulness in constructing barriers to technology transfer, an understanding of this DoI blocking concept will lead to a better understanding of the currently prevailing enabling concept:

We need a different kind of diffusion study from those of the past, one that stresses identification of yet-uninvestigated aspects of diffusion. One such underinvestigated aspect would be diffusion studies that shed the pro-innovation bias. For balance, in fact, we need a number of diffusion researchers with an ‘anti-innovation bias’ in order to correct past tendencies. (Rogers, 1983, p 94)

This paper is organised into four parts. In the first section, we describe more detailed examples of the need for a ‘positive barrier perspective’: situations in which we desire to inhibit DoI. These examples are drawn broadly from IS-unit, intra-organization, and interorganization settings (cf. Prescott and Conger, 1995). In the second section, we review DoI theories of Rogers (1983) that are prevalent in the IT literature, and describe a four-element framework<sup>2</sup> for DoI. In the third section, we use this framework as a basis to advance a new model that not only regards DoI enabling, but also DoI blocking. Through this model we discover a set of barriers that can be used to inhibit diffusion of innovation. In the fourth section, we discuss the potential benefits of this model and briefly consider directions for future research that are opened by our positive barrier perspective.

## Motivation for positive barriers

The general orientation of DoI research deals with DoI factors from an enabling perspective. That is, the orientation is towards creating conditions in which technology is transferred or diffused within or between organizations. However, there are many real-world organizational situations in which the opposite is highly desirable. These situations call for the inhibition or prevention of DoI. A broad example of the desirability of such DoI inhibition can be found in nuclear non-proliferation agreements.

There are also situations in IT settings where the inhibition of DoI is desirable. Examples of such situations include the following.

## Proprietary technology

Consulting organizations use proprietary technology whose confidentiality preserves future revenue for the organization. For example, a firm may use its experience to create an highly effective, yet proprietary, systems development methodology. An unintentional transfer of the methodology itself, either to clients or competitors may damage the organization’s future revenues by improving the systems development capability of their clients or competitors and reducing demand for their specialised competencies.

Of course, few consultants have ‘secret’ methodologies, or try to hide these from their clients. Indeed knowledge about the methods make powerful marketing incentives to sell clients on the consultants’ services. However, it might be useful for the consultants to inhibit the transfer of the technology, its ‘diffusion’ into the client organization, even though members of the client organization are familiar with its details.

## Access technology

Access technology which distinguishes legitimate and illegitimate system behaviour also benefits from limits on its diffusion to potential abusers. For example, banking client–server technology enables access to digital cash, credit services, or funds transfers. Limited diffusion of the minimum technology necessary to begin breaching system security improves the safety of the system. Elementary parts of the technological competence necessary to breach the system security are placed only in the hands of legitimate clients with legitimate motives for system access.

Of course, barriers to DoI will not provide absolute security. Security designers would need to supplement DoI barriers with stronger access controls in most private information systems. However, limits placed on the ability to transfer technology increase the costs of breaching system security by encumbering the culprits’ technology acquisition process. Thus DoI barriers might be motivated for their role in improving the overall security profile of organizational IT. This limitation is discussed further in the final section of this paper.

## De-humanising technologies

Some system development environments, such as Visual BASIC or Delphi, may be undesirable in terms of job de-skilling. For example, software programmers may wish to prevent more artistically rewarding aspects of their work, such as the design and development of user interfaces, from being transferred to the users through database, client–server, visual environments or other prototyping technology. The migration of the high-level design aspects of these positions might leave behind a residue of less-rewarding tasks for the de-skilled programmer positions. In such cases, the programmers may wish to involve the users in the interface design process without fully transferring the technology necessary to complete the design without the programmers’ participation.

Of course, the programmers may lack the official authority in the decision about user-situated design. However, they may realistically exercise authority in the matter because they control the technology. If they can legitimately inhibit its transfer, they may preserve the humanising aspects of their positions and thereby promote the survival of the organization.

## Threatened technologies

Preserving threatened technologies from being supplanted by new technologies is desirable in some cases (especially in the Luddite sense). For example, COBOL programmers and vendors whose careers depend on their experience may wish to inhibit the transfer of alternative programming environments, such as C++, into the organization. Students leaving an academic setting often threaten obsolete technologies with potential transfer of newer technologies from their educational settings. Preserving older technologies preserves the status quo in such organizations.

Of course, the opposite case may arise, where students have trained in obsolete technologies and are introduced into practical settings that employ more recent technologies. The principle remains the same, however, the warrant is slightly different. In this case, the organizational need is to block the return of older technologies, the re-transfer of obsolete technologies that were eliminated earlier.

## Competing technologies

Vendors may wish to block the DoI of their competitors technology into potential client organizations. For example, a service organization that holds a system maintenance outsourcing agreement with a client may be competing with other vendors for the outsourcing of the same client’s systems development effort. An understanding of how to inhibit the transfer of competing technologies into the client organization would improve the potential opportunity for obtaining the development contract as well as the maintenance contract.

Of course, the vendor wishing to gain access to a client with a partially-existing current agreement could benefit from an understanding of how to block the DoI of the current provider. That is, it would improve the chances of the outside vendor (without the maintenance outsourcing agreement) if the technology of the inside vendor (with the maintenance agreement) could be effectively blocked from transferring into the development area.

Each of these situations illustrates a setting in which it is desirable to block or inhibit the transfer of IT into or within an organization. Each of these cases also reveals the political nature of the DoI process. The process sometimes embodies a power struggle between stakeholders: the consultants and vendors struggling with their competitors, programmers with vested interests in one technology struggling with others holding interests in alternative technologies, or system owners struggling with electronic vandals and thieves.

## Four elements in DoI

Rogers (1983) laid the foundations of DoI upon which much IS research has been built (cf. Saga & Zmud, 1994; Prescott & Conger, 1995). Rogers uses the terms diffusion and dissemination interchangeably, because the two ideas are difficult to distinguish in practice. Rogers defines DoI as:

The process by which

1. an innovation

2. is communicated through certain channels

3. over time

4. among the members of a social system (Rogers, 1983, p 10)

We will use these four key elements in Rogers’ definition as a framework within which to briefly consider current DoI theory. On the surface, this may seem to be unrealistically simple, however Rogers and others have developed each key element in extensive detail.

## Innovation

An innovation is a good service or idea that is perceived by someone as new (Kotler, 1988, p 439). The concept usually regards the consumer’s view of recently introduced products (cf. Rogers, 1979; Mowen, 1995), but extends to any other “unit of adoption” (Rogers, 1983, p 11). According to Rogers, it matters little that the idea is objectively new, only that the adopter perceives newness and reacts accordingly.

Rogers recognised that ‘innovation’ and ‘technology were often used as synonymous terms, defining technology as “a design for instrumental action that reduces the uncertainty in the cause–effect relationships involved in achieving a desired outcome” (Rogers, 1983, p 12).

Rogers limits the components of technology to hardware, which embodies the technology as material or physical objects, and software, consisting of the information base for the tool. Technology may be dominated by hardware, or in other cases may be entirely information.

It is clear that innovation is closely related to the development of IS because they often use recently introduced IT products such as computers, database software or networks. But IS innovation may also be dominated by the softer side, information, as for example, in development methodologies. Indeed, even if the systems themselves use older computer technology, they will often be perceived by the user community as innovations anyway.

## Communication

The communication element of DoI requires that participants reach a mutual understanding by creating and sharing information. Rogers suggests that this process leads to a convergence of shared meanings, or a divergence of separate meanings, that participants ascribe to events. The idea that communication consists of a simple message transfer is perhaps too simplistic for practical use in DoI theory (Rogers & Kincaid, 1981).

Rogers distinguished between two types of communication channel: mass media and interpersonal. Mass media regards broadcast by television, newspapers, etc., while interpersonal channels regard a face-to-face exchange between two or more individuals. Interpersonal channels are more effective in persuading an individual to adopt new ideas.

## Time

The time element regards the period during which an individual moves from first knowledge about the innovation through its adoption or rejection (Rogers, 1983, p 20). Two important aspects include the innovativeness in the adopter (the relative earliness or lateness of the adoption with respect to other adopters), and the rate of adoption in a population (the number of adoptions among potential adopters within a given time period).

Rogers recognises several aspects in the time dimension of DoI. These include five characteristics of the innovations that affect the rate of adoption, a five-step innovation–decision process, and five adopter innovativeness categories. The innovation–decision process has been the focus of a number of more recent IS-related studies, which are described by Prescott and Conger (1995) as ‘stage models’. These aspects are described in more detail below since these provide useful footing for the description of positive barriers.

## Social system

The social system constitutes a boundary within which an innovation diffuses (Rogers, 1983, p 24). It is made up of interrelated units, such as individuals, informal groups, or organizations, that collaborate to seek a common goal. Characteristics of the social system clearly affect the rate of adoption.

These characteristics include formal and informal social structure, norms, and opinion leaders (change agents). Prescott and Conger (1995) analyse the target system by distinguishing three impact loci: IS-unit, intraorganizational, and inter-organizational. These characteristics are used below to develop further positive barriers.

## Positive barriers model

The existing research on DoI, when reviewed from the perspective of an ‘anti-innovation’ bias, yields a multicomponent model that considers inhibition of DoI as one primary function. Two components that will be described in this paper are the innovation flow control matrix, and the influence-factor framework.

## Innovation flo control matrix

The DoI literature descriptively addresses the way in which innovations diffuse. It also instrumentally suggests how the flow of innovation diffusion might be controlled. Much of the DoI literature implies an instrumentalist orientation towards maximising the flow of diffusion. The goal is often the widest possible diffusion. This goal is narrowly fixed on a viewpoint we call the ‘push diffusion’ perspective. There are at least three contrasting perspectives illustrated in Figure 1, which represents a highly simplified map of two different types of drivers onto two different modes of innovation flow control. The developer of an innovation is one driver who may wish to influence the degree to which innovation flows. The adopter of an innovation may also wish to control, or drive the innovation flow process. Although there may be other stakeholders in this process, such as brokers, states or societies, the developer and adopter are primarily involved in every DoI act. The other dimension in Figure 1 regards the possible control modes engaged by the drivers. Although this is not always a binary value either, we will simplify our matrix and consider that the drivers are either in a mode that maximises diffusion by promoting transfer of an innovation, or a mode that minimizes diffusion by erecting barriers against diffusion. This could also be regarded as the chief goal of the actors involved in the process under analysis. The resulting four quadrants are (I) push diffusion, (II) captive innovation, (III) pull diffusion and (IV) blocked innovation. Each quadrant is discussed below.

The four quadrants are not equally explored by existing literature. Push diffusion (quadrant I) has been known since the 1950s. Its early form is sometimes called the ‘technology-push’ (Rothwell, 1992) or ‘conventional linear’ (Kline, 1985) model of DoI. In this model, innovation occurs in four stages: (1) scientific research, (2) development, (3) manufacturing/production, and (4) marketing. Later DoI models recognised that market- or need-pull was the genesis of innovation, suggesting that the innovation adopters controlled the DoI process (quadrant III). Under this pull diffusion assumption, the four-stage linear innovation process was preceded by “the perceived and sometimes clearly articulated customer need” (Rothwell, 1992, p 221). More recent work has dismissed the isolation of push from pull effects (Kline, 1985, p 36), and promoted an interactive model, also called the integrated model or the coupling model. The interactive model recognised the interaction of market-pull and technology-push in producing innovation during each of the four stages (research, development, production, marketing). In heavy industries, the interactive model suggested that suppliers and customers should be closely coupled within integrated product development teams. This means that successful DoI is promoted by the interaction of both developer influence in quadrant I and adopter influence in quadrant III.

Quadrants II and IV regard the intentional minimising of DoI by either the developer or the adopter. Much of the work regarding captive innovations (quadrant II) arises among the legal issues of copyrights, patents and licences. That is, developers are often seen to depend on legal remedies to protect their rights and to prevent undesired diffusion of their innovations. This mechanism actually functions by threatening the adopters with sanctions and thereafter depends on the adopters to avoid actively participating in DoI unless compensating the developers accordingly. The work regarding blocked innovation (quadrant IV) typically regards such blocks as dysfunctional, for example, characterising them as ‘resistance to change’ among users.

Our understanding of quadrants II and IV is not as well-developed as our understanding of quadrants I and III. The DoI models focus on maximised diffusion by instrumental measures in diffusion push–pull forces. The major viewpoint of quadrant II is limited to rightsprotection and quadrant IV is viewed as an obstacle to be overcome, where necessary, through a participative socialisation process (or an alternative structure of simple sanctions and rewards). Yet the motives discussed above suggest that there may be justifiable reasons for erecting barriers to DoI, and further work in these two quadrants is needed. In the remainder of this paper, we will focus on models and mechanisms that operate in quadrant II, captured innovations. In this quadrant, we analyse the means (other than the well-known legal means) by which developers might erect barriers to limit the undesired flow of innovations.

## Influence-facto framework

Figure 2 depicts a four-element framework for considering positive barriers as factors in DoI. This framework arises from Rogers’ (1983) four elements, innovation, communications, time and social system, as discussed above. The degree of influence illustrates the typical ability of the parties (developers and adopters) in the diffusion process to manipulate the elements and control the diffusion process. The two parties represent the innovators (developers, at the top of the figure) and the potential adopters (adopters, at the bottom of the figure). The innovation itself is largely under the influence of the developers, while the social system is largely under the influence of the adopters. Communications and time are influenced by both parties. Developers have slightly more control over the communications typically owing to their control over mass media communications. Adopters have slightly more control over time usually owing to their control over their own innovativeness in adopting the developers’ innovations.

The elements of the framework in Figure 2 are divided into two sections. The left-hand section lists selected frameworks or models from the innovation literature that have been presented from an enabling perspective. The right-hand section lists some examples of ways in which these enabling factors may be inverted in order to inhibit or disable DoI. These right-hand items are thus examples of positive barriers to DoI.

In order to illustrate how positive barriers can be derived from enabling factors, we will select the three enabling factors from the literature. These three elements are those most relevant to developers. We will show how these can be used to erect positive barriers.

Rogers’ DoI definition yields a framework that is one of the most comprehensive and enduring, and has had an important effect on research into the diffusion of IT innovations. We use it to frame our discussion below because of its familiarity. However, its analytical scope is limited. It provides a framework, but not a model. The concepts, while familiar and useful, are by no means categorical. Features of the innovation product, for example, will affect the communications, diffusion timing, and relate to the social system of the adopters. Likewise, the social system of the adopters will also impact the timing of the diffusion and the communications paths regarding the diffusion. Also, positive barriers currently in use (given as examples below) often combine features from several of the framework concepts, gaining synergy and increasing the effectiveness of the complete barrier package. Each of our examples necessarily overlaps several elements from Rogers’ definition. Most of the examples are taken from our research activities and illustrate quadrant II, captive innovation barriers. These were chosen to avoid suppositions about the motives underlying the erection of the positive barriers. Illustrations from quadrant IV are problematic (owing to complex underlying conflicts between adopters, adopting organizations, and developers). These problems will be discussed in the section on future research.

## Building DoI barriers

The area influenced most by developers includes innovation and communication. The developer can also influence time, to a slightly more limited extent. We will consider how an example of each of these elements can be inverted into a positive barrier by developers.

## Innovation

Research regarding the diffusion-enabling factors that are properties of the innovations themselves is most strongly developed in the marketing literature. Diffusion enabling features in products can be considered using three levels (Kotler, 1988, pp 445– 446):

1. The core product is the fundamental part of the product—the thing that the buyer is really buying because this core will benefit him or her.

2. The tangible product consists of the features, styling, quality, packaging and the brand name that goes with the core product.

3. The augmented product is an even more expansive bundle. The augmentation may include such things as the physical installation, after sales service, warranty, delivery and credit.

For example, a personal computer sold in Denmark (a very competitive and essentially low-price market) includes all three levels of diffusion-enabling elements of a product. The core part of the PC is the machine itself with a processor, floppy disk drive, CD-ROM drive, hard disk, two small loudspeakers and a monitor. However, since this product is targeted for home-users, the core product includes some pre-installed software on the hard disk like MS-DOS, Microsoft Windows and Works, Lotus Organizer and some Multimedia Sound Software. The tangible product consists of several additional features such as a music and video database, a tax program, a number of games plus two CD-ROMs. Additional features include a computer installation guide, a user guide to the monitor, a software installation guide, and introductory manuals to the pre-installed software. Finally, as an augmentation, the package included three months free hotline service together with a special offer for an extra year. This offer was bundled with a subscription to a well-known PC magazine.

These diffusion enabling elements suggest indirectly how barriers might be erected that block undesired diffusion. The core product can be altered to make it less useful to undesired adopters. The tangible product can be packaged so that it is attractive only to the desired adopters, and less interesting to the undesired adopters. Finally the augmented product can be made such that undesired diffusion is inhibited.

The producers of this particular product have, in fact, erected such positive barriers. The core product includes many megabytes of Danish-language software, such as Windows. This both enables diffusion of the product to desired adopters (Danish market) while inhibiting diffusion into an undesired market (like Germany or Italy where a different pricing scheme is in place). The tangible product does not include originals of the pre-loaded software on diskette or CD. This raises barriers to illegal copying of the software by making additional work for anyone wanting to ‘borrow’ a copy of the bundled software from the authorised adopter (it takes several hours to create ‘borrowable’ diskettes). The augmented product includes a hot-line telephone support service. However, this service verifies serial numbers and telephone numbers when contacted, thus erecting barriers against diffusion to users of other vendors’ equipment.

## Communication barriers

As noted earlier, external communications is strongly influenced by the developer. Kwon (1990) confirmed the presence of a significant, positive association between external communication, rather than internal communication, for the infusion of IT in an organization. This only applied in circumstances where a favourable MIS climate existed. This work indirectly suggests to us that barriers might be erected by developers to prevent DoI into organizations with a favourable MIS climate by limiting external communications.

Drawing from Rogers’ work discussed earlier, a positive barrier could be derived by erecting barriers to external communication with a particular focus on the more powerful interpersonal channels. Such channels could be limited in number, and in choice of media. For example, the number of channels could be limited by a developer policy that all communications regarding a particular innovation must be channelled through a single communications link with the adopter, such as the account executive or salesperson. Proscribing, for example, direct contact between developer software engineers and customer-users would decrease the DoI to the customers. The media choice could be limited by requiring such communications to be either in telephone, post, or fax. Proscribing email, for example, prevents easy electronic distribution among customer departments. Together, the two prohibitions limit the number of channels and media variety, and form a positive barrier to DoI.

An example of how such techniques are currently in use can be found in the DoI Barriers erected by Textware, a Danish software producer specialising in dictionary preparation software. In 1989 they had developed a very innovative piece of software that could help a publisher prepare and structure all the text for a dictionary, usually saving several man-years. The problem related to the fact that the software could run on almost any ordinary PC. It was therefore both very easy to copy and outwardly comparable to inexpensive, massmarket products like spreadsheets or word processors.

The reality of the dictionary publisher’s market, however, was that only a few hundred customers existed for a product that had required a substantial investment. Textware had to erect positive barriers to prevent uncontrolled diffusion of their innovation between and within the dictionary publishers (making it impossible for Textware to recover their investments). Textware erected a positive diffusion barrier by limiting its external communications. Textware used no mass-media, but communicated with major European and American publishers one-by-one. Each publisher was handled separately, and in some cases both the dictionary design system (and the related price) was tailored specifically for individual publishers. By avoiding mass-media communications and tailoring the product, Textware was building a positive communication barrier.

Furthermore the dictionary preparation software was bundled with hardware (a PC+ dedicated systems software) making it a so-called dictionary design workplace. The decision was then only to sell the workplace as a whole and thereby raising an additional positive innovation barrier. This positive barrier prevented pirate copying of the software within the social system (the publisher) to which it was sold. At the same time this bundling decision circumvented the comparison with inexpensive mass-market software, making Textware able to price the workplace according to the huge savings that the publisher could obtain. Textware solved their problem by combining two positive barriers, a communication barrier and an innovation barrier, thereby achieving the synergy that we were discussing above (Pries-Heje, 1989, pp 62–69).

While the foregoing discussion represents the developers’ control (quadrant II, captured innovation) communication may also be influenced by the adopter (quadrant IV, blocked innovation). The adopter has more control over internal communications rather than external communications. Kwon (1990) suggested that communication related to MIS opinion leaders might be crucial: “MIS change agents should identify MIS opinion leaders as target customers, monitors, and/or sponsors.” (p 146). This work indirectly suggests that limiting communications of opinion leaders to those connected with desired innovations would erect a positive barrier to DoI.

Drawing once more from Rogers’ characterisation, the powerful interpersonal communications channels could be directed by the adopters to desired innovations and away from undesired innovations. For example, the adopting organization could carefully direct opinion leaders into interpersonal situations with desired innovations by sponsoring associated conference travel or training courses. The organization could likewise admit visits by representatives and technicians associated with desired innovations while excluding visits by those associated with undesired innovations.

## Time

The three aspects in the time dimension of DoI developed by Rogers (1983) are summarised in Table 1. This work has been further developed and applied in the information systems literature. For example, Kwon and Zmud (1987) further develop Rogers into six stages: initiation, decision, adaptation, acceptance, routinisation and infusion. Such IT studies are summarised by Prescott and Conger (1995) as “factor research studies” (such as innovation or adopter characteristics) and the previously mentioned stage research studies (such as the innovation-decision steps). This work indirectly suggests to us that barriers might be erected by developers to prevent DoI into other organizations by eliminating DoIenabling characteristics from the product. In other words, these analyses help us to understand how to slow down the diffusion process by (using Kwon & Zmud’s stages) delaying the initiation and decision phases, prolonging the adaptation and acceptance process, and inhibiting the routinisation and infusion processes.

For example, we could use Rogers’ innovation characteristics (Table 1) to derive positive barriers: mask its relative advantage, limit its compatibility with the values and experiences of undesired adopters, make it appear difficult to use, make it unavailable for experimentation and keep the results of its use invisible to others. For example, a proprietary development methodology might be contained from DoI by making it incompatible with well known methods, perhaps by using unusual notation; or by not disclosing intermediate analyses, thereby keeping the results invisible.

An example of how such techniques are currently in use can be found in the design of an innovative textbook software product, HyperCase, published in conjunction with a systems analysis textbook (Baskerville et al, 1992). The authors desired to contain the DoI such that it diffused easily, but only in conjunction with the textbook. Easy adoption of the software without the textbook could negatively affect textbook sales. Generally, the developers limited the compatibility by using specialist language from the textbook and embedding many references to specific pages in the textbook. In many cases, the HyperCase software was written such that it closely related to unique features of the book. As a result, the use of the software with other textbooks would lead to ill-defined terms and poorly developed concepts. This incompatibility and the perception of complexity without the related book erected a precise, positive barrier to DoI.

Similarly to our discussion of communication barriers, the above discussion represents the developers’ control (quadrant II, captured innovation) although timing may also be influenced by the adopter (quadrant IV, blocked innovation). The adopter has a large degree of influence over the rate and timing of adoption under DoI. This is largely a factor of the placement of the adoption decision within their realm. This fact is strongly illustrated in Saga and Zmud’s (1994) model of the routinisation process (see Figure 3). Among the variables in this causal model are the earliness of the adoption, work formalisation, and manager intervention. These policies directly influence the administrative infrastructure and the frequency of use, and indirectly determine the standardised and normal use. The infrastructure includes “policies or formal rules associated with an application’s use and the establishment of personnel positions or budgetary lines directly linked to the application, its training, its support or its use.” (p 76). This work indirectly suggests that such policies and rules could support only desired innovations and could erect a positive barrier to undesired DoI.

Note that acceptance of the technology occurs before routinisation. Thus the organization has committed to use the innovation at this time. How can the potential adopters possibly erect positive barriers after organizational acceptance? It is possible to formalise the work by allocating resources such that the frequency of use drops. Further, managerial intervention could promote alternative solutions lowering frequency of use still further. Also, managers could define an administrative infrastructure that discourages standardised use of the innovation.

## Social system

The social system is largely under the influence of the adopters, and positive barriers erected using this locus are located in quadrant IV, blocked innovation. In order to consider the DoI enabling factors (and consequently the positive barriers) relating to the social system, we tie Rogers’ (1983) three aspects of social structure (social norms, opinion leaders and change agents) together with Prescott and Conger’s (1995) three loci of DoI (IS-unit, intra-organizational and interorganizational).

Social structure consists of formal structure and informal structure. System norms are the established behaviour patterns (a range of, or guide to tolerable behaviour) for the members of a social system. Opinion leadership is the degree to which an individual is able to influence other individuals’ attitudes or overt behaviour informally in a desired way with relative frequency. A change agent is an individual who influences clients’ innovation decisions in a direction deemed desirable by a change agency. The IS-unit locus includes IS work groups like applications developers, computer operations, or systems support. The intra-organizational locus includes one or more units of an organization, but the locus is within the organization. The inter-organizational locus involves DoI across multiple companies.

Prescott and Conger (1995) highlight five constructs that are particularly interesting for further study. All these relate closely to Rogers’ (1983) social system elements. These constructs are context, culture, communications channel uses, champions, and fit. Each of these constructs indirectly suggest mechanisms by which positive barriers might be erected to inhibit undesired DoI.

An example of such social system barriers is found in Couger’s (1986) description of the success of ‘soft controls’. Couger compares eleven large companies with regard to the manner in which they controlled the diffusion of incompatible desktop computer technology throughout the organization. Couger found that companies with ‘hard controls’ (policies that dictated certain desktop computer configurations) had no more compatibility than companies with no controls. However, companies with ‘soft controls’ had a larger degree of compatibility. Soft controls involved incentives that attracted adopters to certain desktop computer configurations, such as subsidised prices through vendor bulk-purchase agreements, free internal upgrade installation, and strong internal user support. These were balanced with poor support and no pre-established vendor contracts for other configurations. To a large degree, the undesired DoI is barred by depending on a corporate culture that does not link to that innovation, by preventing its champions, and by creating a social context in which it is ‘harder’ in every way to adopt the undesired innovation.

## Discussion and future research

There are a number of directions in which work can proceed from the positive barriers model of DoI. In this section we focus on some of the more practical aspects that can be learnt from studying the model, and several of the research directions that advance from this model.

## Learning from the model

The application of this model to possible DoI scenarios yields some good indications of the model’s explanatory and heuristic potential. It helps explain why DoI occurs or does not occur in desired-DoI settings, undesired-DoI settings, and settings in which there is a conflict over the desirability of the DoI.

The model can be applied in three steps.

## Modes determination

The DoI setting must be investigated to determine the predominant mode or goal of both the innovation developer and adopter using the innovation flow control matrix. If the developer and the adopter are both in a diffusion mode, then a desired-DoI setting is indicated. This will be called a ‘type one’ setting. If the developer and the adopter are both in a barrier mode, then an undesired-DoI setting is indicated. This will be called a ‘type two’ setting. If one of the DoI parties is in a diffusion mode, while the other party is in a barrier mode, then a conflicting-DoI setting is indicated. Conflicting-DoI settings will be called ‘type three’ and ‘type four’ settings. A type three setting is one which the adopter is in diffusion mode and the developer is in barrier mode. This means the setting spans quadrant II and quadrant III in Figure 1, and the developer and adopter have conflicting goals. A type four setting is one in which the adopter is in barrier mode and the developer is in diffusion mode. This means the setting spans quadrant I and quadrant IV in Figure 1, and, similarly to type three settings, the developer and adopter have conflicting goals.

## Matrix indications

The control flow matrix (Figure 1) indicates the degree to which the positive barriers model is applicable to the setting, and how the influence-factor framework can be applied. A type on setting, (desired-DoI) indicates an interactive DoI setting such as that modelled by Kline (1985) and discussed earlier in this paper. This is considered to be an ideal DoI situation, and the positive barriers model has little to contribute beyond the existing literature on the subject. In type one settings the positive barriers model leads to little additional learning and has little heuristic value. However, if there is a type two setting (undesired-DoI), we find a situation that is not well understood in the literature. The flow control matrix suggests that the setting is still one of ‘interaction’, ‘integration’, and ‘coupling’ between developer and adopter, however, the predominant goal of the parties is the prevention, rather than the promotion, of DoI. In this setting, the influence-factor framework will help to understand the properties of the setting, and indicate possible strategies for further developing barriers to DoI. A type three setting approximates the classic ‘resistance-tochange’ situation, where adopters are not accepting a proposed innovation. This setting also represents a situation in which the early linear, conventional DoI models break down. Like the type two setting, the influencefactor framework will help to understand the properties of a type three setting, and indicate possible strategies for further developing barriers to DoI. A type four setting is similar to a property-rights dispute, where the developer is guarding an innovation to prevent its loss to competitors. The influence-factor framework will also be useful in this setting to help understand the properties of the setting, and indicate possible strategies for further developing barriers to DoI.

## Influence-facto indications

The influence-factor framework (Figure 2) indicates that type one and type two settings are similar in that developer and adopter influence is likely to be evenly applied across the same four factor sets. In type one settings we could expect to find many of the enabling factors from all the areas represented by the left-hand column of Figure 2. Further development of strategies for these settings would also be suggested by these examples. In type two settings, we could expect to find many of the barriers from all the areas represented by the right-hand column of Figure 2. Again, this framework can be used as an heuristic to suggest further strategies for this setting. A type three setting would present a developer exerting influence towards diffusion, and employing enabling factors exemplified by the upper left-hand column for Figure 2. At the same time, the adopter would be erecting barriers such as those suggested in the lower right-hand column of Figure 2. Aside from explaining why specific enabling factors and barriers can be found in the setting, the framework suggests specific strategies for each of the parties: developer concentration on enabling innovation and communication; adopter concentration on barriers in the areas of time and the social system. A type four setting would present a developer erecting barriers exemplified by the upper right-hand column of Figure

2, and the adopter pressing forward the enabling factors suggested by the lower left-hand column of Figure 2. The framework will help explain why the particular combination of enabling factors and barriers are characterising the setting, and also suggest strategies. In type four settings, the developer should be blocking innovation and communication, while the adopter could promote DoI by improving related time factors and social system aspects.

An example of a scenario will help illustrate the use of the positive barrier model. Consider a setting where a developing country wishes to inhibit the widespread diffusion of a new technology until an economically acceptable domestic support infrastructure is available. An example might be a new software product that would entail a foreign support system with unattractive balance-of-payments and unemployment implications. National interests might be served by inhibiting DoI unti a domestic support system is constructed. However, since the developers will lose revenue while the infrastructure is constructed, they will directly or indirectly promote the product anyway. Using the positive barrier model’s flow control matrix, those involved in the developing country could determine that a type three setting is indicated, with quadrant I and quadrant IV engaged from Figure 1. The analysis based on the influence-factor framework should particularly focus on how the developers are enabling time-related and socialsystem-related factors, since these are under the strong influence of the adopter. If, for example, the developers were only weakly enabling time-related factors, the party from the developing country could disable the DoI by erecting solid barriers from the time area. The framework indicates that the adopters can expect to have more influence over time-related elements than the developers. Questions that could lead to such a strategy are suggested by the influence-factor framework. These include: ‘Can we change the relative advantage and how the innovation will work? Can we make it less “triable” and thereby slow the diffusion? Can we make the advantages less observable to anyone outside the target group (those that we desire to block from getting the diffusion)?, etc. An evaluated inventory of possible barriers results. The analysis should lead to a mix of barriers selected from this evaluated inventory. Perhaps the selected mix would represent all the key areas in order to maximise the overall resistance to undesired diffusion, or perhaps it will focus on one key area, such as time in this example, in order to cripple the undesired DoI.

This scenario also illustrates how the simple surface features of Rogers’ model are inadequate on their own for applying these positive barrier concepts. Rather, the full force of Rogers’ underlying details, along with more recent supporting research (e.g., Rogers, 1983; Saga & Zmud, 1994; Prescott & Conger, 1995), is needed to understand, analyse and design positive barriers.

## Future research

This paper responds to Rogers’ call for diffusion studies with an ‘anti-innovation’ bias. This response required an initial groundwork that framed the diffusion perspectives in four quadrants defined by diffusion drivers and their modes. The literature on innovation barriers is dominated by legal sanctions and means for overcoming user resistance. Generally, blocking innovations is perceived in a negative light, an obstacle for progress or a mechanism for theft prevention. There are many positive motives for erecting barriers to DoI. These motives include protection of proprietary technology, limited distribution of access technology or de-humanising technologies, and the protection of threatened technologies.

DoI research intended to describe and promote DoI can be viewed in a reverse sense to develop barriers to limit DoI. To illustrate this potential reversal, we have used Rogers’ (1983) four elements of DoI as an overall framework for DoI barriers, along with more detailed models from Kwon and Zmud (1987), Saga and Zmud (1994), and Prescott and Conger (1995). Positive barriers have been illustrated by examples taken from our own empirical research and from the systems management literature. We have also demonstrated how the positive barriers model can explain and predict, i.e., aid in the analysis of problem settings and lead to the design and implementation of positive barriers.

This work opens several avenues for the progress of future research. More work is particularly needed in quadrant IV, blocked innovations (Figure 1). These barriers can be erected in the social systems of the adopters, perhaps beyond the control of both the innovation developers and the adopters’ organisational management. New frameworks and heuristics are needed to help understand how these barriers are erected and how these can be controlled (both for the purposes of enabling or blocking DoI). This may also demand basic research into the complex underlying conflicts between adopters (or adopting organizations) and developers (or developing organizations), as mentioned earlier in this paper.

A number of researchers have looked at barriers at the strategic level (as opposed to the level of the innovation where we focus). For example, Porter (1980) developed a model of five forces in the competitive structure of an industry. The forces are: (1) threat of new entrants, (2) bargaining power of suppliers, (3) bargaining power of buyers, (4) threat of substitute products, and (5) rivalry among existing competitors. An analysis of each one of the five forces may lead to the identification of positive barriers at the industry level. It is possible that any of these more general barriers at the industry level (e.g. raising switching cost for buyers to minimise the threat of new entrants), may have an equivalent positive barrier at the innovation level (e.g., building switching costs into the innovation). However it will require further research to investigate all the possible links between the possible strategic barriers and the barriers we have examined at the innovation level.

Further theoretical research could develop other aspects of the DoI risk beyond the models discussed in this paper. This research might help develop a more complete inventory of related barriers. Additional empirical work is needed to demonstrate how this perspective can be applied in practice, and to evaluate the conditions under which certain types of DoI barriers are effective in disabling undesired DoI. There are also interesting socio-political possibilities for the use of positive barriers. One example is the use of positive barriers for slowing down technological diffusion into developing countries until the technology can be ‘delinked’ from its developers. Once full ownership and the support infrastructure has crossed the borders, then the barriers could be removed and the technology allowed to diffuse freely within the country. We need further empirical evidence to determine if such barriers can operate successfully in controlling premature transborder DoI.
