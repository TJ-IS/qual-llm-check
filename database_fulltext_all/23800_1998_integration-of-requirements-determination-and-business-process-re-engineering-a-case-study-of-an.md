# Integration of requirements determination and business process re-engineering: a case study of an Ambulatory Care and Diagnostic (ACAD) centre

JAA Sillince and G Harindranath

School of Management, Royal Holloway, University of London, Egham, Surrey TW20 0EX, UK

This paper explores the political character of business process re-engineering (BPR) and its associated information systems (IS) change. This political character means that the scope of business processes and their associated IS, the scale and type of change and the evaluation of BPR success are subject to different interpretations depending on the role and interests of those stakeholders involved with them. Therefore although BPR and IS change are functionally related, even coupled processes, they nevertheless react to different political factors and thus do not flow along in a synchronised, parallel way. Rather, they depend upon other stakeholders, and the choice of which stakeholders to give importance to is greatly influenced by their perceived usefulness in the change process. In the case studied, IS, BPR, the consortium and the builder were all vital to the project’s success, whereas staff interests were excluded or manipulated because they were seen as resisting change. Managing change processes and stakeholders during a re-engineering project therefore involves the motivating and controlling of several parallel processes, some of which are advancing while others are blocked. The reason why some processes and stakeholders gain a voice in the project depends on their ability to reduce uncertainty and thus to move the project forward.

## Introduction

Business process re-engineering (BPR) and information systems (IS) are usually associated because IS is either an enabler or a driver of organizational change, or because BPR creates a need for new IS. However, the assumption in much of the literature is that they are synonymous. The assumption arises from the fact that the relationship between BPR and IS was not well-understood—for example, ascribing too much importance to the IS function. Another problem has been that the political nature of BPR has often been underestimated so that IS became regarded as a technical part of what was seen as a predictable and controllable BPR process. In reality the two processes together with other processes often occur out of synchrony, causing problems of managing BPR–IS projects.

Observers differ about the relative contribution of IS in BPR, between IS as an enabler and IS as a driver of change. Davenport (1993) claims that IS is an enabler and implementer of BPR. This view has been that computer-based tools help to control and systematise BPR. For example, process simulation (Morris & Brandon, 1993; Macarthur et al, 1994) drawing process models (Davenport & Short, 1990), and developing and testing new processes in special computer-controlled laboratories (Sutherland, 1992) have all been proposed as essential BPR techniques. A more ambitious programme for IS is as a driver of change—for example, Davenport and Short (1990) describe a case in which BPR was ‘IS driven’, and Heygate and Brebach (1991), Shillingford (1992) and Aikins (1993) describe particular types of IS which have an inbuilt process-reforming rationale, such as workflow software and document image processing hardware. Hammer and Champy (1993) state that ‘IS’s importance is difficult to overstate’, and examine specific IS examples such as shared databases, expert systems and telecommunications. This view has been challenged, however. Warren (1993) has suggested that ‘BPR and IS are not synonymous’. Similarly BPR is ‘the commonsense practice of redesigning business processes before bringing in technology’ (FinTech, 1992a) and BPR ‘does not have to involve IS at all’ (FinTech, 1992b). Even in BPR projects where IS was centrally involved, there may be sufficient preoccupation with cost-saving to reduce IS’s strategic potential (Coombs & Hull, 1995). Avgerou et al (1995) suggest that the marginal role of IS in BPR provides a challenge to the IS professional to attempt to incorporate organizational design more into its professional procedures.

Although BPR has a specific method, it has not yet been linked within a methodology with IS development. The attempt to integrate has come more from the practical IS development methodologies community (Goodland & Mclean, 1997), where the extension of methodologies such as SSADM into business processes has gone some way toward integration (Goodland & Slater, 1995). Such extensions usually suggest a business activity model should be used to define the business viewpoints, processes, triggers, and decisions. This has been extended to a work practice model which identifies the agents who are responsible for performing the business processes and the place within the organization where such processes occur. However SSADM and other IS methodologies have not been completely successful in their application to the management of change within organizations (Willcocks, 1995). Other approaches to BPR–IS methodology integration also exist, but have as yet not been evaluated. The key problem with creating an integrated methodology is that IS methodologies are all life cycle-based and sequential—as shown in the CASE tools for BRP such as APACHE (Born, 1993) or CADDIE (Farhoodi, 1993)—whereas BPR has much more of a to-and-fro movement as in political argumentation, where changes in direction depend upon evidence, confidence and convincing use of argument. Because of the political nature of BPR it may be that methodologies as understood by IS staff are too structured and serial (Simsion, 1994), and that principles or softer methods such as power audits and ends-mean analysis (Willcocks & Smith, 1995) or social action theory (Hirschheim et al, 1991) should be used. One suggested method of integration may be to include rapid IS development (prototyping) within a BPR process (Kelleher, 1995). These attempts to relate IS and BPR motivate our study, which seeks to find the nature of linkages between IS, BPR, and other processes of change.

## The aims of ACAD

The context for this research is a major health care project at the Central Middlesex Hospital NHS Trust (CMT), an acute hospital in the National Health Service (NHS) employing 1300 people and with an annual turnover of £50 m. Funding, amounting to £16 m, had become available through land sales and central government funding through the Private Finance Initiative (PFI) for the Trust to establish an Ambulatory Care and Diagnostic (ACAD) centre adjacent to CMT. Five theatres for 20 000 cases per year were planned. This form of rapid service delivery and medical process redesign was adventurous but politically controversial and still at an early stage of diffusion into acute care, following successful experimental schemes of world class quality in Australia, Switzerland and the USA (the Mayo Clinic).

ACAD required both radical changes to business and medical processes and social structures, because it focused on rapid throughput and computerised scheduling. Also ACAD required a careful relating of new systems to existing hospital functions and IS. The main top level business processes of ACAD were: to educate and prepare the patient; to regulate and direct referrals to improve predictability; to schedule on the basis of units of time, taking power from doctors and giving it to schedulers; to use predictable flows and processes to design jobs and workloads; to develop system, machine, manpower and building use to maximise effectiveness of jobs and clinical process flows; and to manage the patient quickly back into the community. Although ACAD was planned to be in a new building, it was to be sited within a much larger hospital complex (CMT) and draw staff largely from there. The total ensemble therefore represented a case of BPR since CMT’s existing working practices were envisaged as radically altering due to the new centre.

The IS needed had to network with general practitioners (GPs), the main hospital at CMT and other NHS care centres, and had to solve complex scheduling problems, in order to shorten patient care from typically 4 – 10 days to one day.

The approach not only required radical restructuring of working practices but it also required new technology—for example MRI and CT scanning which enable rapid interactional diagnosis. The ACAD design was intended to facilitate the performance of imagingguided interventions, involving, for example, clinical procedures requiring imaging and endoscopy, or imaging and surgery.

## The ACAD project organization

A software developer, a hardware manufacturer, and a builder were involved as a consortium in ACAD together with CMT. One of CMT’s directors, the director of contracts and clinical activities, was also given the role of ACAD project manager. He acted as chairman of the ACAD steering committee, which included the chief executive, chairman and other six directors of CMT, together with representatives from the other consortium partners, the ACAD architect, and the software requirements team.

There were various groups within ACAD. The design group commissioned architects who began design work on the new building between June 1996 and January 1997 based on a master plan, intending to commence construction by April 1997. The IS group commissioned a software requirements team to produce a requirements document by April 1997, with the intention of software development by the consortium partner responsible after that date. The negotiation group dealt with contracts and agreements between the consortium partners.

## The case study

On 25th July 1996 CMT held a ‘Timeout Conference’ at a London hotel, at which CMT directors and managers, and representatives from the ACAD architect and software requirements team were present. The purpose was to share the key assumptions behind ACAD, and evaluate and agree a final contract date for the ACAD project. As it happened, a contract date was not decided, because at this meeting some of the uncertainties about IS began to surface (see Figure 1, column 1):

The major area of uncertainty is the IS working group. No finalised master contract is possible until all IS issues have been satisfactorily concluded. (director of finance, CMT)

At a steering group meeting of 30th July 1996, it was agreed that the building and engineering group was the key ‘project driver’. The contract between the consortium and CMT was to be signed by April 1997. The meeting also agreed the main rooms needed in the new ACAD building, such as five operating theatres, thirty recovery beds, etc, and reported on several activity simulation exercises performed by an operational research consultancy. Also the high level business processes were defined. Other decisions were made relating to re-engineering of clinical processes.

In particular one area of increased certainty (see Figure 1, column 2) was the four criteria for determining what medical services were to be included into ACAD. The certainty of the building process enabled the consortium stakeholder to become more involved in the project. Thus the building and the consortium were linked in a fundamental way:

The consortium only gets £3 million out of £18 million. So let’s concentrate on the building and engineering of ACAD and give the consortium a simple reassurance of their involvement. (director of contracts & clinical activity, CMT)

Despite this decisiveness with regard to the building process some uncertainties were aired (Figure 1, column 2). One fear was that the work culture of the new ACAD centre would be contaminated by association with existing services at CMT:

Will transfer of many existing out patient services decrease the possibilities of radically re-engineered care being established in ACAD? (director of contracts & clinical activity, CMT)

This exclusion of existing staff (nurses, doctors, GPs) was seen as central to the re-engineering objective:

We should not run the project and plan on the basis of reactionary and negative tendencies of consultants and nurses %The consultants will do as they’re told–it’s a process of re-education–they’re all young. (chairman, CMT)

Another area of uncertainty was the low utilisation rate of imaging equipment, a concern which emphasised the central importance of profitability:

The low utilisation rate of imaging equipment implies where do you put them? Putting them in CMT will compromise ACAD’s philosophy of slick flow. Putting them in ACAD means wastage of resources due to the low utilisation rate. (director of contracts & clinical activity, CMT)

IS was still a major source of uncertainty (Figure 1, column 1). The IS group met on 31st July 1996 to consider the following problem:

The most important uncertainty is IS from [the software developer in the Consortium] who will not sign a deal with us until after the Election [Spring 1997]. (director of contracts & clinical activity, CMT)

This problem raised all sorts of chicken-and-egg uncertainties due to the inter-dependence of IS and building design. Thus although change projects involve parallel processes and stakeholders, these streams are not independent of one another:

The building itself will depend to an extent on what kind of activities IS can do. (software engineer, requirements team)

A project review meeting was held on 12th August 1996. It was decided that the steering committee should concentrate on the building design over the following eight weeks. The chicken-and-egg problem remained as a source of questions or shaky assumptions (Figure 1, column 1) about staff numbers:

IS will bring staff numbers down. (director of contracts & clinical activity, CMT)

## and building design:

What are the implications of IS for building design? They must include: (1) image storage and retrieval; (2) medical records (electronic or not); and (3) patient scheduling. (software engineer, requirements team)

A process modelling meeting was held by the software engineer from the requirements team on 13th August, to develop high level processes and a context diagram. At that meeting it was announced that the consortium building partner had signed a draft agreement with CMT, and that the consortium partner responsible for software development was worried that it was being pushed into signing a deal in December 1996 without any clarity on what kind of system was required.

On 15th August a meeting was held to discuss ‘stepdown’ facilities (beds for those needing to recover from treatment). The position taken was that ACAD was a rapid treatment and recovery facility and was designed to be closed at night. Moreover, the space requirement was too large for ACAD, where there were higher priorities for space. A private hotel was considered and rejected on the grounds that it would compromise access to emergency medical services. It was decided to put the step-down facilities into the main hospital, although it was acknowledged that it still had not been decided what specialties would eventually be put into ACAD and which would be left in the main hospital or even closed. This led to the following criticism (Figure 1, column 1):

Building design is leading IS rather than the other way around. (software engineer, requirements team)

There was a project review meeting on the 19th August which gave rise to some uncertainties about the relationship between the old (CMT) and new (ACAD) buildings (Figure 1, column 2).

An IS subgroup meeting was held on 21st August to agree the project’s IS terms of reference, which were suggested in a report written by the software engineer from the requirements team. These comprised a high level project plan for IS including the relationship between the building design and IS requirements and setting out the main deliverables.

At this meeting the relationship between ACAD and the main CMT hospital was discussed (Figure 1, column 2):

Who pays for external links from ACAD and what’s ACAD’s boundary? My own view is that what’s most important is that CMT should fit into ACAD, not ACAD fitting into CMT. This means that CMT will incur extra costs. (clinical director of anaesthetics, CMT)

Also the scheduling options were touched on. There were divided views between those who wanted JIT scheduling, with patient entry, the entire movement and discharge electronically recorded and scheduled, and those who thought that just entry and discharge data would be sufficient for scheduling to be effective.

The software engineer from the requirements team held an IS group workshop on the 30th August which introduced a process model. There was a March 1997 start date for building work, and the building design was to be frozen by the end of September 1996. Despite all this increase in certainty of IS, concern was expressed that the building design was driving IS design and not the other way around (Figure 1, column 1):

Are we having paper? Will it come over the next few years? What about the boundary of ACAD and external links to GPs? (software engineer, requirements team)

The steering group meeting on the 3rd September considered a revised ACAD position paper on type and volume of activity. At this important meeting it was agreed that ACAD was purely to be for diagnosis and intervention, and that the step-down ward would be in the main CMT hospital (these areas of certainty are shown on Figure 1, column 2). Also there was a list of specialties included in ACAD, and it was decided to include an MRI scanner but to locate a CT scanner in the main CMT hospital (but with bookings done in ACAD). These decisions about space and equipment utilisation were critical and showed that the finance director was at last satisfied that the project could be profitable. The problem of doctors’ clinical concerns was raised and it was decided that the director of contracts and clinical activities would take several consultants to lunch to discuss their worries. The staff stakeholders were seen as resistant yet manipulable. The steering group meeting led to two definitive reports, which clearly stated the main elements of the internal design and its implications for working practices.

At a meeting of the negotiating group on 13th September it was agreed that a full contract would be signed between the other consortium partners and CMT by June 1997. This was an important commitment point because the consortium had hitherto experienced uncertainty (Figure 1, column 4).

The increasing level of commitment was also shown at an IS group meeting on 2nd October, at which it was decided to write a business specification document by the end of November outlining roles, organization structures, and a list of specialties to be included in ACAD and a risk management plan (Figure 1, column 1). Yet despite this increasing degree of commitment the same questions were being asked:

The big question is whether ACAD will be paperless. We should not assume a paperless environment. (software engineer, requirements team)

This was seen to be because:

Re-engineering of processes is taking place at the same time as the specification, and this is very, very difficult. (director of contracts & clinical activity, CMT)

A report to the IS Group dated 30th October set out a list of tasks and their target dates. They were: the creation of the business specification document (by November), analysis of business functions requiring IS support (by January 1997), definition of the IS (by February 1997), creation of the IS functional specification (by March 1997), detailed costings of IS (by May 1997), and the IS contract (by July 1997). Further details about these tasks were given in a report of 16th October. All these decisions increased the certainty of IS (Figure 1, column 1). However, on 30th October the software developer dropped out of the consortium, leaving CMT to search for a replacement.

A large group of sixty staff, including thirty consultants, met with the steering group on 31st October in order to voice their worries about ACAD. They felt that they had two stark choices—either to accept ACAD or to see their departments closed on the main hospital site. This was presented as positive and negative messages:

ACAD has strengthened our future, but the main hospital will dramatically shrink. (chairman, CMT)

The consultants were able to express their criticisms and to vent their anger:

This is a ludicrous concept to have operating theatres without anaesthetics. Even before a clinician can scrub hands after a patient is operated on, the next patient comes. (consultant, CMT)

The reactions of the consultants were considered at a steering group meeting on 5th November. The anger led to the chief executive’s suggested solution of setting up a medical staff focus group. However, the meeting was seen by top management as a refreezing exercise designed to change attitudes away from resistance towards acceptance of change.

## A political process

The BPR process (column 2) changes rapidly backwards and forwards between unfreezing and uncertainty (evidenced by problems, conflicts, and questions) and refreezing and certainty (evidenced by solutions, resolutions, and answers). The IS process (column 1) in contrast, remains unfrozen and problem-bound for the majority of the time, and then refreezes with a large number of decisions. The building process (column 3) is uniformly decisive. The consortium process (column 4) shows a majority of problems.

## Conclusions

Attempts have in the past been made to establish an integrated methodology for IS and BPR. Our analysis, however, suggests the existence of political processes affecting IS, BPR, and other important processes which are often not synchronised. Some processes exist where change is blocked (uncertainties exist, ideas are unfrozen), whereas others exist where progress occurs (certainty exists, ideas are refreezing). These processes—the IS, BPR, building, and negotiating of the collaboration with partners—advance at different rates, sometimes resulting in rapid progress or “quick hits” (Caron et al, 1994).

The assumption that IS leads any BPR project is therefore dangerous:

The myth that IS should lead re-engineering often leads to IS managers starting re-engineering projects only to discover that the required changes to implement the redesigned process are completely outside of their sphere of control. (Davenport & Stoddard, 1994, p 124)

Such demotion of one particular process (such as IS) can have a dramatically invigorating effect upon a BPR project:

What proved particularly important was the decision to relegate the development and implementation of IS to a support role—after processes had been re-engineered, and while the working arrangements were being refined. (Willcocks & Smith, 1995, p 296)

BPR, IS development and other processes are distinct but related. Both the need to control projects, and to maintain consistency between their processes and stake holders, dictate that linkage must be systematic and continuous, but that such processes should have varying priorities—sometimes driving and sometimes being driven.
