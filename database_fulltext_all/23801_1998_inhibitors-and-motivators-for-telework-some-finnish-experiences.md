# Inhibitors and motivators for telework: some Finnish experiences

R Suomi<sup>1</sup> and J Pekkola<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Turku School of Economics and Business Administration, Rehtorinpellonkatu 3, FIN-20500 Turku; <sup>2</sup>Ministry of Labour, Finland Working Environment Division, Team for Information Society, Etela¨esplanadi 4, FIN-00101 Helsinki, Finland

Nordic countries have traditionally been the forerunners in both usage of telecommunication and restructuring of working life. Both elements are strongly involved in telework so there might be a lesson to be learnt from Nordic telework projects. In this article four Finnish telework initiatives are studied. The reasons for starting them are sought and their results are evaluated. Further factors making the daily telework easier or more difficult are explicated. Conclusions are drawn from the cases, and their characteristics are compared with those of other European telework initiatives.

## Introduction

Telework is a rich and much discussed topic with contributions in many areas, such as information systems, psychology of work, organizational studies, environmental sciences and urban planning, just to name a few. This article is a trial to connect telework arrangements and their successes or failures to the broader social context of the Nordic countries, yet keeping information and communication technologies in a central role.

Because of high unemployment and pressure for competitiveness in international business, Finnish society is pressured to look for added productivity and flexibility in production. The Finnish economy suffered more severely than other western European countries by the collapse of the Soviet Union. This eliminated many profitable exports. Furthermore, the strong Markka resulted in a situation in which instead of currency flexibility other means of flexibility had to be found.

One part of that scene is labour market flexibility, where in addition to other structural reforms—such as the concept of lifelong learning, different national retirement and educational plans, and drastic changes in required qualifications for the workforce—advancement of telework has played a key role.

Our research objective is to discover what goals Finnish telework projects set, how they are being fulfilled, and which kinds of difficulties and advantages are met. A second-level objective is to find out whether the Finnish approach somehow differs from the other European countries.

Methodologically we follow a deductive path. From individual cases generalisations are deduced. In the case of Telecom Finland and Nokia Data, data collection was carried out by Juhani Pekkola, whereas the Archipelagoproject and Kuusamo cases are presented based mainly on secondary sources. They were selected because they are very well known and much cited in Finnish telework discussions.

## Telework from the Finnish perspective

Telework is a multi-faceted phenomenon, which should be understood in its wider context, not on the basis of a short definition. Facets of this wider context in the Finnish discussion are, among others summarised in Suomen kansallinen (1995):

telework is a part of a wider change process in working and organizational cultures leading from industrial towards information societies;

production of goods and services will be distributed through binding together geographically distant production entities, and through introducing new partners into established production entities in a way that benefits all, simultaneously possibilities for telework emerge;

an important part of the process is more intensive use of modern information and communications technology;

I organizational cultures become cooperation-seeking and customer-oriented;

continuous education for the workforce (lifelong learning) plays a paramount role;

organization of work is based on flexible job categories and contents.

In fact Finland has many characteristics that make telework a feasible possibility for the development of Finnish working life and society. The long distances and relatively scarce population (5 million inhabitants) make the gains that could be obtained through telework very concrete, especially if there is the important aspiration of the Finnish people of keeping the whole country populated. In addition to a large mainland rural area, there is a large archipelago with its special problems of communication and transportation.

Enabling factors are the high education level of the Finnish workforce and the well developed telecommunication infrastructure available. Finland is among the five most developed countries in the world in the following dimensions (related to the population):

I number of Internet hosts and domains;

I number of telephone main lines;

I number of mobile phone subscribers;

amount of cash in circulation (least, referring to developed electronic payment systems).

Telework awareness in Finland is very high: 75% of Finns are familiar with the term ‘telework’ (Luukinen et al, 1996). Telework potential is high. In total, there are about one million workers active in professions where telework could be a viable solution. In 1980–1989, the total workforce increased by 4.6%, while the number of people employed in information occupations connected with production and distribution of information rose 29%. The absolute increase in the number of people working in information occupations rose more than the total increase in all other jobs (Sorsa, 1993).

Actual telework utilisation is growing fast. A study performed in 1990 showed that some 5% of the workforce performs at least a part of its work at home with computers. In 1993, 8.4% of the wage and salary earners applied telework at least to some extent, the figure for 1996 being 11% (Suomi & Pekkola, 1997). As information occupations employed about 50% of the wage and salary earners, this means that every fifth potential employee actually performs telework (Suomi & Pekkola, 1997).

In addition, the country is culturally and administratively very homogenous, which makes it easier for government to take initiatives to advance telework (in comparison to countries with federal structures and multiple languages). In fact telework projects are strongly supported by government bodies. The Finnish Ministry of Labour is very active in this field, and many universities study and run practical programmes on how to advance telework. In addition, many government administrations and local municipalities run projects in order to advance telework.

There are two more factors that have an effect on the use of telework. First, the country is still very dependent on traditional industries, which offer fewer working places that could be turned into telework than servicesectors. In particular, the traditional Finnish paper industry seems to be increasing its share of the total economy rather than losing it. Second, the small old-fashioned industry structure offers possibilities for telework: the relatively large agricultural population has a great need to obtain some kind of extra work which they could perform during the long winter months whcn agricultural activities run at a lower gear.

Issues connected with environment protection etc. have very little impact in Finland in connection to telework. The country has only one large city, Helsinki, and even there traffic and other population concentration problems have less visibility than in many bigger European or American cities, not to mention developing countries (Table 1).

## Some Finnish telework initiatives

In this and the following section, four Finnish telework projects are described, two in private companies, and two mainly driven by public authorities. In this section we describe the basic premises of the cases, and further analysis on the cases are discussed in a more thematic fashion later. Juhani Pekkola performed case studies in Nokia Data and Telecom Finland which are reported in detail in Pekkola (1993). Reima Suomi has been involved as educator and expert advisor in the Archipelago project, and material is derived from several discussions with the project manager, Tapio Penttila¨, from the University of Turku. Material on the Kuusamo case is mainly based on Juntunen (1993) and Ja¨rvela¨inen (1997), but has also been derived from discussions with the manager of Henry Haglund, Telmo r.y., the Finnish association for advancing usage of telecommunication networks in Finland.

## Case One: Nokia Data

At Nokia Data, an organizational change was initiated in the mid-1980s when it was discovered that there had arisen a need for more intensive communication and integration inside the company. In order to gain this goal, a campaign for a change in organizational culture was set up. One of its most essential elements was communications culture, which could be advanced through more intensive usage of electronic mail. Before long, electronic mail was discovered to be the most appropriate medium possessing excellent prospects for developing the tools and methods of information work. Electronic mail became both a tool and a product for Nokia Data.

The top management of Nokia Data underlined the importance of avoiding formal control and encouraged the personnel into taking initiatives and responsibilities for their own work. Consequently, it was possible to execute liberal personnel policy with support from top management. The formal control over employees’ absenteeism and working hours was reduced, instead more weight was put on control over the results of work. Information work, development of hardware (their prices constantly being reduced), personnel policy and electronic mail enabled the employees to choose their working hours and where they wished to work.

In addition to person-to-person communication, the electronic mail system was developed further towards taking advantage of bulletin boards. These were implemented by work units and project teams and it was possible for anyone to peruse them anywhere, provided that a PC (equipped with a communications program), a telephone set and a modem was at their disposal. Thus part of a person’s task could be accomplished at home. In particular, replying to messages and scanning the bulletin boards for general matters concerning the company was quite common in the evening and during the weekend.

To sum up, telework resulted from the implementation of an electronic mail system and the campaign for a change in organizational culture. Telework was considered to be an option for adding to competitive advantage.

Through the project, telework became a part of normal daily life, and is no more separately tracked in the organization. What is more important is that the conceptual basis was laid down for the successful product Teamware Office.

## Case Two: Telecom Finland

Telecom Finland is the state-owned and controlled telecommunication operator in Finland. It is by no means a monopoly company, and faces, for example, severe competition in local telephone traffic from about 40 private local telephone operators forming a nationwide highquality network. Old monopolies of Telecom Finland are breaking up in other sectors as well, and the last monopoly to remain seems to be that of the Nordic Mobile Telecommunication System (NMT), which is challenged by the European GSM-system, allocated to competition in Finland.

In May 1990 a decision was made to start an experimental project on telework. It was based on an idea of drawing advantage from telecommunications networks to make the activities within the organization itself more effective. Furthermore, it was observed that telework would enable the development of products by taking measures in issues relating to personnel policy besides business activity. Support from top management was needed in carrying out the project because of the objection from the middle management.

The first stage of the experiment started in autumn 1990 by selecting and training the participants and their supervisors. At the turn of 1990–1991, some sixty employees were involved with the experimental project.

On the one hand telework at Telecom Finland was based on experimental projects, while on the other it was based on established practices when it came to the organization of work. In principle the experiment would provide a possibility for the deployment of distance work and lay down a basis for a telework organization.

Organizational changes and the reorganization of work in the autumn of 1991 delayed the implementation of telework. The general trend towards reducing personnel resources led to general uncertainty and lessened willingness to adopt new models of work organization. The widespread recession mentality decreased initiative and offered gloomy future prospects.

Nevertheless, telework seems to be a way of working to be reckoned with, no matter what the circumstances will be in the future. In autumn 1992, the worst appeared to be over and a new qualitative level was attained. The total number of teleworkers at Telecom Finland is approximately 600 employees and is still growing steadily.

Thirty different aspects of making work more effective through telework were identified during the experiment. These were principally related to working environment, information technology, telecommunication, employees’ private lives, the regrouping of staff and organizations into networks, and the general policy of the company. Thirteen dysfunctional points of view regarding personal issues, working communities, the nature and function of the organization, equality in working life, negotiating methods and technical problems were identified.

To sum up, telework was part of a campaign behind which there were individual ideas of re-organising work and tendencies towards giving vent to creative talent.

## Case Three: Archipelago project

A telework experiment of public interest is being run at the University of Turku. The University is running this project to keep the south-western archipelago of Finland populated and attractive for permanent residents. Among many other initiatives, the project is running a telework initiative.

This project has run since 1991. A database of interested teleworkers in the area and of their skills and capabilities have been developed and contains over 200 teleworkers. Nearly everyone in the database has been assigned telework employment opportunities, and some 50 of them have even set up their own commercial business. It has been estimated that working opportunities have been created for 30 workers full-time and 60 workers part-time.

Part of the programme has included extensive education for possible teleworkers. They have been educated in computer usage, telecommunications, group work, marketing, book-keeping, running and setting up enterprises, negotiation skills, etc. For the public audience and political decision makers, there have been selftargeted seminars and educational activities as well.

In addition to the database and educational activities, the project has been very active in marketing telework for possible employers. The idea is to get the large local companies interested in the topic.

Practical telework possibilities emerge not only through terminals at home, but also through teleworkcottages or telecottages which are public houses where users can use modern equipment and obtain help in operational telecommunication matters. The project was running six such telecottages at its peak time, but now their number is limited to one.

Because this initiative is not connected to any particular organization, typical organizational questions do not emerge. It is more a question of organising life in families in rural areas, where some extra occupations are welcomed along side the more traditional ones such as fishing, agriculture or small-scale handicraft.

Despite the massive resources put into the project, there have been difficulties. Finding employers for the archipelago inhabitants is difficult, even though marketing has reached as far as the Stockholm area, but this may be due to shortages in employee skills and perhaps motivation. The project could be accused of being too centrally planned and of running without real local interest.

## Case Four: Kuusamo

Kuusamo is a municipality in East Finland on the Russian border. It is by no means a small community: 18 500 inhabitants live in an area of 5800 square kilometers (one-eighth of the area of the Netherlands). This, however, makes the population density very low, especially when a large number of the population is still concentrated in few areas.

Since 1987 Kuusamo has concentrated on five issues in developing telework (Juntunen, 1993, p 155): (1) development of know-how and culture in telematics; (2) implementation of information systems in business; (3) improvement of technical level in telecommunications; (4) information systems for the tourist industry; (5) emphasis on cooperation.

Several Finnish families and companies have their holiday cottages in Kuusamo and is a key holiday resort in Finland, especially during the skiing season. One of the ideas is to keep the holiday-makers even longer in Kuusamo by allowing them to perform their work through telework arrangements. The chances are good, since the prospective teleworkers are often of a high professional expertise and status and have good housing facilities available in Kuusamo which would otherwise be left unused for long periods.

The municipality is working hard to become a national pioneer and knowledge-centre, especially in tourism. Both systems to be used direct by tourists, and systems to be used by companies offering tourist services are being developed.

In retrospect, the Kuusamo initiative was ahead of its time. Many of its technical solutions were obsolete, and are in many cases now implemented with much less effort through the modern Internet, and especially World Wide Web, technology. Insufficient systems are mentioned many times as reasons why the initiatives did not work out as expected (see for example Ja¨rvela¨inen (1997).

The difference to the Archipelago project in Kuusamo is that in Kuusamo the municipality is the main coordinator of the project, while in the Archipelago-project this responsibility lies with the (state-owned) university. The available resources for the telework project are of course limited.

## The Finnish initiatives studied in deeper detail: motivators, enablers and inhibitors and results of the cases

In this section we discuss in greater detail the main goals and motivations for starting up the telework projects. Then, encouraged by the example given by Richter and Meshulam (1993), we discuss the telework consequences both at individual and organizational levels. Further, we draw some conclusions on the social level.

## Goals and motivators for the projects

The projects described here fall clearly into two groups: those initiated at a public administration level and those initiated in one company. Even though the practical approaches were dissimilar, the final goal of all the initiatives was, however, the same: adding to the feasibility and competitiveness of the organization or area of the project.

Better methods for organising knowledge work were clearly visible in the Telecom Finland and Nokia cases. Both provide products for knowledge workers, and should themselves be showing a good example in applying new techniques and concepts such as telework. Both companies are national key players in their markets, and have a stake in the process of turning Finland into an information society. For Telecom Finland, it was a question of better utilising its own strength, telecommunication networks and producing new concepts and products for the teleworking community.

Explicit attention on attracting a competent workforce was present in all the initiatives. For the municipalities, there is a question of tax income and of keeping certain areas populated. For the organizations, competition for a skilled workforce can be lessened through a flexible working arrangement facilitated among others by telework. Because of high unemployment (between 15 and 20% in Finland from 1992 to 1997) this motivation has disappeared to a certain degree.

From the employer’s point of view, telework should be seen as a means to obtain organizational flexibility. Through telework, it is easier to adjust to business fluctuations and changes in load. Flexibility can manifest itself in time, place, and amount or quality. At least time flexibility seems hard to obtain in Finland, since telework is usually established with the idea of a constant and ongoing employment relationship, which offers no actual flexibility for the employers. With the guarantee of ongoing employment the idea of telework can be sold to trade unions and individual employees. Working relations where the quality of work is changed through telework are more easily established. Arrangements where employers buy working capacity from independent teleworkers are not understood as telework in the limited sense of the word.

Another typical benefit sought from telework is that of savings in room costs. Unfortunately, however, these savings come to the forefront first after a major investment in telework, and the costs associated with the room are usually very inflexible. Furthermore, possible savings in room costs are often more than outweighed by the costs of getting a second set of computers and other equipment for the teleworker at home (Table 2).

In general telework is welcomed by employees, and is regarded as a special advantage and honour. Is telework connected with the crucial question of being able to live in a certain region? In the case of the Archipelago project the answer is clearly ‘yes’.

Even when there is a general positive atmosphere towards telework, organizations struggling with cost savings find it difficult to document the possible savings acquired from it.

Technical problems are a common excuse for unsuccessful telework projects. In our view the technical problems are not usually a major burden and emerge in the setting-up stage of the telework environment, and when the environment is up and running no more major problems usually occur. At least in northern Europe and OECD countries the public networks used are very reliable.

Critical technical dimensions for the teleworker are usability of the working system and response times. Much depends on the system installation to be contacted by the teleworker, and the employers’ computer system. If it has much downtime and high capacity utilisation, the teleworker will suffer from bad system quality. Of course the telecommunication network itself can cause delays, especially in packet-switched connections.

Success or failure of telework arrangements has a strong connection to the organizational and professional culture aspects.

Professional culture is a set of values held by a professional group. How information work is supervised is a central part of the overall professional culture found in the organization. The success of information work is highly dependent on the organization having an organizational culture guaranteeing similar primary preconditions for work for everyone (Blanc, 1988). Our cases show that different professional groups have different possibilities to adopt to telework. For example for EDPprofessionals turning to telework seems to be easier than for many other groups, because of the traditional focus on results (lines of code) rather than used time.

As it comes to the organizational culture, many telework projects were actually seen as manifestations of new organizational cultures. So they in fact collided with the established organizational cultures, but could proceed because of the explicit support of top management, whose task of course is to set the direction for the organizational culture. Telework projects were also used as agents for changing organizational cultures.

Finally, the hard recession ruling in Finland during 1990–1995 affected not only the labour market equilibrium, but also the general atmosphere of innovation and experimentation, as well as financing sources. Companies and even public authorities are less willing to grasp risky projects. Further, the recession has an effect on workforce mobility. Unemployed people have no motivation to move to big cities, but can stay at home in more rural areas where at least they have social connections and where living is less expensive (Table 3).

## Results of the telework projects

## Changes at individual level

Telework has been a target of contradictory views. It is known that telework has been successfully applied to the needs of both employers and employees, that it is costsaving, combines work with leisure, and that it has various positive social effects. Increasing personal freedom, the possibility to concentrate on work and expand the recruiting area etc, have been considered to have motivated workers to enter into telework (Vorjans, 1987; Huws et al, 1990).

But, telework has also been regarded as an exploitation of the female workforce and a means to stimulate home industry disapproved by the trade union movement. The increasing monotony brought on by work routine, the difficulties in supporting personnel coping with the work and in preserving one’s competence are among the problems brought up in connection with telework (Kawakami, 1985; Oldfield, 1991; Vorjans, 1987, p 83).

What sort of effect does telework then have on the total amount of work performed by teleworkers? According to our cases, usually in the beginning the personnel’s working hours will increase as they spontaneously prolong their work days by accomplishing time-consuming tasks. Meanwhile, some extra time is compensated through the eliminated travelling time. In the beginning, teleworkers usually work more than they used to do in normal working arrangements. Since the object of control is no more time-usage, but the amount and quality of the results, even extra heavy workloads because of telework or just in the work content can remain unnoticed. Expanding and changing control systems from time-control to results-control is one of the major tasks and challenges of telework.

For the worker, benefits of telework usually only materialise later. As experience grows and the teleworker does not have the feeling that he has to deliver ‘more’ because of his priority status among the workforce, overtime work will be diminished allowing them to devote increasingly more time to their family life and hobbies. In our cases, those who have been involved with telework have expressed their satisfaction because of the flexibility in time-usage.

How the daily work content of the teleworker actually changes depends primarily on his/her skill level. As for the content of telework, three different views have been presented:

1. The job descriptions may take on a tayloristic trend, for example, due to technical reasons related to the tools and particularly due to the need for the standardisation of decentralised tasks. In other words, there is a segmentation of the tasks, by being split up between several workplaces (Vorjans, 1987; Oldfield, 1991).

2. The second view presented concerns polarisation, that is the view that teleworkers with the best skills and a strong labour market position get a better work content than they did earlier, while those with less skills or a weak labour market position experience the elements of work as well as their work content is deteriorating (Kawakami, 1987).

3. There are views predominantly emphasising the positive effects of telework (Engstro¨m et al, 1990; Huws et al, 1990; The State of California, 1990).

For ourselves, the positive effects were most visible and no hints of taylorism or polarisation could be seen. According to our cases, flexibility made possible through telework is appreciated by the employees, especially as some top professionals perhaps cannot be contacted in any other way than through different telework arrangements. There is even a tendency to see that telework arrangements are some kind of prize for good performance. Often there can be internal difficulties when selected teleworkers meet in their working places as they are easily considered by other employees as some kind of elite with extra privileges.

To sum up, our case studies found almost entirely only positive effects of telework for the individual workers, if the initial added workload and feelings of insecurity in the working and social relationships are ignored (Table 4).

## Changes at organizational level

An empirical enquiry concerning Finnish companies in the information technology sector (Zamindar, 1995) showed that there were three statistically significant factors related to telework diffusion: well developed information technology infrastructure, the small size of the company and the application of team work all suggest success for telework projects. In case studies, resultorientated management and process-guided thinking have been considered as obligatory preconditions for successful telework application.

In the case of Telecom Finland, a radical demonopolisation of the business field led to dramatic staff reductions, an atmosphere where telework is difficult to establish.

As to the application of telework, it is most interesting to see that 80% of the Finnish telework arrangements are unofficial and only 20% of the teleworkers say that they have made an agreement with their superior. So far, systematic telework programmes have been rare, and only in the most advanced—but also the most successful—companies has it been possible by innovative organization of work and by technology to progress to far-advanced R&D, customer commitment and strategic competitive advantages (Suomi & Pekkola, 1997).

## Changes at society level

At the society or nation level, it is not too critical to say that the big advantages and benefits of telework still remain to be seen. We are primarily in a period of research, initial investment and education and concept establishment.

It is important to note, that all the projects studied here had profound social impacts, and actually had a function of bringing Finland closer to the information society. Nokia Data and Telecom Finland, both leaders in their information intensive industries, had to show that they are themselves willing to turn into the new organization cultures demanded by the information society, and that they are not just expecting this from others.

Another main social issue is that of rural development. The issue of local government initiative gains importance from the cases of the Archipelago project and Kuusamo. Both of these projects failed in some aspects. A strong local initiative is needed, but in addition the required resources should be secured. The social challenge of the future is to integrate these two needs.

## Comparison between telework initiatives in Finland and in the rest of the Europe

As can be seen from our analysis, the goals, practical settings for telework projects, as well as the results vary very much between our four projects. It is difficult to state which issues can be generalised and which can not. Here we try to reflect on the European results compared with those of the Nordic societies.

In the Nordic countries (Finland, Sweden, Norway, Denmark and Iceland) the society structure, political system and general standard of living are very homogenous. Yet differences do exist: Denmark is clearly connected to central Europe both geographically and through its longer membership in the European Community. At the other end, Iceland is clearly the smallest society and is geographically very isolated.

Norway, Sweden and Finland have a strong motivation to support the large (northern) rural areas. A wide network of telecottages has been established in the Nordic countries: 10 in Norway, 39 in Sweden, 44 in Finland and 10 in Denmark in 1993 (Qvortrup, 1993, p 17). However, in 1998 there are only a few active telecottage left in Finland.

The Nordic countries have been pioneers in the field of mobile data communications, which is not purely to be seen as technical innovation and infrastructure, but rather as a social innovation paving the way for telework (see Arnbrak, 1988).

In Europe, telework applications have a growing potential. There are over 50 million personal computers in use in businesses in Europe, and over 60% of the workforce is involved in information management activities. In general telecommunication infrastructures are well developed, and the education level of the workforce is high (Johnston, 1994).

The European Community has financed and stimulated actions in the areas of telecommunication. The attention has been Europe-wide. In addition to these European Community level actions, all countries are running their own development programmes.

Perhaps the biggest difference between the Nordic countries and the rest of the Europe can be seen in the area of labour markets. The Nordic labour market is very unproblematic from the viewpoint of telework, with the following characteristics (Bakke, 1993, p 77):

I a fairly egalitarian atmosphere at work;

I a large degree of discretion for employees;

I participation in technology and organization development.

The national labour markets can be characterised by labour legislation and unionisation rates as more or less regulated ones. High unionisation rates and labour legislation supporting employees’ labour market position can be seen as part of the regulation. The Nordic labour markets might be regarded as regulated in comparison with the US and central European ones where unionisation rates are lower and labour legislation allows less substantial benefits than in Finland and Sweden.

In the labour markets of central Europe and the USA, the teleworkers are not backed up by unambiguous legal status with its obligations for the employer, by the generally binding nature of collective agreements or by the support of the trade union movement. Maintenance of the employment relationship and its benefits depends not only on the nature of the telework agreement but also partly on national legislation (see Vega Ruiz, 1992). In central and southern Europe, teleworkers are to a greater extent subject to an unregulated labour market than in the Nordic countries. For this reason, the standing of the labour market and elements of the work of teleworkers in southern and central Europe or in the USA greatly depend on personal skill.

The economic boom and resulting labour shortage in Finland at the end of the 1980s affected the practice regarding the use of labour. International differences are also explained by the high work attendance rate among women and the relatively insignificant number of parttime workers. The welfare state guaranteeing public childcare services for those who want them has also had its effect on the Nordic labour market behaviour. Thus the lack of child care possibilities is no motive for telework in the Nordic countries. In Finland, telework has not been used to draw on labour pools.

In the USA and central Europe, the social benefits in connection with childbirth and the child care services provided by the government are generally more insignificant than in the Nordic countries. When the social norms adopt full-time or part-time domestic work among women, telework will constitute an opportunity for salaried employment to mothers of small children, and this opportunity will certainly be used.

The main reason for the unproblematic nature of telework applications in Finland and other Nordic countries seems to be both the business strategy which is based on high skills among teleworkers as well as on a labour market system which avoids problematic use of the labour force (Table 5).

## Conclusions

Telework is a hot topic in Finland and in the Nordic countries in general, and possible employers are interested in starting telework exercises in principle. Some advanced companies in Finland—mainly in the area of telecommunications and information technology—are consciously using telework as a part of business strategy and personal policy. In working life there exists a growing amount of, usually unofficial, arrangements which are based on information technology and flexibility in working hours. Telecom Finland and former Nokia Data (today ICL) are some of the first examples. It is also possible to find flexible work arrangements in other economically successful companies, such as many publishing houses, Digital Equipment Company and Sol (a major Finnish cleaning firm).

However, the practical arrangements to be made can turn out to be difficult ones. First, the telecommunication costs caused can manifest themselves as being too high, especially in environments needing constant on-line connections, because telecommunication is still quite expensive with deregulation and price reductions first taking their initial steps. Telecommunication costs are visible out-of-pocket costs. Inefficient time usage of personnel has its costs as well, but these costs are opportunity costs and not so clearly seen by the management. Technical problems are a part of the picture, but can usually be solved, when it is not about a major bottleneck in the basic infrastructure such as insufficient data transfer rates.

Secondly, the strong Finnish trade unions can turn against telework, even though in recent times the general opinion has turned increasingly in favour of flexibility of work arrangements. In general, the current high unemployment ratio ruling makes employers less active in taking new initiatives, especially if even a little opposition is met with. However, employees become more active at the same pace.

Thirdly, employers are not accustomed to the idea of telework, and all the possibilities of this working arrangement are often less than well understood and used. There is clearly a need for education and marketing for the telework concept among Finnish companies.

Telework is not something where you should play with the latest technology. The issue is more an organizational one and usually standard products suffice. It is wise to use standard technical solutions that are available in economical and technical terms and which are easy to use and cost-effective for ordinary users. Of course software houses can create special arrangements, but even these applications are usually based on standard information and telecommunication technology. On average it seems that technical possibilities, scenarios of new work organization and economical solutions are coming closer together and we are able to see the increasing scale of business solutions in the area of telework.

Even our short comparison between the Nordic and other European telework initiatives highlighted many differences. There is reason to believe that many environmental factors are still different in other countries, for example in the USA or in Asia.

No conceptual definition of telework was attempted in this empirically-oriented paper. The authors wish, however, to point out that the very concept of telework is a difficult one. If the central elements of the concept are information work, and working mode assisted by information and telecommunication technologies, nearly all modern work in organizations falls under this category. So a technical point of view is not adequate. Rather, the major tensions and most interesting research questions (maybe the very term in itself) in telework materialise in the relationship between the employee and the employer, in the control over work, its timing, tools, organization, compensation and reward systems, and assessment of results.

The major inhibitor for telework seems to be a lack of interest from the side of employers. From this point of view, research on the possible benefits and obstacles of telework should be intensified, mainly from the point of view of individual organizations in competitive situations, but also from the viewpoint of the society as a whole, where evidence of benefits is already better documented.
