# Effects of organizational culture on a large scale IT introduction effort: a case study of the Norwegian army’s EDBLF project

J Tolsby

Oestfold Research Foundation, Institute of Public Economy, PO Box 573 Busterad, 1754 Halden, Norway

This paper documents a process of introducing new information technology (IT) within the Norwegian army. The research suggests that in the intersection between an organization and IT what emerges as most interesting is the organizational culture. Some of the characteristics of the Norwegian army culture, such as using a particular transfer and application scheme and the use of refreshers training, contributed to a high job rotation, causing instability within the organization and jeopardising the day-to-day work routines within the army units. This part of the Norwegian army’s culture combined with an IT system designed in a way that spliced jobs and deskilled workers, while at the same time creating dependencies between different jobs within the organization, contributed to upholding a particular functioning of the organization. Underlying this is the assumption made by the army that as long as the technology is uniform one person can easily be substituted for another. This paper argues that the organizational culture of the Norwegian army was in many ways a hindrance to a successful adoption of IT. Recognising the influence of organizational culture is important, but more empirical studies of organizational culture influence are needed to support the diverse findings presented here and would be a viable and important task in the years to come for anyone interested in a successful adoption of IT systems.

## Introduction

This paper describes the findings of an exploratory field study that was examined to shed light on the impact of organizational culture when adopting a new information technology (IT) system. The study was of an IT project within the Norwegian army conducted during my employment at the Norwegian Army Administration School as a manager of the computer department.

## The EDBLF IT system

The Electronic Data Processing in Local Administration project (EDBLF IT system), one of the largest IT projects ever launched by the Norwegian army, terminated in August 1995. This represented an end to a project that had lasted more than a decade and had cost approximately 635 million NOK (£63.5 million). The stated purpose was to make the army more efficient by introducing an IT system to be used by local army units for administration purposes. The technology in question was to be distributed to 130 units and would affect 2000 employees. The technology to be adopted was that of an integrated application system as illustrated in Figure 1.

The advice to adopt IT came from the external consultancy firm, Teledata AS, who were engaged by the project manager. Teledata claimed that the use of an IT system in the army would reduce time to mobilise and fulfil the wish for a more efficient and rational organization. Another benefit Teledata said would be to make the jobs in the army more attractive and interesting. This would make the army more favourable when competing with private enterprises for the same scarce, competent personnel. Teledata stated that if their advice was not followed the result would be an introduction of computers in an unstructured and less controlled way but the amount of investment would not be less.

The army’s aim when embarking on this project was to use IT to gain productivity growth by reorganization, simplification of rules, economisation and delegation (minutes from steering group meeting, 13th June 1989). The computer systems of the project were intended to make the defence services within the areas of personnel, material, supply, maintenance, economy, construction, operative activity and education more efficient while increasing employees job satisfaction (GIH’s Electronic Datahandling Directive, 13th January 1984, p 12). This project was going to bring the army from stone age to IT-age in one step (interview with project employee and leader of the technology requirement part of the EDBLFproject, September 1995). The overall knowledge of IT within the army was limited at the time of initiation of the EDBLF project.

## Salient characteristics of the organization

A brief outline of the characteristics of the Norwegian army is a prerequisite for understanding the organization studied in this research. The Norwegian army consists of three categories of employees, soldiers doing compulsory service, officers, and civilians (approximately 8000 individuals). Officers and civilians are permanently employed in the army.

Geographically the army is spread all over Norway. The organization has three main levels; the central level with the Defence Command and the Army Material Command, the regional level with the five Land Commands or Regional Commands, and the local level with approximately 130 local units. An adapted illustration of the organizational chart of the Norwegian army is shown in Figure 2.

The Norwegian army is closely connected with words such as obedience, order and discipline. In order to achieve this the army has become a strict hierarchical organization with strong emphasis on structure and formal position. “The military term for management is command, a rather straightforward notion that means the superior gives the order and the subordinate executes them.” (Noble, 1987).

Compliance with this system is enforced in a number of ways, including through officers’ military records. For officers, the military record is a part of their curriculum vitae, and together with formal education, qualifies them for jobs in the army. Their behaviour will be directly reflected in their military record. Reluctance to obey orders could jeopardise a military career.

Reliable and consistent action during wartime requires practice of operations in peacetime. This results in an organization that during peacetime has to practice wartime activities, including military exercises. These military exercises necessitate temporary adjustments to work routines and procedures in the units. They thus impact day-to-day activities and business operations in the units, leaving them without vital personnel resources for varying lengths of time, during which they make no use of replacements.

A second characteristic of the army is the transfer and application schemes. These schemes ensure a high job rotation rate. Under the transfer scheme officers are unable to apply for new posts until they reach the age of 32 to 41 (depending upon their level of education). Officers under the transfer scheme have to accept the posts they are appointed to. Once they reach the rank of Captain, officers are released from this type of arrangement and are under the application scheme where they are free to apply for any post within the army in competition with other officers and according to their competence. These senior officers often accept short assignments lasting a year or two and then they reapply for other posts.

## Defining organizational culture

The transfer and application scheme and the frequent use of military exercises contributed to high job rotation for both the employees and the commanders of local units. This causes discontinuity and instability in the workforce. Both loyalty to orders and the way the managerial role was conducted, supported a particular functioning of the organization. These characteristics compromise the organizational culture of the army. A supportive definition of organizational culture can be found in Keller (1981) and Matenaar (1983). Culture, they say, is a result of collective social behaviour and how individuals think. Culture embraces all kinds of cultural expressions as well as manners and customs, social organizations and institutions, and shared convictions, motives, values and attitudes. Or as Thompson and McHugh (1990) state: “%corporate culture is the core and the glue binding the diverse corporate activities together.” Ernste (1989) define organizational culture as our cultural heritage furthered inside the organization by the influence of internal problems and through interaction with others, giving rise to a corporate culture.

## Research methodology

The research methodology was an in-depth case study of the EDBLF project to introduct IT constrained to two army units. One limitation of analysing a single case is the difficulty of determining whether the patterns found are typical. It is difficult to generalise from one case. On the other hand, studying a concrete case will have the advantage of being able to study a process in depth from start to finish (Hellevik, 1995).

The findings need to be interpreted cautiously as they only reflect the adoption and early use experiences of a sample of individuals within two specific army units in what was a larger implementation process continuing over time. The findings to date are interesting as they reflect people’s initial experiences and assessments of the EDBLF IT-system in the light of their work practices and assumptions about technology. Orlikowski (1992) states that such an initial period following the implementation of a technology provides a brief and rare opportunity for users to examine and think about the technology as a discrete artefact, before it is assimilated into cognitive habits and work practices and disappears from view.

## Data sources and analysis

Data were collected through a variety of methods including interviews, archive material and official documents, over a five month period. Five categories of personnel were analysed in this research: the project manager, programmers at the software producer Cap Computas, the organizational development expert from the consultancy firm Hartmark Iras, two civilian trade unions (FSTL and NTL), and various employees at two army units, VTR and HFS.

The criteria for choosing the informants were to gain a broad view of the project and its history, and to broaden the scope of the research by focusing on both the managerial and workers level of the organization. The selection of the two particular army units was based upon their early exposure to the EDBLF IT-system.

Interview guides were used to loosely structure the interviews with the following in mind. First, to establish how the informant initially got in contact with the EDBLF IT-system. Second, to reveal how the interviewees perceived the organizational development process. Finally, to identify the positive and negative aspects of the process and what could have been done differently.

To support data obtained from interviews, archive material from the project was used. This material consisted of minutes from steering group meetings, plans, and correspondence within the project. Additionally, directives from the Army Command of Staff (Hœrstaben, HST) and parliamentary documents were consulted. Some of the background material was derived from experience as a civilian employee of the Army Administration School?

## Research theme

The aim was to analyse this technology transfer process and to look for conflicts between the adoption of the technology and the organizational culture. This gives rise to the core research question examined in this paper: How did the organizational culture of the army affect the introduction of the new IT?

The next section describes the consequence of the process to adopt new IT and the role of organizational culture upon it. The last section presents the conclusions and some of the lessons learned.

## The role of the organizational culture

The organizational culture of the army manifests itself both through the formal and the informal systems, such as ranking of officers, obedience to orders, the application and transfer scheme, and the use of military exercises. Loyalty is considered extremely important.

Interestingly, investigation indicates that in the area of IT there seems to be less obedience to orders than in other military fields. This seemed to apply especially for younger officers as they often had a specific idea of what kind of computer technology they wanted and how it should perform. They were more familiar with the new computer technology than their older superiors and predecessors. Young officers said that they were listened to because use of computers was a fairly new area in the Norwegian army. As one of them put it:

This is a fairly new area for the army. Those who have some knowledge in the field have been appointed as resource personnel. If we were dealing with a traditional army field, it might have been different. But computers being such a new area, things are different, and there are fewer knowledgeable persons. This has made it easier for those possessing this particular knowledge to be heard.

Contrary to the seemingly increased influence by junior officers when dealing with a new army field, the transfer and application schemes provided the management with an excuse for leaving difficult problems to their successor. An underlying assumption here is a fear of changes in the managerial role as a consequence of the new EDBLF IT system. It became clear during interviews with leading officers that a laissez-faire attitude prevailed towards decisions regarding the importance of the IT system. This is apparent from the following remark made by the director of Hartmark Iras:

You might have a superior in the job for one or two years, and this means, as I perceive the culture, after me comes the flood. What I do does not matter since the manager after me will face these problems. In a project such as this you will have to take unpopular decisions that will have consequences for people at all levels. You must be willing to take risks. There does not seem to be a culture for being tough and making the decisions and then admitting that you were wrong. It is easier to keep calm and only take the easy decisions. Follow the stream and you will not have a blem ish on your military record.

This illustrates how the organizational culture made it easy for management to avoid taking responsibility. Thus, whenever there was a conflict of interest between the new IT-system and the managerial role, the managerial role was prioritised, with consequences for successful adoption of the technology. Assigning credit to younger, low-rank personnel for their computer literacy was seen as a change in the traditionally strictly hierarchical power structure of the army, but nevertheless also represented a threat to the power structures. If people have a poor or inappropriate understanding of the unique and different features of a new technology they may resist using it, or may not integrate it appropriately into their work practices (Orlikowski, 1992). Developments in technology alter the distribution of power and control in the workplace. It is helpful to see an organization as a delicate mesh between a social system and its technical system, and the effect of change in one system will therefore be felt throughout the others (Winfield,

1991). This supports the claim that the army’s culture both had effect upon and was effected by the new ITsystem.

## Users influence

The employees wanted to have an influence on the adoption and implementation process of the EDBLF IT system. This influence was officially ensured by the participation of the elected members from the trade unions in the project steering group. However, once a part of the steering group, the trade union members found they had no real influence on decisions regarding implementation. These decisions had already been made, and were presented to the steering group as a matter of formality. These are some of the remarks made by representatives of the two trade unions:

Why be a member of these groups when you are not listened to? I would assume that a computer system that is wrong for the user would also be wrong for the employer. I have looked at it, and the degree of influence was marginal in the steering group so I was there as a sort of hostage. To become a member of the steering group you have to sell your soul. As one member out of twenty you can oppose the decisions as much as you want, but it is the decision of the majority that counts.

Some of our members were afraid of the staff reduction.

We were there as a rubber stamp.

Being members of work groups would have given us more influence.

It could be argued that the influence of the trade unions was microscopic or non-existent. Their influence may have been reduced for two main reasons, their lack of knowledge within IT and because of the organizational culture. There were therefore no signs of a major change in who decided on IT. Thus, it could be argued that the method adopted to implement the technology accommodated the prevailing organizational culture.

Heatly et al (1995) recognise in their case study that there was a shift in responsibility within the organization affecting the organizational culture since engineers took on the role of system maintenance that has traditionally been reserved for management information system (MIS) professionals. These findings indicate how several organizational cultural elements such as users’ anticipations and expectations and lack of management commitment contributed to some of the frustrations and problems of the project (Heatly et al, 1995). This is in alliance with what has been discovered in the case studied in this paper.

Similarly, Orlikowski in her study ‘Learning from Notes’ points to the fact that in an organization with an individualistic culture, where there are few incentives for co-operation, groupware technology alone will not engender these. This has to do with peoples’ cognitions or mental models about technology and their work, and the structural properties of organization such as policies, norms, and reward systems (Orlikowski, 1992). What Orlikowski points to here is the impact of organizational culture elements and their importance when implementing software systems such as groupware. Based upon the collective organizational culture of the army one should expect the opposite reaction amongst the users, but there was no evidence of this. Thus it could be argued that implementing IT systems requires acknowledgement of the importance of the organizational culture.

## Consequences of the technology adopted

The adoption of IT affected the day-to-day functioning of the units. Some of the interviewees said that changes had occurred in job content, procedures and routines. The trade unions expressed that members were pleased with the simplification of many of the manual routines, giving them quicker and easier access to information. The union representatives also expressed that members from the adoption process of the new IT system had gained more insights into how routines and procedures were carried out by colleagues. The users experienced greater dependence on the routines of others, since the IT system required others to add consistent data at regulated times. The IT system forced a particular organizational behaviour on the users as the following remark shows: ‘The most important thing for us was to become acquainted with the personnel module to make it work practically, and to avoid being criticised by individuals depending upon our data.

When the new information system was introduced it seems that the interdependence between different jobs within the unit increased. A reported problem was the degree of involvement in other employees’ jobs. Whereas before there was no need to become involved in how other people did their jobs, this changed under the new IT system.

At the same time indications were that to some extent the IT which was adopted was a success, especially the office automation package. However, when the employees were asked about the applications within the four administrative areas (personnel, material, economy and education handling), a different picture appeared. Opinions expressed were that this system was very cumbersome and a total failure. The users found the system complex and difficult to use and understand. Users found that the system did not behave in a logical way. The new IT system did not follow the order of how things were done manually before, and did not behave according to what the user was trying to achieve. Due to a premature exposure of the IT system, the jobs were done both manually and on the computer. The users perceived the interface as old-fashioned and represented a set back. As a user expressed: ‘I hope the system will become better when the new Windows interface is in place. We try to be positive since this is something which is inevitable.

Complexity and extent of some of the modules caused additional problems. This was a view shared at both the managerial and employee level. The material module, for instance, contained around 300 screens, many of which were unused by smaller units. This seems to be a confirmation of a classical problem within software development, a problem confirmed in comparable studies. One example is a study of an advanced desktop system installed in a large national bank. The researchers found that the staff rarely or never used many facilities. Four out of 33 facilities accounted for 75% of the utilisation. Half of the facilities provided remained unused after one year (Winfield, 1991).

An issue is whether the system could have been made simpler or cancelled. It would have required money and personnel to alter the IT system. At this stage in the project the process seemed irreversible since too many resources had already been committed. A great deal of prestige was at stake for the project management. As noted by one of the trade union representatives participating in the steering group of the project:

The EDBLF IT system started growing as all computer projects do. One started off trying to solve a particular problem but thousand others arose for the project to try and solve. A result of the general gigantomania of the army in relation to this project became too much and difficult to handle. EDBLF IT system reached a stage where there was too much money and prestige involved. It reached the point of no return; there could be no other solution but to continue. The more funds and the longer the time spent on this project the more difficult it became to scrap the whole project.

At that time in the project there was no clear view of what the IT system was expected to achieve. As noted by the manager of the IT requirements specification in the project:

That was the challenge we were faced with, we knew very little about what we were supposed to find a solution to. They had not started to develop the systems yet; they did not know what kind of system they wanted. There was some talk of a personnel system, a financial planning system, and material system. The amount of the data, their complexity, how to calculate the capacity – none of these things had been defined.

The technology adopted was problematic in terms of the custom designed software. The new software was designed based on existing regulations and data collected at pilot-units, instead of paying attention to what the real needs would be. The custom designed software caused greater dependency between the different employees. The custom designed applications required data to be entered at precise intervals. This meant that being away from work would jeopardise the system since many others were dependent upon you. The introduction of IT made information accessible to more people. The unit was now confronted with a pool of information in a database, requiring regular updates by more people within the organization, whereas before much of the information within the organization was dominated by individuals.

Another consequence of the IT system was the deskilling of the workers. Routines and procedures, which before were carried out by one person, were divided and spread to several people. Army employees were not able to do their job at their own pace at times that suited them. The new IT system forced a particular scheme upon them, causing rigidity in workloads and how the work was carried out, along with increased control (Baethge & Oberbeck, 1989). It fits well into the framework of Taylorism (Moreton, 1995).

In the light of this, the organizational change process appears to have been a disguised form of Taylorism. For a labour process to be true Taylorism, the worker must be independent of the work process, without wishes, wants, qualifications or emotions. A prominent characteristic of Taylorism is how easy it would be to replace the workforce, since they would do routine work requiring little or no qualifications (Baethge & Oberbeck, 1989). In the case of the EDBLF IT system, qualifications were required and replacement of the workforce could not so easily be done.

Designing the IT as a module-based system was done in order to create uniformity. In this way the technology accommodated the organizational culture. As long as the IT system was the same the organization could still have a high job rotation, since, as Taylor indicates, if technology remains uniform, people are interchangeable.

## Conclusions and lessons learned

This paper described how the organizational culture of the army influenced the adoption of IT. I have argued that the transfer and application scheme and the use of military exercises contributed to a high job rotation rate causing instability within the organization and jeopardising the day-to-day work routines and business activities within units. One of the requirements of the IT system developed for the army was that it should be uniform. Based on the anticipation that a uniform system would make it easier for personnel to change jobs. People could, with this system, be more easily replaced. Thus the technology in a way contributed to sustain high job-rotation.

It has also illustrated how the users’ influence upon this IT-system was reduced to a minimum since their elected representatives did not have any real influence in the steering group. The organizational culture in a way contributed to reducing the unions’ influence.

It can also be claimed that the use of military records, combined with the transfer and the application schemes contributed to a managerial attitude of leaving difficult problems for their successor. Toughness to take unpopular decisions was lacking, due to the fact that this could jeopardise a person’s military career. Underlying this assumption is that toughness is required to accomplish a successful technology transfer within the organization.

Interestingly, the research reveals that the formal position of an officer was less important when dealing with a field in which the army had little previous knowledge. This change represented a threat to the traditional power structures, particularly at the managerial level.

An integrated IT system in combination with a particular set of organizational culture elements, as shown in this paper, supported a particular functioning of the organization. Implying that the development and implementation of the EDBLFIT system as it was advocated in this particular case, was unable to alter the organizational culture, making it less receptive to adopting the IT system.

There are several lessons learned in this case study. Organizational culture should be acknowledged when introducing an IT system. User influence should be ensured and their elected representatives should be considered as contributing members to the design of the system. Finally, IT systems should be designed with simplicity in mind, since major parts of a complex system may never be used.
