# Towards the development of measures of perceived benefits and compatibility of EDI: a comparative assessment of competing first order factor models

MC Jones<sup>1</sup> and RC Beatty<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Box 9581, Department of Management & Information Systems, Mississippi State University, MS State, MS 39762; <sup>2</sup>Upham Hall, Richard T. Farmer School of Business Administration, Miami University, Oxford OH 45056, USA

This study describes the development and testing of sets of items to capture constructs that are widely used in the study of electronic data interchange (EDI) adoption and integration. They are the perceived benefits of EDI and the extent to which EDI is compatible with existing organizational practices. Sets of items to measure both perceived direct and perceived indirect benefits are assessed, as are sets of items to measure two types of compatibility: organizational and technological. Findings indicate that the model adequately captures each of the proposed constructs except technological compatibility. Conclusions and directions for future research are presented.

## Introduction

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is an interorganizational system in which standard business documents are electronically exchanged between trading partners’ computers. Over time, EDI allows firms to fundamentally change the way they do business, thereby improving overall firm performance and enhancing competitive advantage (Emmelhainz, 1988; Mukhopadhyay et al, 1995). Firms have also reported a variety of other EDIrelated benefits such as reduced transaction costs, improved information flow, and improved customer service (e.g., Saunders & Clark, 1992; Scala & McGrath, 1992). However, many of these reports have been descriptive, often anecdotal, accounts of success stories (e.g., McCusker, 1993; Arunachalam, 1995; Lummus & Duclos, 1995; Kumar & Crook, 1996). Although there are a number of rigorous empirical research studies that examine successful EDI efforts, many of them are case studies that report EDI efforts in one or a few specific firms (e.g., Jelassi & Fignon, 1994; Mukhopadhyay et al, 1995; Iacovou et al, 1995; Massetti & Zmud, 1996). There has been little effort to assess the validity and reliability of the items on which findings are based. As research in EDI becomes more prevalent, the need for measurement scales that reliably capture various aspects of EDI adoption and integration becomes critical.

“If information systems research is to make a contribution to the world of practice, a well defined outcome measure (or measures) is essential” (Delone & McLean, 1992, p 61). Without measuring instruments that have been demonstrated to have adequate measurement properties, “. . . much of information systems research is largely speculative” (Delone & McLean, 1992, p 61). As a field of inquiry begins to emerge, as is the case with EDI, it often experiences a “proliferation of instruments which fail to meet minimal standards of reliability and validity” (Klenke, 1992, p 325). Because constructs are the basic elements of theory construction, and because the strength of constructs depends on the reliability and validity of the scales used to measure them, assessing such measurement properties is one of the basic building blocks of theory construction in a field of inquiry (Zmud & Boynton, 1991). Thus, this study was undertaken to assess measurement properties of scales that measure two of the constructs found to be important in successful EDI adoption and integration; perceived benefits of EDI and compatibility of EDI with the existing organization (O’Callaghan et al, 1992; Iacovou et al, 1995; Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995). The purpose of the study was to provide information about the validity and reliability of the items most commonly used to measure these constructs. Findings help provide a stronger basis on which to test hypotheses and to build theory about EDI benefits and compatibility: two constructs that appear regularly in EDI studies.

## Theoretical background

Relative advantage, often operationalised as perceived benefits, of an innovation and the compatibility of an innovation with existing organizational policies, procedures, values, and systems are two of the more relevant aspects of innovation adoption (Rogers, 1983). Thus, it is not surprising that many EDI adoption studies address them (e.g., O’Callaghan et al, 1992; Banerjee & Golhar, 1994; Reekers & Smithson, 1994; Iacovou et al,

1995; Mukhopadhyay et al, 1995). These constructs have also been demonstrated to be relevant indicators of the extent to which EDI is integrated within the firm after adoption (e.g., Iacovou et al, 1995).

This study assesses EDI benefits from the perspective of an organization apart from its trading partners and its competitive environment. However, EDI is not a technology that can be implemented in a single firm, and its benefits extend beyond the internal benefits that may accrue to a firm as a result of technological efficiencies (Reekers & Smithson, 1994; O’Callaghan & Turner, 1995; Cox & Ghoneim, 1996). Trading partners can have significant effects on the success of EDI in a given firm. The larger the percentage of its total trading partners with which a firm does EDI, the greater the benefits it can realise (Wrigley et al, 1994; Bjørn-Andersen & Krcmar, 1995; Cox & Ghoneim, 1996). Although the exact numbers differ among firms, and even industries, a critical mass of trading partners is necessary for a firm to realise significant EDI benefits (Wrigley et al, 1994; Bjørn-Andersen & Krcmar, 1995). Because we do not attempt to assess causality or make predictions about benefits, inter-organizational relationships, or organizational performance, we do not examine the constructs in this study from a perspective other than the adopting organization. However, trading partner perspectives from along the firm’s value chain can provide a richer set of data in studies that do attempt to make such predictions.

Although widely used, perceived benefits and compatibility have been applied so broadly and so inconsistently, the results are hard to interpret (Tornatsky & Klein, 1982). Field interviews and focus groups are often used to develop relevant measurement items, and researchers often reinvent the wheel, developing their own items with their own industry samples rather than empirically testing items generated by prior research (O’Callaghan et al, 1992; Scala & McGrath, 1993). Although these provide valuable insight into EDI use, and even provide an empirically generated set of items, there is little evidence that others have used or attempted to validate those items in their own work. Others, although providing well thought-out research studies that have relied on the scales of others, have not provided much information about the measurement properties of those scales in the context in which they were used (e.g., Iacovou et al, 1995). Many of the same items emerge in these separately conducted studies, providing some basis for establishing content and face validity; yet in the few studies where prior work is drawn upon, measurement properties such as content validity, construct validity, and reliability are rarely reported.

## Item generation

One of the first steps in deriving a measurement scale is to assess content validity. This is defined as the adequacy with which the scale captures the domain of the characteristic of interest (Churchill, 1979). Following Churchill’s (1979) recommendation to rely on existing research for generating a sample of items, we relied heavily on existing descriptive and empirical research findings for our items (Table 1). Because existing research has generated items from interviews and field surveys of pre- and post-EDI adopters, and because many of the same items surface in separately conducted studies, we believe our scales are high in content validity.

The criteria for including a set of items in our synthesis were the study in which they were used provided either a fairly comprehensive set of items; a solid theoretical background underlying the set of items used; or a report of measurement properties for the item set. Although many studies provide items that may meet the criteria (e.g., Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995), we relied particularly on those that captured a broad representation of the constructs, with a parsimonious and easily adaptable set of items.

A fairly comprehensive set of items to measure perceived benefits was derived from Banerjee and Golhar (1994) and Iacovou et al (1995). The former is based on prior work; yet no mention is made of prior validation of the scale, and Banerjee and Golhar report only means and standard deviations. Iacovou et al (1995) tested a very similar set of items they derived from Pfeiffer (1992). They indicate that the items actually measure two dimensions of perceived benefits; direct and indirect. Direct benefits include tangible items such as cost savings and reduced inventory. Indirect benefits include intangible items such as improved competitive advantage and better customer service. Items for each construct are provided in Table 2. Iacovou et al (1995) assess adoption and integration potential on the basis of these two dimensions, although they offer no empirical evidence that the items actually measure two rather than one dimension. Thus, we test the hypothesis that the set of items to measure perceived benefits measures both indirect perceived benefits and direct perceived benefits.

O’Callaghan et al’s (1992) items were used to measure two aspects of compatibility; operational and technological. Technological compatibility is the extent to which a new technology is compatible with existing software, hardware, and technology procedures; whereas operational compatibility is the extent to which it is compatible with existing operational procedures. These item sets are fairly comprehensive and results are consistent with other research findings that there are different types of compatibility (e.g., Iacovou et al, 1995). O’Callaghan et al (1992) factor analysed eight items into two dimensions of four items each. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.79 for technological compatibility and 0.85 for operational compatibility. ## Data collection

Surveys were sent to a systematic random sample of EDI representatives in 500 US firms listed in the 1995 Uniform Code Council EDI Member Directory. The survey consisted of questions to measure the items in Table 2 as well as organizational data such as years of EDI experience, extent of EDI use, and external pressure to adopt. The questions to measure the 16 items that comprised the four scales are provided in Appendix A. Data were collected in the Summer and Autumn of 1996. Because items in scales are based on those in prior studies, and the purpose of this study is to assess measurement properties, no pilot study was conducted. EDI representatives were asked to complete the survey and pass a copy on to one of their key users. EDI representatives were those people listed in the directory as responsible for EDI management for the organization. They might or might not be part of the information systems staff. The key user was a non-technical EDI user from a functional area such as purchasing or customer service. In some cases, the EDI representative was also the key user. Both respondents returned a survey in 52 firms, and another 15 EDI representatives who were also users, responded. One user survey was unusable. Thus, there were 118 usable surveys, from 67 firms, for a response rate of 13.4%. The relatively low response rate is consistent with that in other EDI related studies (Teo et al, 1995; Arunachalam, 1995), and in that of other information systems (IS) studies where the initial recipient of the survey is asked to pass a copy to someone else (Jones et al, 1995).

To assess whether respondents were different from non-respondents, the two groups were compared on two characteristics listed in the 1995 Uniform Code Council EDI Member Directory—type of company and EDI standard used. The vast majority of each group are manufacturing firms, yet there are proportionally more among the respondent group (64%) than the non-respondent group (53%). There are also proportionally more retail firms among respondents (13% vs 5%) and more wholesale firms among non-respondents (20% vs 10%).

Three EDI standards that are widely used in the US were listed: VICS EDI which is a standard used widely by retailers and their suppliers, UCS which is used widely by most other industry segments, and WINS which is used largely by warehouse firms. The VICS EDI standard was used by 59% of respondents and 43% of non-respondents. On the other hand, 52% of nonrespondents use the UCS standard compared to 39% of the respondents. There were few using WINS in either group—which is to be expected because the proportion of warehouse firms was low in both groups. Thus, findings are more representative of manufacturers than any other type of company, and of firms that use VICS EDI, suggesting that these firms deal largely with retailers.

A data set containing two respondents per firm is beneficial because it provides data about the same system from two different perspectives (Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995). Any bias that might be introduced by having two respondents is outweighed by this benefit (Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995). Often users are ignored in EDI-related studies, particularly in survey research where a single respondent view is represented. Many of the research findings discussed thus far represent primarily the EDI representative’s perspective. Exceptions to this are case studies that include user views about EDI performance (e.g., Bjørn-Andersen & Krcmar, 1995; Pasini & Chaloux, 1995).

This study assesses whether the scales have adequate measurement properties to be reliably administered to both users and EDI personnel within the same firm. Although hypothesis testing in which predictions can be made is important, providing a valid and reliable measuring instrument for the constructs about which hypotheses are tested is equally important; particularly during the early stages of research about a phenomenon (Bentler, 1978). Because the goal of this research is to assess how well the scales capture individual perceptions, rather than to make predictions about perceptions, the particular firm or system is not as important as the individual respondent.

## Profile of respondents

Although the purpose of this study is to assess validity of two sets of measures, and not to make predictions about EDI usage from findings, knowledge about type of firm, EDI experience, the extent to which respondents use EDI, and external pressure to adopt provides information about the context of the findings. Sixty-four per cent of the respondents are in the manufacturing industry, 23% are in the retail/wholesale industry, and the remaining 13% are in distribution, warehousing, and brokerage. Experience with EDI was quite varied. In 15% of the firms, EDI had been in use no longer than one year, and had been used at least five years in 67% of the firms. EDI had been in use between one and five years in the remaining 18% of firms. User experience with EDI varied as well: 21% had used EDI no more than one year; 45% had between one and four years experience; and the remaining 34% had used EDI more than four years. Some respondents had used EDI prior to joining their present firms, and others learned EDI because it was being used by their present firms when they were hired.

Because external pressure and trading partner power have such a strong influence over the decision to adopt EDI (Webster, 1995), respondents were asked the extent of this power in their firms’ decisions. Trading partner pressure strongly influenced their decision to adopt EDI (mean of 1.84 on a five-point scale, where (1) is strongly agree external pressure influenced and (5) is strongly disagree). Respondents also indicated that the transactions they have converted to EDI are very critical to their firm (mean = 1.68; on a five-point scale, where (1) is very critical and (5) is not very critical). This may explain why they were subject to external pressure. Firms will often endure high costs of implementing and managing EDI in order to retain a customer that they perceive to be valuable to their business (Webster, 1995). Others have found that although external pressure is a strong influencer of adoption, it has little effect on subsequent integration in the firm (Iacovou et al, 1995). Therefore, we include other indicators of EDI usage in our profile of respondents.

A good indicator of the extent to which firms have integrated EDI is the number of trading partners with which they do at least part of their transactions using EDI (Wrigley et al, 1994). Firms that implement EDI because of external pressure from one or a few trading partners may exert limited effort to bring other trading partners on board, and thereby may not attain a critical mass of partners (Reekers & Smithson, 1994). Because other responses were based on the functional area unit of analysis, this is the number of trading partners for the functional area. Thirty-three per cent of these respondents do EDI with ten or fewer trading partners. Another 15.4% do EDI with between 11 and 20 other firms, and 10% have between 21 and 40 EDI partners. Another 19.8% have between 50 and 100 EDI partners, and the remaining 17.6% have over 100 partners.

Another indicator of the extent of EDI integration in a firm is the number of different applications for which they use EDI (e.g., purchase orders, invoices). Over 30% of the firms send at least five different types of transactions using EDI (31.7%), 9.8% send four, 22% send three, 29.3% send two, and only 7.3% indicate they send only one type of transaction. They receive more types than they send: 31.7% receive at least six different types of transactions. Another 12.2% receive five, 9.8% receive four, 17.1% receive three, and another 17.1% receive two. Only 9.8% receive only one type of transaction.

Finally, we asked respondents whether they use manual intervention in their EDI systems. Use of manual intervention is often related to IS maturity (Nygaard-Andersen & Bjørn-Andersen, 1994). Well over one-half (57%) of firms report that EDI is fully automated—there is no manual intervention anywhere in the processing of the transaction set. The other 43% indicate that they are still using manual intervention somewhere in the process.

Because our goal is to provide a set of scales to measure constructs across a variety of firms and segments, no attempt was made to determine if there are differences in perceived benefits or compatibility due to differences among respondents in the variables mentioned above. It may be useful to examine the effect of such variables on these constructs in future studies, using a different set of respondents.

## Construct validity assessment

One of the greatest difficulties in scale development is assessing the accuracy of the measures of the constructs examined (Hinkin, 1995). A key component of this assessment is construct validity, defined as “the degree to which a measure assesses the construct it is purported to assess” (Peter, 1981, p 134). This study assesses two widely tested components of construct validity; dimensionality and internal consistency (Churchill, 1979; Peter, 1981).

Many IS researchers use exploratory factor analysis to assess dimensionality (Straub, 1989; Zmud & Boynton, 1991). This method is useful for testing hypotheses about the number of factors to extract in the absence of a theoretical basis for establishing the number of factors a priori. However, when theory indicates that particular items measure a particular construct, confirmatory factor analysis is useful for testing hypotheses about the adequacy of the measurement properties of those items to measure their hypothesised factor (Bentler, 1978). In this study, hypotheses are tested about how well four sets of items measure four distinct, yet related underlying constructs. Therefore, confirmatory oblique factor analysis was used to assess the proposed model (Figure 1). The maximum likelihood fitting function was used to estimate the model; and a correlation matrix served as the input matrix.

In structural equation modelling, the statistical test is between the actual and predicted matrices (Hair et al,

1992). Thus, the null hypothesis is that there is no difference between the actual model and proposed model of the data. Therefore, the researcher is looking for nonsignificant differences. Small $X ^ { 2 }$ values are associated with large significance levels, and models with significance levels greater than 0.05 are generally considered adequate representations of the data. The $X ^ { 2 }$ for this model is 143.22 $( P = 0 . 0 0 2 )$ indicating that Model #1 is not a good representation of the data. Further analysis indicates that some of the items should be eliminated. Following a rule-of-thumb that items with small squared multiple correlations (SMCs) be eliminated (Loehlin, 1992), three items were dropped from the model because they had SMCs less than 0.20. These were ‘changed operating procedures’; ‘modifications to existing system’; and ‘required substantial site preparation.’ Figure 2 shows the model respecification.

Table 3 provides a summary of results for this model as well as for Model #1 and two other respecifications of the model. The LISREL equation (Jo¨reskog & So¨rbom, 1992) for the first order model is as follows: $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { x } = \Lambda \boldsymbol { \xi } + \boldsymbol { \delta } . } \end{array}$ . In a first order factor analysis, latent variables are represented by $\xi \mathbf { s } .$ . Their relationship with the measured items (x) is represented by the L matrix.

The criteria used to compare model specifications can be classified as measures of absolute fit; measures of incremental fit; and measures of parsimonious fit (Bollen, 1989a; Hair et al, 1992). Measures of absolute fit provide information about how well the proposed model predicts the observed correlations. These include the <sup>2</sup> statistic, the normed <sup>2</sup> ( <sup>2</sup>/degrees of freedom) (Jo¨reskog, 1969; Carmines & McIver, 1981), the goodness-of-fit (GFI) index (Jo¨reskog & So¨rbom, 1992), and root mean square residual (RMSR) (Jo¨reskog & So¨rbom, 1992). Measures of incremental fit compare the proposed model to the null model (Hair et al, 1992). These include the normed fit index (NFI) also referred to as D1 (Bentler & Bonett, 1980), D2 (Bollen, 1989b), 1 (Bollen, 1986), the comparative fit index (CFI) (Bentler, 1990), and the Tucker-Lewis index, also known as $\rho 2$ (Tucker & Lewis, 1973; Bentler & Bonett, 1980).

Parsimonious fit indices are used to determine whether the overall model fit has been achieved by overfitting the data (Hair et al, 1992). The parsimonious fit criteria used in this study are the adjusted GFI (AGFI) (Jo¨reskog & So¨rbom, 1986) and Akaike’s information criteria (AIC) (Akaike, 1974). Some of the fit indices were provided by LISREL, and some were calculated manually.

Model #2 has a smaller <sup>2</sup> (73.71) with a level of significance greater than 0.05 (P = 0.11). Other absolute fit indices also indicate that this is an adequate fit to the data. Incremental and parsimonious indices indicate that Model #2 is a better fit than Model #1. Note that although D1 and $\rho 1$ are larger for the second than for the first model, they are still smaller than the recommended criteria. However, Bollen (1989b) indicates that these two criteria are overly pessimistic for smaller sample sizes. Our sample of 118 may be considered relatively small for structural equation modelling with LISREL (Hair et al, 1992). Thus, interpreting these two criteria in light of this, the absolute, incremental, and parsimonious fit indices largely support the proposal that Model #2 is a better fit than Model #1.

However, upon closer inspection, we decided to examine another model respecification. In a first order oblique analysis the independent variables ( ) are allowed to correlate. The correlation between the s is represented by the F matrix. The F for the relationship between perceived direct benefits and perceived indirect benefits was 0.68. This is higher than those between other independent variables. Thus, we decided to constrain the F between these two variables to 1.0 to determine if the correlation in the unconstrained model is significantly different from 1.0. If not, then these sets of items may not be unidimensional. They may actually measure a higher order factor rather than two independent factors. As indicated in Table 3, the respecified model (Model #2a) was a worse fit than Model #2. Furthermore, a difference of $X ^ { 2 }$ test reveals that the two models are significantly different from each other at $\alpha = 0 . 0 5$ . This provides further support for the proposal that these scales measure distinct, yet related constructs.

Note that technological compatibility is measured with only two indicators in Model #2. Thus, we imposed constraints on the estimates for these items. In a purely exploratory mode, we decided to assess a three factor, 11-item model, in which these two indicators and their underlying construct were eliminated. As shown in Table 3, all measures of fit improve slightly over Model #2. However, a difference of <sup>2</sup> test indicates that the two models are not significantly different at $\alpha = 0 . 0 5$ . Thus, further assessment is needed to determine the appropriateness of eliminating the technological compatibility construct.

## Internal consistency

Internal consistency assessment, using Cronbach’s coefficient alpha, provides further information about the adequacy of the sets of items. Cronbach’s alpha is greater than 0.70 (Nunnally, 1978) for all constructs except technological compatibility (Table 4). Note that eliminating ‘changed operating procedures’ improved the internal consistency for operational compatibility in Model #2 to Model #1. These findings lend further support for the choice of Model #2 over Model #1. The fact that all constructs except technological compatibility are high in internal consistency, lends support that Model #3, with only three factors, may be the best representation of the underlying constructs.

One reason technological compatibility may not be adequately captured is because EDI has already been implemented, and respondents’ memory of changes has faded over time. Although O’Callaghan et al (1992) reported a unidimensional factor with good internal consistency across pre-adopters and post-adopters for these items, their findings may be specific to the one industry or even to the particular set of respondents they examined. It is doubtful that differences between EDI and user respondent knowledge about technological compatibility explain the lack of internal consistency. ## Discussion and implications of findings

Three of the four proposed scales appear to adequately capture their proposed constructs, whereas the fourth, technological compatibility, does not. These findings indicate that the items that consistently emerge in EDI studies do have validity and can be used to reliably measure direct and indirect perceived benefits and operational compatibility. The items used to measure the latter construct address compatibility of EDI with hardware and software, existing systems, amount of computer support, and with the workplace site itself. One possible explanation for this is that respondents may not have been familiar with those aspects of EDI adoption in their firms. Using the perspectives of one or even two respondents from a firm may limit the ability to capture some aspects of the phenomenon under study (Sabherwal & Vijayasarathy, 1994). These respondents were key EDI users (e.g., purchasing managers, customer support staff) and EDI managers. EDI managers were not technical staff in all cases: in some firms, they were also the end user. Thus, these respondents may not have been aware of the extent of changes in hardware, software, and support that were necessitated by EDI.

The items to measure operational compatibility, on the other hand, appear to adequately do so. Findings indicate that EDI is perceived to disrupt the workplace when it is first implemented, to change operating procedures, and to take a long time to learn. Not surprisingly, these items are interrelated. Installation of a new system will likely disrupt the workplace to some extent until users are comfortable with it. The long learning curve likely reflects not only learning how to interact with the EDI software, but learning the new procedures as well.

One item (‘decreased productivity at first’) did not have a strong relationship with the other three. Perhaps the term productivity was too broad. Because the unit of analysis was the individual, respondents may have responded with regard to their own personal productivity levels. They may have perceived that their own productivity performance was as high as it was before EDI was implemented. Thus, while the unit’s productivity may have temporarily decreased while employees adjusted to EDI, the respondents may not have believed their own productivity was related to the changes. With this in mind, it may be best to assess productivity through a more objective measure.

Perceived direct benefits was well measured by reduced inventory and transaction costs and improved cash flow. Not only are these among the most cited benefits of EDI; they are interrelated in practice. Reducing inventory and transaction costs increases cash flow by freeing cash for other purposes.

Finally, the relationship between the items that measure indirect perceived benefits can also be demonstrated. By allowing firms to exchange transactions between computers rather than via paperwork, EDI improves the information flow between them. Better information flow should allow for more efficient internal operations (e.g., order processing, billing, transaction tracking). Finally, improvements in internal operations and external relationships place a firm in a better position to gain competitive advantage. Although these benefits may not occur simultaneously, a firm that utilises EDI effectively should realise these benefits in time. For firms where EDI is relatively new, these items might not provide as adequate an indicator of indirect benefits as they do in firms where EDI is more mature. In 67% of the firms in this study, EDI has been in use five years or longer; thus they have had time to begin to realise these benefits.

## Summary and conclusions

Because validity cannot be established with only one study (Peter, 1981), findings in this study provide a basis on which further research can be conducted. This study offers a valid and reliable set of scales to measure dimensions of perceived direct and indirect EDI benefits and operational compatibility of EDI for both users and EDI representatives. However, because they are based on two perspectives, the scales should be reassessed before using them to draw conclusions about samples comprised of only EDI representatives or only users.

Technological compatibility was not adequately captured, thus it should be further explored. The scale in this study was not powerful enough to capture effects of the various links between hardware, software, support, and existing systems that comprise the firm’s overall information technology (IT) infrastructure. The attempt to assess each component separately resulted in a scale that was neither valid nor reliable. However, infrastructure is a function of the interrelationships among these components. Thus, one avenue for further exploration is the role of IT infrastructure in EDI compatibility.

Another avenue of exploration is the role of IS maturity in the perception of technological compatibility. Perceptions of technological compatibility may be related to IS maturity of the adopting firms (Sabherwal and Vijayasarathy, 1994; Cox & Ghoneim, 1996). The more mature the firm the less disruptive new technology may be because both IS and line staff are familiar with using new technology and with the disruptions that occur during implementation. Therefore, research that provides information about a firm’s IS maturity may provide insight into the perception of technological compatibility.

Others have indicated that EDI integration progresses through stages (Nygaard-Andersen & Bjørn-Andersen, 1994), and perceptions may differ between stages. Firms often ignore technological compatibility and underestimate its effects on implementation in the early stages of adoption (Nygaard-Andersen & Bjørn-Andersen, 1994). Research about the stages of EDI implementation and perceptions of compatibility may provide a better basis on which to assess effects of technological compatibility.

Finally, because the use of computer-to-computer exchange of transactions is continually evolving, another avenue of exploration is the relevance of these items to the adoption of other types of electronic transaction exchange, such as open EDI or Web-based EDI. For example, security may have a greater role in adoption of the latter than in traditional EDI where a third-party is often responsible for security, and the software is proprietary.

Use of and research about computer-to-computer exchange of transactions appear to be growing rapidly and steadily well into the twenty-first century. Valid and reliable scales to measure issues associated with its use are an important foundation on which not only academic research, but also corporate benchmarking can be built. This study contributes to that foundation by demonstrating that three sets of items often used to describe EDI benefits and compatibility appear to have sound measurement properties. Therefore, they can be relied upon from an empirical perspective, in addition to the traditional descriptive perspective, to provide useful information about those aspects of EDI adoption and integration.
