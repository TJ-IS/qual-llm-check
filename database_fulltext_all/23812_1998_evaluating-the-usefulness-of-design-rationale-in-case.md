# Evaluating the usefulness of design rationale in CASE

H Oinas-Kukkonen

Department of Information Processing Science, University of Oulu, 90570 Oulu, Finland

Advanced systems development is composed of constant alternation between designing artifacts and debating the designs. This article evaluates the usefulness of the Debate Browser design rationale system in the MetaEdit1 CASE environment in one field setting. This usefulness is evaluated in terms of both the system’s utility and usability. The research is composed of coaching, observation, and interviews, and it is supplemented through studying the design output. The evaluation suggests that the usefulness of the system is high. It points to advantages in reading design documents, via improved author-reader communication and the understanding of design solutions. This reduces the possibility of errors in design.

## Introduction

Design rationale (Moran & Carroll, 1996) basically means the understanding of why an artifact has been designed the way it has (Fischer et al, 1992), which may include information on requirements, assumptions, decisions and alternative solutions (the term ‘design’ is used here to refer to any activity in the creation of artifacts from the early phases of development to maintenance). The goal of design rationale capture is that decisions would be based more on facts than opinions, and that inconsistencies would become apparent earlier. Design rationale capture may lead to the achievement of: (a) increased rigour and clarity of thinking and augmentation of designer’s memory; and (b) better communication among team members and stakeholders and improved meetings (Conklin & Yakemovic, 1991). In systems development, design rationale capture may also reduce the total development costs by making maintenance easier. When 60–80 percent of all software costs come from maintenance or redesign, even modest success of design rationale systems can provide substantial benefits. Using such systems designers have to structure and summarise their reasoning processes, while the maintainers have to read and understand the design rationale documents.

Software design can be supported by recording the reasoning behind the proposals issued in design together with other software components. The design process can be described as a conversation among the stakeholders, in which they bring their expertise and viewpoints to bear on the resolution of design issues. The goal of the discussion is for each of the stakeholders to try to understand the specific elements of each others’ proposals, and perhaps to persuade others to accept their viewpoint. Design rationale may make it harder to make unconstructive rhetorical moves while supporting other more constructive moves, such as seeking the central question, asking questions as much as giving answers, and being specific about the supporting evidence for one’s viewpoint. Any problem or concern may require discussion, if not agreement, in order for the design to go on. Design rationale may be used for monologues, e.g., between the diversified viewpoints of an individual developer or his roles, and dialogues between many developers in a development team.

The technical construction discussed here is the Debate Browser design rationale system (Oinas-Kukkonen, 1996) developed for the MetaEdit+ CASE environment (Kelly et al, 1996). This article addresses the following research question: How useful is the design rationale system for systems development? and is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces the target of the evaluation. Section 3 describes the research plan and the selection of the evaluation methods. Section 4 describes the results of the evaluation. Section 5 summarises the contributions of the research.

## Target of the evaluation

Most of the design rationale methods are based on the works of Toulmin and Rittel, the father of many of the current methods being IBIS (Issue-Based Information System). The IBIS method (Conklin & Begeman, 1988) has been shown to have many problems and other methods have been proposed (Ramesh & Dhar, 1992). Comparisons of design rationale methods and tools have been established (Jarczyk et al, 1992; Pries-Heje & Malmborg, 1992), and a general framework for evaluating the methods has been developed (Lee & Lai, 1991). The differences among the design rationale methods originate basically from what is made explicit and what is left implicit in each method (Jarczyk et al, 1992). QAR (Question–Answer–aRgument) design rationale method is abstracted from various design rationale methods for our purposes (Oinas-Kukkonen, 1996). It is close to the Potts and Bruns (1988) method, in which the process aspect of systems development is taken into account to a notable extent. The aim of developing the QAR method was not to provide yet another design rationale method, but to simplify the explicit rhetorical structure of design rationale. This was necessary because many design rationale tools which have been developed still have not been adopted and used by practitioners to near their perceived potential (Jarczyk et al, 1992).

QAR provides a regulated discussion of a proposition between stakeholders, i.e. capturing the design rationale. The basic notions in the method are nodes, links and hyperdocuments. Node is an information container, which can contain text or other media, information on the creator of the node, or timestamp of creation. The discussion is expressed using three kinds of nodes: questions, answers, and arguments. The QAR method focuses on the articulation of the key questions in the design problem. Each question can have many answers. An answer is a statement or assertion that resolves the question. Often answers will be mutually exclusive, but that is not required. Each answer may have one or more arguments that either support that answer or deny its validity. Thus each separate question is the root of a (possibly empty) family tree, with the children of the question being answers and the children of the answers being arguments. There is also a particular way of registering that a question has been resolved by agreement by selecting and presenting one of the suggested answers as a decision. There are different kinds of link in the QAR method. An answer always responds to one and only one question. Arguments must be linked to their answers with either ‘supports’ or ‘objects-to’ links. They may be linked to many answers, which are responses to the same question. Questions may generalise, specialise or replace other questions, or may be parents or children to other questions. A node always belongs to a hyperdocument, a collection of discussions, consisting of nodes and links between the nodes. There can be various design rationale hyperdocuments, i.e., an organization mechanism for debates going on on different kinds of subjects. There could be hyperdocuments, e.g., for different organizational, management, research, product, or other problem domains, as well as within a project for analysis, design, implementation or review concerns.

Many computerised design rationale systems have been developed, such as gIBIS and rIBIS (Conklin & Begeman, 1998; Rein & Ellis, 1991), and the implementations so far have shown that hypertext with its nodelink structure suits as a technology for implementing a design rationale system. Debate Browser builds on this IBIS kind of tradition, being a hypertext-based design rationale system (Oinas-Kukkonen, 1996). It has been integrated with the commercial MetaEdit+ meta-CASE environment, which is a fully configurable multi-user and multi-tool computer-aided software and method engineering environment (Kelly et al, 1996). The design information can be represented as either a diagram, a matrix, or a table, each representation being supported by a specific tool. Debate Browser is used to record argumentation in ‘behind design’ decisions. It enables the investigation of hyperdocuments and their nodes and links with two browsers, a Document Browser and a Graph Browser. Document Browser lists textually all the question nodes of a hyperdocument, the answer nodes of an activated question, and the argument nodes of an activated answer. Graph Browser presents a graphical web of the design rationale hyperdocument, and users are able to zoom the web according to their level of interest. Both Document and Graph Browser enable the investigation of questions from different perspectives, i.e., as a plain collection of questions or from generalisation–specialisation, replacement–replacer, parent–child perspective. They also show the nodes not yet investigated by an individual reader, enable node marking, and give statistical information of the design rationale.

## Research setting

The acceptability of systems depends on various aspects, one of the most important of which is usefulness. The usefulness of a system includes both its utility and usability (Grudin, 1992). Nielsen (1993, p 25) defines the usability of a software system based on five subcharacteristics where the system: (a) should be easy to learn so that the user can rapidly start work using the system; (b) should be efficient to use, so that once the user has learned the system, a high level of productivity is possible; (c) should be easy to remember, so that the casual user is able to return to the system after a period of not having used it, without having to learn everything all over again; (d) have a low error rate, so that users make few errors during the use of the system, and if they do make errors they can easily recover from them; also, catastrophic errors must not occur; (e) should be pleasant to use, so that users are subjectively satisfied when using it; they like it.

## Evaluation plan

Even though heuristic evaluation is a basic method for formative evaluation, testing with real users provides direct information on how people use the software system for their particular problems. When evaluating information systems performance, significant difficulties commonly arise from the fact that this is related to the environment in which the system operates and its intended use, and the fact that the intended use can cover a wide range of tasks (March & Smith, 1995). The intended use in this evaluation covers the analysis and design of a software system, and the evaluation criteria are determined for the artifact in this particular environment.

The test took place in a small Finnish software house in spring 1995, and lasted 1.5 months. A workstation with a Windows operating system, a word processor and the CASE environment were needed to carry out the development tasks. The author worked as the main experimenter for the test. The test group was composed of software designers participating in a real system development project aimed at analysing, designing and prototyping new software features for an existing commercial software product (business process re-engineering features for an activity-based accounting system). This implied a complex, long-range test task, but one that represented directly the main area of use of the object of the research. There were no artificial test tasks, as the software designers were simply using MetaEdit+ for software analysis and design. The means of determining when the users had finished each sub-task in the test (i.e., each development phase or step) was the holding of review meetings which inspected how well the tasks had been fulfilled. The prototype was built using another programming environment and a repository management system. The test procedure was tried out beforehand by the experimenter and one of his colleagues.

There were no user aids such as manuals or on-line help systems available in the prototype environment, but the test users did receive a short period of training in the CASE environment, including the design rationale system. The test users were students of information systems and software engineering at the local university, who were participating in a compulsory systems development project organised by the university and run in a software company as part of the later stage of their studies. Five students selected this project out of six alternatives. The students had average skills in systems development methodologies and CASE technology, but they were novice users as regards the specific CASE environment and design rationale systems. Thus they provided a good sample of users. There were three female and two male students in the test group (for anonymity’s sake they are referred to below in the female form).

The experimenter had extensive knowledge of the application and its user interface, because he had designed the application. For this reason, and in order to maintain objectivity, an additional experimenter was hired to do some parts of the testing. This second experimenter was familiar with the idea of design rationale beforehand, but had not used the specific environment or design rationale systems, nor was he familiar with the test methods beforehand. He was allocated a total of 200 hours of work, consisting of orientation and the planning of practicalities (70 h), testing tasks and periodic reporting (80 h), and final reporting (50 h).

## Evaluation methods

Usability engineering research has provided information systems with a number of usability assessment methods, e.g., heuristic evaluation, performance measures, thinking aloud, observation, questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, logging actual use and user feedback (Nielsen, 1993). We selected interviews and a combination of coaching and observation for our purposes. These methods enabled us to evaluate the quality of the system with three to five test users, who provided information about what users think of the system, which helped to recognise misunderstandings in its use. The selection of test methods and their application in our field setting are described below with reference to Nielsen’s textbook (1993).

The coaching method is based on the thinking aloud test, which means that the test users express their thoughts verbally when using the system. The coaching method provided a more natural approach than thinking aloud as such, however, because it was based on situations in which the user(s) and the coach were interacting and the coach was trying to promote purposeful use of the system. This suited our setting well, because the target was an additional feature of the environment and there were no on-line help systems or manuals. On the other hand, coaching helped to minimise the differences in individual skills. The test users were motivated by obtaining more knowledge of the system as they could ask the coach any questions related to the system and he answered to the best of his knowledge. They were also encouraged to comment on the use of the system by answering questions such as: What are you thinking now? What do you think this message means? What do you think will happen if you do so-and-so? Did you expect that to happen? The coaching method was augmented with observation, in that the coach was observing user actions in real work, asking the reasons for their actions every now and then. The coach made notes about his observations and his own actions.

While the design output did not explain why a user acted the way she did, interviews could reveal this. In our case, each designer was interviewed by the main experimenter at the end of the project, the questions being formulated in such a way that they had to be answered with full sentences rather than with yes or no replies, for example: What do you think about this system feature? For what did you use this feature? What features were good/bad and in what situations? Although the key questions were pre-planned, the interviews allowed free interaction between the interviewer and interviewee. Each interviewee was asked questions, such as why she did not use a specific feature of the system. The interviewer remained neutral during the interview and did not express either agreement or disagreement with the interviewee. The secondary experimenter participated in the actual interviews and made tape recordings of them.

The observations and interviews provided reliable data in as far as the experimenters maintained their objectivity. The reliability of usability tests is generally a problem because of the huge individual differences between test users. This applies to our study as well, because it did not address the individual differences between test users. The validity of the usability test means whether the test really measures something of relevance to the usability of real products in real situations outside the laboratory. Because the system was tested with software designers solving real systems development problems in a real social environment, the validity of the test can be regarded as high. It provided direct information about how people use design rationale systems and what their exact problems are. On the other hand, social influences were neglected.

Longitudinal or cross-sectional studies for measuring the impact of CASE tools would strengthen the current results to a greater extent (Coupe, 1994). Longitudinal research requires a group engaged in developing systems first without the tools under assessment and then with them, and cross-sectional research requires simultaneously sampled tool users and non-users. Even though these methods were not addressed in the research reported in this article, this evaluation attempted to overcome the usual reliance only on the system developer’s perceptions and the omission of the system user’s perceptions when measuring the impact of CASE tools.

The direct budget costs of the test consisted of the salaries of the experimenters who planned and ran the testing. Indirect costs other than those allocated to the project in any case amounted only to the time spent by the main test users on the interviews and some other minimal costs such as audio cassettes. The test users were not paid for test tasks, as they would have been participating in the project in any case, and no extra company staff or other resources were used for testing purposes.

## Results

The test use consisted of three phases: (1) definition of requirements; (2) analysis of new features; and (3) design of new features. The designers focused on analysing the problem domain and designing the target system for a total of 484 hours within a period of 49 days, employing a popular object-oriented analysis and design methodology, which was supported by MetaEdit+.

The designers modelled five object-oriented diagrams, while seven debate hyperdocuments (concerning the design rationale) were captured: one at each development phase, two additional ones at the first phase (current systems analysis and domain analysis related hyperdocuments) and two along the project (modelling tool and project management related hyperdocuments). Hyperdocuments concerning implementation, maintenance, further plans, used methodology, software house and research issues remained empty.

There were altogether 264 design rationale nodes created by designers. There were 1.66 answers for each question, and 1.09 arguments for each answer (1.07 links from answers to arguments). All in all, the extent of design rationale capture suggested that the system helped users perform the development tasks to a great extent. The high degree of utilisation of the system also suggested productive mapping of hypertext features to development tasks and efficiency of the system. Altogether 25 warnings or error messages occurred concerning the use of the system, of which 18 arose because of an undocumented feature in the system and seven concerned illegal actions in the system, e.g., trying to define a link before designating the nodes to be linked or trying to find non-existent nodes. The low number of Debate Browser warnings and errors suggested that it was robust and easy to learn and remember. The design output provided no direct information on whether the systems were subjectively pleasing or whether the same goals could have been achieved with a traditional linear format.

## Interviews

The interviews were aimed basically at finding out the proportion of users who say they would prefer this system to some specified competitor. Key comments from the interviews are mentioned below. The examples are selected in such a way that they reflect primarily the general opinion and secondarily the differing opinions. As four of the interviews were conducted in Finnish and only one in English (none of interviewees was a native English speaker), the comments were translated into English as accurately as possible.

All the interviewees perceived the Debate Browser as supporting document reading tasks and that it was worth capturing different design alternatives, the benefit becoming explicitly manifest in further work such as maintenance or re-use. Three interviewees reported that the Debate Browser supported authoring by helping them ‘discover alternative solutions, consider and discuss different viewpoints, and make decisions about them’ or ‘to return to your earlier reasoning and see why you made a certain decision’. Two other interviewees said that they ‘considered alternatives only after they had already made the decision’. Design rationale capture in general was considered important and beneficial by all interviewees. ‘The design rationale should be captured, because afterwards you can check what you have done and why. You can’t carry everything in your head. ‘Others can understand what you have done and why.

Three interviewees felt that the same goals could not be achieved by persisting with a traditional linear format. Their comments were: ‘I don’t believe the same goals could be achieved, because MetaEdit+ can record your thinking.’ ‘MetaEdit+ is better than traditional tools, because linking and argumentation are easy. If comments occur to you as you are creating diagrams, you can record them immediately.’ On the other hand, two interviewees felt that equally good design output could be achieved without the design rationale system. One was of the opinion that the Debate Browser supported decision making better than a text editor, while the other stated that it made no difference in principle whether argumentation was captured using a specified design rationale tool or a text editor, but she felt that the user interface of the tool made it easier to define key questions and find answers, and she was willing to use it again in the future. Furthermore, all the interviewees were of the opinion that design quality was improved by the Debate Browser: ‘% particularly when other stakeholders understand the decisions that have been made’, or ‘reading the thoughts of other designers helps understand the designs better’.

All the interviewees were of the opinion that the nonlinear attributes of the system were mapped onto systems development tasks in a productive manner. For instance, they said: ‘If I were to change to another application for making comments, commenting would be more difficult, and I doubt whether it would happen.’ All the interviewees felt that the Debate Browser supported both communication and thinking (three interviewees defined the system more as a communication vehicle than a thinking aid, while two were inclined to the opposite opinion). Concerning the best features of the Debate Browser, interviewees listed ‘the basic idea of questions, answers and arguments’, ‘organising ideas using hyperdocuments’, ‘collecting nodes as hyperdocuments, or the automatic marking of unread nodes’. The worst feature was ‘the bug in transforming the first question into a hyperdocument.’ A general danger attached to design rationale was ‘too much to read, which may confuse you’.

The Debate Browser was conceived as of easy to learn and remember in all interviewee comments. All the interviewees were highly pleased with the system: ‘You can easily reach the argumentation from the diagrams, and together they form a good combination.’ ‘The best feature is the possibility for recording your own reasoning, even if you do not always have enough time to do so.’ The Debate Browser was perceived to be efficient to use. The capture of design rationale in general was conceived of as difficult by two interviewees, however, one who used Debate Browser in several development phases was of the opinion that a set of basic questions for each phase would make the capture easier, while another was of the opinion that ‘arguments which support one answer and object to another are fuzzy.’ The system was regarded as robust and as having a low error rate. The interviewees nevertheless felt that the Debate Browser helped avoid errors in design: ‘When someone else reads your diagrams, it is easier for him/her to understand you through linking and reasoning. In addition to this, when you investigate your own work, you can remember more easily why you ended up with a specific solution.’ ‘Problems and reasons for decisions are normally recorded better. Even if we couldn’t achieve any better results, we can at least justify our solutions. Nothing is added by accident, because everything needs to be reasoned.’On the other hand, one of the interviewees expressed her opinion that ‘the Debate Browser does not help in the actual thinking process, but only in documenting it. If your logic is faulty, your documents will suffer from the same problem. Use of the Debate Browser does not guarantee that there will be no errors in your design.

Finally, the interviewees felt that the benefit of design rationale was similar at all development phases. Nevertheless, some of the designers who used the Debate Browser at an early phase felt that analysis was the most important application domain for design rationale, because all other later solutions were based on it.

## Conclusions

The design rationale system helped CASE users to perform the intended tasks in our field setting. The evaluation methods pointed to advantages in reading design documents, via improved author-reader communication and the understanding of design solutions. The interviews revealed that the support given by the Debate

Browser for authoring was to some extent controversial, which may be explained by the small number of diagrams, the small number of designers, the relatively low dedication of the designers to the project goals, or the relatively weak support for the multi-user features of the CASE environment. Even so, it should be noted that the reader and author roles are not entirely exclusive, as authoring always includes reading as well and active reading includes the authoring of comments. Moreover, the Debate Browser was regarded as helping to produce designs of better quality.

The designers achieved their goals in all the development phases. Observation and design output did not provide data assessing whether the same goals could have been achieved by persisting with a traditional linear format, but the interviews revealed that the system helped achieve goals in reading and to some extent in authoring.

The interviews and observations suggested that all the designers were pleased with the Debate Browser. They used it willingly and expressed a desire to use it or a similar system in the future. All the test methods provided evidence of the high learnability and memorability of the system. Observation also suggested that the QAR method and the construction-argumentation view of design lying behind the Debate Browser were relatively easily adopted. The research methods revealed some usability problems concerning the Debate Browser, e.g., a lack of reporting and printing facilities and a lack of an undo or change option for a decision. Despite these usability problems, the system was efficient to use. The system was also robust and had a low error rate, and no catastrophic errors occurred. Moreover, the design rationale system enabled the design of less error-prone documents through improved understanding and communication. For the reasons considered above, mapping of the non-linear attributes onto the systems development tasks was also deemed very successful in the Debate Browser.

Overall, the evaluation suggested that the usefulness of the design rationale system for systems development was high in our field setting. The evaluation involved software analysis and design, but design rationale systems may hold even greater promise for maintenance or redesign.
