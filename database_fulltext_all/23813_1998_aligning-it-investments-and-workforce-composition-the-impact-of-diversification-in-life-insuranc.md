# Aligning IT investments and workforce composition: the impact of diversification in life insurance companies

C Francalanci<sup>1</sup> and H Galal<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Dipartimento di Elettronica e Informazione, Politecnico di Milano, Piazza Leonardo Da Vinci, 32, 20133, Milano, Italy; <sup>2</sup>Harvard Business School, Harvard University, Graduate School of Business Administration, Soldiers Field, 02163, Boston, MA, USA

This paper investigates the impact on productivity of combined changes in information technology (IT) investments and workforce composition of life insurance companies. Workforce composition is defined in terms of three broad classes: clerical, managerial and professional. Hypotheses on the combined impact of changes in IT investments and workforce composition on productivity are proposed and empirically tested on a sample of companies from the US life insurance industry. Companies are clustered based on two main strategy alternatives, focus on a market niche vs diversification. In niche companies, higher IT investments accompanied by a decrease in the clerical and managerial components of the workforce are found to have a positive contribution to productivity. On the contrary, higher IT investments in conjunction with a decrease of the professional component show a negative effect. In diversified companies, increasing IT investments show a positive contribution to productivity when accompanied by a decrease of the professional component and a growth in the clerical and managerial component of the workforce. Findings are discussed in the light of fundamental competitive goals in the industry.

## Introduction

Literature surveys by Willcocks (1992), Brynjolfsson (1993), Smith and McKeen (1993) and Wilson (1995) review empirical studies on how information technology (IT) contributes to performance of companies and suggest a number of challenges for future research. A key problem, initially highlighted by Robey (1977), is the deemphasis or the omission of non-technological variables affecting performance. The traditional IT-productivity literature seeks a direct causal relationship between IT investments and productivity, as opposed to viewing productivity gains as a result of an alignment between managerial and technological choices (cf. Markus & Robey, 1988). By not including other important organizational variables, explanatory models of performance become vulnerable to mis-specification and may produce distorted findings.

Towards addressing this limitation in previous research, this report investigates the combined impact of changes in IT investments and workforce composition on the productivity of life insurance companies. The US life insurance industry was chosen for this study because of the historically large investments in IT (cf. LOMA & Arthur Andersen Report, 1984; LOMA & Tillinghast Report, 1988; LOMA Tracking Information Processing Expense Study, 1995), the information intensive characteristic of the business, and the high product innovation that is attributable to IT use. Additionally, due to regulatory requirements, there is an abundance of publicly available data on insurance companies.

The study focuses on the number of workers in three broad classes, clerical, professional and managerial. These classes represent the main work specialisations in insurance companies (cf. Huggins & Land 1992). Determining the appropriate percentage of workers in each class in the light of pressures to downsize and technological developments is an important consideration for insurance companies (Black & Skipper, 1994). The common view in the industry is that clerical staff and managers should be reduced to improve efficiency, whereas professionals, who hold the core knowledge to design insurance products, should be retained and grown (cf. Huggins & Land, 1992). IT enables these changes by simplifying and eliminating clerical and managerial roles, while supporting professionals. Many executives believe that the intellectual capital provided by professional workers in life insurance represents the key source of competitive advantage and associate their prospective success with the ability to leverage the competencies of these workers to deliver increasingly customised products (cf. Francalanci et al, 1995, 1996; Sclafane, 1997).

We have found that the actual relationship between IT, workforce composition and productivity does not always confirm these predictions. Our empirical analysis is based on a longitudinal sample which includes data on 34 US life insurance companies over a ten-year period from 1986 to 1995. In previous research, this sample had provided evidence contradicting common beliefs in the industry, when data are pooled and considered aggregately (Francalanci & Galal, 1998). Here findings are contrasted for two different types of life insurance companies, high diversified vs niche. (Throughout the rest of this article we use ‘low diversified’ and ‘niche’ synonymously). These two categories represent opposite and coexisting strategies in the life insurance industry. The findings suggest that different strategy choices necessitate different choices in aligning IT and workforce composition to achieve higher productivity. Niche companies seem to gain productivity from aligning higher IT investments with decreasing clerical staff and managers and a higher proportion of professionals. The opposite holds for highly diversified companies. This report interprets this divergence in the light of current changes and competitive goals in the life insurance industry.

## The relationship between IT, structure and productivity

The cumulative tradition of IT-performance studies offers ambiguous conclusions. Research on the insurance industry has found both confirming (cf. Bender, 1986; Harris & Katz, 1991) and disconfirming evidence (cf. Franke, 1987; Venkatraman & Zaheer, 1990) of positive performance implications of IT investments. Studies on other industries have also documented contradictory findings. For example, Siegel and Griliches (1991), Krueger (1993), Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1993, 1996), and Mahmood and Mann (1993) suggest a positive relationship between IT and performance, while Loveman (1988) and Strassmann (1990) have found negative or no contribution of greater investments in IT.

Recent surveys have described in detail the methodological and conceptual limitations underlying these studies (cf. Willcocks, 1992; Brynjolfsson, 1993; Smith & McKeen, 1993; Wilson, 1995). Our emphasis here is on a specific conceptual issue with previous research, namely the lack of contextual variables to control for effects other than IT that influence performance. Robey (1977), Caves (1980) and more recently, Scott (1992) have criticised the technologically deterministic models for their incompleteness. Robey (1977) suggests that as a sole independent variable for questions of organizational change or performance, IT is likely to be suspect. We believe that the exclusion of other relevant determinants of performance not only leads to an inaccurate assessment of the influence of technology on performance, but also provides models with limited explanatory value.

Our specific interest is on workforce composition as a structural variable whose relationship with IT has been studied extensively in information systems as well as organizational research. Previous research investigating IT and workforce composition has typically examined the cause and effect relationship between these two variables and offers inconclusive findings. For example, IT was found to both reduce the number of middle managers (cf. Hoos, 1960; Whisler, 1970; Crowston et al, 1986; Brynjolfsson et al, 1994), as well as increase it (cf. Lee, 1964; Blau et al, 1976). IT was also found to decrease the number of clerical workers (cf. Lee, 1964; Klatzky, 1970; Foster & Flynn, 1984; George, 1986). More broadly, IT was found to reduce the overall number of employees (cf. Brynjolfsson et al, 1994), or have no effect at all (cf. Crowston et al, 1986). Rather than focus on the causal relationship between IT and workforce composition, investigating their combined impact on productivity can help reconcile previous conflicting results. Companies aligning IT and workforce composition differently, may also show a corresponding variance in productivity.

## Trends and hypotheses in the life insurance industry

Life insurance companies are differentiated primarily based on their business strategy, that is, the choice of products and markets each of them wishes to compete in. Two distinctive strategy trends appear to be emerging in the life insurance industry. On the one hand, there is an increase of niche companies which provide specialised insurance protection and are able to react quickly to changing customer needs (cf. Greenberg, 1997; Sclafane, 1997), while on the other there is a definite trend towards more large and diversified companies which focus on cross-selling between insurance products as well as related investment products (cf. Connolly, 1995; Friedman, 1996; Piontek, 1996; Jennings, 1997). This study focuses on companies which fall under either of these dominant strategies. (See Appendix 3 for the operating definition of these two strategies). Below, we briefly describe trends in IT expenditure and workforce composition for the two groups of companies. A set of hypotheses on how choices in IT and workforce composition affect productivity is subsequently proposed.

Overall, for our sample of companies, IT expenses to total premium income suggest a steady downward trend from a historic high of 2.5% in 1986 to a low between 1.5–2% in the period 1991–1992. Subsequently, IT expenses increase through 1994 –1995 to the range of 3– 3.5% of premium income. However, when companies are clustered by strategy, a marked variation in IT expenses can be observed between niche companies and highly diversified companies. As illustrated in Figure 1 in Appendix 1, niche companies show considerably higher IT expenses than highly diversified companies. For niche companies, IT expenses to premium income range from a low of around 2% prior to 1992 to roughly 6% of premium income in 1995. While for diversified companies the range is 1–1.5% prior between 1986 and 1992 and then ranges between 1.5–2.5% through 1995.

These changes in IT investments have coincided with changes in structure. During the period 1989–1995 the number of employees decreased to almost 60% of the size of the company in the mid-1980s (see Appendix 2). This general reduction in workers has affected workforce composition differently for niche vs diversified companies. Professionals represent the predominant class of workers in diversified companies, constituting over 50% of the total number of employees since the early 1990s. Niche companies have a generally higher percentage of managers and clerical staff, representing 16% and 42% of the total number of employees, respectively.

These trends have contributed to a number of implicit hypotheses related to how changes in IT and structure should be aligned in the life insurance industry. Notwithstanding the differences between niche and diversified companies, managers’ views on productivity improvement are typically shaped by the practices of the latter group of companies (Black & Skipper, 1994). This may be related to the much higher levels of productivity of diversified vs niche companies. In our sample, diversified companies were on average 50% more productive (as measured by revenue per employee) than niche companies, although both groups of companies show increasing productivity in the study period (see Figure 2 in Appendix 1). While the trends summarised in this section are purely descriptive, observers may mistakingly try to infer causal patterns from them. Specifically, companies show generally increasing IT investments in conjunction with growing productivity. Furthermore, for the more productive group of companies increasing IT investments would be associated with a lower proportion of managers and clerical personnel and a greater proportion of professionals. The underlying explanation being that IT applications contribute to the automation of clerical and managerial work, while supporting professionals without necessarily substituting for them. In order to test such relationships, we first formalise them in the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: Increases of IT expenses in and of themselves lead to increases in productivity.

Hypothesis 2a: Decreases of managers or clerical workers relative to professionals in and of themselves lead to increases in productivity.

Hypothesis 2b: Increases in professionals relative to clerical workers and managers, lead to increases in productivity.

Hypothesis 3: Increases of IT expenses accompanied by decreases of managers and clerical workers or increases of professionals lead to higher productivity.

The different roles played by clerical staff, managers, and professionals in life insurance companies reinforces hypothesis 3 as the appropriate alignment between technology and workforce composition. Professionals are in fact regarded as the class of workers owning the skills and experience to meet customers’ needs by designing adequate insurance products and ultimately delivering the service. Increasing attention to the policy terms required by customers and more customised and complex insurance products as a response are also regarded as critical to a company’s competitiveness (cf. Greenberg, 1997). This product-oriented mentality tends to position other workers in support roles to professionals and, in this sense, favours an increase in the percentage of professionals over both managerial and clerical workers.

The objective of our empirical analysis is to test the hypotheses on the IT-structure alignment outlined above. While the aggregate trends described are useful as a basis for generating hypotheses, they are an inadequate support for validation. Trends are not helpful to understand how changes in IT and structure contribute independently and jointly to changes in productivity. Only through systematic statistical analysis can we be able to distinguish between independent and combined effects and, accordingly, provide evidence to support or refute our hypotheses.

## Model operationalisation and summary of results

The hypotheses defined above are tested using fitted linear regression models based on a sample comprised of 34 US life insurance companies over a ten-year period, between 1986 and 1995 (see Appendix 3 for a description of data sources and triangulation procedures). As summarised in Table 1, our IT, structure, strategy, and performance variables are operationalised as ratios as opposed to absolute values. This represents a common methodological choice that is recommended in crosssectional studies in order to obtain indicators that are more easily comparable across different companies.

Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1993) have proposed that there are three broad categories of performance measures, namely productivity, profitability, and value for consumers. We focus on productivity as a measure of performance that can be more easily tied to changes in organization and adoption of technology. Specifically, ‘revenue per employee’ is adopted as the operating measure of productivity. Profitability measures, such as ROE, and consumer value indicators are influenced by variables related to financial markets that are not always directly controllable by managers and are consequently less suitable for the objectives of our study.

It is important to note that we have assumed a linear relationship between percent changes in dependent and independent variables. The dependent and independent variables were all operationalised as year-to-year differences (indicated by D) rather than absolute values. This allows us to test a linear correlation between changes in ‘revenue per employee’ and in the independent technology and structure variables. The incremental effect of the independent variables on productivity is much more straightforward to compare across different companies where there may be large variance in the absolute values.

‘Total IT expenses’ are defined as investments amortised over a five-year period, a common practice among companies capitalising IT investments. In our amortisation function, we have assumed a lag between IT investments and their effect on performance, assuming that IT’s effects peak after two years and are proportionally lower in the preceding and following years. Amortised investments are an alternative to lag variables, whose chief advantage is to avoid the choice of a predetermined amortisation function. However, they introduce additional variables in the regression models which, in turn, reduce the available degrees of freedom. This, in turn, may result in a decrease of model significance (cf. Afifi & Clark, 1990). Regarding structural variables, note that the sales force of insurance companies is sometimes comprised of external agents that are not included in any of our classes of workers (cf. Black & Skipper, 1994). However, the employees in charge of managing a company’s relationship with its external sales agents as well as internal agents are classified as managers. Also note that both managerial and clerical intensity are weighted against the number of professionals, since they are both aimed at the coordination of professional activities and should be assessed accordingly. Conversely, professional intensity measures the number of professionals against the overall number of managers and clerical staff.

Statistically, interaction variables allow testing of hypotheses on the combined effect of two independent variables on the dependent variable. Their statistical significance in a model indicates that the two independent variables interact with one another in explaining the dependent variable. Excluding a significant interaction variable from an explanatory model would provide incorrect parameter estimates for the independent effects of the interacting variables.

## Discussion

Our first finding is that at an aggregate firm level, changes in IT alone, measured by the coefficient of ‘Dtotal IT expense’, show a significant and negative contribution to improvements in revenue per employee observable in all models. This suggests that companies that make positive changes in IT investments independent of any changes in organizational structure are likely to observe negative performance benefits and provides evidence to reject hypothesis 1. It must be noted that aggregate IT expenses have been criticised for their lack of specificity, while the independent analysis of individual technology applications would be more likely to uncover positive returns on investments (cf. Farbey et al, 1995).

Independent effects of changes in structure, measured by the coefficients for changes in ‘administrative, professional’, and ‘managerial intensity’, are somewhat more varied for different strategic choices. Positive changes in ‘administrative intensity’ and ‘managerial intensity’ not accompanied by changes in technology investments lead to negative contributions to performance for low diversified companies (M1, M2). This provides evidence to support hypothesis 2a for niche companies. However, as companies grow in diversification, an increase in ‘managerial intensity’, contributes positively to productivity (M5), thus refuting hypothesis 2a for this group of companies. Finally, the effect of increasing ‘professional intensity’ without changes in IT investments, leads to increased productivity for niche companies (M3). It is noteworthy that this contribution to performance becomes significant and negative as companies increase in diversification (M6). As for changes in managerial intensity, changes in professional intensity lead us to support hypothesis 2b or refute it depending on the companies’ strategy choices.

To understand the combined impact of changes in IT and structure we focus on the coefficients of the interaction variables. For low diversified companies, the negative coefficient of the interaction variable between ‘total IT expense’ and ‘administrative intensity’ suggests that positive changes in IT investments accompanied by negative changes in ‘administrative intensity’ lead to a positive contribution to changes in productivity (M1). This means that if we increase IT while decreasing the proportion of clerical workers we find increases in productivity. Conversely, increases in IT investments accompanied by increases in ‘administrative intensity’ lead to a negative effect on productivity. These findings support hypothesis 3 and are consistent with the notion that if a labour-substituting technology is introduced without correspondingly reducing labour, we may observe overall reductions in productivity. However, for highly diversified companies the interaction variable is insignificant (M4), suggesting that IT investments in applications supporting clerical workers are not very relevant to performance. It is important to reiterate that when an interaction variable is significant, it is statistically non-meaningful to refer to independent effects of the interacting explanatory variables (cf. Draper & Smith, 1981). In our case, the significance of the interaction between ‘total IT expense’ and ‘administrative intensity’ for low diversified companies indicates that IT and structure do not operate independently of one another in affecting performance.

Similarly, the negative coefficient of the interaction variable between ‘total IT expense’ and ‘managerial intensity’ suggests that for low diversified companies positive changes in IT investments accompanied by a reduction in ‘managerial intensity’ result in a positive contribution to performance (M2). This lends support to hypothesis 3 and to IT-enabled downsizing initiatives of middle managers. In contrast, the coefficient of the interaction variable between ‘total IT expense’ and ‘managerial intensity’ becomes positive when companies diversify (M5). Not only do combined positive changes contribute positively to performance, but the independent influence of managerial intensity is also positive and large. Finally, the combined influence of changes in IT and professional intensity suggests that positive changes in IT accompanied by increases in the proportion of professionals leads to improved performance for low diversified companies (M3). As diversification increases, positive changes in IT together with decreases in the proportion of professional workers seem to contribute to higher revenue per employee (M6).

While we are able to test hypothesis 1 unambiguously using our models, hypotheses 2 and 3 are more elusive. For low diversified companies, we observe that reducing the proportion of clerical staff and managers while increasing IT investments leads to positive changes in productivity. Conversely, positive increases in the proportion of professionals and IT investments lead to greater performance. However, as companies grow in diversification, this relationship changes and increases in the ratio of managers to professionals in combination with positive changes in IT investments are found to lead to performance benefits. The alignment in low diversified companies would support the view that in the life insurance industry more professionals are beneficial to overall productivity. Diversified companies would instead support the opposite effect.

Although these results certainly need additional testing to be generalised, their comparison with the industry trends discussed earlier in the paper highlights conspicuous discrepancies. As a trend, the proportion of professionals is higher with respect to other classes of workers in diversified companies. However, a growth in the professional component of the workforce does not seem to be related to higher productivity, especially when it is accompanied by increasing IT expenses. Conversely, the lower proportion of professionals in niche companies over our study period contrasts with the gains in productivity of companies simultaneously investing in IT and increasing their professional workforce.

Notwithstanding, the findings may in fact be supported by broader industry explanations. A major concern in the life insurance business is establishing longlasting relationships with customers. The rules of the game in this industry have traditionally been relatively clear: customers would be subject to risk and the insurer would offer the professional knowledge to assess those risk conditions and provide an affordable coverage over time (cf. Huggins & Land, 1992; Black & Skipper, 1994; Jones & Long, 1996). Niche companies address specialised customer needs by focusing on a narrow range of products. The professional knowledge to understand specific customer needs, assess risk, and tailor a satisfying policy remains a critical element. On the other hand, being able to reduce the administrative and managerial overhead decreases the distance between professionals and customers and improves efficiency in distributing and servicing the product. In this regard, reliable access to customer and product information through IT is essential. Our results not only conform to these managerial beliefs, but they also provide evidence to support the role that technology plays in helping niche companies change their workforce composition to achieve productivity gains.

Highly diversified companies face a very different scenario. The professional literature has documented considerable turmoil in the life insurance business, where companies not only have difficulties expanding, but also preserving their existing customer bases (cf. Roach, 1991; Taylor & Arthur, 1996; Sclafane, 1997). Customers are becoming more sophisticated and are searching for one stop shopping for all of their financial needs, which include insurance as well investment management. Non-traditional providers of insurance, such as banks, have in fact started to offer insurance coverage among their financial products as a way to address these demands (Brown, 1997; Sclafane, 1997). Accordingly, diversified insurance companies are faced with broader competition from institutions that are traditional providers of other financial services, such as portfolio management, brokerage, and trading.

Evolving to this environment involves adopting a different business model for life insurance companies. Customers are less concerned with trust over a long-term horizon, and are increasingly oriented to the convenience of customised insurance products. In short, life insurance is being commoditised and distribution is becoming the key to reach customers. At the same time synergies among products are being recognised and capitalised upon. For example, when life insurance has been paid out, customers have additional disposable income to invest (cf. Taylor & Arthur, 1996). Insurance companies can use this information to then propose a broader scope of products that meet customers’ other financial needs over time. Sclafane (1997) emphasises how ‘competition has become a matter of knowledge’, referring to the need for preceding competing institutions to offer appropriate financial products to customers based on their history of investments.

To tap these opportunities, diversified companies may regard their professional competence less critically than the ability to coordinate different lines of business and cross-sell based on the knowledge over existing customers (cf. Taylor & Arthur, 1996; Greenberg, 1997). Managers often envision their products as a set of standard modules that professionals should efficiently compose into a customised product (cf. Francalanci et al, 1995). Again, in this context IT is crucial to managing and accessing customer and product information. Our findings confirm this view, by showing improvements in productivity as the proportion of managers increases with respect to other classes of workers. They also support technology as a significant determinant of productivity improvements in conjunction with changing workforce composition.

## Conclusions

The professional literature both in the life insurance industry and in general has frequently questioned how changes in IT and organizational structure should be aligned. Towards addressing this question, this report has focused on the combined impact of choices in IT and workforce composition on productivity. Traditionally, managers have advanced the proposition that IT should be aligned with workforce composition by helping increase the ratio of professionals to other classes of workers. This belief is consistent with the recommendations of management gurus, such as Hammer and Champy (1993), who emphasise the reduction of overall managers relative to professionals and the increase of professionals relative to other types of workers. It is expected that these changes be enabled through increased IT investments which help substitute for certain classes of workers while complementing others. We have tested three hypotheses that summarise these views and found contrasting results for life insurance companies contingent on alternative business strategies. The common notions of alignment between IT and workforce composition appear to hold for niche companies, but not for highly diversified companies.

With respect to previous testing on the aggregate data sample (Francalanci & Galal, 1998), strategy seems to be an important discriminant of the alignment between IT and workforce composition. Previous significance of industry-level correlations suggests the existence of alignment trends with a dominant positive effect on productivity. However, a different alignment between IT and workforce composition seems to be correlated with productivity gains depending on the level of business diversification.

Generalising these results would require additional testing. An important issue is whether the findings hold for insurance companies operating in markets different from the US, with a different regulatory regime and, possibly, a specific industry structure. Our most important contribution with respect to previous IT-productivity literature is methodological. Our results suggest the use of more complete models for how IT interacts with other organizational variables in delivering productivity benefits—e.g., in our case, changes in workforce composition. While our study is limited in sample size and in considering a particular industry, it represents a first step for future researchers to extend IT-productivity models while incorporating additional determinants of performance. This, in turn, would provide a stronger foundation to advance prescriptions on how managers can more effectively integrate their technology with other organizational choices.

## Appendix 3: Data and methodology

## The sample

The data set is comprised of data from 34 US life insurance companies over a ten-year period, between 1986 and 1995. This data set has been selected from a larger sample including 75 companies over the 14-year period 1982–1995. The original sample has been reduced to include only stock companies, thus excluding mutuals and fraternals for which public sources of finance data are not always available. The remaining companies have been analysed for data accuracy and completeness. Companies with less than ten years of available data have then been eliminated from the sample. The time period for analysis has been set to ten years starting from 1986 to calculate amortisations of IT investments over a fouryear period (see earlier in the paper).

Data have also been checked for consistency by triangulating multiple sources. The original data set was obtained primarily from a database maintained by the Life Office Management Association (LOMA). Data on organizational structure were then complemented and validated with information from both annual and 10k reports available on all publicly traded companies. Additional strategy and performance data were also obtained from AMBEST insurance reports and COMPU-STAT, a database of financial reports on publicly traded companies. Overall, the data collection and validation effort was carried out over an eight-month period beginning in January 1996.

Our sample was clustered based on high and low values of diversification. Diversification is defined as an aggregate measure of the extent to which a company participates in different product markets. We have considered five primary lines of business recognised in the industry: Whole, Term, Credit, Group and Industrial life insurance (see AMBEST life insurance reports 1996 for a detailed description). We measure diversification as a weighted average of the shares of the product lines relative to total sales, where the weight for each product line is the logarithm of the inverse of its share (Palepu, 1985). Diversification is defined low and high when it is smaller or greater than average, respectively. Seventeen out of the 34 companies were included in each cluster (n = 170). Note that niche companies have on average a smaller size than diversified companies, below and above 500 million dollars, respectively (cf. Scott, 1992, for the relationship between size and diversification).

Pooling multiple time series was used to increase the efficiency of the estimation and obtain an adequate sample size. However, as described above, the pooled sample may violate the assumptions of fixed variance and independence of observations typical for most linear regression and correlation analyses. Heteroscedasticity between cross-sections means that the error term does not have constant variance over all observations. Serial correlation means that the error terms corresponding to different points in time for a particular time series are correlated. It has been demonstrated that serial correlation has no serious effect on the parameter estimates for fitted regression models. However, it does lead to misestimation of precision and statistical significance of individual estimates, thus making it difficult to test the influence of each explanatory variable (Ostrom, 1978).

A first-order autocorrelation has been specified within units and validated by the pooled Durbin–Watson statistic for the corrected sample. Heteroscedasticity was dealt with by applying the Huber–White sandwich estimator of variance in place of the traditional variance calculation (cf. Huber, 1967; White, 1982). This alternative estimator produces consistent standard errors even if the residuals across groups are not identically distributed.
