# Can a lean e-mail medium be used for rich communication? A psychological perspective

W Huang<sup>1</sup>, RT Watson<sup>2</sup> and KK Wei<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Information Systems, The University of New South Wales, Sydney, NSW 2052, Australia; <sup>2</sup>Department of Management, Terry College of Business, The University of Georgia, USA; <sup>3</sup>Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore, Singapore 119260

Media richness theory has been one of the most influential theories in media choice and use for the last decade. The theory has become a controversial research issue in recent years, because some research findings are contrary to the prediction of the theory and therefore cast doubts on it. Until now, we have not fully known why and how a lean e-mail medium can be used to convey effectively rich information in organizations, contradicting the prediction of media richness theory. This paper intends to address this issue, and, from a psychological perspective, provides a theoretical explanation of why and how rich communication in a lean e-mail medium can be realised

## Introduction

Organizational managers spend most of their valuable time in communication (Mintzberg, 1976; Panko, 1992). To communicate, we need media. Media richness has been a central variable in the research literature of communication (Faulk, 1993). Media richness theory (e.g., Daft & Lengel, 1984, 1986; Trevino et al, 1990) has been one of the most influential theories of media choice and use in organizations (Markus, 1994). It claims that media richness is an objective and fixed property of any communication media. According to this theory, faceto-face is the richest medium that can convey rich information whereas e-mail is a relatively lean medium that cannot effectively convey rich information. Further, the theory proposes that rich media improve the performance of equivocal tasks (i.e., the tasks with multiple interpretations of information) whereas lean media increase the performance of uncertain tasks (i.e., the tasks lack of factual information) (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Hence, media choice and use are a rational process where decision makers only need to match media (rich or lean) with task characteristics (equivocal or uncertain).

Media richness theory has become a controversial research issue in recent years, because some studies report that a lean e-mail medium can be effectively used to convey rich information, which is contrary to the predictions of the theory (e.g., Markus, 1991; Fulk, 1993; Zack, 1993; Walther, 1995; Chidambaram, 1996). This suggests that media choice and use may not be a rational process but a social one. However, why and how a lean e-mail medium can convey rich information has not been fully known. Until now, the debate on supporting versus refuting media richness theory has still been going on in communication research.

Further, the development of emerging virtual organizations (e.g., Business Week, 1993; Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1994; Apte & Mason, 1995) would face a theoretical paradox if media richness theory still holds—on the one hand, e-mail is obviously an important medium available for supporting virtual organizations; on the other hand, because organizations often face business problems with equivocality (e.g., Galegher & Kraut, 1990) that require rich media rather than lean media (media richness theory: Daft & Lengel, 1986), the lean e-mail medium would be a bad choice for supporting virtual organizations. If this paradox is not resolved, the development of virtual organizations appears theoretically unfeasible. As a result, more research on media richness and reexamining media richness theory is important and necessary.

Based on his research work that won the best paper award at the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) in 1993, Lee (1994) articulates how rich communication is realised in a lean e-mail medium using five concepts central to a hermeneutics approach: distanciation, autonomisation, social construction, appropriation, and enactment (the enhanced version of Lee’s ICIS paper was published in MISQ, 1994). According to this hermeneutics approach, after an e-mail message is composed and sent out, the e-mail message can exist as separate from the intention of the sender through distanciation and autonomisation. When the message is received, the receiver(s) can appropriate it by bringing the people and social construction behind it to life. In this way, rich meanings of the message can be enacted and rich communication in an e-mail medium is realised (Lee, 1994). Obviously, this appropriation process based on the social construction is vital to the enactment of rich meaning from the message. This vital process can be further analysed from a psychological perspective. In this paper we address this important issue from a psychological perspective, and at the same time, suggest a solution to the theoretical paradox faced by virtual organizations.

## A psychological perspective

Psychological factors are of importance to information systems (IS) research in many aspects such as decision making (e.g., Davis, 1974; Keen & Scott-Morton, 1978; Todd & Benbasat, 1987) and technology adoption (Sproull & Hofmeister, 1986). Few areas of contemporary organizational science remain untouched by a cognitive agenda (Meindl et al, 1994). Nevertheless, a psychological approach has not been adequately adopted in IS research. We describe below a psychological perspective that will be used to explain why and how rich communication is realised in a lean e-mail medium.

Psychological theory indicates that pattern matching is the basic building block of human cognition (e.g., Lindsay & Norman, 1977; Anderson, 1983), which is governed by structures of categories (Rosch, 1978; Cantor & Mischel, 1979; Higgins & Bargh, 1987). Categories consist of various stereotypes and archetypal situations that can be designated by names such as ‘market’ or ‘training’. An issue can be described by base-rate information (of the issue’s environment) and casespecific information (specific to the current situation). Normally, only case-specific information is used to retrieve the exact or similar categories saved in human memory. When facing an issue for judgment, people often quickly retrieve from their own memories to search for categories to match the current situation of the issue (e.g., Simon, 1981). The human judgmental procedure can be further analysed using the theoretical mechanism of anchoring and adjustment (e.g., Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). The retrieved categories function as a judgmental anchor around which people can make adjustments through comparing and matching the categories to the issue at hand. Hence, the anchoring and adjustment mechanism determines the accuracy of understanding and pattern matching.

The issue of rich communication in a lean e-mail medium will be analysed using the above psychological perspective and the HCP corporation case that is adopted from Markus’ working paper (1991). The case describes an interesting story of how a straightforward and innocuous request about the training of a computer word processing system is converted into a politically sensitive and managerially troublesome issue at HCP. The related background information and empirical data of the case are summarised below.

HCP Incorporated has about 7500 employees and is a corporation involved in risk management. E-mail is used for communication in the organization. Original e-mail messages in the case are listed below. (Note: Markus had changed all the names and supplied the words appearing in brackets).

The turning point of this innocuous request was triggered by the fact that Galle, the only new member of staff at HCP in this case, simultaneously sent her request to both CRANE, the vice president in charge of the data processing unit where technical help for using the computer systems was provided, and Nell (his computer user name was EDHCP), a director in charge of the systems training unit that was quite independent from the data processing unit in organizational power structure. Obviously, there was a certain degree of overlap between the two units in the function of teaching end-users in using computer systems. This embedded ambiguity in function could be the main reason for resulting in the potentially political sensitivity of ‘intervening each other’s turf (Markus, 1991). As a result, this simple training request turned out to involve an extraordinary and complicated ‘political issue’; and therefore, rich communication of the political issue must have been realised in a lean email medium among the communicators, including the HCP’s president (Galle’s e-mail was forwarded to him by EDHCP) and executive vice president who were not addressed by Galle in her original request at all.

From: Galle 16-Apr-1987 07:01 To: EDHCP, CRANE, ALPHA, BETA, GAMMA Subj: [The Word Processing System]

In order to become acquainted with [the] word processing system [on HCP Inc.’s distributed minicomputer system], I would like to request that I be allowed to participate in an extensive training programme regarding same. As there are no individuals in [my branch] who could be classified as an ‘expert’ in relationship to this system, we have the only option of contacting [headquarters] when we encounter questions . . . . The manuals which have been supplied [the branch] are not as comprehensive as the on-hands training I anticipate would be received via instruction from a qualified representative.

Based on this case, we will present a psychological interpretation of how rich information was conveyed through the lean e-mail medium.

Galle’s e-mail message of asking for system training can be mainly classified into two main categories: ‘training issue’ and ‘responsible unit’. The first category involves the stereotype of information as training materials, methods, schedules, and so forth. The second one includes the data processing unit and systems training unit, and their mutual relationship. After receiving Galle’s e-mail, both Crane and Nell were likely to go through a cognitive procedure of pattern matching to read (or termed as ‘appropriate’ by Lee (1994)) e-mail messages in the following three steps. First, the above two categories were anchored in their memories. The specific information of the two categories such as training methods, schedules, and two units’ relationships was therefore surfaced and retrieved from their memories.

Second, they compared and matched the anchored categories to the e-mail messages that they received for further adjustment, if necessary. Since they have been with HCP for some years, they knew the issues of training and the two units quite well. In other words, information pertaining to the ‘training issue’ and ‘responsible unit’ should have been acquired, classified into the two categories, and saved in their memories, even before Galle’s e-mail was sent. Hence, this pattern matching between the saved/retrieved categories in their human memories and the specific issue of training request could be quite perfect without a need for further adjustment.

Third, based on the categories retrieved and matched, CRANE and Nell made judgment and took actions. They would not feel much uncertainty about the category of ‘training issue’ because both knew well how to deal technically with specific training problems. Then, the category of ‘responsible unit’ could be specifically examined. The information retrieved from this category would indicate that the two rather independent units at HCP could be involved in this training issue, and further, any unit of the two that made an explicit decision on the training request would run the risk of intervening the other’s turf (Markus, 1991). Based on this information, they made judgments and reached a conclusion that this was an issue involving highly political sensitivity. In this way (through an anchoring and adjustment mechanism), they enacted very rich information for political games from and beyond the e-mail messages. In other words, rich communication was achieved through the lean medium of e-mail. As a result, they took actions to play this newly identified political game, resulting in the involvement of HCP’s president and executive vice president (see the message-1 to message-3), and converted the originally simple and innocuous issue into a complicated one.

## Discussion and implications

This research note proposes a psychological perspective to explain the important issue of rich communication using a lean e-mail medium. This perspective may provide some useful insights and suggest some important research issues about rich communication using lean media, which might not be drawn from other theoretical perspectives.

(1) The psychological perspective can integrate rational and social views of media choice and use, mentioned in the introduction. Current debates on media choice and use mainly focus on rational and social views to claim the superiority of one view over another (Webster & Trevino, 1995). According to our psychological perspective, media use for effective communication would depend on pattern matching, which is a rational process through anchoring and adjustment. But pattern matching is also based on anchoring the categories of social constructions. The process of classifying social constructions (e.g., in the above case, classifying the ‘responsible unit’ at HCP as a category of linking with political sensitivity) should be a social process, because such classifying largely depends on organizational cultures, norms, and contexts. As a result, pattern matching is also a social process. Therefore, the psychological perspective could support an integrative and complementary view of media choice and use (Fulk et al, 1990; Webster & Trevino, 1995).

(2) People often emphasise case-specific information to make a judgment but ignore base-rate information (e.g. Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Bar-Hillel, 1990; Lim & Benbasat, 1996). Taking the HCP case as an example, let us suppose that the management at HCP reaches a final conclusion on Galle’s training request— offer training to Galle through the help-desk in the data processing department. If Galle’s training request (casespecific information) would actually be quite a common request from other employees at HCP (base-rate information); then, according to the psychological perspective, the management would make the same decision as if the training request was only specific to Galle (emphasising case-specific information), rather than organising a formal training seminar involving many other employees (ignoring base-rate information). This might be a potential problem for rich communication in lean media. To avoid this problem, lean media like email may need to be incorporated with certain structures of decision support systems that specifically support and enhance judgment making through scanning and analysing both base-rate and case-specific information.

(3) The psychological perspective can extend the hermeneutic approach proposed by Lee (1994). First, by proposing one possible and basic appropriation behaviour of pattern matching, the perspective shows how appropriation is actually performed in human cognitive activities in terms of anchoring and adjustment mechanism. Second, it paints a clearer picture about the relationship between social construction and appropriation in the hermeneutic approach. Not all social constructions formed in organizations can be relied on for an effective appropriation to enact rich information from e-mail messages. Instead, those effective social constructions should be able to be classified into known or similar categories, which are subsequently saved into human memories. Only from the classified and retrievable categories, pattern matching, thus appropriation, can effectively enact rich information from lean e-mail messages through the mechanism of anchoring and adjustment.

Third, the hermeneutic approach cannot explain well the fact that different people in the same organization with similar working experience and comparable positions (therefore possess similar social constructions) could enact different rich information from the same lean e-mail message. For example, some people may appropriate one e-mail message based on their social constructions and enact the essential rich meanings, whereas others may perform the same appropriation through similar social constructions but end up with different rich meanings. This may not be explained well by the hermeneutic approach (Lee, 1994), but can be articulated by the pattern matching of the psychological perspective. Although these people possess similar social constructions, they are likely to possess different abilities and skills in cognitive activities of anchoring and adjustment. More specifically, some may anchor the correct categories to the e-mail message whereas others may anchor incorrect or inaccurate categories to the same e-mail message, thus resulting in different outcomes of pattern matching and/or appropriation.

Hence, individual differences in cognition might play an important role in the issue of rich communication via e-mail. As a result, organizations should, if possible, either choose those with abilities and skills of anchoring and adjustment to form management teams, or provide effective training programmes to increase management’s abilities and skills of pattern matching.

(4) The psychological perspective can resolve the theoretical paradox faced by developing virtual organizations, which results from the media richness theory. Because through the mechanism of anchoring and adjustment, a lean e-mail medium could also be used to convey rich information.Therefore, from a psychological perspective, a lean e-mail medium may be a good choice for supporting virtual organizations. Consequently, the theoretical paradox does not exist any more.

Further, the issue of accuracy is of importance to any communication media. The psychological perspective could suggest the mechanism of anchoring and adjustment to enhance specifically the accuracy of rich communication via lean media. According to psychological theory, the anchoring and adjustment mechanism determines the accuracy of understanding and pattern matching. More research on this accuracy issue should be conducted in the future.

(5) A comprehensive review of the research literature of GSS (group support systems, which can be regarded as a special type of electronic communication medium) shows that GSS fail to enhance or even decrease a group’s satisfaction with decision quality and decision process (e.g., McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994; Dennis et al, 1996), which has been one of the main concerns in GSS research literature (e.g., Benbasat et al, 1993). The variable of satisfaction is important to system adoption and use, group longevity, and decision implementation (e.g., DeSanctis et al, 1987; Nunamaker et al, 1991). Since group perceptions of satisfaction are strongly affected by psychological factors (e.g., Higgins & Bargh, 1987), future research from a psychological perspective might help to provide additional insights for this unresolved issue facing GSS researchers.

## Conclusion

Although a psychological perspective could be important to IS research, it has not been widely adopted in IS studies. This research note intends to illustrate the potential usefulness of a psychological perspective by exploring how a lean e-mail medium can be used to convey rich information. Those insights derived from this psychological perspective also suggest some new and interesting IS research issues in the future. When contemporary organizations are facing profound changes (Malone & Rockart, 1991; Lewin & Stephens, 1993), electronic media such as e-mail have been regarded as the backbone of new and fluid organization forms to meet the challenges brought forth by these changes (Orlikowski et al, 1995). As a result, the above discussion and insights drawn on the psychological perspective could be of importance to both organizational changes and effective rich communication in lean media.
