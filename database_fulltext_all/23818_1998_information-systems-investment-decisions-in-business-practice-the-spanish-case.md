# Information systems investment decisions in business practice: the Spanish case

B Escobar-Pe´rez

Department of Accounting and Finance, University of Seville, Avda. Ramo´n y Cajal, 1 41018 Sevilla, Spain

Previous research has reviewed the foundations of the most significant methods for the evaluation of investments in information systems (IS). However, in our view, it is also necessary to provide an updated view on the evaluation methods actually used in practice by organizations. This paper offers a view on the investment in IS in business practice in Spain, analysing: (i) the evaluation methods currently used by Spanish firms; (ii) how companies make decisions concerning investments in IS; (iii) the nature and quality of the decisions; and (iv) the consequences derived from them.

## Introduction

In the competitive environment in which business enterprises operate, information systems (IS) investments have become a key factor in coping with the environmental changes and taking advantage of the opportunities they offer.<sup>1</sup>

Growing competition has led firms to feel the need for careful examination of their operating and capital budgets (Bracchi, 1993). Due to margin reductions, firms more than ever need to make sure that their limited resources are efficiently allocated among the different investment alternatives (Carlyle, 1990).

The critical nature that investment decisions and, in particular, IS investment decisions, have in the firms’ global strategy, has made it necessary to investigate the justifications that companies provide in practice for the evaluation and selection of their IS (Willcocks, 1992). In this respect, the basic question is to determine how firms actually make their decisions concerning IS and how they should do it; this question has to be addressed considering:

1. The decision-making process as a whole. The question is ‘how is the investment decision made?’ It is related to the formal and informal organizational dynamics existing since the project is undertaken until it is revised and approved. In some organizations, these dynamics may have a strong political character, prevailing informal ones (Guimaraes & McKeen, 1988); in others the process is developed in a more structured way (Buss, 1983).

2. The criteria followed in the process. The question is ‘why was the investment decision made?’ Criteria are related to financial and non-financial justifications provided when proposing, evaluating and deciding on the investment project.

The purpose of this paper is to shed light on the IS investment decision-making processes followed in Spanish business practice, as well as on the most relevant criteria followed.

## The meaning of IS investment criteria

Since there is no clear definition in the literature as to what an investment in IS represents (Pini, 1990), or what it includes,<sup>2</sup> we consider it necessary to define what an investment in IS means in the context of this paper.

Capital investments may be defined as those intended to purchase fixed assets and from which a flow of revenue is expected (Ruı´z, 1990). Thus, investment decisions and IS represent a bet on the future profitability of the firm (FASB, 1985).

Bacon (1992, p 335) defined IS investments as “Any acquisition of computer hardware, network facilities, or pre-developed software, or any ‘in house’ systems development project, that is expected to add to or enhance an organization’s information systems capabilities and produce benefits beyond the short term.”

On the other hand, the capital budget has to provide a general framework within which IS-related decisions must be considered. In spite of its limitations,<sup>3</sup> the capital budget is based on the estimation of cash flows, the assessment of value along time and the use of certain techniques which determine the discounted value of the expected inflows and outflows of the project. The purpose is to express all values in homogenous monetary units, so that the budget results in a useful tool for the evaluation and selection of IS investments.

Mahmood (1993) argues that firms feel a growing need for the analysis of IS investments and the search for plausible justifications, that are rigorous and comparable with previous investment decisions. As Earl (1989)

points out, this need seems to be increasing as a result of the publication of studies in the IS-related literature, which question the usefulness of some IS investments and show that they usually do not yield the expected results. However, this should not be understood as questioning the need for IS investment per se, rather as an attempt to focus attention on the need to improve the decision-making process in order to ensure that investments are only made in IS when costs are correlated to their contribution to the competitive capability of the firm (Strassmann, 1985).

Any improvement has to be based on the effective and efficient use of resources. Hence, in order to achieve a greater impact on the profitability of the firm, both the definition of the company’s objectives and the strategic use of resources must be improved (Camp, 1992).

This paper focuses on the utilisation of decision criteria within the context of the global decision process in business enterprises. The decision-making process and, in particular, the evaluation and selection of IS investments has received a great deal of attention in the literature, but there seems to exist little agreement among authors.<sup>4</sup> In spite of the importance attached to the information provided by the decision criteria, only a small number of empirical studies have attempted to analyse the financial and non-financial criteria followed in practice in the selection of IS projects (Bacon, 1992; Farbey et al, 1992; Berghout & Renkema, 1994; Ballantine et al, 1996; Golden & O’Flaherty, 1996). Therefore, it is of interest to examine the importance of the criteria followed in the IS investments decision-making process. According to Bacon (1994), three reasons support this view:

1. The effectiveness of the IS investment is affected by the use of different decision criteria.

2. Decision criteria reflect the degree of management involvement and the extent to which business strategy and IS strategy are integrated, and affect the efficiency in the allocation of resources within the organization.

3. For the financial management and management accounting functions, the use of different criteria in the cost-benefit analysis preceding all IS investment decisions, determines whether or not the project should be undertaken, and therefore, it has a significant impact on the profitability of the firm.

Decision criteria may reflect different attitudes, for example, risk-averse or risk-taking attitudes. As such they may also represent different groups within the company and their relative power: e.g., a conservative entrenched management structure versus a more radical ‘income’ management.

Finally, it must be borne in mind that the usefulness of decision criteria depends on the way in which they are used. Criteria should never become ‘number crunching games’ in which results are forced in order to satisfy certain interests, to the detriment of a rigorous analysis (Alvarez, 1993).

## Methodology, criteria and research sample

In order to shed light on the IS investment decisionmaking processes followed in Spanish business practice, we conducted an empirical study based on the survey by Bacon (1992).

Our survey was undertaken between May and November 1994, and consisted of three mailings sent every two months in order to reduce the non-response rate to a minimum. Since our main purpose was not of a prescriptive but of a descriptive nature, in the design of the sample emphasis was put on the selection process, attaching less importance to the subsequent estimation process (Kish, 1982). As a result, our sample consisted of the 250 largest Spanish companies, based on the sales figures reported in 1992 as shown in Actualidad Econo´mica (1993). A large number of industries were represented (Tables 1 and 2).

Even though the largest firms in each industry are the objective of most research projects and, therefore, response rates are likely to be lower when surveying them, we built our sample on this basis for two main reasons:

1. These are the firms which allocate the greatest amount of resources to IS investments (SMAU, 1992).

2. The way in which the criteria are used requires a sound understanding of the real economic dimension of IS in the organization, as well as of their role in the planning process (Camussone & Occhini, 1992). These requirements are more likely to be met by large corporations.

One of the key questions in our study was to decide the person to whom the questionnaire should be addressed. As a result of the great variety of titles used across organizations to designate similar positions, there was no certainty as to whether the recipient of the questionnaire would be the right person. Since this could have a significant impact on the response rates, we decided to address the letters first to the financial manager, and then to the IS manager. In further mailings the order was inverted, and this resulted in a significant increase in the response rate. A plausible explanation for this could be that in most of the firms surveyed, the IS manager is the person with a highest degree of involvement in the IS investment decision-making process.

The mailing consisted of: (i) a standard covering letter in the name of the Department of Accounting in which the firm’s collaboration was requested; and (ii) a form in which the purposes of the research project and the three questions we wanted to address were presented. This second page included a table listing the criteria we considered in our study.<sup>5</sup>

The list of decision criteria was provided in order to ascertain whether or not they were used, to establish the percentage of projects analysed on their basis, and to develop a ranking of criteria according to the number of projects evaluated and the amount invested in them. In order to avoid the bias that this could introduce in the responses, as the respondents might focus their attention mainly on the criteria included in our list, our questionnaire allowed the subjects to mention other investment decision criteria they could consider relevant.

All criteria considered in our study are intended for the evaluation and selection of IS investment projects, e.g., they are the main factor taken into account in order to determine whether or not a project should be undertaken.<sup>6</sup> Thus, the nature of the criteria is not affected by the global decision-making process in which they are utilised.

For the purposes of our research, we considered fifteen decision criteria, out of which six are of a financial nature, six are management criteria and three are development criteria. Although some presented commonalties in certain areas such as systematic risk assessment or could be applied to several projects simultaneously, the responses received showed that the relevance of each criteria was sufficient to consider them separately in our study.

Furthermore, some problems were solved when the original questionnaire was developed. Among them were the following (Bacon, 1992):

1. The distinction between IS investment projects which support implicit objectives of the firm, from those which support explicit objectives.

## Results

The information contained in Tables 3 and 4 is segmented according to the sales figures reported by the firms and their industry, respectively.

The total number of responses received was 90, out of which 76 were valid<sup>8</sup> and 14 were letters in which firms apologised for not being able to disclose the information requested either because it was considered as confidential (8), because they were suffering from financial distress (4) or because majority shareholders were entering the firm (2).

Thus, the valid response rate was 30.4% (76/250). This percentage is slightly lower than the 35% average rate in this kind of study (Kerlinger, 1975). As a result of the interviews held with the IS managers, we concluded that a low response rate could be the consequence of the fact that none of the criteria considered in our study was actually used in the decision process followed in the firms.

## Financial criteria

Table 5 shows that 86.84% of the firms use, at least, one of the financial criteria in the selection of their IS investment projects; 51.31% use some type of discounted cash flow (DCF hereafter) method, and 82.89% of the companies use some other method.

In spite of the importance attached to them by the literature, (Levy & Sarnat, 1994), the net present value and the internal return rate (hereafter NPV and IRR, respectively), are not classified among the most important in terms of the value of the projects analysed on their basis (ranked ninth and eighth, respectively). Moreover, they are not among the most used, since only 38.16% of the firms reported using NPV and 32.9% reported IRR. Our data indicates that NPV is used more than IRR, although not significantly, which contradicts the evidence provided by Bacon (1994) and previous surveys of capital budgeting practices.

Therefore, there does not seem to be an appreciable difference between accounting theory and practice regarding the use of these criteria. NPV appears as the most appropriate practice, given the limitations IRR presents for the comparison across projects with a different dimension and time horizon (Levy & Sarnat, 1994).

The Profitability Index appeared to be the least used of the methods, since only 6.58% of the firms reported using it, although they use it to analyse a high number of projects (51%). In most cases, it is used combined with the NPV or some other financial criteria.

The fact that financial evaluation techniques are not frequently applied by Spanish firms, lead us to think that there is little need to provide an exclusively financial justification for IS investment projects. Specifically, the assessment of cash inflows is not an easy task, even in the presence of an effective system for the allocation of IS resources among users. Moreover, the implementation of such a system would require a great investment of time and resources, which may make it non-viable.

In spite of the fact that non-DCF methods do not consider the impact of time in the value of money, the risk associated with investments and the cash flows observed after the investment is recovered, the pay-back method is used in 42.1% of the cases. Some firms reported the use of a variant of this method that consists of discounting the net cash flows originated by the project.

Four reasons have been suggested in the literature that could explain the continuous use of the pay-back method, even though it shows a strong short-term bias: 1. It is easy to understand and use (Durba´n, 1984).

2. The complexity of the economic, political and technological environments make it difficult to elaborate predictions and lead managers to focus on the short term. This view is specially appropriate when firms operate in markets where there is a trend towards increasing equipment capacity and reducing prices (Bacon, 1992).

3. In the business environment, changes take place so rapidly that the response time interval tends to diminish constantly. In the case of IS investments, technology is outdated in two or three years (Bracchi, 1994).

4. Market value of shares may be affected by the calculation of short-term profitability obtained with this valuation method (Brigham & Gapenski, 1991).

Budgetary restrictions are generally considered as an important financial criterion in the selection of IS investment projects. Tables 5 and 6 show that they are taken into account by 64.5% of the firms in 63.9% of their IS projects. The reason why they are not considered in all IS project analyses is that it is practically impossible to accurately predict all the expenses associated with IS investments, in order to include them in the budget.

Among the fifteen criteria analysed, budgetary restrictions were ranked fourth in terms of the number of firms taking them into consideration and the value of the projects analysed on their basis (Table 7). This seems to indicate that projects contributing to the profitability of the firm are accepted, regardless of their inclusion in the budget, unless there is a strategic reason that makes them appear as non-viable.

## Management-related criteria

A project’s support for explicit objectives (SEO hereafter), i.e., those objectives expressed in a plan, is the second most used criterion in the selection of IS investments (88.2% of the firms reported to use it for the evaluation of an average of 61.6% of the projects) and the most important criterion in terms of the total value of the projects evaluated on this basis.

A project’s support for implicit objectives of the firm (SIO hereafter), i.e., those underlying the decisions made by the management, seems to be an adequate criterion for evaluating IS investment projects, being the seventh most used criterion (53.9% of the firms reported to use it for an average of 51.8% of the IS investment analyses), and the third most important criterion in terms of the total value of the projects analysed.

One of the reasons for the use of the SEO criterion to the detriment of the SIO may be that, when the former is not used to decide on IS investments, that is, when the expectations are not clearly specified, it is more difficult to control the degree to which the achievements derived from them are in fact attained and, thus, the objectives are reached.

In a changing environment, in which uncertainty prevails, it may not be possible to anticipate and plan each IS investment. There will always be a certain percentage of non-planned projects that should be undertaken. Notwithstanding, in an environment characterised by growing competition, it is necessary to match the objectives established for the IS with those of the firm as a whole, in order to achieve an efficient allocation of IS resources and maintain the competitive objectives of the firm (Francalanci & Maggiolini, 1993).

On the other hand, even though it is not always possible to assess the benefits derived from the IS, it is necessary to relate them to the general objectives of the firm. If the IS are directed towards the achievement of those objectives, there will be co-ordination between IS strategy and the firm’s strategy.

The need to respond to competitors’ systems is used as an investment evaluation criterion by 42.1% of the firms. Although it did not seem to be a relevant criterion in the past few years, it is now considered as the tenth most important by the firms surveyed. This shows the growing importance of IS in competitive markets (Bacon, 1994).

In some cases, an investment in IS which responds to competitors’ movements may not be analysed on the basis of any other criterion, as firms make their decisions in the belief that they “[. . .] cannot afford not to invest in it” (Camussone & Occhini, 1992, p 187). If new information technology is not used, the competitive position and future viability of the firm may be seriously affected.

It is not surprising that 92.1% of the firms consider the support provided by the IS investment to management decision making as the most important criterion. This shows the importance attached to the information provided by IS to satisfy the needs of management. However, it is surprising that firms only use it for the evaluation of an average of 50.1% of their projects, being ranked second in terms of the total value of the investments.

The probability of realising benefits is a risk criterion used by 32.9% of the firms in the evaluation of an average of 38.6% of their projects. This result is striking, since that probability could be integrated in other criteria without losing its relevance (Bacon, 1994). A possible explanation is that the risk calculation:

(i) represents a basic risk, inherent in most investment projects;

(ii) may provide a consistent view on risk to thoughtful management;

(iii) eases the learning process associated with risk and profitability.

## Development criteria

According to the literature, technical requirements of the system should be considered as a primary criterion preceding the investment (Bracchi & Motta, 1989). Our results show that this criterion is in fact used for the selection of IS investments projects by a large proportion of the companies (78.9%), but not all. This seems to indicate that in some cases technical considerations are not a fundamental concern in the evaluation of the IS projects.

The most relevant question in this respect is to what extent these considerations are taken into account by companies. Table 6 shows that an average of 39.2% projects are evaluated on the basis of technical requirements. This leads us to think that IS investment decisions are not strongly influenced by technical restrictions, but rather, by strategic objectives.

The implementation and learning of new technologies did not appear as a relevant concern in the selection of IS projects in the last few years. However, it is now receiving greater attention: 57.9% of the companies apply this criterion, although they evaluate a low number of projects on its basis (24.3%).

The probability that the project will be completed appears to be a risk factor used by one-third (30.3%) of the respondents for the justification of IS investments. This may be considered as a low percentage, especially considering the high rate of failures in this field (McComb & Smith, 1991). If this criterion were used more frequently and these questions were considered in a more formal fashion during the different stages of the development of the IS, the risk associated to the completion of the project would be significantly reduced with the increase in the information available. However, the firms reported using this criterion when analysing 60.9% of their IS projects.

In this respect, it is interesting to note that the probability that an IS project is finished depends on a series of factors, such as the dimension of the project, the technical innovations required, the uncertainty associated with its definition, the complexity of its implementation requirements, the expertise and ability of the staff, the availability and dependence of the staff, the support for management and the involvement of its users. These risk factors may be considerably increased in the uncertainty affecting the firm and its IS (Hares & Royce, 1994).

## Conclusions

This paper provides a view of the decision-making process involved in the selection of IS investment projects, paying special attention to the criteria utilised to evaluate the investment alternatives.

Our results show that management criteria are the most frequently used, followed by development criteria and, finally, those of a financial nature. Among the latter, those which do not consider the discount of cash flows are the most frequently used. Although this finding contradicts the classical view held in the investment evaluation and selection literature, it is consistent with the results of similar studies published recently (Bacon, 1994; Farbey et al, 1994; Golden & Flaherty, 1996).

The reason is usually considered to be the special nature of IS investments. Their nature makes it necessary to provide a justification for them, based not only on financial criteria, but also on qualitative ones—the more the strategic character, the greater the need for a qualitative assessment (Ciborra, 1994).

As discussed above, our results are consistent with some of the trends shown by previous research. However, there are some significant differences, such as:

1. The decreasing trend in the use of NPV and IRR. The results are consistent with the findings of Ezzamel (1992) regarding the decreasing importance attached to financial criteria, which may be due to a growing concern for the strategic character of investments. As suggested by Bacon (1994), financial criteria appear not to be important in the evaluation of very large projects.

2. Likewise, our results contradict the view that IRR is preferred to NPV due to the fact that the former makes it easier to identify a project with a rate of return than to identify it with a certain amount of money.

3. Management criteria are considered as the most important, although there are some divergences among them. The support for management decision making, which was ranked first in Bacon (1992), is now considered as the second most important criterion. Explicit support for the firm’s objectives, ranked second in Bacon (1992) is the most relevant criterion in the light of our results. Moreover, our study shows that the response to competitors’ IS and the probability of achieving benefits have a low relevance in the selection of IS investments.

4. Small divergences appeared to exist among development criteria, IS technical requirements being the only criterion showing a significant difference between previous results and ours.

Given the descriptive nature of this study, it must be borne in mind that the above conclusions may not be generalised. However, we consider it necessary to devote further research efforts in this direction since, given the number of responses received, the analysis of our results may lead us to draw conclusions that will be useful for business companies in their IS investment selection decision-making processes. Some of these conclusions are as follows:

1. The need for the use of financial criteria based on DCF for certain IS projects, but not for others, depending on whether or not the project is strategically necessary for the firm.

2. In all cases, IS evaluation processes require an effort aimed at achieving a more accurate assessment, which does not necessarily have to be of a financial nature.

3. The widespread use of the pay-back method reflects the pressure to take a short-term view of expenditure on information technology. These pressures come both from the constantly changing business environment and the climate of the market. There is added pressure from the characteristics of the technology itself which constantly changes and demands a continuing business-led response.

Given the descriptive nature of our paper, the evidence provided above may be useful for business corporations in that it allows them to establish comparisons between their IS investment decision criteria and those considered here. Thus, we have attempted to bridge the gap existing between theory and practice in this key area of IS management decision making.
