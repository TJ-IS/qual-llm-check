# Quality of IS use

T Auer

Sonera Ltd, Information Management, PO Box 104, FIN-00051 Sonera, Finland

The importance of skills to use information systems (IS) seems to be well understood, but surprisingly only moderately studied. Research on IS adoption has concentrated mainly on the implementation and phases prior to implementation. Post-implementation behaviour has played a minor role. Further, postimplementation studies have concentrated more on usage and user satisfaction rather than the abilities required to use IS in an organizational context. Here we address the emergent issue of the skills to use IS. The research objective is to assess levels of skill in using microcomputer software packages and to understand the role of skills in an organizational context. To fulfill the research objective a case study has been conducted. The results suggest that IS abilities which support work might be at an alarmingly low level although IS usage seems to be active.

## Introduction

The connection between productivity and information systems (IS) can be viewed the way Drucker (1991) does: ‘Whether tools help productivity or harm it depends on what people do with them; on the purpose to which they are being put, for instance; or on the skills of the user’. In IS research, usage is assumed to be directly related to the user-community’s computer and system related skills, but this connection has been mainly studied indirectly using variables which are assumed to have a positive or negative effect on skills (Cheney & Nelson, 1988). Despite some attempts (Nelson & Cheney, 1987; Cheney & Nelson, 1988; Harrison & Rainer, 1991; Nelson, 1991; Nilsen et al, 1993; Suh et al, 1994), we do not have very much knowledge about organizational actors’ abilities to utilise IS in a work context. The main concern in studies related to IS use has been the rapidity of adoption among potential adopters. Thus, a large amount of literature exists about independent variables having an impact on the assimilation of IS at both the individual and organizational levels (Rogers, 1983; Kwon & Zmud, 1987; Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988; Davis et al, 1989; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Igbaria, 1990; Applegate, 1991; Damanpour, 1992; Teece, 1992; Grover & Goslar, 1993). We have a good understanding of the factors which facilitate or inhibit IS assimilation and use, but there is a lack of instruments which identify the state of organizational actors’ capabilities to use IS. Due to the lack of explicit measures, management might also be unable to identify actual problems (Auer, 1995) in IS use. Therefore, organizations may be prone to misjudge organizational readiness to assimilate new technology.

In this investigation the organizational actors’ IS abilities have a central role. ‘Ability’ is defined as a skill required to accomplish an objective (Nelson & Cheney, 1987). From an IS viewpoint it includes both the artifact dimension (the skills to use IS) and work dimension (the skills to combine work and IS), but excludes a third dimension, namely the motivation dimension (realised action in utilising IS) (see Eriksson et al, 1988) Here we underline the need for understanding existing organizational abilities before directing learning efforts, since in a learning process giant strides cannot be taken without conceding considerable risk. It is achieved competence which makes it possible to acquire new abilities (see Huff et al, 1988; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). The learning process itself is long and requires a lot of support until ultimate value effects at organizational level can be realised (see for example Hamblin’s change analysis, Hamblin, 1974).

At the individual level, the changes may be realised relatively rapidly, but the transition of an organizational unit is much more complex and requires both resources and time. In contrast, information technology (IT) can be improved through heavy investments, but this then raises the question of how the more advanced IT can be put into practice. In some cases the same and even better results might be achieved by investing in the use of existing systems. The potential of current systems might only be partially used, and changing existing systems might result in an unnecessary learning process. These statements lead us to evaluate the quality of IS utilisation in a work context. In this paper we consider the level of effectiveness of a widespread type of application i.e., PC office tools, and seek to connect what we observed with other, more general factors.

This paper is organised as follows. First, the concept of quality of IS use is introduced. Second, the research design is presented followed by the empirical data analysis. Finally, both the research findings and implications are discussed.

## Quality of IS use

Although the focus of this paper is on the skills to use IS, we are not concentrating only on skills and the use of IS (see Figure 1). We have to consider the complex interplay of organizational actors, IT and organizational processes in their environment before we are able to draw a realistic picture of skills and how the skills are deployed. Therefore, the following crucial issues have to be taken into account when evaluating IS skills in a work situation.

First, IS skills cannot be interpreted in isolation, because a skill or knowledge without a purpose is useless. The idea should be that IS utilisation is understood on its context (Kling, 1987) and from a wider perspective. This gives the starting point for this study. We have to understand the organizational requirements for IS utilisation. Then we are able to compare the skills to use IS to the organizational goals.

Second, we have to understand the usage situation and factors affecting IT utilisation at both individual and organizational levels. The individual level is crucial, since it is always an individual whose characteristics affect the assimilation process and the way IT is used, but the individual is acting as an organizational member. Organizational context is the environment in which the organization actors are performing their work tasks. Organizational routines and norms direct individuals’ actions, resulting in cumulative IT utilisation to support organizational goals, but by doing that they may also form barriers to learning (Dodgson, 1993; Kim, 1993).

Third, we have to consider the evolutionary nature of organizations. Studies evaluating IS generally assume that the system under study has become stable, instead of examining how the process of developing and implementing the system from its very origin affects outcomes (Rogers, 1983; Kaplan, 1991). The ever changing nature of organizations and IS results from the interaction of organizational actors and their environment.

Fourth, the utilisation of IS is not only a question of actual skills and knowledge, because of ‘mindfulness’ (or ‘mindlessness’) i.e., that users do not always make good use of what they master and know (Salmon & Globerson, 1987). Adopters, rather than making a binary decision to adopt or reject, may choose differing levels of IT use (see the overview of Fichman, 1992) or slow and halt its assimilation (Gruenfeld & Foltman, 1967, cited in Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988). Although skills and knowledge exist, they may be in bad use.

To evaluate the quality of IS use we have adapted the ideas behind a concept of IS-related organizational maturity (Auer, 1995). The concept underlines the need for understanding the existing organizational stance before directing development efforts. The maturity concept refers to a balance between the IT being deployed, and the abilities and views required to master and use IT in an organizational context. The maturity definition is related to usage, management and IT components, and their interaction in a specific environment.<sup>1</sup> The maturity concept points out that it is not sufficient to have quality components, if the components are not in balance. It is even more important to evaluate how suitable the composition of the components is for organizational structures and goals in the business environment. Therefore, in addition to the usage component, we are going to evaluate the interaction of the components in their environment.

The users (or potential users) are the primary unit of an assimilation process and the success of the process can be realised only from the actions of the individuals actually adopting the systems. Users themselves are seldom able to make an independent decision to adopt IS. But users have to continually seek opportunities to improve their work and should suggest new tools to be implemented. They should at least make suggestions about whether an innovation should be adopted or not. This implies the users should have IS specific skills and knowledge in line with abilities to understand organizational issues. Nelson (1991) has used such a classification suggested by Zmud (1983) to assess educational and training needs. This classification separates organizational and functional abilities.<sup>2</sup> Areas or organizational abilities are related to organizational goals and objectives, while areas of functional abilities exist in a specific domain or work unit (e.g., marketing, finance, or IS).

The theories reviewed provide guidelines for evaluating the quality of IS utilisation from the users’ perspective. As a summary, it is stated here that the state of the usage component can be diagnosed by observing holistically, skills and knowledge of how to use IS in an organizational context together with the IS views and actual IS usage. For that purpose a taxonomy of five classes of issue are presented. The list is strongly based on the one suggested by Zmud (1983) and Nelson (1991), but categories have been combined (IS-product and technical skills as IS skills; organizational skills and knowledge related to organizational overview and target organizational unit as organizational skills and knowledge) and two classes to assess IS views and usage have been added. The issues to be analysed are as follows:

## Research design

The objective of this study is to evaluate the quality of IS use. A single-case study project was selected as a research approach (Yin, 1984) The case study process has been supported by quantitative and qualitative means to make triangulation possible and to improve the quality of observations and conclusions (Yin, 1984; Benbasat et al, 1987).

The data collection methods were experimental simulation (see Jenkins, 1985), questionnaire and semi-structured interviews. The assessment process included multiple phases in order to make the simulations in the experimentation more realistic (see George, 1989). First, pre-measurement interviews gave a general view of the object organization and an insight into computer-supported work. Second, preliminary tests were made to be certain that the exercises in the experiment were neither too difficult nor too simple, Third, skills in end-user computing were measured by experimental simulation. To collect background information to be used in the data analysis each participant in the experiment filled out a questionnaire. Finally, post-measurement interviews were used to connect the research findings to organizational reality.

The population of the study included the active users of spreadsheet programs. A preliminary questionnaire was used to exclude non-users. Participants for the experimental simulation were selected based on systematic sampling (Babbie, 1973) from the population of active users. Systematic sampling was based on a list of users with a sampling interval of four. The first person to be invited was selected at random.

The experimental simulations were executed in the object organization’s PC classroom. The classroom was equipped with seven microcomputers. An experiment comprised several exercises, with a different set time allocated to each exercise. The participants had to execute commands that are generally needed in different work tasks. In other words, the simulations were not connected to any specific work task. The researcher controlled the test situation carefully. All participants did the same exercise at the same time.

The topics in the semistructured interviews were related to IS experience and actual use, IS skills and knowledge, work tasks, and work processes. Furthermore, business goals and practices, and how IS should be used to support these goals, were discussed with management.

## Analysis

The research object is a Finnish conglomerate operating in the foodstuffs, animal feed and chemical fields. In 1993, the company employed an average of 1900 people and the total turnover was US\$ 614m. The data obtained is analysed in three phases. First, the population and diffusion of IT is described based on the frequency and amount of use. Second, IS skills are analysed in relation to the overall use of IT as brought out by the experimental simulation (cluster analysis). Third, quantitative data is bound with the qualitative data, and final conclusions are drawn.

## Participants and IS usage

Most of the participants were between 30 and 39 years old (Table 1). Age did not vary greatly among users of the software packages, although the participants in the graphics tests were slightly younger. Participants in the spreadsheet and graphics tests had the highest educational level (65% and 79% respectively had master’s degrees) as well as the highest organizational position.

The participants seemed to be experienced microcomputer users (Table 2): 60% had longer than three years experience at the time of testing, and only one person had used microcomputers for less than one year. Only the users of graphics programs were less experienced. The number of inexperienced users was low for two reasons: a preliminary questionnaire excluded non-users, and the object organization had invested in microcomputers for several years.

## Users’ skill profiles

In this study the software packages’ exercises were appraised with an integer scale from one to four (1 = unknown task, 2 = major problems, 3 = minor problems, 4 = successful execution). The skill levels of the users are interpreted based on the frequencies of the exercises (see Table 3). Second, cluster analysis (Ward’s method) was used in order to form homogenous user groups. The scale used does not fulfill the scale requirements of selected clustering technique. Therefore, the ordinal data has been transformed into binary form, which indicates the presence or absence of characteristics. Here, the binary data shows whether an exercise has been successfully executed or not. The groups formed are shown in Table 4. They have been named according to the skill profiles of their members. In order to be able to perform the cluster analysis, participants who did not execute all the exercises related to a particular software package were excluded. Therefore, the number of observations varies between the two tables.

The resulting categories are ‘pre-novice’, ‘novice’, ‘routine’, ‘advanced’, and ‘expert’. Users who belong to pre-novice or novice groups are almost unable to use the software package. Routine users are able to manage the software package in most common daily operations, but minor exceptions might cause unanticipated problems. Advanced and expertise users have reached a level where they can use IS effectively—if they want to. This classification implies the skills to use IS, but not the abilities to support work with IS. This classification is nevertheless usable when the results, i.e., user profiles, are interpreted in the work context.

Skills in writing memos, saving and opening files and editing texts were at a high level. These tasks are the very first with which word processor users have to deal. Although the results were relatively good, there were some difficulties with these exercises. In text save and open operations, as many as 47% of users experienced problems. Text modification, printing and the use of multiple documents together formed the second category of variables. In text modification, the frequencies underlined differences between participants. Participants also had extraordinary difficulties with printing. While users were able to print a whole document, the actual task was to use the print preview command. This indicated that users were lacking the basic skills to manage minor exceptions. When it came to manipulating several documents there were even bigger problems. The third category of variables featured only one exercise: recording a macro. Macro recording was an unfamiliar command for most of the participants. This result is not very surprising; macros are usually the last aspect of a word processor that users found useful in their daily work.

Reducing the number of clusters to four, three and two yielded the following further points. The expertise group managed fluently with all of the exercises. The advanced users managed with the most common tasks, but both the novice and pre-novice users met problems even in relatively ‘easy’ situations (e.g., modifying text).

The spreadsheet exercises were also planned to measure ‘every day’ operations. For example, macros were excluded from the tests, because in the pre-tests it became obvious that users were totally unfamiliar with them. The variables could be divided into three sets. The differences between variables were not as high as in the word processor tests, but differences within the variables were much bigger. Results in the first class—enter data, file operations, and calculations—were very good. In the next set of variables—including copy commands and cell formatting—the results were not so good, and the differences within the variables rose. For example, onethird had either major problems, or did not recognise the command at all. The third set of variables included printing and graphics commands. These latter tasks indicated a low level of skills.

Three clusters were formed. The expert group, which was clearly the largest, completed the exercises fluently. The members of the second group, called ‘routine’, were able to enter data, save and open files, and make formulae, but other commands caused problems. The group members had enough skills to update existing tables, but they could not make new ones effectively. The third cluster was named the ‘pre-novice’ group. They were able to enter data into cells, but everything else was impossible for them.

Two of the graphics program exercises, make text and bar charts, were executed well. But there were difficulties even with save commands. In the modification, participants had to change page orientation, modify a bar chart scale, cut a slice from a pie chart and change graph colours. The free drawing exercise, including the use of a symbol library, was known by less than one-third of the users, and the automation of printing was even less familiar.

The cluster analysis formed two clusters. The advanced group completed the graphics program exercises well, although the free drawing command relatively problematic. Members of the novice cluster group were able to make a simple text chart, but other tasks, like save command, were too difficult for them.

## IS utilisation in an organizational context

The interviews made it possible to re-evaluate the results of the quantitative analysis. We interviewed 81 people at their workplaces. The persons to be interviewed were mainly those who had participated in the tests, but their peers and superiors were also included.

In this section the emphasis is on the comparison of the diagnosed state of IS utilisation with organizational requirements defined by the management. The comparison revealed that usage abilities remained at only a satisfactory level (see Table 5). The people working in administrative (mainly from accounting departments) functions were skilled in microcomputer use. They were adept at using word processors and making simple spreadsheet models. On the other hand, they did not have the ability to construct more complex models to perform for example economic analyses. This became clear from the models they had developed and their usage skills. This profile demonstrates an ability to use IS in operational tasks, but more abilities would be needed to support planning processes with microcomputers. In production they needed flexible tools for planning. To support the planning process extremely adept users had applied microcomputers effectively, but these adept users were not as numerous.

Thus, the overall appraisal of IS abilities in production activities remained lower than in the administrative functions. The biggest problems seemed to exist in marketing activities. For example, in a SBU’s marketing department microcomputer software packages were only marginally used: only one of four sales managers used microcomputers. In another SBU each marketing and product manager used a word processor (at pre-novice or novice level), but only two (of five) a spreadsheet program and one a graphics program.

Although IS formed an important part of organizational routines the utilisation profile implied that the potential of the systems was only partially in use. Furthermore, IS skills may still remain low compared to the potential and work requirements, despite the systems appearing to be in active use. Investments in complex new systems seemed to be put in question as it is the existing skills which make it possible to acquire more demanding computing skills and adopt IS (see application maturity in Huff et al, 1988). In other words, knowledge barriers (see Fichman, 1992) against adoption existed since the absorptive capacity of the object organization (see Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) seemed to be insufficient for efficient IS assimilation.

The state as diagnosed caused problems in the division of labour too. First of all the willingness to utilise IS in supporting work was heterogeneous. This was also noticeable from the usage and skill profiles. Due to the organizational actors’ low computing skills, their unwillingness to use IS and their low computing skills, someone else had to do their IS-related work tasks (i.e., delegation in use). Such delegation in the use of IS had created pressure to recruit new assistants, and diverted people to secondary tasks.

People with well-developed abilities to utilise IS were in a difficult position. They were expected to help other users, whose state of learning was low, and this had diverted personnel from their primary tasks to work of secondary importance. For example, it turned out that specific marketing and sales people had been delegated to make manual reports, even copying information from the screen, and sending reports daily to their colleagues and superiors. The adoption of the idea of ‘unofficial support was curious, since peer support should facilitate learning, not assist secondary work.

Most of the users saw the situation as optimal (as well as the management), since the solution gave some additional resources, but from an organizational perspective human resources were badly used.

One problem with the organizational learning process was the fragmentation of IS use. Individuals were learning to use the systems, but due to incompatible systems (e.g., non-standard software packages) and organizational barriers (e.g., dysfunctional communication between functions) learning had been neither cumulative nor incorporated into organizational learning (see Kim, 1993). The fragmentation suggested lack of managerial coordination, but the effects were to be seen in the usage component.

It has been found that active people tend to adopt IS without managerial direction, but people with low adoption characteristics seemed to wait until management gave a directive to adopt (as Leonard-Barton and Deschamps found, 1988; see also Rogers, 1983).

This was the case in this study, too. Some users were competent to utilise IS, but the majority lacked IS skills, and were unwilling to use IS in their work. This was due mainly to middle management’s low abilities in managing the integration of IS and work. The middle managers did not properly understand how they could utilise microcomputers. Awareness of the possibilities and potential of IT was high, but understanding of what to do and how to utilise IT was relatively poor. For example, it was generally stated that microcomputers should and could be used to support planning activities, but middle managers had an unrealistic (e.g., resources needed, suitable software package) picture of how this goal could be reached. Some regarded microcomputers, and IT generally, as unusable and the others believed microcomputer software packages to be the solution to improving even operational work processes.

Furthermore, no-one minded how many manual phases the work required; there was a missing link in achieving successful practice. It was possible to recognise this from several failures in the assimilation of new IS due to poor project management in user organizations. In general, it seemed that microcomputers were artifacts with a lot of unused potential or they were used indirectly.

Altogether, evaluation of the quality of IS use identified the following problems:

Pressure existed to recruit new assistants because specialists’ and managers’ computing skills were in part low, and someone else had to deliver reports to them.

People with well-developed abilities to utilise IS were in a difficult position. They were expected to help other users whose abilities remained at a low level and this led to a distortion of priorities, distracting personnel from primary tasks to work of secondary importance.

Planning processes appeared to be uncoordinated, where specialists and middle managers had themselves to collect the necessary information from several sources. This caused the duplication of work resulting in the underutilisation of software packages.

The user organizations were not able to demand changes in their systems although clear configuration errors existed. They were not sure whether they were inept at using the system or the configurations were dysfunctional.

Due to low and diverse IS abilities the assimilation of software packages at work group or business unit levels failed time and again.

The problems were caused by inadequate skills and knowledge, and the dysfunction of operations, not by the IS in use. The object organization operated with quality microcomputers and modern local area networks, although the quality of the installations was poor in some cases. The microcomputers suffered from non-standard solutions even within work groups. This is perhaps the traditional case when investments in IS have been notable, but the IS utilisation has not been considered from organizational (i.e., business goal oriented) perspective. In conclusion it may be said that competent users existed, but microcomputer use had not been incorporated properly into organizational routines.

## Research findings and discussion

The amount and frequency of use was generally high. Microcomputer software packages had been accepted, but their potential advantages had not been realised. In functions other than accounting, systems improvements would not benefit the organization until ability increased correspondingly. Generally, a higher level of learning is required because users must understand how to use microcomputer software packages.

It is stated here that in an organizational context higher overall abilities are more important than having few extremely competent persons in a user organization. Despite their professional capability they might be ‘forced’ to help their peers and their primary tasks will suffer. Furthermore, the assimilation of emergent microcomputer-based technologies requires a critical mass of more than a few. An understanding of the extant situation becomes necessary—skill, knowledge, motivation, views—because any great advances in the assimilation process cannot be made.

The research findings suggest that neither usage, motivation nor skills are sufficient to describe IS utilisation. Putting a software package into only limited use may result in monotony and hinders learning, and skills without an organizational goal are useless. Skills at individual level can be used to diagnose existing abilities to establish development programs, but not how they are put into practice. The skills might exist in the organization, but they might be badly used (motivation or managerial direction).

The technological imperative is a term of the past; the technology will improve the productivity of organizational processes. New technology might be easier to use and offer new opportunities, but it is not enough as this study has shown. At the same time, users are required to have more demanding abilities; they have to be able to access and refine information. In this situation easeof-use is important and IS usage skills are mandatory, but knowledge about the existing information and possibilities of IS is perhaps more crucial.

It might even be questionable whether it is reasonable to invest in new technology if the user organizations abilities to utilise IS are at a low level. The same, and in some cases even better results might be achieved by investing in the use of existing systems. Full advantage may not be being taken of current systems and changing existing systems might result in a new and unnecessary learning process.

In ongoing studies—following the assessment—the object organization is being closely tracked to realise the progress of organizational maturity. The follow-up is based on a qualitative analysis, but after a few years a laboratory experiment could be applied again to attain more formal information. Furthermore, several case studies in different companies have to be carried out before the results can be generalised, but generalisation is nevertheless possible in a scientific manner. Even after generalisation the results have to be understood in their context. Comparisons between organizations have to be made qualitatively, taking into account organizations cultures and environments.
