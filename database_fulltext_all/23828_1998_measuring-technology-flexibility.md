# Measuring technology flexibility

KM Nelson<sup>1</sup> and M Ghods<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Kansas, Division of Accounting and Information Systems, Summerfield 345, Lawrence, KS 66050, USA; <sup>2</sup>The Boeing Company, PO Box 3707, Seattle, WA 98124–2207, USA

This research investigates technology flexibility, which is the technology characteristic that allows or enables adjustments and other changes to the business process. We develop dimensions and determinants of this phenomenon and demonstrate a methodology for the validation and the study of flexibility. The results of a test of software system flexibility are reported. Technology flexibility has two dimensions, structural and process flexibility, encompassing both the actual technology application and the people and processes that support and use it. The flexibility of technology that supports business processes can greatly influence the organization’s capacity for change. Existing technology can present opportunities for, or barriers to, business process flexibility through structural characteristics such as language, platform, and design. Technology can also indirectly affect flexibility through the relationship between the technolog maintenance organization and the business process owners, change request processing, and other response characteristics. These indirect effects reflect a more organizational perspective of flexibility.

## Introduction

Flexibility is a key characteristic desired in both technology and business processes (Thompson, 1967; Davenport & Short, 1990; Harrington, 1991; Davenport & Prusak, 1998). The quest for flexibility is driven by shorter product cycle times, global competition, constant demands to reduce and control costs, and the need to answer to shareholder demands (Markus & Benjamin, 1997; Brynjolfsson et al, 1997). In an attempt to respond to the need for flexibility, organizations typically either deploy new ‘flexible’ technologies or attempt to change existing technology (Ahituv & Newmann, 1990; Allen & Boyton, 1991; Hammer & Champy, 1993; Markus & Benjamin, 1997). No matter which option is chosen, these technologies will be required to continually adapt during their operating life. This paper examines the flexibility of technology after it is installed, considering both the structural flexibility of the technology and the flexibility of the people and processes that support and use it. Flexibility can be positively or negatively impacted by factors such as coordination with or resistance from users (Gobbin, 1998). Therefore, our conceptualisation of process flexibility includes both the support and the user organizations.

We do not assume that all technology needs to be flexible. Structural and organizational flexibility are investments that need to be cost justified in relationship to the business process. Changing traffic volumes in organizations will require more or less flexibility over time. Previous research has shown the flexibility should have “little penalty in time, effort, cost, or performance”

(Upton, 1995, p 73). Organizations must choose to make investments in technology flexibility, just as they choose to invest in speed or durability. This research seeks to help managers holistically identify and understand technology flexibility when it is required. It is our hope that this understanding contributes to a higher return rate on technology investment when flexibility is the goal.

One of the first places that organizations have tried to gain flexibility through the use of technology is in the manufacturing area (Gerwin, 1987, 1993; Upton, 1994). In a recent study of 61 North American paper mills, managers rated 40% of flexibility improvement efforts to be unsuccessful or disappointing (Upton, 1995). The primary cause of these disappointments was the reliance on technology alone to provide flexibility. It was the interaction and alignment of technology and people that produced flexibility in successful projects. The combination of technology and people was the key to achieving flexibility.

Technology flexibility is usually evaluated from a purely engineering perspective (Schwan & Jones 1986; Gibson & Senn, 1989), taking only the technology into consideration. Technology has often made organizations more rigid rather than more flexible to being too time consuming to redesign or reflecting obsolete world views (Hedberg & Jonsson, 1982). Existing technology can present opportunities for or barriers to business process flexibility through structural characteristics such as language and design (Schwan & Jones, 1986).

Technology can also indirectly affect flexibility through the relationship between the technology support organization and the users, change request processing, and other response characteristics. These indirect effects reflect a more organizational perspective of flexibility (Huber & McDaniel, 1986; Evans, 1991). This paper combines the engineering and organizational perspectives to ask the question, ‘What makes a technology flexible?’ We address this question by developing and testing a measurement model of technology flexibility.

## The business process change environment as a driver of technology flexibility

We define a business process as a set of interdependent tasks that produce a specified output for a particular customer or market (Thompson, 1967; Harrington, 1991; Davenport, 1992). Business processes often have long periods of relative stability followed by shorter periods of revolutionary change (Tushman & Romanelli, 1985; Gersick, 1991; Romanelli & Tushman, 1994). Turbulent changes in the environment can result in the need for rapid, radical changes in ways of doing business (Tushman & Romanelli, 1985; Miles & Snow, 1986; Meyer et al, 1993). These changes can take a complaisant management team by surprise. The characteristic of adaptability allows organizations to engage the unfamiliar. This characteristic can be defined as “the capacity to expand niches or to find new niches” (Allen & Boynton 1991, p 940).

While adaptability characterises revolutionary changes in the business process environment, adaptation is the optimisation of a particular niche or business process (Huber, 1984). Business process improvement (BPI) and total quality management (TQM) are examples of initiatives that emphasise adaptation. Both techniques stress incremental change and improvement (Allen & Boynton 1991). The change environments of adaptability and adaptation require a definition of flexibility that facilitates technologies to function well during stable periods of incremental change and during periods of revolutionary change (Hammer & Champy, 1993). Flexibility can be defined as yielding the best possible performance in the face of environmental variability (De Groote, 1994). In most organizations, the business process environment contains both incremental and revolutionary variations. We incorporate these ideas in our definition of technology flexibility:

The ability to adapt to both incremental and revolutionary changes in the business or business process with minimal penalty to current time, effort, cost, or performance.

## Dimensions of technology flexibility

We propose that the time is right for a broader perspective of technology flexibility (Garud & Kotha, 1994). We suggest using a systems approach for adopting process and technical dimensions of flexibility (Wertheimer & Wertheimer, 1954; Thompson, 1967). Huber and McDaniel (1986) define organizational flexibility as “the ease with which the organization’s structures and processes can be changed” (p 583). This definition can be applied to technologies. The structure of a technology can be viewed as the design and organization of the programs and data contained in the technology application. The processes of a technology may include the management and technical processes and procedures used to support the application, as well as the expertise of the support personnel and the users of the technology. These two dimensions of flexibility can combine to impact overall flexibility. For example, a technology application can be written in a way that makes changing a data element a relatively quick and easy task. However, the change request process that initiates this change could be bureaucratic and time consuming, decreasing the overall flexibility of the entire technology. This two-dimensional conceptualisation of flexibility is appropriate for technology as it captures both the technological and process characteristics of the system. Each of these individual dimensions, as well as their interaction, can impact system flexibility.

We define the dimensions of technology flexibility as structural and process flexibility (Huber & McDaniel, 1986). Structural flexibility reflects the ability of the design of a technology to be adapted to changes in the business process and is pro-actively designed into the technology.

Structural flexibility is the capability of the design and organization of a technology to be successfully adapted to business process changes.

The dimension of process flexibility refers to the ability of a technology’s technical and management processes to accommodate change.

Process flexibility is the ability of people to make changes to the technology using management processes that support business process changes.

We propose that both of these dimensions must be measured to completely capture technology flexibility.

## Determinants of structural flexibility

The dimension of structural flexibility has three determinants that represent the structural characteristics and organization of technology: modularity, change acceptance, and consistency. These determinants have been derived from interviews with practitioners and chosen for their applicability to technologies. These determinants were then compared to measures of psychological flexibility, consistent with prior research using that uses the brain as a metaphor for modelling flexible production systems (Garud & Kotha, 1994). In behavioural psychology, the number of different arrangements that a subject can make by moving an object or objects into different forms or patterns reflects cognitive flexibility (Scott, 1966). Using this characteristic as guide, we examine the modularity present in a technology.

One way to achieve technology flexibility is through modularity (Schwan & Jones, 1986; Premelani & Blaha, 1994). This approach has been used with success by corporations such as Mrs Field’s Cookies (Haeckel & Nolan, 1993). By structuring technology with smaller modules, changes that involve adding or removing functions is simplified. The technology can potentially support a greater number of arrangements and modifications. The structuredness of a system is often a reflection of the tools or methodologies used to develop it. Modularity is defined as:

The degree of formal design separation within a technology.

Modularity can contribute to flexibility by providing manageable units of programs or hardware that can be modified as business processes change as well as the ability to easily create or destroy modules (Yourdon, 1986).

The acceptance of pressures to change is another indicator of psychological flexibility. This is measured by a person’s ability to ‘cope’ with change (Scott, 1966). Can a person accept change and to what degree (coping) is this change integrated into the person’s makeup? Human beings are equipped with various amounts of ability to cope with or accept change. Likewise, technology is developed with various amounts of restrictiveness or flexibility (Silver, 1991). This restrictiveness or flexibility may be intentionally or unintentionally a part of the technology design. An example of an intentional design that enhances change acceptance is the inclusion of numeric control tables that users can access. An example of unintentional restriction of flexibility are systems that do not accept dates beyond the year 1999. Change acceptance is defined as:

The degree to which a technology contains built-in capacity for change.

The ability of a person to integrate different psychological regions of thinking when solving puzzles or problems indicates cognitive flexibility (Kounin, 1941). Psychology assumes that different concepts will remain in independent cognitive regions until the biological– psychological system integrates them. Similarly, the ability of data and components to be integrated consistently across a technology application can greatly affect flexibility. If data in an application are integrated and the results are inconsistent representations or performance, the technology change process becomes very complex. This complexity will impact the ability to support business process changes. Data that are maintained in one location and integrated consistently throughout the system can be more readily changed. This is especially significant in the case of reverse engineering of technology (Premelani & Blaha, 1994), where an existing application is dismantled to accommodate major change in structure, language, or platform integration.

Technologies that have high consistency should be more adaptable to changes in the business process environment. Consistency, the third determinant of the technology dimension, is defined as:

The degree to which data and components are integrated consistently across a technology.

## Determinants of process flexibility

Rate of reversal (Wertheimer & Wertheimer 1954) is one way of measuring human psychological flexibility. This refers to both the physical and mental abilities needed to shift from one task or perception to another. When the rate of transition is faster, the individual is considered to be less rigid and more flexible. Similarly, the speed of transition of a technology indicates flexibility. This rate of response is impacted by the people and management processes employed in changing technology. It is possible for a software change that takes two hours to program to be caught up in a change request process for more than a week. It is also conceivable that a two-hour change could be held up due to incomplete or unclear user requirements.

Rate of response is often measured as cycle time in technology support. When this operationalisation is used, it is important to consider both the actual time it takes to make the change as well as the time involved in approving or processing the request for change. The response of the technology support group to the users is often impacted by process characteristics such as prioritisation, limited resources, or approval processes which are unrelated to the amount of time it takes to actually perform a change. This is the process rate of response that is captured in this research, which includes all of the time it takes to make changes, not just the actual labour hours required. Rate of response is defined as:

The degree to which changes can be made to a technology in a timely manner.

The expertise determinant of technology flexibility is derived from the psychological flexibility concept of facilitation of communication. Facilitation of communication is the ability of a subject to articulate knowledge about an object or circumstance, and to construct inquiries leading to additional knowledge of the area in response to variations in stimuli (Wertheimer & Wertheimer 1954). In humans, this construct is measured through a range of descriptive behaviours ranging from pre-verbal expression to complex instructions and conversations. The ability to communicate knowledge about a technology and to construct inquiries that lead to new knowledge about the system is a result of the level of expertise present. It is also a result of the ‘expertness’ of the documentation and management processes of the system. Accurate version control and complete documentation allow more rapid and accurate response to incremental changes in the business process. The system becomes less dependent on the availability of expert individuals and is less susceptible to personnel turnover. Expertise is defined as:

The degree to which up-to-date knowledge about the operation and maintenance of a technology exists and is communicated.

We define coordination as the extent that interdependent parts of an organization function according to the needs and requirements of the other parts and of the total system (Georgopoulos & Mann, 1962). The interdependent organizations that need to coordinate in the case of technology are the users and maintainers of the application (Haeckel & Nolan, 1993). When these groups coordinate successfully, flexibility can be enhanced. Barriers to flexibility can arise when changes to the technology are not agreed upon or mutually understood (Nelson & Cooprider, 1996). Standards agreed to and used by both groups can enhance coordination of action. Another characteristic of coordination is the ability of the user group to communicate changes in the business process to the information systems developers and maintainers. In this way, coordination can lead to flexible technologies that adapt to the business processes. Coordination of action is defined as:

The degree to which the technology maintenance and user organizations operate according to the requirements of each other and the total organization.

## Measurement and validity

It is important to validate the constructs of technology flexibility through measurements (Straub, 1989; Newstead & Munro, 1991). In a review of survey research, Newsted and Munro found that very few studies adequately demonstrated validity and reliability.

Measurement is the process of linking of abstract concepts to empirical determinants (Bagozzi et al, 1991).

These constructs are often not directly observable, but are believed to be latent in the phenomenon to be studied (Lucas, 1991). Ives and Olson (1984) provide a good example of this, defining system success as a latent construct. Flexibility is another example of a latent construct.

Measurement models are used to describe how well observed indicators serve as a measurement instrument for latent variables (Joreskog & Sorbom, 1984). Measurement models of data are specified by the researcher based on theory. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is used when the research design hypothesises dimensions a priori. In CFA, precise descriptions of latent variable structure and indicator loadings are specified by the researcher. CFA requires the use of analysis of covariance structure (Byrne, 1989). This technique is the basis of the LISREL approach to data analysis that we use to test a measurement model of software system flexibility.

The validity testing of the measures of latent theoretical constructs in a measurement model must be explicitly defined and specified. Bagozzi et al (1991) provide an explicit criteria for measuring the validity of a measurement scheme as an operationalisation of a specified theory. This approach is a holistic one, in that the validity of the measurement instrument and the underlying theory are tested simultaneously. The six components of construct validity as prescribed by Bagozzi (1980) are theoretical meaningfulness of concepts, observational meaningfulness of concepts, internal consistency of operationalisations, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and nomological validity. ## Methodology

The technology flexibility measurement model was tested using the example of software flexibility. The methodology used was a combination of qualitative and quantitative techniques. The discovery phase consisted of deriving candidate questions from the work of Scott (1966) and questions from the California Psychological Inventory (Megargee, 1972). The manufacturing engineering literature provided another source of candidate questions, generated from the work of Gerwin (1987, 1993) and Upton (1994, 1995). The organizational literature was also a source of potential questions. Structural questions were derived from Blau and Schoenherr’s (1971) number of major subunits questions, and coordination questions were suggested by Georgopoulos and Mann (1962). Based on these questions, a discovery interview instrument was developed that explored the theoretical and observational meaningfulness for the candidate questions and generated additional questions.

The discovery interviews were conducted using open interview techniques with probes (Rossi et al, 1983). The respondents for this phase of the research represented the same organizations as the respondents in the testing phase, but were an independent sample. Twenty-eight discovery interviews were completed with technology practitioners and users. Respondents were asked what characteristics of software systems most supported business processes. Based on the answer to this question, probing questions were asked to elicit further attributes specifically concerning structural and process flexibility. Attributes of flexibility were asked as a direct question, as well as indirectly in relationship to cost-effectiveness, reliability, growth potential, and other characteristics offered by the respondent.

A pretest sort of the candidate indicators was then conducted with three PhD students, two faculty members, and fifteen managers from six participating organizations to further strengthen observational meaningfulness.

A total of 23 pilot questionnaires were collected from users and technologists in four organizations. The pilot questionnaire had two sections: structural flexibility and process flexibility. Each question was rated on a sevenpoint Likert scale. After completing the pilot questionnaire, each respondent reviewed each question with the researcher for content, clarity, and meaning. Through these interchanges, candidate questions were further refined and selected for inclusion in the final research instrument. Based on the results of the pilot test, at least four questions for each characteristic of flexibility were retained for the testing phase of the study. The criteria for keeping questions was clarity, meaningfulness, ability to measure the construct, and understandability. This technique was employed to once again test observational and theoretical meaningfulness of constructs.

The basic design of this study was a cross-sectional field study. The sample was a heterogeneous set of representative business processes and software systems. This sample was drawn from twelve organizations chosen for industry diversity, ease of data collection, and availability of metrics information, making this a convenience sample. The determinants of software system flexibility were measured on a seven-point Likert scale survey instrument. Respondent groups represented 116 software systems across twelve organizations. For each represented software system, information was collected from expert respondents: one or two maintainers and one or two users of the system. ## Results

A measurement model of software system flexibility was tested using LISREL confirmatory factor analysis. Anderson and Gerbing (1988) recommend assessing model fit through a series of hierarchical models. These models are considered to be alternative nesting models, with each subsequent model estimating more of the theoretical constructs to one another.

Table 3 provides a summary of the goodness of fit results for the models tested. Using LISREL CFA, a high P level indicates a lower likelihood that a better fitting model exists. The BBI is an indicator of the practical significance of the model in explaining the data. The computation of the BBI is:

$$

where $\mathrm { F } _ { 0 }$ is the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value of a null model specifying mutual independence among the indicators, and $\mathrm { F _ { k } }$ is the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value for the specified model. The rule of thumb is that BBI should be greater than 0.90 (Bentler & Bonnet, 1980). The GFI and AGFI provided by the LISREL program measure how much better a model fits the data than no model at all. AGFI should be 0.90 or better.

## Discussion and conclusions

The presentation of technology flexibility with two dimensions has theoretical, methodological, and practical implications. Theoretically, this two-dimensional approach gives researchers a new way of approaching the problem of flexible technologies. The engineering and organizational approaches are combined to yield a more complete view of the phenomenon.

The primary methodological contribution of this study is the development of a measurement model for technology flexibility. This model gives researchers an empirical starting point for examination of technology flexibility. The use of discovery interviews, pretest sorts and pilot tests can result in a strong, empirically supported measurement model of technology flexibility.

When the decision has been made that existing technology must be replaced to gain flexibility, the costs of gaining process flexibility to support new structural flexibility should be considered in the overall project costs. It is often recommended that technology spending be divided in the following way: 25% for hardware, 50% for software, and 25% for implementation support (Edwards, 1995). While this is typical of the thinking in many groups today, the breakdown leaves little room for investment in process flexibility.

It is possible that many of the disappointments with new technologies may result from a failure to consider process flexibility. Upton (1995), in the case of computer integrated manufacturing, states that, “Not only is computer integration not the panacea for flexibility problems but it also comforts managers with the thought that they are doing something, when all along they should have been doing something else” (p 83). This something else, in the case of technology, is process flexibility.

Tools and techniques do exist to increase structural and process flexibility. Organizations can use software process improvement or re-engineering techniques to examine and modify the processes, communication, and coordination between technology support personnel and users. Software tools that can reverse engineering existing technology can be used to gain structural flexibility. Process flexibility can be enhanced through the use of process maturity frameworks such as ISO 9000, SPICE, and SEI/CMM (Sanders & Curran, 1994). These frameworks imbed standards into technology support processes, allowing the processes to more readily support changes in technology and the business. An example of these frameworks supporting flexibility is the SEI/CMM Level 3 Key Process Area (KPA) of intergroup coordination (Paulk et al, 1995). This KPA has been found to contribute to improved performance and increased coordination of action (Nelson et al, 1998).

From a practical standpoint, this study provides engineers and users with a new perspective of how to build and maintain more flexible technology. With an understanding of technology flexibility as a two-dimensional phenomenon, we can design to incorporate both structural and process flexibility. This will extend the flexibility work in the areas of structured programming (Yourdon, 1986) and object-oriented design (Topper, Oulette, and Jorgensen 1994), as well as the work in manufacturing (Gerwin, 1987, 1993; Upton 1993, 1995).

With the advent of enterprise systems (Rosenthal, 1994) and the inter-networked organization (Kanada et al, 1997), flexibility becomes an issue involving more than individual technologies. This study, along with others (Duncan, 1995), lays a foundation for future research into environmental and networked determinants of technology flexibility. Through careful measurement and re-validation of the constructs in this study, as well as others, we have the potential to understand and facilitate technology flexibility that supports flexibility in business processes.
