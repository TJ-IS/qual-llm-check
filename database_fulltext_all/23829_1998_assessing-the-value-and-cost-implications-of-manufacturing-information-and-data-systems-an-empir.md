# Assessing the value and cost implications of manufacturing information and data systems: an empirical study

J-N Ezingeard<sup>1</sup>, Z Irani<sup>2</sup> and P Race<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Henley Management College, Greenlands, Henley-on-Thames, Oxfordshire, RG9 3AU; <sup>2</sup>Department of Information Systems, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex, UB8 3PH, UK

Although manufacturing information and data systems (MIDS) form an integral part of a manufacturing organization’s infrastructure, the growth of corporate IT expenditure has left many companies questioning the value of their MIDS deployment. The use of traditional appraisal techniques to justify investments in MIDS typically relies on measures based on direct cost savings and incremental future cash flows. These techniques are considered no longer appropriate because of the largely intangible cost and benefit dimensions of many IT projects. This paper presents the results of a research study based on interviews in eighteen manufacturing companies, which investigated the methods used to assess the value and cost of MIDS investments. After showing that the concepts used to justify value vary significantly between respondents, the research indicates that intuition plays an important role in value prediction and assessment. A comparison between small and large companies indicates that the systems implementations are perceived in a better light in smaller companies. The results obtained are then compared with recent research.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) are often designed to support and improve medium- to long-term business, based on a variety of corporate goals. These goals are typically formulated both tangibly and intangibly, and act as clear deliverables. However, while the benefits of a system designed to ‘cut costs’ are often immediately quantifiable in financial terms, other projects aiming to ‘improve customer service’, or to ‘offer better market information might, in the short-term, be impossible to measure and attribute. Manufacturing industry is spending increasing amounts on manufacturing information and data systems (MIDS). Increasingly, these systems do not only cover functions traditionally associated with manufacturing (such as requirements planning or scheduling) but also often integrate wider business functions such as purchasing and accounting. They are therefore often used to support and improve a variety of strategic and operational functions (Randhawa & West, 1992).

The portfolio of savings and benefits achievable from such investments would suggest that the corporate justification process would simply be a capital budgeting formality. However, this is often not the case. The justification of MIDS is considered difficult because many of the benefits are not quantifiable, and are therefore omitted from the formal justification process. Furthermore, the full range of cost implications associated with a MIDS deployment often complicates the evaluation of these projects. Many cost factors (direct and/or indirect) are often not acknowledged and therefore go unaccounted for in the investment decision-making process (Irani et al, 1997). Hence, traditional approaches to investment justification would appear to be no longer appropriate (Hochstrasser, 1990).

In this paper the results of interview-based research are presented. This multiple enquiry contributes to the understanding of the practice of value assessment for MIDS project implementations in the UK. In the context of this paper, value assessment is to be understood as a broad expression, encompassing both formal and informal evaluation of MIDS. The authors compare the results obtained from the sample with the results obtained in other recently published IS literature. The paper concludes with a review of the value and cost implications that follow from the study.

## The need for value assessment in manufacturing IT/IS projects

The need for better value assessment is generally argued in the management of IT/IS literature along three aspects which can be linked to a typical project life cycle: firstly justification, secondly implementation and thirdly steady state.

The argument for better value assessment at the project justification stage is derived from evidence of costly failures. The IS function in many organizations is regarded by ‘general management’ as a substantial expense, with this function costing between 1 and 10% of organizational revenue (Strassmann, 1990). However, despite this substantial commitment, many organizations are unhappy with the performance of their IS (Lincoln, 1990). Consequently, there are as many failed IS implementations as there are successful ones (Remenyi, 1996). Rhodes (1991) indicates that 89% of the 400 companies surveyed failed to meet the criteria for the successful exploitation of IT; 32% admitted they were unsuccessful. A similar message is echoed by Bessant (1991), who claims that within the context of advanced manufacturing technology, IT rarely lives up to the promises made, and it has even led to reductions in productivity growth. Information system failures are considered the primary cause of corporate views to downsize or out-source IS facilities (Remenyi, 1996). Further evidence focussing on MIDS is offered by Burns et al (1991) and Primrose (1990), who indicate various degrees of dissatisfaction with manufacturing resource planning (MRPII) implementations. Successful implementations of IS can bring many advantages to organizations, including enhanced efficiency and effectiveness, as well as competitive advantage. However, failed systems are costly, not only in financial terms but also to the prestige and morale of the organization.

Secondly, it is argued that a better understanding of value would aid the management of IT/IS projects by providing measurable performance indicators throughout the project implementation. Roberts and Barrar (1992) identified fifteen factors for MRPII implementation success. Amongst these, monitoring and evaluation featured as key success factors. High standards of evaluation were present in successful implementations but were totally absent in unsuccessful ones. Empirical research by Willcocks (1994) shows less disappointing figures for IS projects completed within time and under budget. However, project planning estimates do tend to improve with greater IS experience, that is more experienced staff and/or organizational experience (Kearney, 1990; Sequent, 1991; Willcocks & Margetts, 1994). It should be noted nevertheless that an overstatement of time and cost may be a politically astute move by those responsible for IS delivery. In one case reported by Willcocks (1994), a computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacture system was implemented into a small manufacturing company within the £2.1 million budget, though two months outside the deadline. In this case, the project manager revealed political astuteness and gained a reputation for effectiveness. Clearly the political issues inherent in IS evaluation and measurement need to be borne in mind when metrics, control parameters and what amounts to success/effectiveness are being defined.

Thirdly, once a ‘steady state’ has been reached, measuring IS performance is the sine qua non to improving a system’s value. According to Willcocks (1994), successful users of IT are generally those who control and measure their IS effectiveness. The main problem appears to be how to assess the value of IT, as there is currently no generic framework for value assessment. According to Butler Cox (1990), “the ideal answer would be a simple, all-embracing formula that measures both the total cost of the technology, in all its forms, and the added value to the business.” However, neither success nor failure can be defined in straightforward terms. As far as IS failure is concerned, Remenyi (1996) proposes a framework based on ten common information system mistakes, whereas DeLone and McLean (1992) argue on the other hand, that IS should be treated as a ‘multidimensional construct’, and identify six factors in assessing the success of an IS infrastructure. However, all major IS value measurement techniques published in the past decade are based on a variety of different dimensions and variables.

## Research methodology

Despite the problems identified above, MIDS practitioners continue to make decisions about investing in IT or choosing to configure or operate their system in a particular way. They must rely on some form of assessment, albeit not always a formal one. The aim of the research presented here was therefore to attempt to further the understanding of how value is perceived and assessed.

In view of this aim, the methodology chosen for the investigation was a structured interview survey of managers in eighteen UK manufacturing companies. The first goal was to identify a pattern in the current practices in value assessment in MIDS. To this end, a large proportion of each interview was used to gather information on whether value assessments were taking place. If they were, the stages in the system life cycle (investment justification, project implementation or steady state) at which they occurred were identified. The second goal was to qualify to what extent ‘value’ was made up of subjective variables dependent on individuals’ intuition and their organizational settings. The interviewees were therefore also asked to identify the parameters being measured or estimated and how they could be brought together into a single figure of value. A large number of questions were open ended, which it was felt was necessary if perceptions were to be investigated. The questions are listed in Appendix 1; questions 9 to 15 were only asked where companies conducted formal assessments.

The sample of companies identified in Figure 1 include large, medium and small companies (SMEs) within UK manufacturing industry. Care was also taken to provide a representative cross-section of the various levels of manufacturing complexity, ranging from small batch hand assembly operations to repetitive manufacturing and process flow-line manufacture. This diversity of company size and process type was intended to test the supposition that problems with evaluation would be greater in small manufacturing companies. Initial points of contact within the co-operating organizations were with the IT/IS department or the person responsible for IT/IS implementations. However, in some cases it was possible to speak to people from more than one function. The survey was stopped when it was felt that enough richness had been obtained from the process, and very little insight would be gained from continuing the exercise. The sample size is similar to that of other studies investigating the perception of IS, such as Currie’s study of decision making on computer-aided design (Currie, 1988).

The interviews were organised on the basis of a chronological account of the system implementation. The analysis presented below follows a typical IS project life cycle, starting by looking at the needs that brought about the project, through to system selection and implementation, and finishing with an assessment of the systems in place. In analysing the data, some aggregation was carried out where appropriate to provide measurable figures. However, in other instances, it was felt more appropriate to quote specific data where this adds richness to the findings.

## IT/IS implementations in the survey sample

Personal computer (PC) based systems were favoured by almost all the smaller organizations (those employing less than 100 employees). Three-quarters of the larger companies surveyed (those employing over 300 employees) used mainframe-based systems.

However, with regard to software, it was found that half the projects were integrated suites of manufacturing software. The remainder comprised financial systems, purchasing systems and management information systems. In most cases (fifteen companies) new software initiatives were accompanied by new hardware platforms. Amongst the companies visited, almost half used packaged solutions and half chose to develop from scratch. From those that chose packaged solutions, almost 90% were small companies, whilst of those who chose bespoke systems, over 80% were large companies.

Hence, it can be seen that the sample chosen provides a cross-section of companies using different but functionally similar systems.

## User expectation of value: project initiation phase

The first question asked during the interview process was ‘why were the projects undertaken?’ The responses fall into seven major categories and are summarised in Table 1.

Most companies were able to quote a reason for the implementation of their new system outright. These reasons were then often complemented by a list of ‘secondary’ factors, with most respondents having expectations of general improvements being made up from a set of specific local improvements. The results presented in Table 1 would also suggest that the majority of respondents indicate a business need rather than technology as the driving force for their investments in MIDS. Although system obsolescence was quoted by eight companies, only three did not mention any other expected benefits as a reason for their capital expenditure (companies 5, 8 and 11). However, in the case of company 11, the interviewee’s response links to a need to upgrade their system and obtain cost savings:

I had an antiquated system that I couldn’t expand any further and it was costing a fortune to maintain.

In a similar fashion, the project leader in company 8 linked the implementation of MIDS to a need to achieve the benefits originally planned for the old system:

The original computer system had come to the end of its useful life, it never achieved what it was originally designed to achieve.

The finance and IT manager of company 5 said of the old system that:

It was also restricting the development of the business.

While IT projects were initiated because of a specific business need, it also emerged from some of the interviews, that not everybody was convinced that the system would help satisfy that need. The general manager of company 4 quoted the need to get better management control over sales, together with the need to improve stock control and production planning. He further said:

It was a feeling that we should get on the ‘bandwagon’, we were very unsure as to what it would be able to offer us but we thought from what we were hearing, that it would be the right thing to do.

All the large companies listed the expected business benefits before the investment went ahead, and out of those, only two specifically admitted to not attempting to quantify these benefits. The interviewee at company 16 said:

We listed them but we didn’t put pound notes against them.

However, the interviewee at company 20 did not intend quantifying the implications of their IT/IS infrastructure in financial terms and said:

We do in functional terms, definitely but not so much in value terms.

However, amongst the small and medium companies surveyed, only companies 5, 6, 8, 10 and 12 formally listed the expected benefits, and only the largest of these (company 12) quantified the expected benefits. Therefore, over half adopted a more pragmatic view, feeling that it was a ‘leap of faith’ and that a computer system was a necessity.

When the companies applied specific requirements to a set of packages, some respondents listed several criteria used for system selection. The ‘flexibility’ argument was quoted most often, indicating that in the case of most ‘in-house’ selections, the team responsible for choosing the packages were concerned that the software should be able to cope with future changes in the business, as well as being compatible with existing data. Only one respondent (company 2) seemed to indicate that, in his view, flexibility was too expensive:

Too often IT systems are put in where they are not needed. Small companies are not in such need of complex IT systems. I won’t pay £20,000 to get a system which does everything.

Once a short-list of systems had been established based on functionality, a number of criteria not listed in Table 2 were cited as having an influence. Apart from cost, the ‘name’ of the supplier was seen as important, together with the quality of their publicity literature. For instance the IT project leader in company 8 said that:

Of the twelve systems identified all could have done, all would have probably achieved the correct funtionality. I would say that the quality of information from some was much better. Most important criteria.

Surprisingly, only one respondent indicated that other companies’ experience with potential systems had been taken into account during his company’s (company 14) system selection process.

## Perception of value

Interviewees were also asked a series of questions, leading to the question:

Do you think that the company is getting good value for money from its IT systems for manufacturing?

The results are summarised in Table 3.

It can be seen from Table 3 that of the companies surveyed, large organizations appear to have poorer perceived returns on their computer systems investment. It could be argued that they are more self-critical than the smaller firms, or that they have better assessment methods. There is of course always a possibility that some respondents from small companies were also less prepared to admit that a poor investment has been made or that some respondents in the large companies sector were over pessimistic.

Once interviewees had been asked whether they thought they had obtained value for money from their investment in IT, they were asked for evidence to support their claim. Value is generally understood as a ratio between benefits and costs, and not surprisingly, all the respondents in the sample used either or both of these to justify their opinions about value. Perceived reasons for value not being obtained are listed in Figure 2.

It can be seen that one of the benefits quoted most often for gaining value falls into efficiency gains (identified by seven companies). These gains are generally ‘local’ for the small companies or more ‘global’ in the case of larger ones. Interestingly, a number of respondents listed the low cost of their system as a reason for which value was being obtained, but one interviewee (the logistics manager in company 13) also admitted not being able to say whether or not the system was of value, owing to a lack of understanding of the full cost implications. It is also interesting to see that the benefit/cost ratio was only used by three respondents to justify their answers about value. The first was the training and systems manager in company 10 who argued that although the system was expensive in terms of upgrades it gave the company increased flexibility, and tangible benefits in the costing area. The other two respondents (from company 15) argued that the system costs were too high and that it did not achieve expected benefits.

## Perception of costs

The principal areas of cost quoted by the respondents are shown in Figure 3 which ignore certain areas identified by Irani et al (1997). This suggests a lack of understanding of the cost implications of MIDS, in the companies surveyed. Only the most obvious costs are always mentioned: hardware and software.

This therefore raises the question of whether the majority of respondents accounted for the full cost implications of their MIDS. The empirical evidence would suggest selective project costing. In one extreme company, the manufacturing services manager of company 20 said that his organization was:

Frightened by the amount spent on the system. Therefore we don’t like to calculate this figure.

In a similar fashion, the manager of the IT department in company 16 admitted that:

We didn’t want to convince ourselves that the project wasn’t worthwhile, and therefore accepted not being too keen in costing everything.

## The role of intuition during formal value assessment

It emerged from the interviews that five of the eighteen companies sampled made no formal financial estimates as to the worth of their IT system. These were mostly smaller companies. The remaining thirteen did so with varying degrees of rigour. Where no value assessment practices were in place, it was clear that intuition played an important role in value assessment, at all stages of the IS life cycle. For instance, when talking about the investment stage, the finance and IT manager of company 5 said that:

The feeling was strong that we had to do something.

All the companies who had said that they conducted evaluations, did so in the phase preceding the project. This was considered as the financial justification for the project. Of the thirteen companies, only two assessed projects once they were complete (companies 13 and 16) and only company 13 checked the value of certain key financial parameters at a midway stage of the project. Finally the interviewees in these companies who conducted formal value assessment were asked whether they thought their evaluation techniques were effective. The interpretation of their replies is summarised in Table 4.

There is a contradiction between respondents in company 15 where the IT manager felt there was no problem but the logistics manager felt that too much ‘subjectivity went into the investment decision-making process. These responses would suggest that there is a problem in a majority of companies surveyed, with regard to their use of assessment techniques. An interesting contradiction is apparent here, in that although most companies were not satisfied with their evaluation techniques, they all seem able to offer views on whether value for money was being achieved. Here again, it seems that people seem to be ‘happy’ to rely on their intuition when looking at IS performance in manufacturing. Although the perceived value of IT is traditionally captured during the investment justification stage, Carlson (1992) points out that no single measure exists as a comprehensive appraisal technique, while a similar message is echoed by Farbey et al (1992). However, Primrose (1991) claims that there is nothing special about IT projects, and that traditional appraisal techniques used for other advanced manufacturing technologies can be used for IT projects.

There is much evidence from the IS evaluation literature that there is a large difference between a functional system, and a system that is achieving full value. It can be argued that functional parameters are given priority in a number of situations, simply because they are easier to quantify, and the managers sponsoring the project are able to check these quantities. It is currently difficult to monitor the progress towards a point of value because no suitable framework has been devised. In the majority of companies surveyed here, MIDS projects were deemed complete when the system was ‘turned on’ and functional aspects of the project were ‘on-line’. However, most respondents admitted that their investment in MIDS had rarely realised their planned benefits, and following the functionality testing stage of the project, the implementation team moved to other activities. The realisation of the benefits was therefore often left to occur ‘naturally’, at the discretion of the users. Postimplementation audits were rarely carried out and therefore full functionality and benefits accruals cannot be verified.

## Comparison with other studies

The results of this study indicate that the majority of large companies surveyed tend to be dissatisfied with the value of their MIDS. These large ‘dissatisfaction’ figures are in line with the literature reviewed earlier; most companies do not think that value is being obtained from their IS or are unsure about it. On the other hand, the smaller companies in this study report higher levels of satisfaction. This may be due to lack of experience amongst other factors. In a similar fashion, it is likely that the overall level of skills of the company will have an influence on value perception (Ezingeard & Grieve, 1995).

Just under three-quarters of the sample surveyed conducted some form of financial evaluation of their IS. This figure is higher than the figures obtained by Farbey et al (1992) but similar to those obtained by Currie (1995) and Willcocks (1994), as shown in Table 5.

Further differences between the sample surveyed in this study and that used in other studies, lie firstly in the fact that the data were collected through structured interviews rather than a quantitative questionnaire, such as Kumar’s (1990) study. Brooks’s (1994) large scale surveys in the manufacturing sector did not concentrate on the issue of value assessment. As a result, the findings presented in this paper are ‘richer’ but cannot be generalised to the whole of the UK manufacturing industry. Other studies on value assessment include those by Farbey et al (1992), Currie (1995) and Willcocks (1994) but these did not include a high proportion of SMEs in their sample, and only Currie (1995) focused on the manufacturing industry.

The results of this survey have shown that when an evaluation process is carried out, the focus is largely on the functional aspects of the software systems, rather than any evaluation against business objectives. This conclusion is surprising, as the driving force behind many of the MIDS investments identified in the survey appeared to be business driven. There also appeared to be widespread difficulty in accounting for the intangible dimension of MIDS. Paradoxically, intangible benefits are quoted most often during the justification process. Table 6 summarises the positive responses obtained in the survey about quantifying intangible benefits. Most of the smaller companies in the sample made no attempt to justify their investments formally, and it has been established that half of the companies surveyed justified their investment as an ‘act of faith’. Even if the larger companies always conduct some kind of justification for their MIDS investments, often they too felt that the techniques they were using were less than satisfactory. This is very much in line with the findings of Currie (1995).

The end of the implementation process is seen by most companies as the point where the system is functionally complete. However, value or performance was never mentioned as an indicator that was measured. Very few companies appear to optimise the system’s achievement of its planned benefits, as a formal stage of the project. At that point, the majority of project leaders said that the predicted benefits of the MIDS implementation had not been achieved. This is very much in line with Kumar (1990), in which it was found that evaluation criteria were almost always functionally biased.

## Conclusion

Despite the difficulties in assessing the value of IS in manufacturing, there is substantial evidence in the literature that some form of value assessment is needed at all stages of IT projects but these are very rarely carried out to a satisfactory standard.

One of the views often quoted for this lack of formal value assessment processes is that traditional approaches to project justification focus on non-strategic, short-term, tangible benefits, with the larger ‘strategic’ picture often unaccounted for during the formal justification process. This is increasingly a problem in manufacturing information and data systems whose functionality spans across wide functional areas. The problem appears further complicated by the largely ignored or often underestimated costs associated with IT/IS investments.

By a closer investigation of the value assessment practices in eighteen manufacturing organizations of varying size, we have provided a better insight on how the broad concept of ‘value’ is understood and assessed in the companies surveyed. It emerges that this concept of ‘value’ seems to be dependent on intuition and implicit expectations for the information system implemented. This conclusion can be drawn from three reasons. Firstly, there seems to be a lack of understanding of the impact of the information systems, certainly from a benefits, and to a lesser extent from a costs point of view. Only the most obvious costs and benefits were always mentioned. With regard to benefits, the majority of savings expected from investments in MIDS, were included under the umbrella term of value. Secondly, the process of value assessment itself very often relies on intuition, particularly in the smaller companies. Thirdly, even though the majority of benefits that were hoped for were financial benefits of ‘value’, those companies that did monitor the performance of their IS tended to monitor functionality, with very little assessment of realised value benefits.

In addition, three factors seem to indicate significant differences in the perception of value of MIDS between small, medium and larger companies. Firstly, few smaller companies list the expected benefits or quantify them. Secondly, most small companies in the sample do

not attempt to justify their investment formally. Thirdly, smaller companies’ perceptions of benefits point towards more satisfaction with their investment than larger companies.
