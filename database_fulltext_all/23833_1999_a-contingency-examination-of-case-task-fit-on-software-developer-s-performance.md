# A contingency examination of CASE-task fit on software developer’s performance

VS Lai

Department of Decision Sciences and Managerial Economics, Faculty of Business Administration, Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, Hong Kong

This study examines the CASE-performance relationship, contingent on the task characteristics of software developers. Contingency theory is used here as a framework to explore the CASE-task interactions that may have been important in motivating the performance of IS professionals. The results indicate that CASE-task fit contributes significantly to the performance of software developers. Implications of the findings, from both a research and a managerial perspective, are discussed in the paper.

## Introduction

Optimal usage of new technologies and the effective management of their organizational diffusion are vital for the success of MIS organizations (Agarwal et al, 1991). In spite of the need to keep pace with technological progress, the capability of most organizations to absorb and apply information technology (IT) has lagged behind the opportunities available (Raho et al, 1987; Niederman et al, 1991). Therefore, it is not surprising that facilitating organizational learning of a new technology and understanding its impact have been identified as key concerns (Grover & Sabherwal, 1989; Niederman et al, 1991; Brancheau et al, 1996). This is especially apparent in the case of computer aided software engineering (CASE) tools.

CASE tools have been advocated as a possible means for easing the software development and maintenance bottleneck. However, the results of prior research (such as Finlay & Mitchell, 1994; Iivari, 1996) on CASE contributions are to some extent paradoxical, particularly concerning the realisation of productivity and quality gains. It has been estimated that two years after acquisition, seventy percent of the tools are no longer used (Chau, 1996). For those still in use, it has been estimated that only a small percentage of the intended audience is using them in an effective manner (Iivari, 1996). A recent survey of fifty Fortune 1000 companies about their experiences with CASE also indicated that only twenty-five percent of the respondents felt that they had achieved the expected benefits of the tools (Yuen & Spurgeon, 1992).

Despite significant progress in the design, functionality, and operational effectiveness of CASE tools in recent years, very little research has been published about their implementation (Orlikowski, 1993; Wynekoop, 1992). Many research studies acknowledge that technology characteristics have a distinct influence on implementation success, which is judged, to a great extent, by the performance of individuals using the technology. Many research studies also show that task characteristics may play a significant role in moderating this technology-performance relationship. Adopting a technology-task fit approach to explain how technology affects performance has been missing or has only been implicitly discussed in these studies (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). Not until recently has there been research on investigating the fit of technology-task on individual performance. This research is important as the interaction effect of technology and task may have a significant influence on the users’ attitude to technology utilisation, which in turn affects their performance. The basic argument of this research stream is that a technology is most effective when it fits the task, meaning the functionality of technology must correspond to the task requirements of users. A technology-task mismatch will slow down the performance of decision making since the users need to adapt the technology to their tasks.

This study investigates why, after an initial decision to adopt CASE technology, many organizations have substantial difficulties in effectively using it to improve the performance of software developers. It achieves these objectives by examining and comparing the CASE experiences of 167 system developers. The primary objective of this study is to apply contingency theory to examine the CASE-performance relationship, as it applies to the task characteristics of software development.

## Theoretical background

The theoretical foundation for this research stems from contingency theory, which has served as an important building block for theory construction in several areas of research. The contingency approach, developed in the late 1960s, is a way of reasoning about organizational problems and their solutions. The basic notion of this theory is to fit specific techniques or concepts of managing to the specific situation at hand in order to attain organizational objectives most effectively (Mescon et al, 1981).

Contingency theory has been applied frequently to explain many management and organizational phenomena. However, its application in management information systems (MIS) is still limited. Use of this theory to investigate the contingency of innovation-task factors on individual performance has drawn even less interest. Although several IS studies have indicated that IT characteristics and task characteristics significantly influence IT success and performance, little attention has been given to the possibility that the performance of individuals and the success of IT may be contingent upon the fit of the adopted IT and the task to be performed. Kim (1988) was one of the few MIS researchers who attempted to answer this research issue by drawing on findings from contingency research on IS structure. In this empirical investigation of hospital structures, he postulated that the performance of a subunit of IS was determined by its coordination modes, but was contingent upon the task in terms of task predictability, problem analysability, and task interdependence. Kim and Umanath (1992–1993) criticised this work and pointed out that all task variables and coordination modes should be evaluated simultaneously in a single model instead of independently as if all contingency relationships occurred in isolation. For this reason, Kim and Umanath re-examined the same research issue: the impact of structural variables on the perceived effectiveness of a subunit of IS, using task as the contextual contingency influencing this relationship. Their findings indicated that in determining the subunit’s performance, a relationship exists between the task context and the organizational structure.

Galletta and Hufnagel (1992), with the application of contingency theory, developed a model of end-user computing (EUC) policy to examine the relationships between the context of an organization, the development of EUC policy, and the content of the resulting policy statements. Organizational compliance was introduced as an important outcome measure in the model. Their results suggested that organizational compliance levels were highest in companies which had comprehensive and restrictive EUC policy statements and formal mechanisms for monitoring compliance. These results suggested that policies and the extent of their formality were effective mechanisms for monitoring compliance with EUC.

In a more recent study, Raymond et al (1994) applied contingency theory to investigate the correlation of technology-structure fit and organizational performance. They studied 108 small and medium-sized manufacturing companies to find whether IT and structural sophistication are stronger in high-performing firms than in lowperforming ones. Their findings did not provide a full support for the contingency relationship of technologystructure on performance, but they concluded that more sophistication in IT management, when matched with an appropriately formalised and differentiated organizational structure, could contribute to firm performance.

A review of the above and other MIS work in contingency theory research reveals that many studies have attempted to identify an association between certain defined circumstances and specific aspects of MIS. Once such an association is indicated, efforts are made to illustrate an appropriate matching between situational factors and IS characteristics. Variables which have been identified as contingent variables in IS research can be separated into four categories: environmental variables, task variables, technology variables, and organizational structure variables.

This research does not seek to test contingency theory, which has been criticised for lacking empirical validation and embedding symmetrical and non-monotonic assumptions in the theoretical assertions (Schoonhoven, 1981). This symmetrical property suggests a non-monotonic effect (high–high, low–low, high–low, and low– high combinations of the values of two different variables) rather than the usual assumption that an effect is constant (only low–low combination) over all values of the independent variable. In this study, contingency theory is used as a framework to explore the technologytask interactions that may have been important in motivating the performance of IS professionals.

## Research model and hypotheses

The model designed for this study is depicted in Figure 1. This model not only attempts to explain the impact of CASE tools on the IS professional’s performance, with IS task influencing this relationship, but also attempts to examine the individual impact of CASE and task characteristics on performance. This approach facilitates comparison of the impacts resulting from independent CASE and task variables, as well as describing the simultaneous contingency relationships of all variables.

## CASE characteristics

In the context of classical diffusion theory, an innovation that possesses certain characteristics, such as complexity, compatibility, and relative advantage, will have greater potential for rapid and widespread adoption and diffusion than one that lacks one or more of these properties (Tornatzky & Klein, 1982; Rogers, 1983). This finding has received much attention among MIS researchers, and several important studies (such as Iivari, 1996) have been conducted to validate the applicability of this theory in the context of information technologies.

According to Rogers (1983), relative advantage is the degree to which the innovation is perceived to be superior to the idea or artifact it supersedes. McClure (1989) considered CASE to be the most complete software technology yet to support the entire system development life cycle. It serves software developers with a set of well-integrated, labour-saving tools linking and automating all phases of the software life cycle. Organizations can implement CASE tools to speed up and lower the cost of systems development, create programs that are easily maintainable, achieve high-quality programs with predictable behaviour, and simplify programs and the program development process (Martin & McClure, 1988). CASE tools can also be implemented to overcome performance gaps and deficiencies in software engineering (Premkumar & Potter, 1995). From these viewpoints, the following hypothesis is proposed:

## H1: The perceived relative advantage of CASE tools has a positive impact on the performance of software developers.

Compatibility is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being consistent with the existing values, past experiences, and needs of the organization adopters (Rogers, 1983). Previous IT diffusion studies have claimed that successfully implementing an innovation should result in as little individual and organizational change as possible. The objective of CASE technology is to support the use of structured methodologies in every phase of the software development and maintenance process with a set of integrated tools. These tools are compatible with traditional software technologies, but more integrated, with the emphasis on the automation of the systems development life cycle, which makes the manual structured methodologies practical to use. Various structured methodologies, including Information Engineering, Structured Systems Applications Development Methodology, Yourdon, Jackson, and Warnior-Orr, are used in systems development. These specific methodologies are somewhat different from each other and, unfortunately, are not all supported by any available CASE product. Hence, an organization adopting a CASE technology incompatible with their existing design methodologies may involve changes to system development practices, IS culture, IS staff work practices, and perhaps organizational structural hierarchies (Orlikowski, 1989; Premkumar & Potter, 1995). Based on these claims, it is expected that there is a positive relationship between the compatibility of a CASE tool and the performance of software developers. Hence:

## H2: The perceived compatibility of CASE tools with their existing IS environment has a positive impact on the performance of software developers.

Complexity is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being difficult to use (Rogers, 1983). Leonard-Barton (1988) and Meyer and Goes (1988) found low complexity or high ease of use of an innovation to be positively related to the implementation success of a variety of IT innovations. It has been shown that the perceived complexity of voice and e-mail (Adams et al, 1992), material requirement systems (Cooper & Zmud, 1987), and personal workstations (Moore & Benbasat, 1991) are negatively related to their implementation success. Wynekoop (1992) also identified this relationship for CASE tools. Further, it has been claimed that, to reduce perceived complexity, there should be adequate and timely training not only in the use of the tool, but also in the required software development and project management philosophy (Roberts, 1992). Based on prior research, we postulate that:

H3: The perceived complexity of CASE tools has a negative impact on the performance of software developers.

## Task characteristics

The task characteristic is defined as the nature of the IS development activities performed by software developers during the system development life cycle. It has been suggested that task characteristics have a direct effect on individual performance. Guimaraes et al (1992) specified that certain task characteristics such as task structure, uncertainty, difficulty, interdependence, and variety would affect the user’s attitude and involvement in IS, which would consequently influence the success of the newly adopted innovation. In addition to these general characteristics of tasks, several researchers engaged in IS research have adopted other task characteristic variables and have still found task variables to be a significant factor in explaining various IS phenomena such as IS structure, information requirements, and IS success. Following the work of Ito and Peterson (1986) and Goodhue (1995), only complexity and interdependency of task characteristics were considered in this study.

Task complexity represents the degree of variability, difficulty, and uncertainty of IS activities that software developers need to resolve. When variability and complication in the task domain are low, the work performed tends to be more structured and predictable, and the information processing requirements tend to be minimal (Umanath & Kim, 1992). However, as task variability, uncertainty, and complication increase, the problem becomes more unstructured, and the assessment of events and the specification of appropriate rules and procedures becomes difficult. Software developers can be repeatedly frustrated by the difficulties of identifying unfamiliar programs, and determining how to design, access, and integrate these with an existing information architecture. Therefore, software developers may be forced to seek help from automated tools that can increase software development efficiency and effectiveness. However, when software developers find they are unable to generate requirement and design specifications, it becomes highly unlikely that they can use CASE to resolve these problems. Most CASE tools, except lower CASE, are equipped with automated specification analysers and generators, which allow software developers to design and organise system specifications in more efficient and effective ways. Hence, system developers are expected to have a greater appreciation of CASE, which should improve the software developer’s performance.

H4: The performance of software developers will be significantly improved when a CASE tool is adopted to handle complex IS tasks.

Task interdependency is the degree of collaboration required to complete the job. While many IS applications are essentially stand-alone systems, with little or no interdependence, most IS development efforts require sequential interdependency, where the output of one software developer becomes the input of another developer (Umanath & Kim, 1992). Goodhue (1995) posited that when tasks are interdependent, there will be a greater demand for reconciling data, programs, design, and systems due to the incompatibilities associated with these components. In the context of CASE, most of the available tools provide collaborative support capabilities sustaining structured systems analysis and design techniques (such as joint application design) and their integration (Summer, 1995). Vessey and Sravanapudi (1995) indicated that interdependent tasks within complex systems require the collaboration of many specialists, over substantial periods of time. In such an environment, a tool that supports a work group in a collaborative systems development environment, and with the capabilities of coordinating interdependent design activities and project management, would foster a positive user attitude towards CASE, and perhaps, improve the performance of software developers. Hence,

H5: The performance of software developers will significantly improve when a CASE tool is adopted to handle more interdependent IS development tasks.

## Matching CASE and task characteristics

Prior research on technology-task interaction has been limited. Although several studies have been conducted to investigate the efficacy of technology and task on individual performance, their interaction effect on the software developer’s performance has seldom been addressed. In the past, a few theorists have developed conceptual models that attempt to describe how organizations can match different technologies to their work units in order to achieve high unit performance (Nadler & Tushman, 1977; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). The basic notion of these models is that a proper interaction and fit between technology and task in an organizational unit will result in high unit performance.

Agarwal et al (1996) stated that the performance and success of any IS development effort is critically contingent on the quality of the IT employed to support systems development. They applied the theory of cognitive fit and posited that superior problem-solving performance will result when the problem-solving task and the problem-solving tool fit each other. Similarly, Sinha and Vessey (1992) tested an extended cognitive fit model that included a ‘problem-solving tool’ as a determinant of problem-solving performance. They believed that when the problem-solving tool and the task to be solved were matched, the problem-solving performance of the decision maker would be significantly more effective. In another study, Goodhue (1995) argued that for an IT to have a positive impact on individual performance, the technology must be utilised, and the technology must be a good fit with the tasks it supports. So Goodhue proposed a Technology-to-Performance Chain model which suggested that task-technology fit, when decomposed into more detailed components, could be the basis for a strong diagnostic tool to evaluate whether the IS in a given organization meet user needs. Along the same line of research, Keller (1994) found support for the contingency theory hypothesis that the fit between technology and information processing needs predicts project performance.

In the context of the software development environment, successful system implementation requires a high quality design, which, in turn, depends on how well the requirements have been modelled. A good, easy-to-use CASE tool, especially when it is compatible with the IS and task contexts, would provide tremendous assistance in resolving complex activities involving multiple stages and multiple actors. A mismatch between CASE technology and task would slow the IS developers’ performance by requiring additional attention and effort from them in order to improve the CASE technology and procedures. Worse, a technology-task mismatch may even prohibit the exploration and adoption of CASE technology within the user’s organization. Hence, IS professionals should develop a full understanding of their IS development requirements prior to finding a matching CASE tool if the resulting performance and productivity gains are to be high.

H6: Software developers with a CASE-task fit will be higher performers than those lacking such a fit.

A set of six elaborated, more specific contingency hypotheses were postulated to hypothesise which CASE variables must match with which task variables to yield higher performance. Analysing these sub-hypotheses allows us to investigate the masked effect of CASE-task fit, such as the differing directional matches among the CASE and task characteristics which are concealed when CASE and task characteristics are taken to be unidimensional constructs.

H6a: Software developers with a level of task complexity matched with a level of CASE advantage to resolve such tasks will be higher performers than those lacking such a match.

H6b: Software developers with a level of task interdependency matched with a level of CASE advantage to resolve such tasks will be higher performers than those lacking such a match.

H6c: Software developers with a level of task complexity matched with a level of CASE compatible with such tasks will be higher performers than those lacking such a match.

H6d: Software developers with a level of task interdependency matched with a level of CASE compatible with such tasks will be higher performers than those lacking such a match.

H6e: Software developers with a level of task complexity matched with a level of CASE complexity they can handle to resolve such tasks will be higher performers than those lacking such a match.

H6f: Software developers with a level of task interdependency matched with a level of CASE complexity they can handle to resolve such tasks will be higher performers than those lacking such a match.

## Research method

## Construct operationalisations

A set of thirty items pertaining to the research was identified based on an extensive review of MIS, technology management, and innovation and diffusion literature. Multi-item indicators were used for measuring these research variables based on the extent of agreement or disagreement with the item on a five-point Likert-type scale. A brief description of these variables is shown in Table 1.

Work performance is defined here as the perceived improvements associated with the accomplishment of expected requirements on the part of individual software developers. The operational measures for this dependent variable were based on the eight-item scale developed by Orlikowski (1989). This scale assesses the degree to which the software developers perceive that their work productivity and quality have improved and the degree to which the software developers perceive that their managers and customers have greater satisfaction with their work. In addition, the scale assesses the degree to which the software developers perceive that CASE tools have reduced programming costs, management, and effort.

The proposed innovation characteristics (i.e., relative advantage, compatibility, and complexity) have been assessed by previous diffusion of innovation studies for a variety of IT innovations. The operational measures used in this study were derived from an instrument developed by Goodhue (1995) and Premkumar and Potter (1995) to measure the perceptions of adopting an IT and CASE innovation within an organizational context.

Several instruments were considered as candidates for the task characteristics study. However, the instrument developed by Van De Ven and Ferry (1980) for job assessment was the most widely used and accepted in this field. This instrument was later modified by Sanders and Courtney (1985) and Guimaraes et al (1996) in exploring the factors associated with decision support systems (DSS) success. In this study, we have utilised Sanders and Courtney’s instrument to measure task complexity and interdependence. However, we have reworded their items so that the instrument is applicable to the CASE environment.

There are several possible functional forms for operationally defining the contingency relationships, including multiplicative model, matching model, multiple maxima, and interaction effects with split sample analyses (Schoonhoven, 1981; Argote, 1982). This study adopted the absolute-difference fit measure developed by Alexander and Randolph (1985) to evaluate the CASEtask fit on performance. This technique has been used with great success by David et al (1989) and Keller (1994) in their studies. Venkatraman (1989) has evaluated this technique and found it appropriate for testing relationships when fit is theoretically defined as a match between two variables that are independent of the dependent variable. Keller (1994) also argued that this technique can reduce several problems that are usually associated with contingency theory. These problems include the measurement error and interpretation problems associated with residuals from regression equations, the multicollinearity and interpretation problems that arise with the use of interaction terms, and the problems of restriction of range, reduced sample size, and interpretation of the magnitude of fit that occur with the splitsample approach.

With Alexander and Randolph’s approach, the CASEtask fit was defined as the absolute difference between the values of matching CASE and task characteristic variables (i.e. fit = uCASE variable − task variableu) using five-point scales. The approach assumes that for each value of a CASE characteristic, there is a best value of matching task characteristic that results in high performance. Using complexity-compatibility fit as an example, there is always one level of task complexity, which, when associated with a certain level of CASE compatibility, results in a high level of work performance. For instance, if task complexity has been rated scale 1, the prediction is that the best CASE compatibility level would also be scale 1. In this case, the complexity-compatibility fit = u1 − 1u = 0. In general, the closer the fit of the two matching variables, the greater the performance of software developers. A fit score of 0 would indicate a perfect match and would predict high performance, while a fit score of 4 would be a perfect mismatch and predict low performance. To minimise bias in the wording or scaling of questionnaire items, the item scores were first standardised using Z scores prior to the computation of difference scores.

## Data collection

A detailed questionnaire that measures the various research variables was developed and pilot tested through personal interviews with five senior IS executives in the software development department of four local companies. These executives provided detailed feedback on the clarity of the questions and the overall comprehensibility of the instrument. The result of this pilot study led to some adjustments to the content and format of the questionnaire and terminology used in the survey.

The revised questionnaire, along with a cover letter explaining the nature of the study, was administered to 279 attendees at a CASE software meeting. To encourage participation, all participants were assured of confidentiality throughout the study. At the end of the conference, 162 attendees completed and returned the survey. Seventeen attendees returned their surveys late by mailing them directly to the author’s office. After evaluating the responses, it was found that twelve responses were unusable owing to insufficient data or no practice of CASE technology in their organizations. The removal of these unusable responses gave us a total of 167 usable questionnaires, which represents a response rate of 59.9%.

## Construct validation

Various validity and reliability properties of the questionnaires were tested before its use. Content validity, which assesses the completeness and soundness of the measurement, was established through the careful selection of items that had been previously validated in prior studies. To further reduce the possibility of any non-random error, five IS executives were asked to review the questionnaire for validity, completeness, and readability. Their suggestions were carefully reviewed and the questionnaire was adapted accordingly so that it would better reflect industry practices and naming conventions.

Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to assess measurement reliability. The analysis showed that the reliability of variables was significantly higher than the 0.5 to 0.6 range suggested by Nunnally (1978) for the early stages of basic research. The research variables were also checked for convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was evaluated by examining if the theorised items cluster together to form a single construct; and discriminant validity was evaluated by examining the cross-loading of items on multiple factors to determine if items are not highly correlated with others (Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995). Principal component factor analysis was used to test these two validity properties. Factor analysis for the items representing work performance, CASE characteristics, and task characteristics was performed. Only those factors with eigenvalue greater than 1.0 and items with factor loading larger than 0.5 were used in the study. The results, along with Cronbach’s alpha, are presented in Table 2. The analysis indicated that all items were grouped as had been anticipated.

## Data analysis procedures

Hierarchical regression analysis was performed to assess the incremental contribution of the proposed independent variables to the prediction of the software developer’s performance. The usefulness of an antecedent variable in explaining variance in work performance is determined by the increment in the correlation coefficient (R<sup>2</sup>), after the given variable is added to the regression equation (Guimaraes et al, 1992). Hence, a full hierarchical procedure will require the performance of a series of regression analyses in a specific order, each with one or more independent variable(s) than the previous regression equation. In this study, the performance factor was first regressed on CASE characteristics. Next, the two task characteristics variables were added to assess their incremental contribution to the performance factor. Finally, the CASE-task fit variable was added to the previous regression equation to test the performance of the software developers.

## Results

## Sample characteristics

The characteristics of the sample are shown in Table 3. The largest number of respondents (44) were from the manufacturing and processing industries, representing 26.3 percent of the responding companies. Companies in the category labelled ‘other’ included those from real estate, agriculture, and energy distribution.

Of the 167 respondents, seven were top-level managers (such as VP of MIS, CIO, and MIS directors), 33 were middle-level managers (such as MIS managers, project managers, and programming managers), and 127 were software professionals (such as systems analysts, systems designers, programmers, and software engineers). Of all the respondents, 45 (26.9 percent) had a graduate degree and 109 (65.3 percent) had an undergraduate degree. Most of their degrees were in computer science, followed by engineering and business. The age of the participants ranged from 26 to 59, with an average of 36.34 years. Their average length of service in their current job was 3.5 years. On the average, they had been working for 10.3 years in software development and using CASE technology for 3.2 years.

## Hypotheses testing

The means, standard deviations, and matrix of intercorrelations among the variables are depicted in Table 4. The correlation matrix indicates that work performance is highly correlated with task and CASE variables. It also indicates that some of the independent variables are significantly intercorrelated, but none of them has a correlation exceeding 0.5. The maximum variance inflation factor of 3 indicates that multicollinearity is not a severe problem that would prelude interpretation of the regression analyses. The results of hierarchical regression analyses are presented in Table 5. The importance and usefulness of a given independent variable as a predictor of performance is indicated by a $\Delta R ^ { 2 }$ symbol, representing the increment in unique variance it explains. The values of all $\Delta R ^ { 2 }$ of our variables were cumulated for each group and for the model. The results show that all factors, except task interdependency, complexityinterdependency, and complexity-complexity fit, are significant determinants $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ of CASE success. The overall cumulative $R ^ { 2 }$ also indicates a value of 0.59, indicating the ability of our model to explain 59 percent of the variance of the software developer’s performance.

The individual $\Delta R ^ { 2 }$ value is used to validate our postulated hypotheses. With the exception of complexity, all other variables are found to be positively correlated with the implementation success. Of the twelve hypotheses postulated for this study, five (H2, H6, H6a, H6b, and H6c) were found to be significant predictors of performance at $P < 0 . 0 1$ , and four more (H1, H3, H4, and H6d) at $P < 0 . 0 5$ . Only task interdependency, complexitycomplexity fit, and complexity-interdependency fit were found to have an insignificant influence on performance.

## Discussion of results

## CASE characteristics

Systems development is considered to be an extremely complicated task. Selection of a CASE tool to supplement and support the software development activities is regarded as a key decision, the outcome of which will either foster or deter the performance of software engineers. Our study indicates that CASE selection should rest on three important CASE characteristics: relative advantage, compatibility, and complexity. This finding coincides with the results from prior studies. As indicated in Table 5, these three variables also explained 22 percent of the variation of performance $( R ^ { 2 } = 2 2 )$ .

Emergence of relative advantage as a key determinant of performance suggests that an organization will not invest its scarce resources and efforts in CASE if the technology cannot address its software development needs and meet its system performance requirements. In the past, millions of dollars were invested in IT, but unfortunately the money was often not effectively used to facilitate organizational performance. Organizations nowadays have not only become aware of the IT capabilities, but also perceive a genuine need for its capabilities to enhance their business performance. With such an awareness of and urgency for IT, organizations have to make significant efforts to adopt the best CASE and put it to effective use. This, in turn, encourages more applications of CASE in software development to improve work performance.

The data in Table 5 also indicate that compatibility $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 , R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 1 )$ is the most influential performance variable in CASE implementation. This finding is significant as it reinforces the belief that no one likes change. Adopting a CASE tool incompatible with organizational values may cause a conflict of interest and a shift of political balance; whereas implementing a product incompatible with organizational information architecture will create a confusion of work design, a readjustment of work habits, and a decline in work performance. In fact, the importance of compatibility highlights the fact that the various standards, software, and technologies used in the traditional IS development environment are quite different from those used in the CASE technology. Adoption of CASE in software development demands the establishment of suitable interfaces to legacy systems. This will probably bring in significant complexity, which many organizations are either unprepared or unwilling to undertake. In addition, overcoming the various pockets of resistance and training software developers to use CASE effectively is time consuming. It is no surprise that the compatibility of CASE with an organization’s IS environment emerges as a key determinant of the software developer’s performance.

The study also found the perceived complexity of CASE tools to be negatively correlated with performance. As stated by some respondents, ensuring the smooth application of CASE technology requires a substantial degree of technical competence. It takes time and expertise to incorporate complex CASE technology in an organization. Very often, in order to operate the technology successfully, software developers have to apply the notion of learning by doing to capture such expertise and technical know-how. Along the same line of thought, the use of complex technology is not a single event but a process of knowledge accumulation, which consequently has a negative, or perhaps delayed, impact on its contribution to work performance. CASE vendors, thus, should try harder to help organizations prepare and equip themselves for CASE implementation, and not simply push the potential benefits of CASE technology without specifying that its complexity could offset such benefits.

Although CASE characteristics can significantly influence the work performance of software developers, by themselves, they cannot enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of software development. To facilitate the successful use of the chosen CASE, we need to take into account other IS factors associated with this technology. These include the competency of the software developers, the maturity of the systems development environment, and the internal support of management.

## Task characteristics

The complexity associated with a programming task is another performance determinant of CASE implementation $( \beta \ : = \ : 0 . 1 4 , \ : P \ : < \ : 0 . 0 5 )$ , suggesting that software developers should use CASE tools to support the development of software that involves complex processes characterised by a high degree of uncertainty and difficulty. This is because increasing the complexity of a task may inflate the information requirements (both in terms of the volume of data and the richness of the information) placed upon the task executive, which will require greater discretion on the part of the software developers. Many programming activities of sophisticated system software and application packages tend to possess these characteristics. The structured emphasis of CASE technology on programming standards and procedures and management control is the prime reason why CASE is ideal for complex tasks. This finding strongly suggests to software engineers that CASE tools, if used, could simplify the development of software, thereby reducing development and maintenance backlogs.

The interdependency of tasks is found to be unrelated to performance. This implies that CASE tools could be adopted to automated software development activities of all tasks, regardless of whether or not completion of these tasks requires collaboration among the members of the unit. One possible explanation of this is that many software development applications, which are developed with CASE tools, are essentially stand-alone systems with limited interdependence in the task. An alternate explanation may be that the programmers have not exploited the capabilities of CASE in supporting team collaboration. In the latter case, the project managers should foster team collaboration in software development by encouraging the use of CASE tools. In addition, seminar or on-the-job training should be provided to acquaint developers with the advanced features and capabilities of CASE.

The majority of CASE tools currently on the market are designed, for the most part, for the single user. The critical capability to coordinate interdependent activities and project management for multiple work groups and problems are still considered inadequate. Ideally, CASE should provide three levels of collaboration support to software developers (Vessey & Sravanapudi, 1995). The most basic type of support (taskware) is the offering of automated tools to help users perform the tasks at hand. The next level of support (teamware) is allowing users to share raw materials (such as data definitions) and work products (for example data flow diagrams) among group members. The highest level of support (groupware) is typified by the need for group members to communicate with each other explicitly using messages, rather than implicitly through work products. Unfortunately, current CASE technology only provides some of the collaboration capabilities that allow IS work groups to work effectively. The tools, in general, provide the strongest support in information sharing, but the weakest in cooperation. The non-emergence of CASE, contingent on task interdependency, as a determinant of performance is probably due to this reason. Hence, our respondents suggested that the capability of the next generation CASE should be broadened to support team activities, with more generic collaborative support features provided as standard system features.

## CASE-task fit

Although CASE characteristics are important determinants of performance, the explanatory power of the CASE-task fit is even better. As indicated in Table 5, when the CASE-task fit variable is added after the separate CASE characteristic and task characteristic variables in a hierarchical regression, it adds significantly $( \Delta R ^ { 2 } =$ 0.31) to the amount of explained variance. This means that when IS managers choose CASE technologies to assist IS tasks, they must define matching CASE characteristics to the nature of the task, the degree of interdependency, and the level of difficulty. These aspects of IS tasks must be consistent with the CASE technology chosen if performance is to be high and applications to be widespread.

Of the six pairs of CASE-task characteristic fit, three pairs (advantage-complexity, advantage-interdependency, and compatibility-complexity) are significant at $P < 0 . 0 1$ . These account for a total $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.25, which is just slightly less than all CASE and task variables individually cumulated $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 8 )$ . This indicates that when CASE technology is being considered, its relative advantage as compared to other products must be a better fit to the tasks confronted by software developers. This finding has significant implications, suggesting that the performance of software developers is highly dependent upon the usefulness of the chosen CASE tool in simplifying the IS tasks and collaborating on the IS activities performed. In fact, the development of complex IS systems requires work-group members to share information and perform interdependent tasks, prior to the creation of such identifiable, communal products. In such a complex environment, the capability of a CASE tool that has the advantage of suitably coordinating a group working on a single complex problem or a number of work groups focusing on multiple complex problems is critical.

Not only should a CASE’s relative advantage match with the characteristics of the IS tasks, the CASE’s capability should also be compatible with the complexity level of the tasks performed. To deal with task complexity, CASE offers various design methodologies, software quality assurance practices, software tools, and programming languages. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the appropriateness and compatibility of these CASE capabilities to fit the IS tasks performed. Can CASE improve the solvability of the problem by offering a structured methodology to systematically disentangle its complexity? If such a methodology exists, can it be adequately applied to resolve the complexity of the IS task? Our findings suggest that for individual performance to be significant, the CASE tool must be compatible and adequate to deal with the complexity of the software development tasks.

Two CASE-task fit variables that are not found to be significant predictors of performance come from CASE complexity-task matching. This means that no matter what the complexity or interdependency level of a task is, the performance of the software developer can only be improved by one percent $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 1 )$ , if a complex CASE tool is adopted. In reality, most CASE tools are complex as their fundamental architecture is the provision of collaborative software development support applicable to a wide range of work group situations from the early analysis stage to the late maintenance stage of the entire systems development cycle. Unfortunately, many software developers have expected CASE to be a ready cure for their complex systems development tasks without realising that there will be a period of adaptation and learning associated with the adoption of this technology. Hence, as the complexity of CASE increases, the psychological state of dissonance occurs, and this, in turn, affects the performance of software developers. If this reasoning is appropriate, the design of intelligent technical means, such as tutoring, hypertext and expert system features, that increase the understandability of a CASE tool without impairing its functionality should become an integral part of the technology.

## Conclusions and implications

The CASE-task fit is of practical applicability and should encourage both management and IS professionals to consider more fully the ‘matching’ issues of technology and organization needs. This study is one of the first to demonstrate that a fit of CASE and IS task can deliver considerable benefits to the IS workgroup as their performance can be greatly enhanced with an appropriate technology that ‘fits’. More significantly, the study discovered that this ‘fit’ contributes an astonishing explanatory power $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 1 )$ to the performance of IS developers. This is a major discovery as technology characteristic variables $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 2 )$ have long been proposed by the innovation diffusion theory to be the most significant determinants in technology adoption and diffusion. In this study, we confirmed the validity and usefulness of the innovation diffusion theory; however, we also found CASE-task fit to be a more important performance determinant than the CASE or task variable alone.

Findings from the present study suggest some normative implications for the effective adoption and use of CASE. For complex and unstructured tasks, the systems developers perform best when the CASE characteristics are compatible with the task characteristics. However, if the chosen CASE is complex, the systems developers may be distracted from the project’s proper focus, or they may become frustrated or confused, resulting in a possible impairment in systems development performance and project quality. Since the learning curve associated with any new technology is fairly steep, care should be taken in selecting the right set of technology characteristics contributory to the tasks, since inadvertent selection of the wrong tasks/technologies could inhibit effective usage and create negative attitudes. Hence, matching CASE-task characteristics would help reinforce the effective use of information technology and would lead to synergistic problem solving.

The impact of task characteristics on performance is less significant than that of CASE and fit variables. Our findings indicate that CASE contributes significantly to performance when complex tasks need to be resolved. However, the unexpectedly insignificant performance contribution of CASE in supporting the systems developers with interdependent tasks deserves further investigation. It may be that the achievement of collaboration, though supported by CASE, relies on individuals. When tasks require interdependence, individuals cannot perform well if people in other work areas do not do their jobs well. This performance chain effect is more significant when a greater number of individuals or work units are needed to handle a high level of task interdependence. Any collapse in this interdependence chain will harm the performance of the project, even in the presence of CASE support. This finding shows IS management that supervision and project management techniques must be applied to the entire collaboration process to ensure the achievement of CASE benefits in systems development.

Since collaboration among IS professionals represents an important aspect of the software development process, it is clear that CASE’s collaboration features need to be broadened to facilitate the very difficult task of coordinating activities among software developers. However, no study has ever addressed how, when, and what developers communicate during the development process (Vessey & Sravanapudi, 1995). This is a very significant software engineering research topic that needs urgent attention and investigation. Without the clarification and definition of the structure and flow of tasks during the software development process, it will be difficult to design and incorporate effective collaboration technology to CASE tools.

The concept of fit in technology adoption and diffusion research needs much more work. The measurement of CASE-task fit used in this study has had no previous empirical test. Additional studies of this measure of fit in IS research should determine if it remains useful and appropriate in other settings and technologies. For example, would it be possible to extend the generalisation of CASE-task fit to other information technologies and task characteristics? If so, do we need to devise a more appropriate method of fit measurement for MIS phenomena, rather than following the measures formulated by non-MIS researchers? Overall, this study suggests that CASE-task fit can enable IS professionals and researchers to better understand the adoption and use of CASE as related to the performance of system developers. However, this finding needs further testing in other technologies, task characteristics, and settings.
