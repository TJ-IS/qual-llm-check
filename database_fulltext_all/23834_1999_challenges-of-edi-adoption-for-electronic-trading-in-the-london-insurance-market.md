# Challenges of EDI adoption for electronic trading in the London Insurance Market

MI Barrett

Department of Accounting & MIS, Faculty of Business, University of Alberta, Edmonton, T6G 2R6, Canada

This paper describes an in-depth case study, carried out over the period 1993–1997, of the attempts made to introduce an EDI service, LIMNET EPS, to support the placement of risk in the London Insurance Market. The research approach adopts a process-based methodology and develops a theoretical basis on the cultural assumptions of technology which draws on technological frames and structural culture. The main body of the paper then describes and analyses the initiation, development and adoption challenges of the LIMNET EPS service. It is concluded that there are significant organizational and social issues which need to be addressed in the adoption of EPS across this market. Specifically, our analysis suggests that the low levels of EPS adoption are related to incongruences in the cultural assumptions held by key market participants in three domains: the nature of technological change, the nature of business transaction, and the importance of market institutions. Finally, we believe our theoretical basis may be valuable in research and practice to assist in the early identification of new evolving forms of electronic commerce.

## Introduction

Over the last decade, electronic data interchange (EDI) as an interorganizational system (IOS) has been increasingly used by large aggregates such as markets to significantly change the way business is conducted between organizations in a number of industries such as the transportation industry (Johnson et al, 1992; Premkumar et al, 1997), and the automotive industry (Webster, 1995; Mukhopadyay et al, 1995; Reekers & Smithson, 1996). Recent research has examined the introduction of EDI across a number of sectors in the UK’s financial services industry: the London Stock Exhange (Clemons & Weber, 1990), the life insurance market (Knights & Murray, 1994), and banking (e.g. Fincham, 1994). The continued importance of this technology is reflected in the projected growth of development of interorganizational systems which seek to seamlessly link trading partners in a particular sector. For example, the UK EDI Association has estimated a growth rate of approximately 25% per annum in the use of EDI over the next three years (Newing, 1995).

Despite this growth, firms who have sought to adopt EDI continue to experience mixed results with a combination of success stories as well as failures (Premkumar et al, 1994, 1997), and this suggests that the introduction of EDI is not always easy or profitable. A number of authors (Swatman & Swatman, 1992; Scala & McGrath, 1993; Webster, 1995; Reekers & Smithson, 1995) have recently suggested that the major problems concerning EDI adoption include social and organizational issues, though there is a dearth of in-depth studies to support this claim. Scala and McGrath (1993) in their broad assessment of advantages and disadvantages of EDI identified social and organizational issues such as impacts on organizational culture and structure, and low levels of adoption. The need for further EDI research which focuses on such issues is emphasised by Swatman and Swatman (1992) in their conclusions of a literature survey on the topic:

Research into EDI must, therefore, place less emphasis on the ‘communications’ issues associated with this topic and concentrate on the organizational, social, managerial, and strategic issues which still remain largely incomplete.

In addition to re-focusing the issues being addressed, we suggest that there is a need for further research that takes a dynamic process perspective in understanding EDI adoption. As Premkumar et al (1997) note, most of the studies to date (e.g. Reich & Benbasat, 1990; Iacovou et al, 1995) have analysed factors, typically four to five variables, that influence the adoption of EDI. Recent research has continued using this factors-based approach but has sought to develop a more comprehensive model for EDI adoption. For example, Premkumar et al (1997) have identified three broad categories of variables and examined their impact on EDI adoption. Such studies are often useful in sensitising the researcher to issues of importance, and, in some cases, to help develop guidelines to overcome constraints that may inhibit EDI adoption. However, the factors-based approach tends to adopt cross-sectional survey methods, with little consideration of the dynamics of the process of adoption. In his review of the adoption and diffusion of different classes of technology, including EDI, Fichman (1992) points out that such cross-sectional survey methods are often insufficient, and proposes that in-depth case studies be carried out using longitudinal methods or ethnographic research methods.

Our research seeks to make a contribution to EDI research by adopting a process approach which involves the use of a longitudinal in-depth case study. To this end, our paper examines the attempted adoption of a key EDI service, LIMNET EPS, in the London Insurance Market. The EPS system was one of a number of EDI initiatives developed to improve the productivity and reduce the operating costs of this highly traditional market-place in an effort to minimise economic threats to the survival of this market.

In an unprecedented fashion, the key market associations established a joint venture, the London Insurance Market Network (LIMNET), and were committed to the development of EDI-related services on this network to facilitate electronic trading in the market. A number of these LIMNET services have enjoyed significant growth of use over the last few years despite an initially slow adoption rate. In 1993, these achievements were recognised at a national UK Electronic Data Interchange conference with LIMNET successfully winning the British Telecom Award for Innovation in Electronic Trading. However, up until the end of the research in 1997, one of the LIMNET services introduced in 1992, the electronic placing support system (LIMNET EPS) which supported the front-end risk placement process between brokers and underwriters, has continued to experience very low levels of adoption.

Some of the key questions motivating our research study were thus: why has EDI not been adopted successfully to support the risk placement process in the London Insurance Market? In what ways, if any, have organizational and social issues been significant in explaining the low levels of adoption? Our research involved a longitudinal study over a period of four years from late 1993 to late 1997, and we started our work by investigating the initiation, development and attempted adoption of LIMNET EPS across the market. At a later stage, we broadened our study to include other network services across insurance markets in London and globally, as well as to examine strategic issues in the development of placing systems between 1987 and 1997. However, in this paper, we will only report in detail on the attempted introduction of the LIMNET EPS system and examine key issues surrounding its adoption.

In the following section, we describe the research approach adopted in this study providing details of methodology, theory and method. In section three, we give an overview of the case which describes the manual risk placement process, the initiation and development of LIMNET EPS, and its attempted introduction across the market. Next, the cultural assumptions of the key market participant groups are discussed and analysed highlighting the incongruencies that exist. In our concluding section, we address the above research questions and, in so doing, provide some implications for theory and practice.

## Research approach

We sought to understand why EDI was not being adopted successfully to support the risk placement process in the London Insurance Market. In doing so, we specifically wanted to focus on the significance of organizational and social issues in explaining the low levels of adoption. As such, our approach was to acquire in-depth knowledge of the human stakeholders concerned with the initiation, development and potential adopters of the EPS system, and the changing contexts within which the attempted introduction of electronic trading was taking place. Walsham (1995) classifies studies which collect data in this way as ‘interpretive case studies’, and there is now a growing body of work in the IS literature (Markus, 1983; Zuboff, 1988; Orlikowski, 1991; Walsham, 1993) based on this approach which utilise significantly different methodologies, method, and theory. In the following sub-sections we make explicit the methodology, theory and method adopted in our study.

## Methodology

Our methodology emphasised the importance of subjective meanings as symbolic action in the processes through which humans construct and reconstruct their reality (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). This required the use of field studies of humans in their social settings so as to describe, interpret, analyse, and understand the social world from the participant’s perspective. That is, the process was viewed as a social one, and the research aimed to capture the complex, dynamic nature of the social phenomena which are both context and time dependent (Pettigrew, 1990, 1997; Hinings, 1997). The research sought to understand the process in practice from the participants’ perspectives, with a primary focus being the meaning of the technology to the individuals and groups, as well as their expectations around its use (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994), particularly with respect to their social, cultural, and work contexts.

We designed our study as longitudinal, collecting data in five phases over a period of four years. This approach is an appropriate one in seeking to address the ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions (Yin, 1989) associated with a process study. Our intensive longitudinal case study concerned the development, introduction, and use of electronic trading in the London Insurance Market with a particular focus on the electronic placing support system, LIMNET EPS. The longitudinal design of the study sought to observe and collect data on changing circumstances, perceptions and actions over time, and as they occurred. This was supplemented by detailed historical reconstruction of earlier periods prior to the start of the research study in 1993. The main period of historical reconstruction started at the birth of the London Insurance Market Network (LIMNET) in 1987 up till the start of the real-time research period in October 1993. However, this was further supplemented by a historical study of the market over its 300-year history with a particular focus on the last few decades.

## Theoretical basis

Interpretive approaches to gathering research data require an underlying guiding theory. Furthermore, as we may expect in a longitudinal study, the theoretical basis of our study evolved over time in response both to our deepening understanding gained through the collection of field data, and to our changing ideas concerning appropriate theory (Walsham & Sahay, 1996). The emerging themes of our study helped us to focus on theoretical ideas which were useful in illuminating our analysis of the phenomena. At the outset, we were influenced by structuration theory, though it was always viewed as implicitly guiding the fieldwork rather than being explicitly used in the analysis of the case data. This approach to the role and use of structuration theory sought to avoid a number of problems recently associated with the use of structuration theory for empirical work (Clark et al, 1990; Garnsey, 1992; Garnsey & Kelly, 1995).

For purposes of this paper, we will focus on the theoretical developments which were drawn on in establishing a cultural analysis in examining the process of EDI adoption, an approach which has recently been highlighted as being valuable for IS research (Walsham, 1993; Robey & Azevedo, 1994). Technological frames and ‘structural culture’ were the key developments which allowed us to establish our theoretical basis which focuses on the cultural assumptions of technology of key relevant groups. It should be noted that technological frames and structural culture are at the very least consistent with, if not directly influenced by, elements of structuration theory. They are, however, more easily operationalised, and for this reason we found them useful in the development of the theoretical basis.

Orlikowski and Gash (1994) introduce the social cognitive concept of technological frames, or interpretations of technology, of key groups in organizations—such as managers, technologists and users—as a useful analytic perspective for making sense of, and anticipating, particular actions and meanings around the introduction of new information technology. Technological frames identify that subset of members’ organizational frames that concern the assumptions, expectations, and knowledge they use to understand technology in organizations. The findings of their empirical study suggest that where incongruent technological frames exist, organizations will tend to experience difficulties and conflicts around the implementation and use of these technologies. At an early stage of the research, this concept of technological frames proved to be useful in developing our cultural analysis. It sensitised us to consider the relevant groups and to carefully examine the expectations, actions and meanings surrounding the introduction of new information technology.

However, as our field study advanced it became clear that the meaning structures suggested by the socio-cognitive concept of technological frames needed to be embedded in structured contexts such as norms and relations of power. It is for these reasons that Thompson’s (1990, 1995) concept of ‘structural culture was particularly attractive as a complement to technological frames in helping to develop our theoretical basis. Indeed, this approach is consistent with Thompson’s (1990) intent to complement the ‘symbolic conception of culture (Geertz, 1973), which focuses on the patterns of meaning embodied in the symbolic forms exchanged in social interaction. Geertz suggests that one can understand social situations only through appreciating the meanings they hold for people in a given culture (Prasad, 1997). However, Thompson argues that this conception of culture gives insufficient attention to the structured social relations within which symbols and symbolic actions are always embedded.

His ‘structural conception’ of culture seeks to rectify this deficiency by emphasising both the symbolic character of cultural phenomena and the fact that such phenomena are always embedded in structured social-historical contexts and processes. In other words, cultural analysis should seek to understand symbolic forms in structured contexts (Thompson, 1990). The contexts and processes are structured in various ways such as by: asymmetrical relations of power, differential access to resources, and institutional mechanisms for the production, transmission, and reception of symbolic forms. For example, in understanding the way speech is interpreted by different individuals, Thompson emphasises the need to move beyond an analysis of the internal structural features and to attend to ‘the social contexts, institutions and processes within which the speech is uttered, transmitted and received and by analysing the relations of power, forms of authority, kinds of resources and other characteristics of these contexts’.

Our theoretical basis as illustrated in Figure 1 draws on this structural conception of culture and the concept of technological frames. The basis suggests that each of the relevant subcultures actively involved with the introduction of EDI services hold cultural assumptions about EPS adoption, and their subsequent actions are both constrained and enabled by the deeply embedded social practices (or institutions) of the organization. These cultural assumptions consider not only patterns of meaning or meaning structures held by these groups, but how they are embedded in relations of power and norms of the organization.

For example, managers and IT personnel leading the introduction share interpretive schemes as to the intentions, meanings and expectations associated with EDI adoption. They often create procedures, guidelines and develop systems which may reproduce or change the social practices of the organization. In attempting to adopt and use the technology being implemented, users also draw on existing social practices of the organization. If the technology is adopted, users appropriate technological features and enact a set of social practices which reproduce or change existing institutions and social practices.

The theoretical basis guided the research by providing a frame of reference, which was drawn on in conducting the fieldwork (c.f. Layder, 1993) and in the subsequent analysis. As such, the role and use of the theoretical basis was as a ‘sensitising device’ (c.f. Giddens, 1991) in thinking about research issues, analysing the data, and in developing insights. We have resisted the use of abstract concepts from our basis in toto throughout our analysis and discussion, but rather to use them in a sparing and critical fashion to describe and illuminate key insights (Giddens, 1991).

## Method

The research design and site selection strategy was to choose three of the seven global brokers (constituting 85% of the market volume of trades) who were involved with the EPS introduction. The two brokerage firms with the highest levels (relative to other firms) of market-wide usage of the LIMNET EPS system were chosen along with a third who had the lowest levels. The choice of the four underwriting firms selected was also influenced by usage levels, but the key decision that was made involved using Lloyd’s underwriters as opposed to corporate insurers (e.g. Commercial Union). Staff from other key market entities were also interviewed. These included: Lloyd’s, market association directors, IT suppliers, and Limnet Ltd which was responsible for coordinating LIMNET activities including the LIMNET EPS implementation. Within each of these firms interviewees included brokers and underwriters, IT directors and staff, and senior management.

The LIMNET EPS champions who were responsible for the implementation of EPS in their respective organizations guided the initial selection of interviewees in each organization. Within each organization, the strategy was to interview both individuals known to have relatively significant EPS use and those that did not. Other important interviewees were often highlighted from market trade publications. In addition, in each interview we consciously asked each person who else in the market we should interview (Kling, 1987). Repeat interviews were conducted at each phase with LIMNET directors and implementation managers as well as the designated EPS champions.

As Table 1 shows, we carried out 103 interviews with individuals at different hierarchical levels in five separate phases of the research. To facilitate the longitudinal nature of the study, nine repeat interviews were conducted, which included three brokers and underwriters, four IT directors, and two senior managers. These individuals were identified following the analysis of phase 2 and then subsequently interviewed in phases 3 to 5. Amongst other issues, the interviews focused on stimulating discussions around the meaning of the technology to the different groups and their expectations around its use (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). Extensive notes of interviews were taken during the first two phases, and this was followed up by the use of tape recording with repeat interviewees when sufficient trust and positive rapport had been developed. The earlier stages of the research involved more open-ended interviewing whereas the later stages focused more narrowly on the emerging concepts and themes.

In addition to recollections of key participants on past as well as ongoing events, a number of primary and secondary sources were also analysed. Primary sources included strategy plans, mission statements, notes and memoranda from market-wide meetings, newsletters, and annual reports. Secondary reports included sectoral studies, trade journals, Lloyd’s publications and newsletters, local newspapers, and historical reports on the market. Finally, system demonstrations of old and new versions of the EPS software and computer based training materials were observed so as to better understand the proposed changes in methods of risk placement across the market. Furthermore, in the first three phases of the research, detailed observation based on ethnographic principles were carried out. At one level, such observation aided our understanding of the unique social and work practices of participants in the London Insurance Market which is located in the City of London. At another level, detailed observations of risk placement activity were studied between underwriters and brokers in a variety of settings: the Lloyd’s Underwriting Room, insurer’s offices, popular coffee houses and pubs. After each session, notes documenting the observation were recorded shortly thereafter. In many cases, the semistructured interviews helped illuminate, clarify and modify some of the observations, whilst in other interviews the researcher’s assessment of a particular interaction or event was confirmed (Prasad, 1997).

The preliminary analysis of the data involved categorising the data from all the interviews and observations. This process involved data preparation which identified and later combined statements with similar meanings. For each of the sub-cultures, interview transcripts were examined to identify statements or actions that reflected assumptions or expectations surrounding the use of electronic trading for risk placement (c.f. Orlikowski & Gash, 1994), as well as its implications for the operation of the market. Furthermore, we were sensitised to understanding the risk placement activity in the structured context of the London Market involving operating norms and relations of power. This approach involved reading the data and sorting it into categories suggested by the data. Once this process was completed for each sub-culture, it was followed by an analysis between the subcultures to determine whether the categories generated by each sub-culture’s data reflected common themes (Eisenhardt, 1989; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994).

Upon identification of these themes, checks were made throughout the field data to examine to what extent the categories and themes provided as full a coverage of the data as was possible (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). This iterative examination yielded a set of themes which constituted the key domains of the main sub-cultures assumptions. These domains were further examined for similarities and differences at all levels across the market. Finally, the evolving themes and domains were shared with key market participants at all levels for their feedback and discussion.

## Case description

In this section, we start out with a brief description of the London Insurance Market and the traditional business process for insurance risk placement. We then describe some of the key events which took place in the initiation and development of LIMNET EPS and conclude by summarising some of its key adoption challenges experienced during the last five years.

## The London Insurance Market

The London Insurance Market is a key international centre serving primarily international commercial clientele with insurance and reinsurance services. It is an important part of the UK general insurance industry built up around Lloyd’s of London, and attracts over £4 bn. in the form of invisible export earnings annually from its primary business activity in globally traded insurance and reinsurance. The market comprises over 500 independently owned and managed brokerage and underwriting businesses. These businesses form a complex network of linked but diverse players, including underwriting groups, brokerage firms, and market leaders. While there is no central authority governing the market, each participant is affiliated to a trading association. These participants include: Lloyd’s, representing underwriters (insurers) of all types of marine, non-marine, and aviation risks; and three market associations, the Lloyd’s Insurance Brokers’ Committee (LIBC), the Institute of London Underwriters (ILU) representing company marine underwriters, and the London Insurance and Reinsurance Market Association (LIRMA) representing company non-marine underwriters.

During the early 1990s, the market experienced tremendous financial difficulties for a number of reasons including: huge insurance losses, global competition, and high operating costs partly as a result of manual paper-based systems which have been in operation over the 300 years of the market’s existence. Since the financial crisis of the early 1990s, and culminating in its 1993 Business Plan, the market has set out a radical programme to transform the operation of Lloyd’s and the London Insurance Market. Electronic trading was to be imposed upon the market with strict implementation deadlines so as to facilitate the streamlining of business processes in an effort to improve interorganizational efficiency and the quality of customer service.

## Risk placement in the London Market

The placement of insurance risk started in London close to the end of the 17th century at Edward Lloyd’s coffee house. Since that time, these placing practices have continued to be the norm in Lloyd’s of London and across the London Market. Figure 2 illustrates the risk placement process.

At the start of this process, a client requests coverage from a broker on a specific risk (e.g. marine cargo or shipping vessel). Once the risk details have been determined, the Lloyd’s broker or a technician creates the risk package as a paper document called a ‘slip’ which includes the value of the property, period of insurance, and the like. The nature of the complex risk may require the broker to provide supporting information (e.g. structural drawings, building materials) to assist the underwriter in the evaluation of the risk. The broker presents the risk package to the ‘lead’ underwriter who assesses the risk to determine an appropriate premium to be charged and whether or not any special conditions should be added. In making his decision to accept the risk, he may request additional supporting risk information from the broker and pose any queries he may have regarding the risk. Ultimately if the risk is accepted, the ‘lead’ underwriter will usually negotiate with the broker in setting the appropriate financial terms such as premium rates and adding any special conditions under which the risk will be insured.

As the risks placed in this ‘subscription’ market are often large complex risks, it is often necessary for the broker to ask also a number of ‘following’ underwriters in the London Market to subscribe to the risk under the same terms and conditions set by the ‘lead’ underwriter. This process is continued across the market and, if necessary, accessing other global insurance markets till 100% of the risk has been insured. The deal is eventually finalised between client, broker and underwriter. Each participating underwriter accepts a proportion of the risk, the client pays the premiums, and the brokerage firm receives its commission. Indeed, unlike the stock market, the negotiation and placement of the risk is often just the beginning of the insurance transaction and could involve renewals, endorsements or changes to the policy, and claims on the policy in the future. Each of these activities would then be manually transacted in a similar manner as previously described.

The physical market-place may now be larger than in the past but the process of brokers queuing to visit a number of lead and following underwriters who may be willing to participate in a given risk continues to this day.

## The initiation and planned operation of LIMNET EPS

Following the insurance losses experienced in the late 1980s across the market, there was an increasing awareness of the benefits to be gained by developing common network applications. The London Insurance Market Network, LIMNET, was created by market participants, ILU, LIRMA, LIBC, and Lloyd’s to enhance the efficiency and quality of service provided by the market. Shortly after the network was developed, these sponsors established LIMNET Ltd as a coordinating body charged with creating a coherent cross-market approach to EDI developments, strategic planning, and the development of network standards and applications. EDI-based applications were developed on this market-wide network infrastructure in an attempt to streamline business processes, and thereby lower costs and improve quality of service. The earlier EDI services on LIMNET included: the recording of policy and accounting details; preparation and settlement of accounts including outward reinsurance; the notification, and the recording and updating of information regarding claims.

In November 1990, a joint market initiative (JMI) for electronic placing support (EPS) was established. A key focus was to develop a set of standards for building an EPS system which would be consistent and compatible between members of the trading associations in the market. The EPS system has been described in market publications as ‘a computer system, common across the London insurance market, which enables brokers to offer risks electronically to underwriters at Lloyd’s and the company market’.

The planned operation of LIMNET EPS was as a batch EDI system using value added data services on an IBM network to co-ordinate the passing of risk packages between brokers and selected underwriters. Risk packages sent by brokers on the network would consist of five distinct parts: (1) a package header which identifies the risk, the name of the originating broker, and a security reference; (2) electronic slip text; (3) a formatted common core record (CCR) to assist in electronic closing and accounting; (4) supporting information such as statistics and reports to assist in the evaluation of a risk; and (5) a message which lists underwriters in the order the risk package should be offered. At the start of the placement process, brokers would be responsible for inputting an electronic risk package including the CCR. In addition, the electronic risk package would also include slip text details and other supporting information for the risk. The broker would then determine which market to approach and which underwriters should be allowed to access the risk package. To control this access, the broker would have the option to attach a security reference number to the risk package before transmission until he has had the opportunity to discuss the risk, either face-to-face or via the telephone with the underwriter(s) he has given access.

The broker would then transmit the risk package electronically in batch mode over LIMNET to the relevant market’s electronic placing support system, whether Lloyd’s LIRMA or ILU which would in turn divert the risk package to the relevant underwriter’s system. This batch EDI concept uses the IBM Management Network System (MNS) to co-ordinate the passing of risk packages between brokers and selected underwriters. Each EDI user has its own post box into which it puts outgoing messages along with the electronic addresses of other EDI users who may participate in the risk. Transmissions to, and retrievals from, the network would take place on a regular basis between agreed hours each day Monday to Friday. Batch transmissions of risk packages would then be retrieved from the network on a ‘drip feed’ basis, at least every 10 minutes, and loaded onto the system. At this point, they would become available to any nominated participant. In 1993, this batch process was further improved with the drive to move the entire market to ‘event driven EDI’. This means that as soon as a business event takes place, the appropriate data is shifted to the next event so that there is no batching or queuing. It can be viewed as a continuous send/receive session with no unit process delay in the trading function.

Upon receipt of the risk package in the ‘quotation phase, the underwriters could signify their desire to participate, perhaps subject to certain conditions. An electronic dialogue may ensue, which could be supplemented by selective face-to-face consultation whenever the parties considered this to be beneficial. For each complex risk, the business decision calls for detailed analysis based upon the interpretation of large volumes of textual and statistical material. Underwriters may decide to place a ‘firm’ order by writing an electronic line. Electronic line details would then be transmitted to the broker who monitors underwriters’ responses on their screen until the risk is fully placed. Throughout the placement process, the underwriter may request a visit from the broker at any time. Once the broker has completed placing the risk, an electronic contract would be formed and signed line details would be provided electronically to underwriters via the EPS system.

Following the placement of the risk, the electronic record can then be used to efficiently drive the closing process by automatically feeding the transaction details to the electronic closing and accounting application, and other back end processes such as claims.

## Adoption challenges of LIMNET EPS

Between January and March 1992, Lloyd’s and the market associations carried out user testing of the new EPS system, and conducted further training with participating underwriters and brokers. Shortly thereafter, on March 30, an EPS pilot was launched. The news headlines proclaimed that history had been made in the London Market with the electronic placement of the first risk through the LIMNET EPS system. At that time, it was frequently described as symbolising the beginning of the end of an era, and was being hailed as ‘Lloyd’s most important step to date regarding new technology and electronic data interchange initiatives’. The day after the launch The Times newspaper reported:

The demise of the paper slip, which has been used for centuries by brokers at Lloyd’s came a step closer yesterday with the launch of a limited electronic trading system in the insurance market. The first electronically broked risk was placed with a Lloyd’s syndicate by Sedgwick at 8:28 a.m. yesterday. (Pryn, 1992)

The excitement surrounding the progress made with the successful EPS trial led to significant optimism amongst market leaders. Some leaders believed EPS would be available across the market by the end of 1992, and were already revising the future role and use of EPS. A top market leader expressed the view that a ‘bigger impact’ was expected from EPS than was initially envisaged: ‘I think we are going to see an element of pure screen trading sooner rather than later’. (Lloyd’s List, 31 March 1992).

This initial wave of optimism, however, began to wane by the end of the year, at which point it became clear that there was very little support for EPS by brokers and underwriters in the market. Even amongst the pioneer firms involved in the initial implementation phase, there was a slow rate of adoption of use of the EPS system. A key market leader recalled the lack of activity following the initial launch:

After the EPS launch, there was a deathly silence . . . There were cultural hang-ups, EPS went from a situation of no exposure to maximum exposure in 6 months . . . The market had no time to digest it . . . a lot of practitioners were sceptical of its use . . . There was no mandatory date at the time, and the first version was not very good.

Just under a year after the initial launch, in February 1993, the cumulative risk total traded with the support of EPS was only 600, a minute fraction of the total risks (in the 000,000’s) placed yearly in the market. It was widely agreed by market leaders and other participants that the rate of adoption had been at ‘the pace of an arthritic tortoise’. The chairman of LIMNET at that time expressed the disappointment of EPS usage as well as the market’s determination that it would succeed:

A year ago we launched the first joint market initiative on electronic placing support. One has to admit that usage of EPS has been disappointing but I’m confident that the situation will improve once the market realises that EPS exists to support the placing process and not replace it with remote trading . . . Like it or not, electronic communications are here to stay and the development of EPS will no doubt prove to be one more milestone in the development and future success of the market.

A number of issues contributed to these low levels of EPS adoption including: technical limitations of the system, insufficient financial considerations such as subsidies to support market-wide diffusion, and business issues such as leadership and a common strategic vision by all participants in the value chain. However, it is widely agreed across the market that the single most challenging issue concerns the human and social aspects associated with the evolution of this market-place to include an electronic trading environment. In particular, the majority of brokers and underwriters were sceptical of the ‘support’ role EPS was to play in the placement process as espoused by market leaders. Instead, LIM-NET EPS was widely perceived by brokers and underwriters to be ultimately representing a form of ‘electronic negotiation’. Furthermore, it was feared to be merely the ‘thin edge of the wedge’ which, if adopted, would transform the existing market-place to one where pure remote trading would be the norm for risk placement within a short period of time. These concerns were particularly heartfelt for ‘following’ underwriters and junior brokers whose work functions involving little negotiation or discussion and could easily be automated at a distance.

During 1993, Lloyd’s deepened its commitment to LIMNET EPS by requiring that all underwriters and brokers trading in the market put an electronic record of their insurance risks through the system by January 1, 1996. A key market leader involved with the EPS introduction explained that the idea of getting Lloyd’s involved came out of a cross-market group steering committee meeting:

We concluded that we would not get anywhere without critical mass . . . but we had no authority to make it mandatory . . . we then decide to ask Loyd’s to use their authority over the brokers and underwriters.

By December 1994, a second graphical user interface (GUI) version of EPS was implemented which was not only easier to use but included additional technical features deemed necessary by users after the first attempt at implementation. Despite the progress on system development and Lloyd’s imposed deadline, only 10% of risks were recorded through EPS on January 1, 1996. The deadline dates shifted a couple of times and then disappeared. In September 1996, the four participants of the joint market initiative (JMI) issued a joint statement to the market re-affirming their commitment to EPS whilst acknowledging that certain risks were not easily supported by EPS. By the end of the research period in September 1997, the usage of EPS had declined to 6%, mainly for endorsements and renewals to existing risks on the system. There was little optimism from chief proponents as to the future of EPS and the general view was that it had been an important first step to electronic commerce. An excerpt from a joint market statement by market leaders explained it thus:

We recognise that EPS has not met market expectations and that elements of its current design may not be appropriate for certain risks. However, EPS provides an important first step for the development of electronic commerce. (LIMNOTE, September 1996)

These events highlighted the significant change in perceptions by market leaders and IT professionals. In 1992, some leaders expected a quick uptake of remote trading. This optimism was initially threatened but later bolstered in 1993 when Lloyd’s provided significant support and introduced mandated deadlines. However, following the low adoption of EPS after the 1996 deadlines, leaders became much more pessimistic about EPS adoption and started to admit that EPS had some key limitations which were adversely affecting its adoption. On the other hand, there was little change in perception over time by brokers and underwriters concerning the adoption of EPS. While the deadlines seemed to have some initial impact in encouraging use by some brokers and underwriters this was somewhat shortlived.

## Cultural assumptions of market participants

The approach suggested by the theoretical basis facilitated an analysis of the cultural assumptions held by each of the key sub-cultures in the London Market: IT professionals, senior managers and brokers and underwriters across the market. What were their intentions, meanings and expectations associated with the adoption and use of LIMNET EPS in the London Market? The neat categorisation of such broad sub-cultures in this large market is not without its problems. We are particularly conscious of some differences within the senior manager group and across brokers and underwriters.

Firstly, there were and still are some senior management personnel in the underwriting and brokerage firms who are sympathetic to the preferred use of traditional placing practices in the market, having been themselves promoted from the level of broker or underwriter. However, as the financial crisis in this market worsened, there was significant congruence amongst the senior managers’ sub-culture.

Secondly, there was some incongruity of cultural assumptions of technology within and between brokers and underwriters across this large market. There were, for example, a small minority of brokers whose cultural assumptions about the LIMNET EPS system differed from the large majority of brokers. These brokers were able to conceive of opportunities from potential improvements in efficiency and effectiveness associated with LIMNET EPS. Such individuals were either IT literate or were keen to develop IT literacy, and believed that, as early EPS adopters, they had good prospects for career advancements. Another legitimate concern we could have concerns the different cultural assumptions one may expect between brokers and underwriters who have distinctive functions in this market. While the latter is true, the brokers and underwriters shared very similar cultural assumptions, perhaps since they both enjoyed the personal relationships and the negotiations associated with risk placement in this market.

Our analysis illuminated the cultural assumptions of each group and the incongruencies of cultural assumptions across these different market participants which led to a slow technological adoption of the EPS system.

## Senior managers

The senior management sub-culture in the market consisted of: the senior management at Lloyd’s, Market Association leaders, and top level managers of underwriting and brokerage firms involved with the study. Their cultural assumptions are summarised in Table 3.

The cultural assumptions of technology between the senior management sub-culture and the IT professional sub-culture were strongly aligned. Senior management envisaged that, in the short term, the majority of the risks could be placed through the EPS system without any face-to-face negotiations. To support this belief, they often argued that a substantial amount of business was already being placed by London brokers with markets outside London through other media such as fax and telephone. In these situations, the use of face-to-face interaction was necessary only at periodic points such as yearly trips to maintain personal relationships.

The senior managers also agreed with the IT professionals that the main purpose of the EPS system in the short term was to improve significantly the efficiency and effectiveness of brokers and underwriters, as well as the London Market as a whole. In particular, the development and use of LIMNET EPS was considered to be highly symbolic in signalling to the outside world that London was at the forefront of technological developments, as well as reinforcing its status as an important insurance market globally.

Some senior managers saw the potential of using LIMNET EPS system to improve their ability to monitor the activities of brokers and underwriters in the market. In effect, the system was expected to facilitate better visibility and control of brokers and underwriters in the placement process. A senior broker manager explained: ‘it is (currently) difficult to measure the productivity of brokers. No one keeps tabs as to whether the broker is indeed placing business’. The power and basis of control in the market currently lies in the personal relationships and market intelligence of individuals. However, with the LIMNET EPS system, senior managers expect that the network will make visible information on pricing and past quotes which will enable a shift in the power and decision making from the broker or underwriter to senior management. This would allow negotiations and special deals between senior management of broking and underwriting firms in the future.

This was particularly heartfelt by ‘following underwriters’ and junior brokers whose tasks seemed most susceptible to this form of trading even in the short term. First, the use of the EPS system for risk placement would often involve a duplication of effort requiring manual placement in those markets with no access to EPS. Second, the new requirements on brokers to input the CCR was viewed simply as extra work and additional responsibility. Whilst this new step in the process would benefit the London Market, brokers did not perceive any individual benefits. Along with this difficulty of perceiving benefits from EPS adoption, brokers and underwriters believed the underlying assumptions of EPS significantly undermined some of the deeply embedded practices (or institutions) of the market. These practices which include: face-toface negotiations in a physical market-place, the development and maintenance of personal relationships, and localised trust relations are regarded as key reasons for the market’s success over a long history.

Face-to-face negotiations were deemed critical to both brokers and underwriters for quite different reasons. Brokers needed this personal contact in order to facilitate effective negotiations with underwriters in the best interests of their clients. A key broker explained:

The business is based largely on relationships and trust. This is why it is so vital to carry out business in a face-to-face manner . . . You are negotiating the business. It is important how well you put across the case . . . You use a lot of different skills in negotiating. You emphasise and de-emphasise certain aspects, handle objections . . . It is a sales situation.

Underwriters strongly favoured face-to-face negotiations in the context of scarce underwriting capacity and huge underwriting losses experienced during the early 1990s. This form of interaction would allow them, at any given moment, to assess the full range of cues such as body language and tone of voice to help determine whether the risk should be accepted, and if so, at what price. Linked to this expected reduction in face-to-face negotiations, brokers and underwriters were concerned that the future of their work in risk placement would involve limited social interaction between them.

A senior underwriter put it this way: ‘there is a great deal of inertia with respect to the use of EPS by underwriters . . . they don’t want to be typing into keyboards all day’. Furthermore, for underwriters and brokers alike, the use of the EPS system involving asynchronous communication was perceived to largely eliminate the spontaneity and ‘excitement’ of the negotiation process that face-to-face communication allows. The automation of their work was also seen to ‘drive out the mystique often associated with financial service jobs in the City of London.’ Brokers and underwriters feared that remote trading associated with the introduction of the EPS system would erode the source of Lloyd’s competitive advantage in the global market-place in which a new risk product can be quickly developed through face-to-face interaction amongst brokers and underwriters in a discrete geographical market-place.

The importance of continued business relationships in the insurance market was often raised by market participants to contrast with the nature of trading stocks on the London Stock Exchange. A senior broker explained:

In the stock market, the transaction is a simple buy/sell, and is completed once a price has been decided on . . . with insurance, the negotiation involves terms and conditions and once a price is negotiated, the transaction has just begun and could involve renewals, endorsements, and claims in the future.

Underwriters and brokers alike believed that the adoption of the EPS system would potentially undermine the underlying principle of trust in the market, the Lloyd’s tradition/rule of ‘Utmost Good Faith’. This rule essentially states that a broker must display all relevant information about the client and the risk that he has in his possession to underwriters. In the EPS environment where face-to-face interaction may no longer be mandatory, it was deemed necessary for the EPS system to be able to represent all details about the risk and the client accurately. A key issue concerned the possibility of the broker inputting all relevant information about a risk into the EPS system, particularly when the system was being used for screen-based trading. As such, the EPS system was perceived to be inappropriate for carrying out risk placement in the London Market.

Finally, brokers and underwriters were also concerned that the EPS system may be used by each other in ways which would potentially involve a change in the deeply embedded power relations which are inherent in traditional risk placement. For example, the underwriters feared that ‘brokers would flood the market by sending risks to many underwriters at the same time’. In this new approach, the high status accorded to the underwriters through the queuing and physical presentation of risk would not be easily reproduced. Furthermore, many underwriters felt they could be seriously disadvantaged in a business sense if they were unable to receive and negotiate risk placement as soon as a risk was sent out by a broker on the network. On the other hand, brokers feared that underwriters would use the technology to go ‘direct, conducting business with other brokers outside London or with clients’.

## Incongruencies in cultural assumptions

An analysis of the cultural assumptions of the different sub-cultures concerning the adoption of the EPS system in the London Market suggests that there is substantial incongruence between the intentions of the IT professionals and senior managers, and the perceptions of the users. Specifically, the following three overlapping domains were identified:

1. Nature of technological change—refers to the participants’ perception of work activities will change with the adoption and use of new technology.

2. Nature of business transactions—refers to the participants’ perception of key charactersistics of business transactions and to what extent they may be supported by new technology.

3. Importance of market institutions—refers to the participants’ perception as to how key market institutions involving deeply embedded social and work practices may be undermined by the adoption of new technology.

As discussed earlier, there were common cultural assumptions between IT professionals and senior managers, and for this reason, we have combined them in our market comparison. ## Nature of technological change

It professionals and senior management viewed the introduction of the EPS system as fundamentally changing the business practices and culture of the market. They also envisaged substantial benefits in terms of major productivity improvements, both for individual firms and for the London Market as a whole. However, users viewed EPS as providing at best incremental improvement in communications to support existing placing practices. As such, they did not support the introduction of the EPS system viewing the system suspiciously as radically changing the placement practices and the market culture within a short period of time. ## Nature of business transactions

Brokers and underwriters believed the majority of the business transactions traded in the market to involve a high level of complexity requiring significant discussion of the transaction details and a need for negotiation over the terms and conditions of settlement. On the other hand, IT professionals and senior managers believed that only a small percentage of the risk placement transactions had complex product descriptions which required discussion and significant negotiation in the short term. As such, most transactions were viewed to be simple, standardised ones. However, they believed that the current technological limitations of the technology in transmitting the detailed descriptions of the risk between brokers and underwriters was reinforcing the perceived level of complexity of transactions. Linked to task complexity, brokers and underwriters perceived that the majority of transactions involved a high level of expertise requiring a series of visits to a number of lead and following underwriters. However, IT personnel and senior managers believed that only a small number of complex business transactions required high levels of expertise, and even then this would merely involve visits with a small group of ‘lead’ underwriters. Finally, IT professionals and senior managers believed that the EPS system would ultimately lead to the demise of the paper slip and little need for face-to-face communication. As such, remote placing would be viable for the majority of transactions traded. On the other hand, for underwriters and brokers alike, the sole use of the EPS system involving asynchronous communication could not support the negotiation process that face-to-face communication allows. As a result, it was perceived to be of little use in a remote trading role except for a few standard type contracts.

## Importance of market institutions

Underwriters and brokers as potential users of the EPS system believed that the underlying assumptions of the technology undermined key institutions in the market which they viewed to be the cornerstones of past success in conducting risk placement. These institutions include: the importance of face-to-face interaction in a physical market-place, the importance of localised trust through face-to-face interaction, and the on-going development and maintenance of personal relationships. Underwriters and brokers believed that the underlying assumptions of EPS would lead to the demise of face-to-face interaction and little need for personal relationships. In contrast, IT professionals and senior managers believed that there was ‘an unfounded fear of having no face-to-face communication’, and that the anxiety expressed through the market story that ‘they (the brokers) have to see the white of their eyes, and to see if their hands are trembling when they (the underwriters) sign the slip (risk)’ was a case of ‘vested interest getting in the way of progress’. They also believed that market practices could operate on the basis of new forms of trust in practices such as remote trading only partially supplemented with existing market institutions such as face-to-face interaction. Secondly, while brokers and underwriters were aware of the asymmetric power relations which often existed between brokers and underwriters, the social nature and comfortable work routines of the job were considered more important. In contrast, senior managers and IT professionals viewed EPS as levelling the playing field between brokers and underwriters and allowing a shift in focus to market efficiency issues. For example, the widespread use of EPS would allow increased visibility and control of the placement process as the network would record and monitor the trades between market participants.

## Conclusions

Our theoretical basis was particularly useful in explaining the adoption challenges of EPS adoption across the London Insurance Market. In doing so, the basis not only captures the assumptions held by the various sub-cultures concerning the expectations and use of the EPS system but examined symbolic forms such as the risk placement activity in structured contexts. In other words, to understand the perceived consequences of electronic placement support in the London Market we must not only understand the meaning structures held by participants of the placing activity but also the relations of power and institutional mechanisms embedded within risk placement activities across the market. By examining the adoption process in this way, our research builds on and complements previous work by Schein (1992) on cultural assumptions and also that of Orlikowski and Gash’s (1994) concept of technological frames. Furthermore, we believe our use of a longitudinal in-depth case study has highlighted the value of adopting a processual approach in examining EDI adoption. This approach helped us examine the changing interpretations of key actors concerning the introduction of EPS over time. It is fair to say that there has been little movement by brokers and underwriters as potential users of EPS. However, IT professionals and market leaders, after initial optimism as to its potential for remote trading in the short term have taken a more measured view of EPS. They not only recognise that this may not be a reality in the short term but have re-evaluated its appropriateness in supporting some forms of risk placement.

Returning to the key questions discussed at the beginning of the paper and which motivated our research study, the low levels of adoption of EPS across the London Market are significantly related to organizational and social issues. Our study suggests that a key reason for these low levels of adoption is an incongruency of cultural assumptions between different sub-cultures. Three domains have been identified: the nature of technological change, the nature of business transactions, and the importance of market institutions. Clearly, the results of our study in this distinct market are not generalisable across a wide range of contexts, though the domain areas that emerged from our research may be useful to implementors of new technologies involved with the transformation of a market-place. For example, we believe that implementors of other new electronic commerce activities may benefit by carefully eliciting and examining the cultural assumptions (as defined here) of new technologies held by traders. In doing so, the implementors must not only consider the meanings and expectation of the technologies but relate and embed them in their structured contexts. This would involve understanding and modelling power relationships as well as other forms which are defined by market norms. Early determination of key areas of incongruence amongst different participants may allow implementors to better anticipate and subsequently manage the adoption process by seeking to reduce the anticipated and emergent difficulties and conflicts.

Furthermore, the study also throws into question conventional wisdom concerning the role of top management or leadership in the adoption process. The lack of effectiveness of mandated deadlines by market leaders suggests that further problematisation is needed to understand what it means to have top management support or appropriate leadership in the management of the adoption process. Finally, there is a growing integration of electronic commerce innovations, collaborative technologies, and other electronic communications that are being used to facilitate electronic trading environments in a wide range of sectors. The use of these information and communication technologies to support rapidly evolving forms of commerce, such as Internet Commerce, emphasises the need for future complementary research to understand other key domains which may be important in their future adoption.
