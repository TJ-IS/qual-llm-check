# Information systems development: a perspective on the challenge of evolutionary complexity

M Lycett and RJ Paul

Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK

This conceptual paper proposes that the methodical approach to information system development leads us to design systems that are unable to deal with the challenge of evolutionary complexity. This is examined through the use of a systemic framework that describes evolutionary complexity in terms of interaction between the concepts of distinction/connection and variation/selection. In applying these concepts to the social world we are led to conclude (a) that social regularities are emergent and not a priori given and (b) that these emergent regularities are constantly shifting and evolving. This has strong implications for the methodical approach to development, which we argue assumes social structures, mechanisms and pro cesses as ‘invariant regularities’ that only have to be revealed to be understood. This difference leads methodical development to produce static systems that have to work in a dynamic world. The paper concludes by outlining a proposition in response to the challenge of evolutionary complexity, one where design is considered as an inherently ongoing process.

## Introduction

Computer-based information systems (CBIS) may be argued to be intentional artefacts that model given aspects of business organization. Since the mid-1950s the growth, diffusion and use of computers within business has been profound, in some cases changing the nature of business itself. During the early part of this period, what were seen to be flaws in the development process resulted in the much cited software crisis; systems took too long to build, cost too much and did not work very well. The increasing complexity of systems further compounded such problems and many researchers in the field have subsequently sought a solution through the imposition of method (Baskerville et al 1992; Friedman & Cornford, 1989; Hirschheim et al, 1995; Fitzgerald, 1996). That said, the assumption that the methodical approach addresses the problems of the software crisis is by no means proven (Fenton, 1993; Fitzgerald, 1996).

In such a light Paul (1994) proposes that the most generous observation that can be made about information systems development is that most systems (if not all) disappoint. This may be defined twofold. First, in terms of project disappointment, where an information system development project does not deliver that which is required of it. Second, in terms of system disappointment, where the information system as an ‘organizational model’ does not provide the service required of it. The cost of project disappointment is discussed predominantly in terms of monetary loss and UK public sector projects alone are estimated to have cost taxpayers over £5 billion across the last twelve years (Collins,

1994). System disappointment is less quantifiable, though it is estimated that some three-quarters of all large systems either do not function as intended or are not used at all (Gibbs, 1994). Not surprisingly, the search for an understanding of failure has spawned several major academic works (see, for example Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987; DeLone & McLean, 1992; Sauer, 1993; Beynon-Davies, 1995; Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996).

Adopting a systemic perspective, this conceptual paper argues that systems disappointment results from current development ‘understanding’ producing information systems that are unable to deal with the challenge of evolutionary complexity. This is examined through the use of a framework describing evolutionary complexity in terms of the interaction between the concepts of distinction/connection and variation/selection. In applying these concepts to the social world we are led to conclude (a) that social regularities are emergent and not a priori given and (b) that these emergent regularities are constantly shifting and evolving. This has strong implications for the methodical approach to development, which we argue assumes social structures, mechanisms and processes to be ‘invariant regularities’ that only have to be revealed to be understood (Baskerville et al, 1992). The failure to understand these implications leads to the development of static systems that are required to work in a dynamic world. With this made clear, the paper concludes by outlining a proposition in response to the challenge of evolutionary complexity where design is considered as an inherently ongoing process.

## Understanding evolutionary complexity

## A systemic approach to complexity

The label ‘information system’ implicitly states a facet of the field’s conceptual grounding. Many of the concepts that pervade both the method and practice of information systems development stem directly from developments in General Systems Theory (Bertalanfly, 1968), cybernetics (Weiner, 1948; Ashby, 1956) and systems thinking in general (systems analysis and systems engineering for example). The subject matter of systems thinking is that of organised complexity (Checkland, 1981). This may be argued to be philosophically motivated, in contrast to recent scientific efforts found within the field of complex systems. The latter seek a unified theory of complexity based on the premise that simple rules underlie many extremely complicated phenomena in the world (Horgan, 1995). Here, we seek to facilitate understanding and not prediction, following an approach that rests on the principle of holism; that ‘the whole is greater than the sum of its parts’. Historically, this approach has grown through reaction to the method of mainstream science, which traditionally ‘reduces’ a problem area into separate parts. The assumption is made that the parts under examination can be optimised and are the same when examined in isolation as they are when part of the whole. As a general approach to complexity, this has been argued to be lacking when applied to problems that lie beyond the physical regularities of the universe (such as those of the social sciences).

Angell (1990, p 169) argues that systems thinking involves the ability to grasp the essence of an idea whilst accepting the implied vagueness and limitations of weak definitions such as the following:

A system is a perceived complex assembly of parts connected together in an organised way, so that it has a coherent identity and supposed objectives, and is self-protecting and purposeful.

This underlies the general philosophy herein. Namely, that a system is something that is defined by an observer and not something that is necessarily objectively given; the correspondence between description and ‘reality’ in social contexts being complicated by the transitive nature of both. Thus, the concepts that have emerged from work in the area essentially provide a language for description and understanding. Checkland (1981) proposes three forms of motivation for such description. Firstly, where the aim is to observe and describe in order to find out whether intelligible description is possible. Secondly, where the aim is to make use of the systems description in some kind of problem solving. Thirdly, where the aim is that of design; that is, consciously bringing about changes in some part of reality. The second and third of these motivations constitute the focus of this paper.

In rudimentary terms, systems descriptions will contain various properties such as boundaries, environmental interaction, components, structure and the means by which the system retains it’s integrity/identity (Checkland, 1981). The systemic model of organised complexity rests upon the principle that there exists a hierarchy of levels of organization for a given system. Each level represents a simplified interpretation of the complex behaviour of the level below and is characterised by emergent properties that do not exist at the lower level. These properties result from ‘synergetic’ interrelations of the component sub-systems. Communication between the components of a system is necessary for it to regulate and control itself and to maintain an ‘identity while interacting with its environment. Communication is closely related to control and causal mechanisms, both of which are present in all systems and have the aim of achieving a condition of equilibrium between a system and its environment (Skyttner, 1996). We assume a general familiarity with these concepts and will concentrate herein on the concepts of evolutionary complexity and structural plasticity.

The model of organised complexity may be further characterised in terms of the dimensions of distinction and connection (Heylighen, 1999). Distinction corresponds to the variety within a system, accepting that there are different parts of the system that behave differently. Connection binds these parts, acknowledging that they are not completely independent and that knowledge of one part may allow the determination of features of other parts. In the extreme, distinction leads to randomness or chaos, where all parts are completely independent of each other. Connection leads to order and stability, which may be illustrated through the concept of symmetry (where one part of a pattern is sufficient to reconstruct the whole). Complexity is thus represented through ‘symmetry breaking’, depicting the interaction between order and disorder. Heylighen (1999) concludes that complexity increase when the distinction and/or connection within a system increases in a given dimension. Where this increase is spatial, the effect is structural; where it is temporal, the effect is functional.

Accounting for the descriptive nature of systemic explanation, distinguishing distinction and connection in realistically complex systems is a far from trivial matter, which perhaps adequately sums up the problems of systems analysis and design. In relation, we propose the following:

Firstly, account must be taken of Edmonds’ (1999) constraint of complexity to the ‘language of representation’. This accepts that different concepts of complexity will emerge depending on the form of representation chosen, the type of difficulty focused on and the type of formulation desired within the representation. Common representation moves the subjective aspect of complexity to the inter-subjective (promoting shared and consensual understanding of a limited domain).

Secondly, within this limited domain, the observer generally picks out those distinctions that are perceived as most important, creating abstract classes of similar phenomena and neglecting differences that may exist between members of those classes. To a great extent, the reliability of the resultant model (as an adequate representation of its real world counterpart) will critically depend on the degree of independence of that which was included in the model and that which was not. Heylighen (1999) argues that this degree of independence represents the ‘objective’ complexity of the system.

## The dynamic aspect of change

Building on the conception of complexity, some dynamics may be added by conceptualising change as interplay between the hypothetical forces of variation and selection (Heylighen, 1999). Variation may be defined as that aspect of a process that creates configurations different from previous ones. This may be internal, where the components of a system or their interrelations change, or external, where a system is brought into contact or combined with other systems. Selection may be defined as the elimination or reduction of part of the variety of configurations produced through variation. The existence of selection stems from the argument that not all variants are equivalently stable or capable of production/ reproduction. Again, this may be internal, where the components of a system or their interrelations fail, or external, where a system fails because it is not adapted to its environment.

An innovative link between variation and selection may be found in the work of Maturana and Varela (Maturana & Varela, 1980; Maturana, 1981; Varela, 1989). This is based on a sharp distinction between the systemic concepts of organization and structure that differ from their common language meaning. Organization represents the abstract relations between components and the necessary properties of components that define the unity as belonging to a particular class. For example, an information system may be defined by describing the necessary relations between the components of people, technology and formal rules. In contrast, structure describes the actual components and relations of a concrete example of a given system. As an example, assume an information system consists of the relations between the members of a marketing department, four networked desktop computers and the embedded procedures of the ‘Acme Marketing Group’. Members of the department may leave and be replaced, further computers may be added and the network technology upgraded and alike, but the system is still seen as that of the ‘Acme Marketing Group’. This presents structure in an unusual light, referring to both the static and dynamic elements, the distinction with organization being the difference between the concrete example and the abstract generality lying behind the examples (Mingers, 1989).

The implications of this are as follows. Firstly, there may be many different structures that are representative of the same ‘organization’. Secondly, the structure may have many properties and relations not specified by the organization (and that may be irrelevant to it). Thirdly, the structure can change, or be changed, without necessarily altering the organization. The role of organization is in specifying the necessary properties of components and their relations and in providing the ‘identity’ of the system.

The changes of state that a structure undergoes occur in response both to internal dynamics and environmental interaction. Traditionally, the systems literature sees such interaction in terms of ‘input’, ‘transformation’ and ‘output’. Maturana and Varela’s conception is distinctive in that they view environmental disturbance in terms of ‘perturbations’ that trigger changes in the state of the system. The nature of such changes, and the interactions that are possible, are determined by the systems structure (for example, if there is no receptor for a particular perturbation then it will not be picked up). Change is allowed by the structure in maintaining the identity (or autonomy) of the system and may either preserve the existing structure or alter it (perhaps radically).

In the latter case, the dynamics of structure mean that it exhibits ‘plasticity’, which provides the mechanics for a system to become structurally coupled to its environment and to other systems. Structural coupling provides a reformulation of the concept of adaptation that proposes that an environment does not determine changes, but instead enables states from those made possible through structural plasticity (Mingers, 1989). In evolutionary terms, either structural plasticity will occur and maintain a system, or will not and the system will disintegrate. It also accepts the concept of equifinality—that there may be several equally effective, internally consistent patterns between the system and its context. That said, we still have a relatively static conceptualisation; either the system fits its environment and is stable, or it is not and is required to adapt. Further dynamics may be added thus (Heylighen, 1999).

I It is more realistic to postulate a ‘reality’ of systems continuously adapting to compensate for environmental perturbations.

I An environment cannot be taken as stable and a priori given. It may be more realistic to characterise the environment itself as consisting of many systems evolving toward ‘trade-off’ points.

To accommodate the first of these points, it may be argued that the variety of actions a system is able to execute must be at least as great as the variety of environmental perturbations that need to be compensated. In essence, this understanding describes Ashby’s (1956) Law of Requisite Variety. The larger the available counteractions (a) the larger the set of perturbations that can be compensated for and (b) the larger the domain of potential environment situations in which the system can survive. The latter point above provides an illustration of Van Valen’s (1973) Red Queen Principle, which proposes that a system must continuously develop in order to maintain its fitness relative to systems with which it co-evolves. A ‘trade-off’ point recognises that those systems with sufficient variety to cope with their immediate environment will likely maintain their present level of complexity.

Evolutionary complexity represents a balance between these principles. The strong connection of distinct components, achieved through interaction between selection and variation, will produce tightly bound systems in which internal variation is constrained. These systems will continue to undergo free external variation until a combination is found in which they (as components themselves) establish a set of weakly fit connections. This produces a less strongly bound ‘higher-order systems, which can then undergo free recombination with other systems producing yet higher-order systems (ad infinitum). Figure 1 illustrates this process. Importantly, the view of evolutionary complexity as the dynamic interplay between order and disorder is characterised by the fact that no part or aspect of a complex entity can provide sufficient information to predict (actually or statistically) the properties of the other parts. This clearly has implications related to the concept of modelling complex systems.

## Social complexity and design

## Evolutionary complexity at a social level

Given the description above, it may be proposed that evolutionary complexity is a characteristic of both the natural and social world (as observed by humans). What is important about objects in the natural world, however, is that the investigator is able to identify the mode of operation of natural structures, mechanisms or processes which they themselves do not produce (Bhaskar, 1979). The relationship is that of ‘concept to thing’, where only the observers knowledge may be subject to scrutiny (changing knowledge of unchanging thing). This situation is complicated in the social world where the object of investigation exists within a pre-interpreted reality. The relationship is that of ‘concept to concept’, where both the observer’s knowledge and knowledge of the observed may be placed under scrutiny (changing knowledge of changing knowledge).

This presents information systems development with the double jeopardy of evolutionary complexity, as it is in the social world that the process takes place. Information systems development is a product of human action, a striking feature of which may be argued to be intentionality. This may be defined as the capacity of an individual to represent entities and states of affairs of the world to themselves through beliefs, desires and alike (Searle, 1983, 1984, 1995). To say that a mental state is intentional is simply to say that it is about something or directed at something. At an individual level, intentionality is not ‘value free’ by virtue of the process of socialisation: it is rooted in the individual’s culture, education and experience (which may be argued to shape the distinctions and connections perceived by that individual). At a collective or societal level, this intentionality becomes embroiled in an ensemble of structures, practices and conventions (the forms, rules and norms of business organization for example) within which individuals exist, reproduce and/or transform.

For the purpose of considering computer-based information systems within organizations, we may frame such intentionality within three interacting circuits (Scarborough & Corbett, 1992):

Circuits of power. Those where the political considerations of stakeholder groups are the primary concern. In terms of dichotomy, this revolves around the tendency for such groups to use the CBIS to reproduce or expand their power and influence, set against the loss of such power and influence—via the CBIS—to a wider range of groups, contexts and knowledge.

Circuits of meaning. Those where the objectivity of the material form of the CBIS may disguise levels of subjective intent and knowledge, intentionally or unintentionally. Thus, on the one hand, stakeholders may translate their interests into powerful and coherent ‘ideologies’ whilst, on the other, implicit and protracted use of the CBIS may ‘reframe’ the way the real world is perceived—thus reshaping values.

I Circuits of design. Those that can broadly be seen as the flows of knowledge, information and artefacts between the processes of invention, exchange and use (characteristics of the innovation process). This may be seen as the political and ideological ‘frame’ within which new technologies and new ideas may be evaluated.

These circuits may be seen as selective mechanisms acting on variations in individual intentionality. The ensemble of structures, practices and conventions that individual’s reproduce or transform (Bhaskar, 1979, 1989; Sayer, 1992) may impose limits on intentionality, through selection or connection, but they do not determine it. That said, they would not exist unless individuals did reproduce or transform them, thus social structure and human agency may be seen as existentially interdependent but essentially distinct. The collective level is both the condition and continually reproduced outcome of human agency—the duality of structure. Human agency is work that is the normally conscious production, and normally unconscious reproduction, of the conditions of production (which include the collective level)—the duality of practice. Collective levels therefore represent higher-order systems that emerge from variation and selection at lower levels.

The ontological implications of this are important (Bhaskar, 1979, 1989):

I Firstly, social structures, mechanisms and processes do not exist independently of the activities that they govern.

I Secondly, social structures, mechanisms and processes do not exist independently of the agents’ conceptions of what they are doing in their activity.

I Thirdly, social structures, mechanisms and processes may only be relatively enduring (that is, that their causal tendencies may not be space-time invariant).

This relational and emergent view entails that a computer-based information system, in social form (as distinct from its material guise), consists in the relationships between people, or between such relationships and ‘nature’ as the object causally presupposes. The ‘reality’ modelled by a computer-based information system is an ensemble of structures, mechanisms and processes that (a) exists only as long as they are being exercised, (b) is exercised through intentional activity and (c) that is not necessarily space–time invariant. The methodical import of the ‘openness’ of such systems is that any assumption that ‘invariant empirical regularities’ exist is highly questionable.

## Abstraction as the ‘tool’ of design

During the analysis and design stages of the development process we attempt to model aspects of social complexity using a variety of tools, techniques and methods of abstraction. The approach is methodical, often sold in the form of ‘packaged’ methodologies and encompassed within a project mentality. In this respect, there is a strong ‘orthodoxy’ of approach (see Iivari, 1991; Hirschheim et al, 1995). At the abstract level, the methodical approach is inherited from science, drawing on formalism and notions of optimality (Banbury, 1987; Baskerville et al, 1992). It seeks a universal approach to a wide family of problem situations, reducing them to an abstract set of symbols (often diagrammatic), thus allowing elements to be manipulated with a finite set of operations in order to deductively arrive at a ‘solution’. At the philosophical level, the methodical approach to development aims to assure that we develop credible knowledge about the present and possible future states of nature (Baskerville et al, 1992, 1996).

Pressman (1994) describes software design as the process through which requirements are translated into a representation in the form of software. It is the means of reconciling the complex social reality described above with the precisely specified operations required by a computer (the transition of the computer-based information system, as social model, to the physics of the material embodiment). To aid this process, most authors note the importance of abstraction. For example, Budgen (1994, p 18) states:

. . . abstraction plays a very important part, since to build manageable models of large and complex systems we need ways of abstracting their critical features for use in forming our models . . . Essentially, abstraction is concerned with the removal of detail from a description of a problem, while still retaining the essential properties of its structure.

Such abstractions are commonly embodied in representations, which examine components and interrelations from different perspectives. Budgen (1994) notes several common forms, these being (a) structural, concerned with the relationships between objects in the design model, (b) behavioural, concerned with the causal links connecting an ‘event’ to an appropriate ‘response via necessary conditions, (c) functional, concerned with what a system and its identified objects actually do and (d) data-modelling, concerned with data structures and the relationship between them. These different dimensions help to get a better grasp of intrinsic complexity by reminding us to include distinctions on different scales and in different temporal and spatial dimensions. The form of representation is partly constrained by the nature of the computer itself, which expresses complexity through a large number of physical switches and its ability to complete many simple binary operations in a very short time. To this extent, the development of software represents a quest for greater abstraction in support of greater complexity (Laplante, 1995).

If one accepts the complex nature of the social world described above, them some problems exist. These hinge on the difficulty of the ‘concept to concept’ relationship, where both the ‘observer’s knowledge’ and ‘knowledge of the observed’ may be placed under scrutiny (changing knowledge of changing knowledge). In terms of the abstraction process, there are several consequences of this that may naturally limit knowledge gained (following Bhaskar, 1979, 1989). Firstly, unacknowledged or unconscious motivation and tacit skills may limit stakeholders’ understanding of themselves. Secondly, unintended consequences and unacknowledged conditions may limit the stakeholders understanding of the social world. These account for views expressed in the literature that ‘users cannot know what they want’ and ‘cannot explain what they know’ (see Ackoff, 1967; Parnas & Clements, 1986; Baskerville et al, 1992; Jones & Walsham, 1992; Paul, 1994). Thirdly, some knowledge relevant to the problem situation may be impossible to express within the descriptive techniques available (Stage, 1991); part by reflecting a view that descriptions of software that abstract away its complexity often abstract away its essence (Brooks, 1987). Lastly, that there is a question relating to the extent that an organizational problem exists as an independent reality that can be modelled (Boland, 1979; Checkland, 1981).

## The challenge of evolutionary complexity

## The imposition of static identity

In dealing with future states of knowledge the consequences discussed above are exacerbated. The emergent argument proposes that social structure, mechanism and process may only be relatively enduring. Logically, this would indicate that any ‘model’ of them is only relatively enduring. Variation and selection in the social world means that the abstraction of entities and their connection cannot be absolute. In contrast, methodical development assumes that social structures, mechanisms and processes are ‘invariant regularities’ that only have to be revealed to be understood (Baskerville et al, 1992). The process for revealing these regularities is bound by a project mentality, which limits the time and resources allocated to development. This combination, engenders a dangerous philosophical habit that leads to the ‘reification of abstractions’ (see Jensen, 1981). Developers abstract from a changing, evolving context and apply the abstraction as a fixed concrete property.

Though some approaches allow for limited iteration in the design process, most suffer in that they abstract a ‘fixed’ view of distinction and connection from the social world. Thus, the ability of the technical complexity to satisfy the social complexity of the system (the internal variety) is implicitly designed in. As previously discussed, the practical adequacy of the resultant model (as an acceptable representation of its real world counterpart) will critically depend on the degree of independence of what was included in the model and what was not. In the short term such approaches may adequately represent complexity. In the long term it is argued that the resultant systems will always disappoint (or fail outright) as they do not allow internal variety to evolve in line with their environment. They represent temporal ‘snapshots’, which ultimately leave us with static systems that have to operate in a dynamic world (Paul, 1994; Lycett et al, 1997). This is illustrated at Figure 2.

The paradoxical situation that faces those responsible for the maintenance and evolution of a complex system relates to how a stable system can be enabled to change and evolve (de Rosnay, 1979). In the case of methodical development, two approaches can be identified. Firstly, the reactive one of maintenance, the pragmatics of which provides credibility for the argument to date. This has been argued by some to consume some 70–80 percent of total development cost (see Gilb, 1988; Pressman, 1994) and can consume up to 90 percent of intellectual effort (Giddings, 1984). Crucially, around 88–98 percent of total maintenance cost is spent on enhancement (Fitzgerald, 1990)—adapting a system to a changed environment. Thus, maintenance currently provides the mechanism by which the information system evolves with its environment. Research undertaken by Lehman and Belady (1985) concludes that, during this process, the complexity of a software system increases unless active work is taken to maintain or reduce it. This phenomena may be argued to be the Red Queen Principle at work; an information system is introduced to coexist with an environment, the sophistication/complexity of the environment evolves (in part through the impact of the information system), in turn requiring the information system to evolve (ad infinitum).

The second approach may be sub-divided, but hinges around the proactive approach of the designer attempting to ‘future-proof’ the system. One perspective that may be adopted is that of attempting to predict and/or control change. For example, within the context of a development cycle, change analysis attempts to discern which requirements are most sensitive to change and which areas of design are most sensitive to changes in requirements (see Fitzgerald, 1990; Strens & Sugden, 1996). A second perspective is that of attempting to cater for every perceived requirement of a design. For example, in a commercial context, many developers attempt to design software in a generic enough fashion to ‘please most of the people most of the time’. In this regard, the bloated nature of much modern ‘shrink-wrapped’ software is the lament of some notable authors (see Wirth, 1995; Yourdon, 1996). A third perspective, which combines the generic approach with a more dynamic conception of maintenance, is that of incremental release (or evolutionary development). This represents the popular model of system development in the commercial world, with the first iteration of the system providing essential operating functions and iterative enhancements being periodically distributed as updates (see Gilb, 1988; Tully, 1984; Avgerou & Cornford, 1993).

## A proposition as response

In attempting to predict change and provide adequate flexibility to meet it (during the development lifecycle), the difference between planning for the future and planning the future is confused (Paul, 1994). The concentration of minds on the likelihood, possible effects and cost of change is laudable, but accounting for a series of ‘what-ifs’ is no guarantee of ‘what-will-be’. The lesson that we should learn is that design should be thought of as ongoing process, not a predictive or contingent one. Olerup (1991) notes that approaches based upon this principle are rare. With this in mind, we propose that the current develop-from-scratch techniques that dominate development thinking must eventually give way to techniques that emphasise construction from reusable building blocks. We view this as a fruitful research avenue in attempting to develop systems capable of dealing with evolutionary complexity. It provides the broad direction for our central proposition:

If we cannot develop information systems with sufficient variety to respond to complex and dynamic environments, we should seek to develop information systems to evolve sufficient variety.

Our research currently explores this proposition through the notion of component-based evolutionary information systems (CBEIS). CBEIS may be defined as componentbased information architectures that are able to inherit or acquire modifications dynamically in response to evolutionary complexity. This proposes that the ability of the technical complexity of the system to satisfy the social complexity (the internal variety) is not implicitly designed, but is the result of component assembly (which entails that function is enabled through structure). Where feedback derived from social complexity is positive, change and/or growth in the structural assembly of components allows the system to move toward emergent goals. Where feedback derived from social complexity is negative, change in the structural assembly of components allows the system to regulate the divergences of positive feedback and stabilise itself. Paradoxically, we may refer to the conjunction of these as ‘dynamic stability’.

A component-based approach offers the chance for a system to exhibit ‘structural plasticity’ whilst retaining its organization. We argue that this provides a more dynamic mechanism for dealing with evolutionary complexity than current development approaches allow. Several perspectives may be distilled that attempt practically to inject plasticity into the structure and function of a system that stems from the following polarity. In the first case, there is a perspective where the dynamics of design are dealt with through external-reference and externalmodification. In the second case, there is a perspective where the dynamics of design are dealt with through the mechanisms of self-reference and self-modification. With the former case, it is possible to use emerging ideas on component-based software composition to develop, maintain and dynamically evolve the system in the context of the human activity system that it serves. Here, ongoing design is achieved through an architecture that allows components to be removed, replaced and reconfigured without perturbing other parts of the system (Nierstrasz & Meijler, 1995). The latter case builds upon the former, but seeks to facilitate automated feedback mechanisms that, in turn, trigger automated mechanisms for construction, maintenance and evolution. These aim to give the system a limited form of autonomy in dealing with evolutionary complexity and represent long-term research.

For both approaches, to refuse to acknowledge the more enduring concepts of software development would be naive at best. Indeed, it is a logical extension of principles such as modularity and software reuse that provide the enabling factors of our research. Our quarrel with the limitations of the methodical approach is resolved through the principle of ongoing design. A system is not designed to be ‘right’ or ‘correct’, it is designed to be structurally coupled in the short-term and provided with a design framework allowing for structural plasticity in the long-term. The methods involved in the design and construction of the system, its components and its evolution may be wide and varied but should remain subservient to these principles. This understanding, the stateof-the art in these areas and the practical issues of component-based development form our ongoing research focus. A major systems solution provider supplies the context for the research.

## Conclusion

Though there has been much progress made in information systems development, the systems themselves continue to disappoint. Advocating a systemic approach, this paper proposes that systems disappointment results from current development ‘understanding’ producing information systems that are unable to deal with the challenge of evolutionary complexity. We have examined this through the medium of a framework describing evolutionary complexity in terms of interplay between the concepts of distinction/connection and variation/ selection. In applying these concepts to the social world we are led to conclude (a) that social regularities are emergent and not a priori given and (b) that these emergent regularities are constantly shifting and evolving. There are strong implications in this for the methodical approach to development, which we have argued assumes social structures, mechanisms and processes as ‘invariant regularities’ that only have to be revealed to be understood (Baskerville et al, 1992). In the long term it has been argued that methodical systems will always disappoint as they do not allow internal variety to evolve in line with the environment. They represent temporal ‘snapshots’, ultimately leaving us with static systems that are asked to operate in a dynamic world.

This understanding has led us to propose a shift in the design process to account for evolutionary complexity. This shift suggests that design should be an inherently ongoing process, not one where the designer is required to predict or control change and/or to articulate every requirement of a design. Our belief is that information systems should not be developed as static entities but should be allowed to grow and adapt, having a genuine historical identity extended and unfolding in time. In relation to this assertion, we propose no ‘silver bullet’. Our own research seeks to examine how componentbased architectures may aid the search for adaptive ability using structural plasticity as a guide. This underlies a belief that ongoing design may be achieved through an architecture that allows components to be removed, replaced and reconfigured in a more dynamic fashion than maintenance currently allows. The research is still in its infancy; we venture that major improvements in information system development will only occur when a shift in the design process, to account for evolutionary complexity, is widely accepted and acted upon.
