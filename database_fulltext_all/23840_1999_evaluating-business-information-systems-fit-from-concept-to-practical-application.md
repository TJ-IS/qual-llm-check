# Evaluating business information systems fit: from concept to practical application

P Kanellis, M Lycett and RJ Paul

Department of Information Systems and Computing at St John’s, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK

Attempting to explain information systems success and failure is a complex task. To measure it is perhaps more complex and compounded by difficulties related to the concept of measurement in a social context. The prevalent approach taken to date revolves around the concept of ‘fit’, as defined by the contingency approach in organizational theory. In reviewing this, a proposal is made for an interpretive approach to measurement. This paper attempts to provide such an approach through the development of a framework that aims to improve understanding for the researcher and practitioner alike. The formative part of the framework seeks to collect data relating to the fit of the information system and its environment along the dimensions of decision making, innovation and information acquisition and distribution. The latter part of the framework uses repertory grids to gain an understanding of both what is perceived as wrong with the system and what is perceived as wanted from the system; the latter acting as a basis for corrective action. A description of action research applying the framework in an organizational setting is given and representative empirical results are examined. The paper concludes with a discussion of the value of the framework and the major conclusions that can be drawn from its application.

## Introduction

There have been numerous attempts documented in the literature that try to identify what causes the success of failure or an information system (see Swanson, 1974; Lucas, 1976; Benbasat et al, 1981; Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Ives et al, 1983). The majority of early attempts focused on a single (or small number of) measurement criteria and argued rather optimistically with regard to their strength as sole determinants of the success of an information system. More recently, however, an increased awareness of the complexity of the issue has prompted the research community (a) to question this line of work and (b) to doubt any propositions of single criteria as definitive success variables. As a result, more pluralistic approaches have started to appear based on interpretations of case studies rather than surveys and laboratory experiments (Christiaanse et al, 1986; Noble & Newman, 1993; Sauer, 1993; Beynon-Davies, 1995).

In furthering such lines of enquiry, this paper presents an interpretive approach (Boland, 1985; Orlikowski, 1991; Orlikowski, 1993; Walsham, 1993; 1995) to the measurement of information systems success. We begin with a justification of such an approach based upon a critical examination of the concept of ‘fit’ as defined in organizational theory (subsequently borrowed by the information systems field). The perceived shortcomings of this approach led us to reappraise our conceptions of both success and measurement. In turn, the outcome of this exercise provides the basis for the development of a framework which aims (a) to allow both the researcher and practitioner to gain an understanding of the information system in situ and (b) to serve as a basis for corrective action where necessary. This is achieved through the structured and mediated acquisition of knowledge.

The formative part of the framework is a measurement vehicle that is used to facilitate an understanding of information systems success in terms of decision making, innovation and information acquisition and distribution. The primary use of the construct is as a tool to facilitate understanding, the results themselves being used as a form of communication. The latter part of the framework uses Repertory Grids (Kelly, 1955) to elicit an understanding as to the fit of an information system and to provide the basis for corrective action.

The framework is exercised in the context of a large and devolved electricity generation company. Three sites were used for the application of the framework, which was used on three differing systems that were present at each site to allow for the triangulation of data. Sample results are presented in two stages. Firstly, from the application of the formative stage of the framework dealing with the ‘raw’ measurement results. Secondly, from the latter part of the framework which builds an understanding of the ‘raw’ results. The application of the framework leads to a discussion of the benefits of use and to the main conclusions that may be drawn from the exercise.

## The concept of information systems fit

For thirty years the issue of fit between an organization and its strategy, structure, processes, technology and environment has served as a building block for theory construction and research in strategic management. Many different conceptualisations and operational tests of fit can be found in the literature (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Thompson, 1967; Galbraith & Nathanson, 1979; Miles & Snow, 1984; Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Venkatraman, 1989; Miller, 1991). Because of the multiplicity of the components covering a range of different types of phenomena in any given environment, research has typically focused on specific fits between specific pairs of components. There have been many attempts to apply this line of reasoning to the information systems field (see Gordon & Miller, 1976; Ewusi-Mensah, 1981; Ein-Dor & Segev, 1982; Daft et al, 1987; Leifer, 1988; Raymond et al, 1994; Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). In general, such research is argument that any determination of information requirements must be based upon the organizational use to which the information system is put. Similarly, the success of any information system must be measured in terms of what it accomplishes in the organization; i.e., its context (Pliskin et al, 1993).

What Iivari (1992) calls the ‘orthodoxy trap’, has resulted in most research regarding measurement of information systems fit falling under the contingency approach. In other words, the objective is a ‘direct’ approach determining what the relevant factors of a pair of components are, then developing a standard solution with standard metrics. In a review of management information systems (MIS) contingency research papers, Weill and Olson (1989) found that over 70 percent followed a model with a view that ‘the better the fit between contingency variables such as strategy, structure and technology, the better the performance’.

Several criticisms may be offered here. Firstly, it is noted that performance is a construct that is value laden, highly dependent on the position from which it is viewed (Weill & Olson, 1989). Secondly, we would posit the following differences between the social and natural realm (Bhaskar, 1979): (a) that social structures do not exist independently of the activities that they govern; (b) that social structures do not exist independently of the agents’ conceptions of what they are doing in their activity; and (c) that social structures may be only relatively enduring (that is, that their causal tendencies may not be space-time invariant). Building on the second point, in the social world, the objects of investigation do not exist or act independently of our knowledge of those objects. There is, therefore, a causal interdependency between the ‘process of knowledge production’ and the ‘production process of the object concerned’. In combination, these views have led us to seek an interpretive understanding of information systems success and its measurement.

Paradoxically, much of the literature surrounding information systems success deals with information systems failure (see Lucas, 1975; Markus, 1983; DeLone & McLean, 1992; Sauer, 1993). This perhaps difficulty in defining success as an objective entity existing ‘independently’ of its effects: thus, it is often more conveniently discussed in terms of ‘what it is not’. The social nature of such conceptions is emphasised by Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987), whose extensive survey defined the overriding generic concept of failure as that of expectation. Their work ‘stresses the importance of understanding how various stakeholders comment on the value of the IS [information system]: failure is the embodiment of a perceived situation’ (Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987: p 264, emphasis added). This highlights the fluidity and inter-penetration between technological and social views and leads us to propose the following points. Firstly, that plurality is unavoidable. Secondly, that success is a perspective that emerges from the social and technical interplay within an organization. Organizations are open systems where, we would propose, invariant empirical regularities do not hold in the sense that they do in the natural sciences (where systems can be experimentally closed and initial conditions controlled). Accordingly, we surmise that there can be no single account of success but only different perceptions influenced by context.

The methodological import of this view has implications for measurement; principally, that it becomes a tool to aid understanding and not to facilitate prediction (Bhaskar, 1979). As a result, the measurement construct that we will outline below is intended to serve as a form of communication where the results provide a ‘map depicting given aspects of the ‘organizational territory’, which can subsequently serve as a basis for corrective action. The ‘pragmatics’ of the ensuing understanding or explanation are expressed in terms of practical adequacy (Sayer, 1992); knowledge gained must generate expectations about the world that are actually realised and are intelligible and acceptable. In covering the criteria for practical adequacy, an approach was visualised where both stakeholder and researcher were involved in an interactive communication process, projecting an interpretation of the situation and process within which they were involved. Such a high level of accuracy in reporting the case also allows the researchers to tell the truth as they see it. In addition, because any constructs of measurement are seen as analogies which interpret ‘reality’, and communicate the reality so interpreted, any conflicts between an interpretive paradigm and quantitative methods are eliminated (Jackson, 1993).

## Background to empirical study

A large and devolved electricity generation company in the UK provided the setting for the study. The company had recently privatised and was evolving from a period of relative stability. The outlook regarding business objectives were uncertain. The company was therefore seen as an attractive opportunity for action research (Checkland, 1981; Gummesson, 1991; Jonsson, 1991), which may be defined as a planned intervention by a researcher into some naturally occurring events (Gill & Johnson, 1991). This was undertaken via a secondment to the Information Technology Strategy and Planning Unit for a six-month period.

The company’s information systems were in place before the study started. In considering the business changes that had occurred since, the organization was anxious to know how their systems had fared and to identify any possible misfit. The information system examined was a work management system—monitoring and reporting on parts required for scheduled projects across various plants. Though work management may be seen as a fairly stable business process, changes had occurred that are broadly summarised as follows. Firstly, the general approach relating to the way work was undertaken at power stations was much changed, manpower levels were significantly reduced and planning departments were dismantled at privatisation. Secondly, greater emphasis was placed on cost/benefit, dictating a change in the general maintenance philosophy.

The first stage of our interpretive approach to measurement was concerned with initiating a heuristic learning process between researcher and stakeholder. Nineteen interviews with business unit managers, and senior system personnel in charge of information systems development or commercial operations, were conducted. The aims of this were threefold. Firstly, to identify the perceptions prevalent about the systems. Secondly, to find out how they thought about and would approach an attempt to measure the fit. Thirdly, to understand needs regarding the flexibility required from systems. Our findings indicated that the ways in which people attempt to acquire an understanding of what the business ‘fit’ of a system is, and measure it, were numerous and mixed. Opinions range from ‘I am not sure if you are able to measure it’ to ‘Fit is the ability to interchange information across systems’. Given the broad range of opinion, some form of ‘normalisation’ was required to provide an inter-subjective foundation for measurement. This empirical evidence did, however, reinforce our theory-based conclusion that measurement is a subjective exercise and any quantitative attempt to assess the fit of an information system is tied to the measurement of perception.

To facilitate the normalisation of opinion, the literature was examined for an inter-subjective basis upon which the measurement of perception could be agreed. This examination led to a tentative decision that the fit of an information system could be quantitatively measured along three processes defined by and dependent upon information—decision making, innovation and information acquisition and distribution. Huber and McDaniel (1986) argue that, as a by-product of change, decision making becomes a primary objective—requiring greater timeliness, acceptability, implementability and technical and economic effectiveness. Huber (1984), in a similar vein, notes that, in addition to decision making, the processes of innovation and information acquisition and distribution are of equal importance; the need for frequent and rapid innovation is echoed by others (see Child, 1987; Kanter et al, 1992; OECD, 1992). The rationale for the above, together with a description of them as core objectives and processes that the company should have (and that the information systems should be able to support and enhance) were then discussed with all nineteen interviewees for verification, comments and possible enhancements. Their responses were all positive and the three ‘dimensional anchors’ were acceded to unconditionally.

Following this phase, a questionnaire format was chosen as the vehicle for measurement. This decision was made in weighing the richness of results that could be obtained in using an interview format against the number of geographically dispersed company sites and number of people that we wished to participate in the research. In this respect, time was a noticeable constraint. The decision was also very heavily influenced by the company’s request that the vehicle of research should be something that they could reuse at a later date with a minimum of fuss. With the decision made, the questionnaire was used to determine:

The perception of the validity of the three-dimensional anchors (as a check on initial accession).

The perception of pertinent aspects of each anchor, upon which what we have termed a ‘bound of disappointment’, could be constructed.

A starting point for decision making was provided by Lindblom’s (1959) synoptic process which comprises the five steps of: (1) identify problem(s); (2) generate alternative solutions; (3) evaluate and choose between alternatives; (4) implement the chosen solution; and (5) maintain the solution via monitoring, review and appraisal. That said, the drawback of the model is that it assumes both ‘perfect knowledge’ and ‘perfect rationality’ (Wilson & Rosenfeld, 1990). These notions have been refuted and attention is drawn to Hickson (1990) who reverses this conventional view, arguing that those involved in the process bring with them preconceived solutions. Though these may not fit the decision required, they may do the next time. Empirical evidence leads Hickson et al (1986) to a view of decision making in terms of process types co-related to the level of complexity faced by the decision makers and the level of political activity aroused.

On the basis of this characterisation of decision making, alongside the work of Huber (1984), Huber and McDaniel (1986) and the interviews, the following constructs of decision making and information acquisition and distribution were chosen; (a) speed and frequency, (b) quality/confidence, (c) complexity, (d) risk/ uncertainty and (e) political influence/meaning. The measurement constructs relating to innovation were based on the work of Swanson (1994) who provides a basic typology of information system innovation and seeks to establish relationships between information system innovation and business innovation. Taking what Swanson (1994) terms ‘Type II and III’ innovations (information systems in the service of basic business processes and products) the following constructs were chosen; (a) administrative process improvement, (b) business process improvement, (c) business product/service improvement and (d) the level of integration of the business with suppliers, distributors and/or customers. The concept of ‘added value’ (Porter, 1985; Porter & Millar, 1985) was appended to the construct list to provide an indication as to whether the perceptual value created by the information system, in the context of its activity, exceeded the perceptual cost of performing that activity.

The objective of attempting to codify the measurement of the fit was set because more users would be able to share perceptions of that fit. As the level of codification increases, the ‘sharing of information can be facilitated provided the code is easy enough to learn quickly, and that the analogy (the code) is credible and acceptable’ (Jackson, 1993: p 176). The questionnaire, provided at Appendix I, was designed with the above in mind. Questions were presented in the form of positive statements, requiring agreement or disagreement in the form of a five-point Likert type scale. In addition, for each question relating to the constructs above, the respondent was asked in the form of a binary construct (yes/no) whether the information system was adequate. This was designed to allow statistical validation (or otherwise) of the constructs.

To compensate, in small part, for the loss of contextual richness that is inherent with the questionnaire format, it was completed with the researcher present. This was to allow any ambiguity to be resolved and to provide for a level of interaction. The comments and observations not only helped to improve the questionnaire itself but also provided valuable ‘insider’ knowledge that has aided the subsequent interpretation of the data. For each given information system the questionnaire was administered across three company sites for the purpose of obtaining triangulated data (Jick, 1979; Patton, 1990). Three systems were measured in total. At each site the questionn was distributed to ten users, in various departments, in order to get a cross-sectional view of the system. These ‘users’ were all experienced with the system under consideration, having worked with it since its introduction in most cases. Accordingly, they had all been with the company for a considerable length of time and had all experienced the privatisation process and the changes that it had induced.

## Representative empirical results

The results for one system at one site are presented as an illustrative example. A simple frequency analysis showed overwhelmingly that the dimensional anchors, as presented, were perceived as valid. Dichotomising the measurement scale, 100% agreed that decision making was an important facet, 90% agreed that innovation was an important facet (10% strongly agree, 80% agree), and 100% agreed that information acquisition and distribution was an important facet (30% strongly agree, 70% agree). In asking the user to answer positive Likert type questions, reinforced by a dichotomised question as to the adequacy of the information system, the aim was to ascertain if a statistical relationship existed between these two variables, which related to the significance of correlation.

To test this, the results obtained were dichotomised such that 1 represented ‘strongly agree’ and ‘agree’ and 0 represented ‘undecided’, ‘disagree’ and ‘strongly disagree’. This process yielded a x range (the independent variable) that represented an artificial dichotomy and a y range (the dependent variable) that represented a true dichotomy (yes/no with regard to adequacy). On the basis of the pairing of artificial and true dichotomy, the Phi Coefficient was chosen as the method of calculating the correlation (see Kaplan, 1987: p 234). This measure of association (that broadly holds that when  = 0 it follows that x and y are statistically independent) was calculated using the following formula (Ferguson & Takane, 1989: p 452):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \phi = \frac {B C - A D}{\sqrt {(A + B) (C + D) (A + C) (B + D)}} \\ \text { where: } \\ f = \text { the   phi   coefficient } \\ A = \text { the   cell   frequency } (0 + 1) \\ B = \text { the   cell   frequency } (1 + 1) \\ C = \text { the   cell   frequency } (0 + 0) \\ D = \text { the   cell   frequency } (1 + 0) \end{array}
$$

All results were positive, thus showing a correlation between the variables concerned—the aggregate means being 0.4545 for decision making, 0.5421 for innovation and 0.8942 for information acquisition and distribution. The significance of these results was tested against a chisquare table with one degree of freedom (Ferguson & Takane, 1989) using the following formula:

$$
\chi = N \phi^ {2}
$$

where N = the sample of N individuals.

The results of this process gave results of 0.2, 0.1, and 0.01 respectively. It was thus concluded that this was sufficient to state that the association between the Likert type and dichotomous variables for each question was not due to chance.

For the construction of the bound of disappointment, three items were established. Firstly, the current level of satisfaction, obtained by calculating the percentage of users who were totally satisfied with the system (identified as those with a 1 + 1 pairing). Secondly, the relative level of disappointment, obtained by calculating the percentage of users who were not satisfied with the system (identified as those with a 0 + 0 or 1 + 0 pairing); adding the percentage of satisfied users and dividing by two to obtain the mean figure. Lastly, the absolute level of disappointment, taken as a constant that was approximated in this case. The relative level of disappointment equates to the cut-off point whose lower range fails to satisfy most users. The absolute level represents a ‘quality constant’, which in our view should at least be approximated by any information system perceived as adequate (the level pragmatically decided by the company in question). The bound of disappointment is presented graphically at Figure 1.

In relative terms, the system was found to be disappointing in the several areas. For decision making, disappointment was noted in aiding the speed and frequency of decisions, aiding the quality of decisions and offering a means to influence the decision-making process. For innovation, disappointment was noted in improving the integration/co-ordination with suppliers, distributors and/or customers. For information acquisition and distribution, disappointment was noted in the reliability and validity of information and the acquisition of information to reduce risk. In absolute terms, the system was perceived to disappoint in all but two areas, these being improving administrative process, under the innovation dimension, and aiding speed and frequency under the information acquisition and distribution dimension.

## Establishing the usefulness of the measurement construct

The results of the study tell us how the respondents perceive the information system at a particular point in time, essentially providing a static representation of a changing world. If we recapitulate on previous argument, we have also noted that, whilst measurement may be necessary, understanding assumes the place of accuracy and prediction. The purpose of the measurement construct was to provide a form of communication with which an understanding could be sought. The results provide a metaphorical ‘map’ of a ‘territory’, which is the information system in situ.

Weick (1990) proposes that such a map should be judged, not by its level of accuracy, but rather on its strength to help people differentiate and make comparisons with what they already know. In other words, to engage them in an adaptive process. Maps are tied with action which generates movement, it is this movement and continuous adaptation that results in the map becoming the territory (a process which is shown at Figure 2):

Maps activate self-correcting action, so they are real start ing points that soon become real fictions based on experience which serves to update a static representation of a changing world. Discrepancies between maps and current experiences stand out because comparison is made possible . . . It is the tight coupling between maps and action that tightens the coupling between maps and the territory. A map provides a reference point, a beginning, which often becomes secondary once [this] activity gets underway (Weick, 1990: p 9).

The results of the study would appear to indicate that corrective action is required if the map is to be ‘indicative’ of its territory. The next part of the process therefore focuses on the following. Firstly, on understanding why the results are as they are. Secondly, on making stakeholders aware so that they form the locus of corrective action/adaptation. To this end, the results of the study were used to construct and administer Repertory Grids (Kelly, 1955).

Kelly developed the Repertory Grid technique to operationalise his theory that individuals develop a ‘personal construct system’ to interpret, anticipate and thus hopefully control their personal environment. Based upon their personal construct systems, individuals attempt to respond to the external world by interpreting situations and behaving accordingly. The primary aim of the technique is to elicit an individual’s cognitive structure without the imposition of the researcher’s ‘frame of reference’; it has subsequently, however, found a wider range of applications (see Fransella & Bannister, 1977; Stewart & Stewart 1981).

The range of variations of the Repertory Grid technique provides evidence of its suitability over different research scenarios and designs. It is perhaps best described as a set of related methods that use a common rubric, rather than as a single method (Reger 1990). There are three data collection decisions for the development of a Repertory Grid:

The identification of elements. These may be a person or an object, or a property of a person or an object (such as the fit of an information system). In this instance, the elements were taken as the measurement constructs previously discussed (and shown at Figure 1 and Appendix 1).

The elicitation of constructs and contrasts. These allow the respondent to make sense of the elements provided by the researcher. A number of techniques have been developed for this and consideration of these has prompted us to choose the minimum context form for our study (see Fransella & Bannister, 1977 for descriptions of each method). Broadly speaking, this encourages the practitioner to think of the data in terms of similarities and contrasts between the results, which allows a comprehensive picture to be formed where the reasons for poor fit are represented as constructs and the polar opposite (corrective action) as contrasts.

The statistical matching of elements and constructs. There are three general methods, sorting, ranking and rating. Each of these methods is designed to cater for large sample sizes and provide a solid basis for extensive qualitative or quantitative analyses (see Fransella & Bannister, 1977 for descriptions of each method). At this stage of our research, neither our stated objective/rationale for using the Repertory Grid technique, nor our sample size, justify statistical matching.

A sample Repertory Grid is shown at Appendix 2. For each system, in some instances, the triangulation of data across sites investigated showed differences in perceptions of fit. By picking the two company sites where the information system was perceived as having the same level of fit, and a third site where it has a different one, respondents were asked to consider and explain such differences.

For the purpose of illustration, two examples are now discussed, derived from Repertory Grid-based interviews with two senior managers in charge of systems development. The first example relates to the triangulated data obtained from Question 1(a) of the questionnaire (see Appendix 1). ## From measurement to understanding

Example 1: Speed and frequency of decision making In this example, the results from the empirical study showed what may be described as a ‘high’ level of fit at Site C and a ‘moderate’ level of fit at Sites A and B. Differing views were expressed by interviewees regarding the results at Site C. One interviewee felt that the high level of fit resulted from the system exceeding expectations, when first implemented, in combination with a lower level of use. Hence, the result may be explained in terms of the system’s ‘history of use’. Another interviewee felt that the result was odd; as Site A and Site B were using the system to a greater extent, he would have expected the system to help them more than it helped Site C. He followed his thoughts, however, with the following remark:

Site C is quite a different site to the other two and, if it actually did stick out from those, that would not surprise me because they run the actual software in a totally different way. At Site A and Site B every job that is done will go through the system; whereas at Site C only the bigger jobs will go through it.

Consider now what the first interviewee said regarding the system at Site C.

It could also be the amount of data that they put in—they select it at the outset, and therefore they are working with a much smaller volume of data. They get their answers quicker, and they get the answers they need: whereas maybe at the other sites they have got all the data and it is a bi confusing for them.

Though the two respondents may have expressed different views, it is not difficult to see that, in a group situation, it would not have taken them long to reach an agreement (and to adjust their maps accordingly). In analysis, the two excerpts above talk about the same thing. The explanation that was given by both, was that the current usage of the system at each Site (as opposed to the usage that it was designed for) is generally less frequent and less intensive than it was historically. The construct that was drawn out through the use of the Repertory Grid technique was that the system was perceived as being designed for complexity and detailed analysis. The contrast that was drawn out was that the perceived business need was for simplicity and ease of use (see Appendix 2).

## Example 2: Co-ordination

In the second example, the results from the empirical study showed what may be described as a ‘moderate’ level of fit at Site A and a ‘low’ level of fit at Sites B and C. The perceived low fit at the latter sites was attributed to the way working methods have changed since the time that the system was designed; when the need for extensive co-ordination was never envisaged as a requirement. One manager explains:

We started this process when there was a central planning department, and that disappeared as we were working through the implementation phase. Once this was lost, it meant that people were having to work right across different departments. ‘Electrical’, ‘Mechanical’ and ‘Instruments are an integral group now, whereas, previously, they were separate. Of course, the new structure does not reflect the way they used to work—which is what [the system] was meant to fit into.

Thus, the construct drawn was that of the inadequate provision of internal integration. The contrast drawn was that of flexibility, one of the respondents indicating that the system needed this: (a) in the way that users are allowed to enter data; and (b) in the way that subsequent analysis is done. The more simple these aspects are kept, the easier the co-ordination and the smaller the number of people involved in the process. Hence, improvement in intra-unit integration could be achieved when the information system allows for a small number of individuals to share a work flow, and to do it quickly and simply. For this to happen, processes have to be slimmed down, become less formal and be more flexible. In turn, this means devolving a lot of responsibility to the individual. Thus, by shaping the organizational culture and structure which determine such processes, overwhelming formality is not transferred to the information system which, as a result, becomes more simplified.

## Conclusions

The work herein has sought to provide an interpretive approach to the measurement of information systems success, with the objective of improving understanding for the researcher and practitioner alike. It began with a review of the literature related to the concept of fit, which led us to reappraise our conceptions of success and measurement. For success, we have proposed that there can be no single account but only different perceptions influenced by context. For measurement, we have proposed that it does not hold the same status as it may in the natural sciences. It does, however, have value as a communication tool whose results can be used to facilitate understanding. From a weak interpretivist perspective, this resolves the tension created by mixing quantitative and qualitative research methods; the former simply acts as a means of obtaining data that provides a basis for interpretation by the latter (see Walsham, 1995).

In this case, data was obtained using a questionnairebased measurement construct, which was designed to be a convenient and simple means of getting data from a number of people at a number of sites and visualising it via the Bound of Disappointment. This was operationalised in the context of a large and devolved electricity generation company where three different systems, present at three different sites, were measured. The quantitative data obtained allowed for triangulation and provided the grounding for what we have metaphorically described as a ‘map defining the organisational territory’. Repertory Grids provided the tools for eliciting such explanation, using constructs and contrasts to gain an understanding of both what was perceived as wrong with the system and what was perceived as wanted from the system.

Clearly, there are limitations in an exercise of this sort. Firstly, any measurement construct may be argued to be a static concept and valid only for the time evaluation and/or reappraisal take place. Secondly, to the extent that individuals are confused, unaware or deliberately deceptive, some findings may be misleading (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). These are both important points. In the former case, this is one reason that we have sought to make the measurement process conceptually simple so that it can be used to monitor the quality of fit at regular time points. In both cases, however, this only provides the ‘limited’ part of the equation. The true value is in the interpretation process where, via the structured and mediated acquisition of knowledge, both the researcher and practitioner gain an ‘in situ’ understanding of the information system that can serve as the basis for corrective action. The measurement construct, in tandem with the Repertory Grid technique, adequately provides this in practical terms. The combination of construct and contrast allows intelligent (and reasonably specific) consideration as to why aspects of an information system may or may not be perceived as a success. It is this feedback that induces awareness to change and provides positive stimulus for corrective action.

In reflecting on the findings of the work, we would broadly conclude that poor fit in the systems examined relates to the inability of the information system to respond to changes in the business case. Change had severely affected the main information systems in the organization, resulting in structural, process and technological misfit. To this extent, the empirical study provides support for Hirschheim et al’s (1995) view that, at any time, an information system can only approximate the changing requirements emerging from the constantly shifting trends and policies of organizational life. More specifically, it has shown us that (a) the project-based nature of development is clearly vulnerable to unknown and discontinuous change, and (b) building a system on the premise of agreeing on a ‘set of requirements’ and freezing them at a certain point is a recipe for disappointment. This offers valuable insight with regard to how future information systems should be developed with evolutionary aspects forming the focus of our ongoing research (Paul, 1994; Lycett & Paul, 1997).

## About the authors

Panagiotis Kanellis is currently working for Andersen Consulting in Greece. He was educated at Western International University (USA) in business administration (BSc), at University of Ulster in computing and information systems (PG Dip) and at Brunel University in data communications (MSc) and information systems (PhD). His research interests revolve around information systems success and failure. These include studying the effect of unpredictable business change via the history and evolution of information systems, the measurement of the fit of an information system with its environment and assessing how the benefits that information systems may provide can be improved over time.

Mark Lycett is currently a lecturer at Brunel University. He was educated at Oxford Brookes University in computing and business (BSc) and at Brunel University in information systems (MSc, PhD pending). His recently completed doctoral studies relate to the challenges faced by information systems development and research interests include component-based software development, software reuse, domain engineering and the use of developmental tools and techniques. Prior to his educational studies he spent 12 years in industry, the latter part with BAA plc as a consultant in the company’s Technical Services Division.

Ray J Paul is currently Professor of Simulation Modelling at Brunel University where he has been since 1992. He was educated at the University of Hull in mathematics (BSc) and operational research (MSc, PhD) before being appointed by the London School of Economics, where he taught information systems and operational research for 21 years. He has published widely in book and paper form (two books and over 100 papers in journals, books and conference proceedings) and has acted as a consultant for a variety of UK government departments, software companies and commercial companies in the tobacco and oil industries. He is currently director of two research centres at Brunel University, the Centre for Applied Simulation Modelling and the Centre for Living Information Systems Thinking.
