# Empirically testing the benefits, problems, and success factors for telecommuting programmes

T Guimaraes<sup>1</sup> and P Dallow<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Jesse E. Owen Chair of Excellence, College of Business Administration, Tennessee Technological University, Cookeville, TN 38505; <sup>2</sup>Information & Communication Systems Department, City of Fort Collins, 300 LaPorte Avenue, P.O. Box 580, Fort Collins, CO 80522–0580, USA

The benefits of telecommuting have been recognised by government and business organizations throughout the world. As companies implement their telecommuting programmes many problems are encountered which require management attention. For organizations contemplating the implementation of such programmes it has become exceedingly important to know what can be done to improve the likelihood that the many potential benefits will be derived and that the many possible problems are reduced or eliminated. What organizations should do to accomplish this was the quest for this study. The voluminous literature is mostly comprised of anecdotal evidence, personal opinions, or experience based on a single organization. A thorough survey of the relevant literature was undertaken to exhaustively identify the many potential benefits, problems, and the proposed success factors for telecommuting programmes in practice. Using the benefits derived from telecommuting programmes and their impact on company performance as the measures of programme success, six main success factors were empirically tested with a sample of 316 telecommuters from eighteen companies. The results corroborate at least partially the importance of carefully considering the characteristics of supervisors, employees, tasks, and work environments, as well as management support and problems encountered, for the success of telecommuting programmes.

## Introduction

Telecommuting (defined as working from home or alternate workplace) seems to be a growing worldwide trend. The number of telecommuters in the US today is 8.8 million, and it is estimated to jump to 25 million by the year 2000. Most (57%) of telecommuters are men and 43% are women (Sullivan, 1995). Advances in technology, workforce demographics, government regulations, employee motivation, and the need for a lowcost employee benefit are the biggest factors pushing managers to seek more information and training on how to effectively use flexible work arrangements as a management tool. In 1983 it cost over \$10000 to equip a home with a PC, phone, fax, etc, but in 1994 the cost was less than \$3000 (Sullivan, 1995). Many nations are investing in telecommuting programmes, including Great Britain, Finland, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, and Sweden (Humble et al, 1995). It is being strongly encouraged in countries with dense population such as England and Japan because of the extreme costs of real estate in overcrowded cities. A British Telecom report reveals that one in five British workers now spend at least some of the week working from home, and they expect that time to increase even further. It also revealed that those most willing to give it a try were people in the south (47%), where avoiding the hassle of commuting was considered to be one of the biggest plus points. The biggest area of growth, however, is likely to come in the north and Scotland, where as many as 42% expect to increase the number of hours spent teleworking. (‘British Business Moves Home,’ 1994). In the US, the number of conventional corporate telecommuters has increased from 2.4 million in 1990 to 6.6 million in 1994. The increase is expected to go on into the foreseeable future (Girard, 1997). At AT&T, 35 000 employees telecommuted at least one day a week in 1994, as compared to 21000 in 1993. AT&T also utilises virtual offices, with 12000 employees working in 1994 and 5000 in 1993 (Noble, 1995). In the San Francisco Bay area close to a third of the 3 million workers do at least some of their work from home, with their number continuing to increase by 10–15 percent annually (Anonymous, 1997a). A telecommuter is an office worker who performs a job, part time or full time, away from the corporate office building, typically at home or in a satellite office. Generally, telecommuters use computer and related technology to keep in touch with their companies and supervisors.

As discussed below, telecommuting has many implementation problems (Haddon & Silverstone, 1993; Greenbaum, 1998), but the incentives for doing it are many.

(1) By telecommuting, companies comply with the

Clean Air Act’s traffic reduction requirements (Davis, 1995). The federal Environmental Protection Agency has been pushing ECO (Employee Commute Options) legislation, where large employers in metropolitan areas with severe ozone or carbon monoxide pollution would develop plans to increase ride-sharing during peak commuting periods (Scheier, 1995).

(2) Many companies with telecommuting programmes have experienced an increase in productivity (Davis, 1995; Xenakis, 1997; Trembly, 1998). Another study found telecommuters are more productive and have better home lives since they started working at least part time from home. Of all the workers polled, 75% indicated higher productivity, and for 83% their home life got better (Sebastian, 1995).

(3) The cost of telecommuting equipment is relatively low compared to regular offices. Nevertheless, the issue of cost division may be important in some situations, as discussed below. It has been estimated that the cost of equipping a regular telecommuter is about \$4000, with upkeeping costs at about \$2100 yearly (Sebastian, 1995). For example, IBM created a virtual office in Cranford, NJ, USA, where employees from five scattered New Jersey locations have been consolidated at a refurbished warehouse. Total office square footage to house these employees has been reduced from 400000 to 100000, all on one floor. At a given time, most employees are on the road, visiting customers, or at home. They have been equipped with IBM ThinkPads, mobile phones, and beepers to conduct business away from the office. The motivation for the programme was cost reduction and productivity improvement. Proposals to customers are produced on the spot with no need to go to the office for further information.

Telecommuting activity varies widely by industry; the greatest users are in the high technology industries (44%), followed by the services, banking, and retail industries (Davis, 1995). More than 50% of IT consultants telecommute, and 93% want a telecommuting contract (Roche, 1998). Another survey found that the insurance sector reported the highest use of telecommuting with 54% of respondents, up from 32% in 1994. Of the respondents from all other industries, 49% offer it or are considering telecommuting arrangements to their employees (Jones, 1995). Most telecommuting occurs among professional employees and is less common among employees of public non-profit industries (Davis, 1995). It is estimated that 7.6 million Americans telecommuted in 1995 and that is expected to grow to 25 million by the year 2000 (Anonymous, 1998). In 1995 many US companies (18%) already had work-at-home programmes (Greengard, 1995). The percentages were 42 and 51 for 1996 and 1997, respectively (Anonymous, 1997b).

The literature contains a few empirical studies whose objectives are validation of knowledge about telecommuting (Duxbury, et al, 1992; Venkatesh & Vitalari, 1992; Frolick et al, 1993; Ruppel & Howard, 1998). However, most of the literature consists of positive personal opinions and success stories of specific telecommuting programmes. The evidence of actual benefits and problems is relatively superficial, mostly amounting to individual company cases and personal opinions of consultants and other vendors. This study’s main purpose is to use a more systematic and rigorous approach to accomplish the following two objectives: (1) testing the impact of telecommuting programmes in terms of benefits derived and on organization performance; and (2) developing a more general perspective on the actual benefits, problems, and the success factors important to increase the former and reduce the latter.

## Telecommuting success factors: theory and hypotheses

There are dozens of factors that can potentially influence the extent to which the end results from the telecommuting programme will affect the organization’s business performance. Based on the extensive literature survey directly related to the study objectives, these factors can intuitively be grouped into employee supervisor characteristics, employee characteristics, the characteristics of the work environment, the characteristics of the business tasks involved, the level of management support, and the extent to which the company encountered problems while implementing the programme. Each of these major constructs and hypothesised relationships are discussed in more detail below, followed by the two measures of telecommuting success used in this study: benefits derived, and impact on organization performance.

## Independent variables

## Supervisor characteristics

Many managers are uncomfortable that they cannot see their employees working, some believe that, left on their own, telecommuting employees will sit around in their pyjamas watching television and neglect their work. However, experience indicates that this fear is considered groundless (Penn, 1995). The two key issues are: (1) how to communicate directives, establish goals, and develop time frames; and (2) the management of worker performance.

Companies with little work autonomy, that use timebased methods of work supervision, and whose decisionmaking processes are centralised and hierarchical, may find it difficult to implement telecommuting without changing these aspects of organizational structure. Such variables appear to be among the major determinants of which organizations have successful telecommuting programmes (Humble et al, 1995).

Perhaps the most difficult adjustment to telecommuting is the liberalisation of supervisory attitudes. Telecommuter self-control replaces direct supervisory control (Hartman, et al, 1992). Companies with a rigid, hierarchical command and control structure may find the virtual office to be a cultural and organizational earthquake (Kirkley, 1994). Leadership styles are important for a successful telecommuting programme a ‘supporting approach’ or a ‘delegating approach’ are thought to be the most useful. The supporting approach uses low directive and high supportive actions. Employees are highly involved in problem solving and decision making. The delegating approach uses low directive and low supportive behaviour. Staff members assume almost total responsibility for projects, and have considerable autonomy (Kirvan, 1995).

Telecommuting does require that managers look at the measurable, deliverable (quality and quantity) aspects of work produced by the employees they are supervising (Sullivan, 1995). Managers can learn to judge employees on actual work produced only, and not on presence, engaging personalities, or just looking busy (Kirkley, 1994; Chae, 1995; Chaudron, 1995; Greenwald, 1995). Many managers have embraced management by objectives (MBO) which call for managers to evaluate employees on output rather than activities. An MBO system or equivalent is a prerequisite for a successful telecommuting programme (Alvi & McIntyre, 1993). Managers and employees should meet to outline exactly what duties and goals the employees are to accomplish, how long it ought to take them, and the resources needed to achieve the results. Once these goals are established, managers need to devise a method for monitoring employees’ performance (Chae, 1995). Supervisors of telecommuting employees will require training in techniques of results-based management, job analysis, work specification, and performance appraisal, as well as in communication skills (Humble et al, 1995). Thus, we propose:

H1: Supervisor characteristics are positively related to telecommuting success.

## Workers characteristics

Not everyone is suited for telecommuting. It can be difficult for telecommuters to adjust to working at home after spending their lives in corporate environments (Wheelwright, 1995). The telecommuting candidate needs to work well independently, enjoy responsibility, easily conform to a trusting relationship, and have personal motivation (Weiss, 1994). The first identifying characteristic of a potential telecommuter is the wanting to do it. Second, they must be technically skilled and have substantial experience performing the required tasks. Finally, telecommuters tend to look for different kinds of satisfaction from their work. They generally are self-directed and prefer to work with less social interaction than many in-office workers (Humble et al, 1995). An effective selection policy is considered by some as the most important factor for the success of telecommuting. Besides selecting people that are good performers and have a proven track record, another recommended worker characteristic is to be a self-starter (Chae, 1995). The home worker needs self-discipline. It helps to focus on the task at hand—and to know when to quit working for the day (Alvi & McIntyre, 1993; Bond, 1995; Dunkin & Baig, 1995). Telecommuters require considerable training to effectively use computers, software, modems, fax machines, e-mail, and voice mail, as well as have some training in office organization and time management to enable them to manage their work load and to separate work and home life (Alvi & McIntyre, 1993; Wheelwright, 1995). Thus the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: Worker characteristics are positively related to telecommuting success.

## Work environment characteristics

Telecommuters must realise that the same rules that apply to them in the office are also applicable when they telecommute. Distractions and work interruptions must be avoided or the telecommuter will not be successful (Mogelonsky, 1995). The telecommuter work environment should be viewed as an extension of the central office. The telecommuter has to do more than perform his or her work well. The work needs to be planned and presented in a way that avoids a decline in workplace visibility and maintain an impression of a daily presence at the office (Challenger, 1992). Thus the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: Work environment characteristics are positively related to telecommuting success.

## Task characteristics

A well-defined job description is the key to telecommuting. After the job has been defined, the manager and the telecommuting employee need to agree on what is expected to be accomplished and when (Anonymous, 1995). Selecting the jobs for telework should precede identification of specific teleworkers. Important factors to be considered include job content, the resources required to perform the work, required security of the data created/used, and the nature and extent of required interaction with other people (Wilkes et al, 1994).

Research, communications, customer service, sales, and publicity are obvious jobs whose functions mesh neatly with work from remote locations (Weiss, 1994). A wide variety of jobs are amenable to telecommuting: accountants, architects, consultants, data entry clerks, insurance brokers, lawyers, project managers, systems programmers, telemarketers, etc. Many types of tasks are well-suited for telecommuting arrangements such as software engineering, computer simulation, technical design, computerised project tracking, computer programming, data entry, design (especially computer-aided design), project planning and control, technical drawing, and technical writing and editing (Humble et al, 1995). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4: Task characteristics are positively related to telecommuting success.

## Management support

Despite the many reported benefits, there is a great deal of reluctance by many corporations to pursue widespread implementation of telecommuting programmes. Some of this reluctance can be attributed to management fear of losing control of the workforce, difficulty of managing office assets, costs involved in maintaining dual sets of assets, and fear of legal and insurance complications (Lavallee, 1993; Wilkes et al, 1994; Humble et al, 1995; Mokhtarian & Salomon, 1996). The success of a telecommuting programme will ultimately be dependent on the vision and support of senior executives (Solomon & Templer, 1993). One way to ensure such support is the existence of a champion—someone who has the authority and strong desire to ensure that the programme succeeds. This person should be sufficiently senior in the organization to engender enthusiasm among participants and acquire resources critical to the programme (Wilkes et al, 1994). Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5: Management support is positively related to telecommuting success.

## Telecommuting implementation problems

In some cases, there are dangers in telecommuting. Information is being sent back and forth between many telecommuters and their employers. Such information is susceptible to unauthorised access and interference. Employees worry because they are out of sight of management (Noble, 1995; Gray, 1995) and fear missing out on communication (Adams, 1995; Connelly, 1995; Humble et al, 1995). Career movement may be inhibited over time, a number of factors to account for this scenario. First, there are political factors. Since telecommuters are away from the office, they receive less visibility and exposure. Second, considerable information is exchanged informally in organizations and if an employee is not physically present, they don’t have access to these exchanges and are ‘out of the loop’. Networking activities that may be important for advancement prospects are restricted. Telecommuters indicated that this may not be just a restriction within the present department, but may also restrict movement to other areas of the organization. Extra organizational assignments and responsibilities are less likely to occur. Meetings may be missed and, at times, training and developmental opportunities may be restricted. Some telecommuters believed that their telecommuting had led to less favourable evaluations from their supervisors. In addition, some noted restricted pay prospects as a pitfall of telecommuting. In some cases, organizations assume that since at-home duties are more limited than in-office duties, telecommuters can receive lower pay. In other cases, telecommuters may not be eligible for bonuses (Hartman et al, 1992). Telecommuters often do not have necessary supplies or equipment; may have too many family interruptions and household distractions; have no separation of work and home life; dislike not having a regular routine; and become workaholics (Lavallee, 1993). Telecommuting is often still thought of as a frivolous work option for part-timers and people who are not serious about their jobs (O’Leary, 1991).

Insurance carriers may assess additional insurance fees depending on the location of the teleworker and their equipment (Wilkes et al, 1994). Other costs companies might expect are additional training, computer and telecommunications (new installations, upgrades, maintenance, and support), and more rigorous performance evaluation.

In some cases the convenience of telecommuting is offset by increased employee stress because people working at home against performance measures would be more likely to devote more than the normal eight hours daily to their jobs (Foegen, 1993). Telecommuters should be compensated at the same level as their counterparts in the organization who work only at the office but some companies have used their telecommuting programmes as a way to lower labour costs by drastically cutting the benefits and wages of telecommuters. Claims of worker exploitation have prompted lawsuits and the unionisation of some telecommuting workers. The United States courts and the IRS have determined that compensation discrimination based on where the employee works is not permissible (Humble et al, 1995). Some employees reported that they dislike the setup of working out of their home, feel demoralised by their company’s insistence that they do it, and balk at the extra expense of setting up a space (Adams, 1995). Companies have difficulty measuring employees’ daily productivity (Connelly, 1995; Mogelonsky, 1995), and measurement becomes even more difficult when employees work at home a varying number of days per week and the manager has to separate in-office productivity gains from telecommuting improvements (Korzeniowski, 1995).

The Occupational Safety and Health’s mandate for safe working conditions applies to all worksites, including employees’ homes and telecommuting centres. Thus, a company is well advised to investigate a telecommuting site for potential hazards and eliminate them, even though a company has little control over the day-to-day safety of a home office (Fitzgerald, 1994). There are many questions to be answered before embarking on telecommuting programmes, i.e., how to handle Workman’s Compensation claims; who pays if the telecommuter is hurt while working at home? Who pays if the computer is stolen from the employee’s home? How can a computer problem be addressed if the employee does not know how to fix it? Who pays for the telephone costs? (Wilkes et al, 1994; Derfler & Greenfield, 1995; Greenwald, 1995). There is strong consensus among managers in companies with telecommuting programmes that it is useful to draft an agreement or contract outlining the responsibilities and expectations of the parties involved and addressing such issues. The agreement might well include an ‘escape clause’ that allows either party to return to office work if telecommuting is not working (Alvi & McIntyre, 1993). Encountering these problems might greatly detract from the expected benefits and favourable impact of telecommuting on company performance. Thus, the final hypothesis:

H6: Implementation problems are inversely related to telecommuting success.

## Dependent variables (alternative measures for telecommuting success)

Benefits derived

This study focuses on telecommuting benefits from a company perspective, including direct benefits to individual employees that are ultimately a benefit to their employers. The societal benefits from telecommuting, such as reduction of air pollution, traffic problems, road wear and tear, etc, are not addressed here.

Telecommuting reduces required office space, company energy consumption, janitorial services, employee sick time, parking facilities, and reduces employee turnover (Kirkley, 1994; Smith, 1994; Wilkes et al, 1994; Noble, 1995). Telecommuting increases productivity due to the increased flexibility of working at home; focuses on results rather than presence at the office; offers fewer distractions at home; and increases use of time to work during the time one would normally be travelling (Dunkin & Baig, 1995; Jones, 1995; Noble, 1995; Penn, 1995). Some surveys telecommuters report that their biggest reason for working at home is that they can get more work done (Humble et al, 1995; Derfler & Greenfield, 1995). A study investigated the impact of telework programmes on teleworkers and their organizations). Teleworker productivity was consistently higher than non-teleworker productivity (Wilkes et al, 1994). The average office employee works 50 hours a week including travel, but only 20 hours are productive. Home workers do 45 hours, but 40 of these are productive (Barber, 1995). Telecommuting initiatives report productivity increases between 20% and 40% (Alvi & McIntyre, 1993; Kirkley, 1994; Weiss, 1994; Barthel, 1995). Avoiding long daily commutes, workers increase the amount of time during which they can be productive and decrease the amount of stress associated with their work lives (Adams, 1995; Barthel, 1995; Chae, 1995; Chaudron, 1995; Mogelonsky, 1995). While a person may not feel well enough to come in to the office for a full day, he or she may feel well enough to telework from his or her home for a few hours or, in some cases, for the entire day (Wilkes et al, 1994). Far fewer sick days are reported from telecommuters. Many workers don’t ‘peak’ until late afternoon, so the standard 9-to-5 routine sacrifices considerable energy (Weiss, 1994).

To avoid wasting employee time during natural disasters, some companies in California have used telecommuting extensively due to the roadway damage of earthquakes (Sullivan, 1995). Teleworking can be an integral part of a company’s disaster recovery programme (Meall, 1993). Work time that would otherwise be lost due to ice storms, earthquakes, floods, and other problems of this nature could be minimised (Wilkes et al, 1994). High costs for benefits, taxes, and insurance have caused a number of US and European companies to consider telecommuting programmes. By classifying their telecommuting engineers and other professionals as independent contractors, rather than as company employees, organizations can save about 30% to 40% of labour costs normally spent on benefits, taxes, and insurance. Telecommuting has been used for affirmative action goals, as it facilitates employment for disabled workers and can aid women going back to work following maternity leave (Humble et al, 1995).

Telecommuting can provide many benefits to employees, thus increasing their motivation to work for the company. Benefits as a reduction in car insurance rates and savings on clothing, gasoline, and dry-cleaning costs are common (Caudron, 1992). Telework has been cited as a way to retain skilled employees or experienced staff, as a way of recruiting staff under conditions of labour shortage, and as a way to overcome shortages of office space (Haddon & Lewis, 1994). Companies have found that job satisfaction increases markedly (Weiss, 1994). The flexibility of the telework programme reduced employee turnover (Chaudron, 1995; Duffy, 1995). Recruitment and retention of people whom you might not otherwise be able to recruit and retain is a big advantage in telecommuting. It is also a very effective way to accommodate employees with health problems and those recovering from disabilities (Greenwald, 1995). Specific cases were identified where teleworkers would have left the employ of the company if not for the telework programme. Absenteeism was reduced and teleworkers reported working from home on days that they were ill and would have called in sick if they were not teleworking (Wilkes et al, 1994; Greengard, 1995).

Many employers are beginning to realise that they cannot afford not to offer flexible work arrangements. The cost associated with replacing an experienced, valuable employee is estimated at 150% of the employee’s salary, according to a major pharmaceutical company (Sullivan, 1995). Telecommuting can be used as a strategy to attract, motivate, and retain key talent and the best employees (Baig, 1995), and might offer relief to employees struggling to balance budgets by reducing the costs of transportation, parking, food, and even clothing (Alvi & McIntyre, 1993). Telecommuters report more time with the family, more leisure time, lower transportation and clothing costs, and lower stress levels (Humble et al, 1995). It increases the territories from which organizations can recruit, creating a larger pool that can contain a larger number of top candidates (Chae, 1995).

## Organization performance

Many top managers have realised that telecommuting can help improve organization performance in many ways and be much more than a convenient way for employees to do their jobs. For example, telecommuting can provide many benefits to employees, thus increasing their motivation to work for the company (Caudron, 1992). As noted above it has been used as a way to recruit and retain skilled employees (Haddon & Lewis, 1994; Wilkes et al, 1994; Duffy, 1995; Chaudron, 1995; Greengard, 1995; Chae, 1995; Baig, 1995). Further, a positive impact on company performance can be derived from substantial increases in employee productivity associated with telecommuting (Derfler & Greenfield, 1995; Dunkin & Baig, 1995; Humble et al, 1995; Jones, 1995; Noble, 1995; Penn, 1995). These productivity improvements are derived in a wide variety of ways, including more time spent with customers, fewer distractions at work, focus on results rather than presence at the office, and improved employee support as required by the telecommuting programme. As mentioned earlier under the ‘benefits derived’ from telecommuting, teleworker productivity (defined in different ways by different authors) was found to be consistently higher than for non-teleworkers. On average, telecommuting initiatives report productivity increases between 20% and 40% (Alvi & McIntyre, 1993; Kirkley, 1994; Weiss, 1994; Barthel, 1995).

## Methodology

## Questionnaire construction and validation

The questionnaire used was developed based on an extensive survey of the literature. It was tested for readability, and content relevancy and completeness in relation to the study objectives. This testing was conducted through reviews by five telecommuting programme managers.

## Sampling procedure

This field test used three short questionnaires to collect matched data from the internal auditor director (IA), the telecommuting programme manager (TPM), and from fifty randomly selected telecommuting employees (nonsupervisory) from each company. The IA and TPM from twenty-nine companies known to the authors as having a well established (at least two years in operation) telecommuting programme were invited to participate in the study. The questionnaires from twenty-three IAs and twenty-two TPMs who accepted the invitation produced eighteen matched pairs. For the eighteen companies with these matched questionnaires, fifty telecommuters were randomly selected and asked to voluntarily participate in the study. Of the 900 questionnaires mailed out, 321 were returned in time for inclusion in data analysis. five telecommuter questionnaires were not used due to missing data. The remaining 316 usable questionnaires provide a response rate of 35%, which is acceptable for studies of this type (King & Teo, 1996).

## Sample demographics

The companies represented in the sample range widely in terms of gross revenues, with three companies having \$600 million or less, six having between \$600 million and \$1 billion, and the other nine having above \$1 billion. The telecommuting programmes in the sample have been operational for an average of 6.5 years, with a minimum of 2.5 years and a maximum of 13 years. The formal education level for the employees are approximately 28% high school, 45% bachelor’s degree, the rest graduate degree or higher. Of the 316 respondents, the majority (58%) went to the office once a week on the average, some went twice a week on average (29%), and some less than once a week (13%).

## Construct measurement

All questions, unless otherwise indicated, were scored using a five-point scale where 1 represented ‘not at all’, 2 ‘to a minor extent’, 3 ‘to a moderate extent’, 4 ‘to a major extent’, and 5 ‘to a great extent’.

## Data analysis

The average and standard deviations for each item in the questionnaire were computed. Factor analyses for the items in each main variable were conducted as the basis for their validation and as a prerequisite for assessing their internal reliability through Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. To test the proposed hypotheses, Pearson’s correlation coefficients and multivariate regression analyses were computed.

## Construct validity

Several precautions were taken to ensure the validity of the measures used. Many of the recommendations by Carmines and Zeller (1979) were followed. To ensure content validity, a thorough survey of the relevant literature was undertaken to understand the important aspects of each major variable and its components, and not neglect important dimensions of any variable. To further reduce the possibility of any non-random error, the main source of invalidity (Carmines & Zeller, 1979, p 15), a group of five practitioners with extensive experience about telecommuting programmes reviewed the questionnaire for validity (measuring the phenomena intended), completeness (including all relevant items), and readability (making it unlikely that subjects will misinterpret a particular question). Some questions were reworded to improve readability; otherwise, the items composing each major variable remained as derived from the literature.

As proposed by Carmines and Zeller (1979), “construct validation focuses on the extent to which a measure performs in accordance with theoretical expectations” (p 27). To ensure construct validity, the theoretical relationships between the constructs should have been previously established, and these relationships hopefully have been empirically supported by different studies over time. As discussed earlier, the theoretical underpinnings of this study are relatively well established, with most of the items in each construct having been addressed before by several authors. However, the psychometric qualities of many of the measures for the main variables were tested here for the first time.

The items comprising each major variable were subjected to a principal component analysis followed by a varimax (orthogonal) rotation to identify composite factors. To be included in a given factor the item is expected to load unambiguously (i.e., with one loading of 0.5 and no other loadings greater than 0.4—criteria suggested by Magal et al, 1998). As suggested by several researchers (i.e. Nunnally, 1978), the minimum eigenvalue for which a factor is to be retained was specified as 1.0. This procedure produced single factor solutions for four of the eight main variables. The exceptions were supervisor characteristics, worker characteristics, benefits derived, and problems encountered. Supervisor characteristics broke down into two main factors: one named managerial characteristics, which includes items 2, 5, 6, 7, and 9; the other named personal characteristics, which includes items 1, 3, 4, and 8. Similarly, employee characteristics broke down into two factors: personal characteristics (items 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 11, and 12), and professional characteristics (items 6, 7, 8, 9, 13, and 14). Derived benefits broke down into three sub-groups: productivity items (1, 2, and 3), employee recruitment (items 4 and 6), and employee motivation (items 7, 8, 9, and 10). Item 11 did not properly load into any subgroup, but was kept as part of the main variable for further analysis. Item 5 also loaded above 0.4 in other subgroups and was extremely correlated with items 7 and 8 (Pearson’s coefficient of 0.89 and 0.81, respectively). Thus, it was dropped from further analyses. The problems broke down into two main groups: operational issues (items 2, 3, 4, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, and 16), and employee isolation (items 5, 6, 7, 8, and 10). Items 1 and 9 did not load properly, but were kept as part of the main variable for further analysis. Second order factor analyses on the extracted factors indicate that they can be combined into main single factors corresponding to the main variables in the model tested in this study. Given the stated objective of this study, further analyses were focused on the combined factors.

## Construct reliability

Since many of the measures used are new, it was deemed important to re-test their reliability. Carmines and Zeller (1979) identified four basic methods to assess a measure’s reliability (re-test, alternative-form, splithalves, and the internal consistency methods) and discussed their strengths and limitations. The main advantage of the internal consistency method is that it requires a single test, in lieu of splitting or repeating of items. ‘By far the most popular of these reliability estimates is given by Cronbach’s alpha’ (p 44) which ‘in most situations provides a conservative estimate of a measure’s reliability (p 45). The authors go on to say ‘that although more complex computationally, alpha has the same logical status as coefficients arising from the other methods of assessing reliability’.

Several authors have proposed different acceptable levels of reliability coefficients. For example, Nunnally (1978) suggested a coefficient of 0.50 or higher and Treacy (1985) proposed that a value of 0.7 or higher would suffice. Srinivasan (1985) and Magal et al (1988) contended that when using a not validated data gathering ins in exploratory research, a reliability coefficient of 0.5 or higher is acceptable. Van de Ven and Ferry (1979) posited that in this type of research even a value of 0.4 or higher will be sufficient. In our case, the reliability coefficients of all the factors were higher than 0.70.

## Results

## Regarding supervisor characteristics

Table 1 shows that on the average the weakest supervisor characteristics are a lack of regular feedback to telecommuters and a lack of trust on the employee’s ability to telecommute, thus suggesting a need for improvement in these two areas according to telecommuting employee opinion. On average, the strongest supervisor characteristics were a results oriented perspective and flexibility in dealing with issues affecting the telecommuter. The relatively large standard deviations indicate that there is considerable difference of opinion among telecommuters about their supervisors’ characteristics.

## Regarding worker characteristics

Table 2 indicates that on the average telecommuters have a positive attitude toward telecommuting and receive good support for the telecommuting arrangement from their supervisors, according to telecommuting programme managers. On the other hand, the latter on the average think telecommuters are conscientious about work time and work well with other people slightly less than ‘to a moderate extent’.

## Regarding work environment characteristic

One of the insights from Table 3 is that, on the average, the telecommuting environments have a little more than a moderate amount of distractions. Based on the relatively large standard deviation, the issue of telecommuters suffering from a lack of presence in the office seems to vary widely from company to company, even though on the average the rating by programme directors exists only moderately.

## Regarding task characteristics

Based on the ratings in Table 4, the relatively low average and standard deviation indicate that for most telecommuters there is little need for access to equipment, materials, or services available only at central offices, On the other hand, contrary to what has been recommended in the literature, based on the relatively large standard deviation one can say that there are considerable inter-company differences as to the extent to which telecommuting objectives and expectations are clearly defined.

## Regarding management support

Table 5 indicates that on the average, managers seem to be providing less than ‘moderate’ encouragement for the use of telecommuting and showing interest in having employees satisfied with the telecommuting programme. The relatively large standard deviations show that management support along each item varies considerably from company to company.

## Regarding benefits derived

Among the main benefits from telecommuting, Table 6 shows reduced employee expenses, reduced company space needs, and increased employee job satisfaction. However, the relatively large standard deviations indicate considerable inter-company differences in terms of employee job satisfaction.

## Regarding implementation problems

Table 7 indicates that on the average the implementation problems occurring to the greatest extent are the required adjustment in philosophy and procedures, employee isolation from peers, and the reduction in support services to telecommuters. The problems occurring to the least extent are worker tele-exploitation, increases in employee at-home costs, and increases in the company operating costs due to telecommuting.

## Impact on company performance

The impact of telecommuting on company performance shown in Table 8 is on average somewhat disappointing, with less than a moderate positive impact on all items except on company cost reduction programmes. This suggests that on the average companies are not using telecommuting as a strategic tool or are unsuccessful in doing so. However, the relatively large standard deviations for some items indicate large inter-company differences and that some companies are getting considerable positive effects in these areas.

The means, standard deviations, and the matrix of intercorrelations among the major study variables are presented in Table 9. H1: Supervisor characteristics are positively related to telecommuting success.

H3: Work environment characteristics are positively related to telecommuting success.

## Conclusions and recommendations

The primary objective of this study to identify and empirically test the determinants of telecommuting success proposed in the literature has been met. The results corroborated, at least partly, all six hypotheses tested, and developed and tested a multivariate regression model which explains significant proportions of the variables representing telecommuting success; derived benefits, and impact on company performance. This study organised and empirically confirmed much of the scattered recommendations for successfully implementing telecommuting in organizations. In summary, before embarking in a corporate telecommuting programme, top managers must carefully consider a variety of important issues.

## Management approach

The importance of management support has been corroborated in this study. To insure that, the many potential benefits of telecommuting identified in this study must be made apparent to managers. A strong understanding of the potential benefits, and also of the many implementation problems discussed here, may help managers understand the need for changes in managerial philosophy and practice to reduce the problems and increase the likelihood of deriving the promised benefits. As found in this study, telecommuting success requires substantial changes from traditional management approaches: managers and telecommuters need to trust each other; telecommuter performance is to be assessed against clearly set objectives, and results, not by mere presence in the office. Managers will be forced to lead more and supervise less. The manager’s job will be to energise, motivate, and empower employees to do their work more productively.

## Planning objectives and performance measurement

Telecommuting requires careful planning and preparation. It should not be done quickly because it requires a lot of changes in corporate thinking and procedures, which in turn, will have major implications for the company and its employees. Before embarking on a major programme a pilot is recommended to iron out the problems, ensure benefits, and minimise unnecessary company turmoil. Early in the planning process, employee unions should be consulted as to their general feelings and guidelines for telecommuting to avoid unpleasant surprises later on.

Due to a more remote supervision approach, performance standards and continuous evaluation is important. Determining the primary objectives and how the company will benefit from the telecommuting arrangement are the most valid basis for defining specific telecommuter performance measures. Managers should expect a regular activity status report on the telecommuter’s progress. It also might be useful to visit the telecommuter’s home office. The planning process needs to consider a wide variety of issues important to the programme success: i.e., what kind of equipment is needed? Who will pay for it? Who is responsible for maintaining it? Are the necessary security measures available to protect the necessary electronic communication? Can we ensure the safety of workers at home, and will they have adequate resources needed such as furniture, lighting, and electrical outlets?

## Selecting employees, tasks, and supervisors

The results from this study clearly indicate the importance of carefully selecting the prospective telecommuters, the tasks they will perform, and the supervisors in charge. It behoves telecommuting programme managers to use the list of important characteristics developed in this study to ensure appropriate selection.

## Policy setting

The complexity of the process required to successfully implement a large telecommuting programme requires considerable attention to formalisation of its rules and procedures. A company’s telecommuting policy should include the rules used for employee selection, cost absorption of specific items, and personnel procedures that ensure fair treatment of telecommuters. The availability of a written policy can be very useful in explaining why some employees were selected for telecommuting and others were not. For each telecommuter or telecommuter group, it may be useful to draw up an agreement that outlines employee work goals and a timetable for achieving them. The agreement has to specify the length of the telecommuting arrangement; the employee’s work schedule at home; the means of communication to be used with the home office (phone, fax, etc); who will pay for the equipment, services, and supplies used; specify requirements to attend meetings at the office or other on-site events; and the conditions for promotion. Whenever possible, telecommuters should receive the same pay and promotion opportunities as other employees so they remain motivated.

To address all of the relevant issues in teleworking, a committee composed of key individuals from across the organization can be formed. Departments such as Human Resources, Legal, Corporate Training, Facilities Management, and Corporate Security can be helpful members in such committees, in addition to the departments of the proposed teleworkers. Additionally, outsiders can be quite useful in bringing a more unbiased and fresh perspective. The potentially important issues are many and managers are encouraged to consider the following: retaining standard managerial practices whenever possible, the same job titles and responsibilities, and treating data security as in the office; determining how frequently telecommuters are to check in to the office; encouraging telecommuters to develop routines and maintain regularly scheduled work hours; and making sure they can manage dependent care and personal obligations.

## Establishing the necessary telework environment

The results from this study suggested that the benefits from telework are directly related to the characteristics of the telework environment. Also, implementation problems were found to be inversely related to it. Thus, it is important that companies provide the necessary infrastructure. An important component of the telework environment is the technology available to support the process. It has to be compatible, as well as comparable, to what is being used in the office so that the telecommuter can be ‘in touch’. Thus, it is important to use teleconferencing technologies to bring remote workers closer to the office. To that effect, during the planning stage a complete list of needed equipment, services, and supplies is to be developed. The areas of weakness and their implications to employee productivity need to be carefully considered before embarking in a major telecommuting programme.

The issue of telecommuter isolation needs to be carefully considered during the programme planning process. Several measures can be taken to reduce this problem: providing meeting schedules in advance; being present at key office events (social, athletic, or special occasions); having office memos mailed to home address; installing an answering machine so as not to miss phone calls; keeping the supervisor personally informed about work progress; notifying telecommuters of urgent meetings and expectations, such as specific days they are expected to work in the office; sharing information and assigning projects to keep them involved with other employees; limiting telecommuting to fewer than five days a week thus forcing personal interaction with co-workers on a weekly basis; and most important, letting telecommuters know how often management expects to communicate with them and through what means.

## Future research

While this study strived to provide a relatively rigorous test for the determinants of telecommuting programme success, it does have numerous limitations that should be construed as opportunities for future research. It is based on the experience from telecommuting programme of 18 companies representing a convenience sample. Also, the statistical analyses used here repeated the company data for each of its telecommuting employees and failed to recognise the possible effect of using instead a larger number of companies with data on one telecommuter per company or the average for the telecommuters in each company. Thus, it might be useful to retest the determinants of programme success with a larger and random sample. As mentioned earlier, the component items for each construct’s measure were derived from an extensive survey of the literature and were tested for face validity by a group of telecommuting programme managers. Over time, however, the nature of such programme may change, as well as their potential benefits, implementation problems, and success factors. Thus, the measures used here may require updating and validation in the future. Last, telecommuting is conceptually a very complex phenomenon, with many different facets and perspectives. It is presently far from well understood and continues to change in terms of the relative importance of the relevant constructs. Future research must consider the likelihood that the present model requires revision at the conceptual level as organizations continue to experiment with new technologies and new approaches to telecommuting and their experience becomes publicly available.
