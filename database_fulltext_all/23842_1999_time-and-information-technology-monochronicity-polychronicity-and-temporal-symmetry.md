# Time and information technology: monochronicity, polychronicity and temporal symmetry

H Lee

Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge UB8 3PH, UK

This paper investigates how information technology affects temporal aspects of organizational work beyond speeding up business processes. It has developed the dimensions of temporality of business processes and applied them to describe and analyse temporal changes of export-related work in trading companies using EDI. In the departments studied, information technology transformed temporal profiles of work and also created a temporal symmetry between work groups interacting with each other. This result is compared with a previous study (Barley, 1988. In Making Time, Temple University Press). This temporal transformation of work by information technology has rarely been explored in information systems research as well as organizational studies. At this stage of research, it is difficult to decide which temporality, monochronicity or polychronicity, information technology facilitates, or whether information technology will always create temporal symmetry. More research on the relationship between time and information technology is required. Whatever temporal consequences of information technology are supported by future research, it is believed that this temporal consideration has value in information systems development and implementation. By taking into account polychronicity, monochronicity and temporal symmetry, we will be able to develop information systems which better fit users’ temporal behaviour.

## Introduction

It is generally accepted that information technology, when implemented successfully, speeds up business processes at an enormous rate and saves the adopting organizations a huge amount of time. In spite of its significance in temporality, research on temporal impacts of information technology is limited in scope and superficial in depth in both management/organizational studies and in information systems research. There are some studies on cycle time reduction in relation to just-in-time (Sakakibara et al, 1997), time-based competition (Stalk, 1988; Stalk & Hout, 1990), fast-cycle (Bower & Hout, 1988) and time compression (Gregory & Rawling, 1997). In business process reengineering, Davenport (1993; Davenport & Short, 1990) also emphasised the sequential capability of information technology in that ‘IT can enable changes in the sequence of tasks in a process, often allowing multiple tasks to be worked on simultaneously’ (1990, p 17). However, they take it for granted that these temporal effects of speeding-up, saving time and reducing cycle time come from information technology, and tend to suppose that they do not require any enquiry. In brief, efforts to explain temporal changes in relation to information technology lack in the current research.

The purpose of this paper is to investigate how information technology affects or transforms temporality in organizational work or business processes. The following section starts with relevant studies in the literature. Among them, Barley’s work (1988) is important in that he first addressed the question empirically in direct relation to computers. Describing findings of the case study on which this paper is based, the following sections compare the results from both studies, which leads to a concept of temporal symmetry. The final section discusses limitations of the study and its implications for information systems research.

## Temporal cultures and information technology

## Monochronicity and polychronicity

Hall (1959, 1966, 1983), an anthropologist, provides a point of departure for the study of temporal aspects in organizations. He distinguishes between monochronic and polychronic cultures, which are the two different ways in which societies organise time in their everyday life, especially in working life. In the former, people do one thing at a time while in the latter several things are done at once. For the nature of the two cultures, he emphasises that they are arbitrary and learned. As for the monochronic time in particular, he says;

Because it is so thoroughly learned and so thoroughly integrated into our culture, it is treated as though it were the only natural and logical way of organising life. Yet, it is not inherent in man’s biological rhythms or his creative drives, nor is it existential in nature (Hall, 1983, p 45).

It is simply one aspect of socialisation. At the same time he argues that “without schedules or something similar to the M-time (monochronic) system, it is doubtful that our industrial civilization could have developed as it has”. This corresponds to Mumford’s (1934) and Thompson’s (1967) arguments on clocks and the development of the modern industrial age. According to Mumford, the mechanical clock, not the steam-engine, is the key machine of the industrial age. Thompson (1967) also showed that the shift in time sense caused by the mechanical clock affected labour disciplines, and further contributed to the shaping of the industrial capitalism, and conversely that new capitalist manufacturing systems made use of the clock and clock time to discipline workers and internalise these work disciplines. In this process, the ‘task-orientation’ of time organization in which work proceeded to ‘natural rhythms (think of labour from dawn to dusk in a farming community) gave way to ‘labour timed by the clock’ (pp 59–60). An important implication of Thompson’s argument for this paper is that capitalist work disciplines or ways of working were made possible by the clock. Then we can make an analogous point that computers in these days are changing our way of working, especially in terms of temporality. This is the starting point of this research.

Schein (1992) applied the distinction to organizational settings at a conceptual level. Because monochronic time is more efficient to control and co-ordinate human behaviour, he suggests, it is well suited to the management of large systems. It is taken for granted in most organizations as the only way to get things done efficiently. However, polychronic time is more effective to build relationships and to solve complex problems. It is therefore considered more suitable for the early stages of an organization, for smaller systems and for organizations where one gifted person is the central point of co-ordination.

This application to organizational settings is a sensible one, but Schein’s matching between the two temporal cultures and their suitable organization types has a weakness in that he does not take into account the role of information technology which affects contemporary organizations in a variety of ways. Today information technology is imposing a new dimension which cannot be left out in organizational analysis. In addition, Hall and Schein, an anthropologist and an organization theorist respectively, do not seem to judge which is the better way for organising time. Regardless of their intention, however, their discussions about the two temporal cultures implicitly suggest that monochronic cultures are superior to polychronic cultures in terms of productivity in organizational work. One of the points I am making through this study is that it is not the case when information technology is involved.

## Temporal changes in radiology departments

Barley (1988) first employed the distinction to address empirically how time is affected by computers. He investigated how the introduction of computerised equipment into radiology departments in hospitals altered the temporal order of the departments. In so doing, he compared two radiology departments which used traditional Xrays and computer-based equipment respectively.

Radiologists in the departments of traditional X-rays are often summoned by technicians seeking the assistance of the radiologist on duty as well as by physicians and colleagues requiring his consultation. The flow of a radiologist’s day is fragmented into a large number of discrete events with a relatively short duration, in an unpredictable sequence, with a vaguely estimated temporal location and irregular rate. Accordingly they find it difficult to deal with one event at a time, and prefer ‘multiple lines of simultaneous action’ (p 142); they operate in a polychronic way. The technicians’ work goes on exactly as dictated by a daily schedule of patient appointments; they operate in a monochronic way. The two professional groups live in different worlds of temporality, in other words, in temporal asymmetry (Barley, 1988, pp 126–127; Zerubavel, 1981, p 64). This asymmetry often causes a problem. When a technician needs a radiologist, he often has to search around for the radiologist on duty and, even after he finds the radiologist, he waits because the radiologist could be on the phone, or consulting other doctors, or reading films. Technicians feel frustrated on such occasions and are discontent with their relationship with radiologists. It is when technicians become ‘aware of the potential discrepancy between their structured, linear world and the temporally unpredictable world of the radiologist’ (p 145).

The computer-based equipment such as ultrasound and the CT (computerised topography) scanner has altered the situation. When assigned to CT duty, the radiologists consult more or less exclusively with physicians who ordered CT or ultrasound scanning. By limiting the scope of the radiologists’ responsibilities and by distancing them from other technologies, CT duty has changed the temporal order of radiologists’ work; task duration is lengthened and the rate of task switching is slowed. Along with these changes in the structural aspects, the flow of the radiologists’ daily activities has become closely tied to the CT scanners’ schedule which shapes the temporal organization of experiences of the technicians in charge of the machines. By monitoring the day’s schedule and the progress of the technicians’ work, the radiologists could estimate the sequence, temporal location and rate of recurrence of their primary duties. Now the radiologists on CT duty can operate based on the schedule, that is, in a monochronic way.

Barley concluded that the new computer-based equipment increased the monochronicity of radiologists’ work by restructuring the duration, sequence, temporal location and rate of recurrence of events in their working day. Thereby it created the symmetry of temporal organization between radiologists’ and technicians’ work. His investigation goes beyond these structural aspects of temporality. The increased symmetry contributed to the decreased conflict between radiologists and technicians, and changed the social relationships between them.<sup>1</sup>

## Temporal impacts of KTNET

This section describes part of the case study on which this paper is based. It examines how information systems affect the temporality of work. The case study was conducted in two trading companies which were connected to their partner banks by EDI technology for their export-related transactions. They are involved in a trade automation project of Korea Trade Network (KTNET). Before describing the findings of the study, the research methods employed are presented first.

## Temporal dimensions and research methods

To analyse changes in temporality, a set of concepts (Table 1) have been developed to represent various aspects of temporality. They are concerned with what constitutes temporality, and are based on some previous research on time in social psychology (Schriber, 1986; Schriber & Gutek, 1987) and sociology (Zerubavel, 1981). The six dimensions are used to describe temporal shifts in business processes caused by a new information system.

Data were collected through company documents, interviews and observations. The KTNET has changed export procedures in the two trading companies studied. Because import procedures are in many cases the mirror image of export ones, this paper only deals with export here. Of the export administrative business, four domains to which KTNET had been applied were examined: export letters of credit advice, export licenses, local letters of credit and negotiations. This paper describes export licenses (E/L) in detail.

## E/L procedures in trading companies

After an export contract is completed and its letter of credit is delivered, trading companies need an export licence (E/L) from banks. Two different kinds of teams in the companies are involved with the E/L application: business departments and assisting teams. While business departments deal with main export businesses of their specialised items, assisting teams support administrative activities of business departments. Each assisting team supports several business departments.

## Procedures before KTNET

E/L procedures went before KTNET as in Figure 1.

1. A worker (usually female) in charge of E/L in a business department prepares documents such as a letter of credit (L/C), a copy of the contract, the specification of export items, etc. for E/L application and fills in a form.

2. She enters data into MIS.

3. She carries the set of documents in person to her department’s assisting team.

4. In the assisting team, a worker (usually female) in charge inspects it and gets it signed unless faults are found.

5. After collecting the documents, the worker in the business department puts them in order before submitting to the bank concerned.

6. Then she goes to the bank branch to submit them.

7. Returning to her office, she enters some data in the E/Ls issued into MIS.

In the above process, data entry into MIS (2 and 7) did not always take place at the fixed sequences in the process. When busy, they could be omitted and performed together in batch later after each E/L was issued.

For the worker in the assisting team, it did not take her much time to deal with an individual application.

She was often interrupted by the E/L applications brought to her; she found it difficult to deal with other tasks properly, and also to make a schedule for E/L as well as other tasks. Set times were agreed between the trading company and the bank involved for transmitting E/L applications: 10.30, 13.30 and 15.00. The banks return the E/Ls issued at 12.00, 15.00 and 16.30 respectively. Therefore, business departments have to send applications to the assisting team before the three set times.

In the new system, the E/L worker in the assisting team is able to make a schedule for the day. Before KTNET, she could not do anything else properly because applications for E/L came from several business teams quite irregularly and sporadically throughout the working day, though there was a rule of thumb. Now they come regularly at the fixed times, so she can deal with other jobs while waiting for them to come. “It’s a kind of multi-tasking, which was unthinkable under the old system”, said the IS staff in the company. The E/L worker in the assisting team expressed her contentment with the new system that “It is convenient to set specific times for the work, and therefore to be able to make a schedule for the day. I can prepare for tasks in advance”.

However, the new system has imposed a new temporal restraint on workers in business departments. In the past, they visited the banks concerned when appropriate and necessary, normally twice a day, in the morning and in the afternoon. Presently they transmit electronically E/L applications three times a day, before 10.30, 13.30 and 15.00 to the assisting team via the internal MIS. They do not have to go to banks in person at all, which is a benefit to them. However, they have to meet the three newly set times. They consider them as deadlines which have not existed so far. The workers interviewed in business departments said that for the system to succeed, it should allow E/L applications to be transmitted anytime whenever necessary.

## Procedures after KTNET in Company B

This is realised in Company B where the EDI system is applied in a more advanced form. This company removed assisting teams (called here E/L teams) from E/L procedures. Figure 3 shows the procedures in Company B.

1. A worker in a business department enters E/L EDI data and E/L specification data into the system.

2. She transmits E/L applications via KTNET to the bank concerned, and the bank returns the issued E/Ls via KTNET.

3. She prints out the E/Ls in electronic form into paper form.

Here workers in business departments can transmit E/L applications directly to the bank via EDI whenever they want. There is no deadline to be met. They process E/Ls according to their own schedules or the requirements of specific E/Ls. Corresponding banks check and respond every hour.

## Changes in the six dimensions of temporality

## 1. Duration

Duration refers to the amount of time spent to complete a task or an activity. A task is composed of several unit tasks. For example, as seen above, the E/L application consists of 7 unit tasks<sup>2</sup> or steps before KTNET. To be exact, duration is the sum of time spent to finish each unit task plus time for moving between steps. The exact measurement of time in work is a main job of time (and motion) studies in the Taylorist tradition. Such measurement was not attempted in this research. In fact, it was almost impossible to measure the duration of each unit task and compare between before and after KTNET. Most of the ‘before’ work procedures were reconstructed from documents and workers’ memories. Even in the present work procedures it was difficult to measure a standard (or average) time consumed to process a specific unit task for one order because most work is done in batch rather than dealt with one by one. Furthermore unit tasks (or steps) themselves change in number, order and content so that it is inappropriate to compare their duration between before and after KTNET. In examining ‘duration’ in this study, therefore, only some major steps are included. Another justification is that changes in duration are partly reflected in the dimension of cycle.

In the E/L application, duration is reduced significantly for two reasons. Some documents are not required any more at the beginning. No duplicate data entry is done because data on the issued E/Ls are already entered by the bank, which is one of the major benefits that EDI provide.

## 2. Sequence

Sequence is the order in which activities and tasks take place. A new technology may cause changes in the order in which work is done. As in the duration, however, the comparison of sequences before and after the introduction of a new technology is not easy because it may bring a radically new way of working in which old unit tasks disappear or at least it reduces the number of steps required to complete the whole job.

There has been a little change in sequence in the E/L procedures. Before KTNET, data entry into MIS did not have to be conducted at fixed points (steps 2 and 7) in the process. Because MIS was separated from the process of E/L, it was possible to skip it and to enter data collectively later when less busy. Under the new system, as the E/L procedures are incorporated into the MIS, the whole process begins with the data entry, which cannot be omitted.

## 3. Temporal location

Temporal location means when or at which points of time (of day) activities and tasks take place. Tasks are usually not conducted at random points of time. They tend to be carried out at specific points of time customarily or by some technological restraints. A new system employed by an organization can shift temporal locations of some activities. It has two possibilities, which are quite opposite to each other; the new technology may release some activities from temporal restraints imposed upon them so far, and on the contrary it may impose such a new restraint on them that they are tied up with newly designated specific points of time.

E/L applications came to the assisting team and then to the bank irregularly during the working day. Presently, however, applications in Company A to the bank are made at fixed times and the bank returns the E/Ls issued at the corresponding fixed times. In Company B with an advanced system, applications are made at any time of the day whenever they are necessary.

## 4. Deadline

Deadline is the fixed time by which work should be completed. Contrary to general expectation, deadlines are not universal in all tasks. Although the principle of ‘the faster, the better’ is taken for granted for most tasks, deadline which is formally specified is often not found.

For workers in business departments in Company A, the newly fixed times function as deadlines. Previously such deadlines did not exist because workers in business departments carried their own E/L applications in person to banks whenever it suited them after inspection by the assisting team. They processed E/L according to their own schedule or requirements of the specific E/L. In Company B, in contrast, such deadlines do not exist. Applications can be made at any time in the day whenever necessary.

## 5. Cycle

Cycle refers to the periodic regularity in which work is completed repeatedly. Previously, in both the trading companies, there existed an approximately half a day cycle in processing E/L. In this cycle, workers in charge of E/L visited banks twice a day; applications which were submitted before lunch time were collected in the afternoon whereas those submitted in the afternoon were returned the next morning. In Company A, there are now three cycles a day which begin at 10.30, 13.30 and 15.00 and finish 12.00, 13.00 and 16.30 respectively. Company B has shorter cycles in which applications are sent whenever necessary and the bank responds every hour.

## 6. Rhythm

Rhythm refers to the alternation in the intensity of being busy within a cycle. It can be likened to rhythm or dynamics in music. It is closely connected to cycle and deadline. We are not evenly busy while doing something. Just as there are rhythmic alternations of strong and weak stresses in music, we have alternating turns of busy and less busy periods in all the activities we are doing.

There is no difference in rhythm before and after KTNET. Regardless of shorter cycles, most work takes place in the morning. So most workers in charge of E/L are busier in the morning than in the afternoon. There is also no difference on the weekly basis. Mondays and Fridays used to be, and are still, busier than other weekdays. Table 2 summarises changes in the six dimensions of temporality.

## Temporal profiles on the site of KTNET

In the E/L procedures before KTNET, the worker in the assisting team used to receive applications from several business teams irregularly and sporadically throughout the day. While some business departments passed the applications as they occurred, others did on their own scheduled times. Because E/L applications were considered to have to be processed on the day, the worker in the assisting team dealt with each application immediately as it arrived. Her tasks could come to her at any time and she coped with them in a polychronic way.

The introduction of KTNET has brought about a significant change in the temporal profiles. KTNET has allowed the assisting team to establish the three deadlines by which business departments should submit E/L applications in order to be transmitted to, and returned from banks on the day. Now business departments operate in a monochronic way. Tasks come to the assisting team at predetermined times, and the worker in the team deals with them monochronically. In Company B, the advanced system enables the business departments to work in a polychronic way by allowing them to transmit E/L applications anytime necessary.

## Discussions: monochronicity, polychronicity and temporal symmetry

A comparison between the radiology department case and the KTNET one shows interesting and significant points of discussion on the temporality of work and information technology. Before we go further, the terms of ‘monochronic’ and ‘polychronic’ are refined, and a new notion of ‘domains of temporal behaviour’ is introduced. They are important to understand the following discussions.

In a simple definition presented at the beginning, monochronic means doing one thing at a time while polychronic means doing several things at once. Hall extends this distinction to imply that individuals following the polychronic way of working place less value on temporal order, accept events as they arise and engage in multiple activities simultaneously, whereas people following the monochronic way seek to structure activities and plan for events by allocating specific slots of time to each event’s occurrence (Barley, 1988, p. 158). For Barley, the distinction between monochronic and polychronic mainly means whether an individual worker follows (or sticks to) a predetermined work schedule or not. While technicians followed a daily patient schedule, radiologists did not have such a schedule.

## Two domains of temporal behavior

Furthermore we need to distinguish between the two separate, though closely connected, domains to which discussions about monochronic and polychronic times can be meaningfully applied. The one is the way in which tasks and events occur in a temporal sense. We call it ‘temporal behaviour of events and tasks’. While some events take place in an unexpected temporal way, i.e. irregularly, sporadically, unevenly and not following a fixed schedule, others come in an organised temporal way, i.e. regularly, following the predetermined, or at least predictable, sequence. The former is polychronic, the latter monochronic.

The other domain is how workers organise their time to deal with tasks and events. It is concerned with the way of working and named ‘temporal behaviour of workers’. Some may deal with tasks and events spontaneously as they arise and may perform several things in any order during a given period of time whether they occur regularly or not. This is a polychronic way. Others may deal with events regularly at specified times and conduct one thing at a time, designating some slots of time for specific tasks. This is monochronic. By intersecting the two domains, we have a matrix of temporal behaviour in work environments as in Figure 4, from which the following four possibilities arise.

## Possibility I

Events take place in a monochronic way, that is, regularly, in sequence and at specific times. Workers in charge also operate in a monochronic way; they normally perform one task at a time. If a proper arrangement is made in order to coordinate both temporality, things will go smoothly.

## Possibility II

Events take place in a monochronic way, whereas workers in charge operate in a polychronic way. They can cope with tasks spontaneously as they arise. It is expected that tasks are completed without difficulties.

## Possibility III

Events happen in a polychronic way, namely irregularly and sporadically throughout the day. However, workers in charge operate in a monochronic way. They cannot cope with the tasks properly, which might lead to a bottleneck or a conflict among workers or departments on the work flow. The bottleneck can be alleviated or removed in two ways; whether temporal characteristics of events are transformed so that they take place in a monochronic way or workers are trained so that they can cope with tasks in a polychronic way.

## Possibility IV

Events take place in a polychronic way and workers operate in the same way. They can deal with tasks spontaneously as they arise and perform several tasks at a time. In this case, tasks are expected to be completed properly and timely without a separate coordinate arrangement unless there is too much work loaded on each worker.

Among the four possibilities above, possibility III draws attention because it may produce a conflict which should be sorted out in organizational measures. This was illustrated by Schein (1992, p 114). According to him, polychronically driven work may frustrate the person who is working monochronically, which can lead to a conflict between the groups involved. He takes an example of the interaction between an air traffic controller (polychronic) and the pilot of a single aircraft waiting for landing clearance (monochronic).

This notion of temporal behaviour is also useful when we describe and explain workers’ behaviour in work environments in relation to information and communication technologies. An example comes from an unintended use of a product of computer-mediated communication: voice-mail. One of the main functions of voicemail is to answer phone calls when the recipient is away. However, some people use it even when they are available to answer phone calls. They often ignore telephone rings to continue with their current conversation. Because voice-mail answers the calls, they can reorganise their working hours in their office without being interrupted by calls at unwanted times. In other words, voice-mail opens a possibility of transforming events happening in a polychronic way into ones occurring in a monochronic order.

## Temporal behaviour of workers

3. Temporality of technicians in the radiology departments remained monochronic, whereas that of radiologists changed from polychronic to monochronic.

4. In Company A, the monochronicity became dominant in the temporal behaviour of business departments. On the other hand, the polychronicity became ruling in the same departments of Company B.

5. In Company A, the temporal behaviour of assisting teams shifted from polychronic to monochronic. In Company B, the assisting teams were removed from the process.

## Temporal symmetry between work groups

6. A temporal symmetry was created between the two groups involved—between radiologists and technicians in the radiology departments and between business departments and assisting teams in Company A. Although the symmetries were made in both cases, we need to differentiate them in terms of the social relations involved. In the radiology departments, radiologists are higher in the hierarchy than technicians; they have power to dominate technicians’ time, which was a source of conflict between the two professional groups. In trading companies, such conflict did not exist. The assisting teams supported business departments and in many cases followed business departments’ (temporal) requirements.

7. This temporal symmetry is not applicable in Company B because the assisting teams were removed from the process.

In addition, from the cases we can find two further points that are relevant to the discussion on temporality, work and information technology.

8. As for the direction of change, the shift from polychronicity to monochronicity is the main one in the radiology departments and in Company A. As seen above, for example, the temporal symmetries were made towards monochronicity. On the other hand, in Company B with an advanced system, although the change is not obvious, the general orientation is towards polychronicity. The fact that polychronicity remains in the advanced system indicates that Hall’s and Schein’s assumptions on polychronicity and monochronicity need to be complemented at the advent of information technology in organizations. As opposed to what Barley and Hall assumed, polychronicity can be a more desirable situation which IT can support more effectively than monochronicity.

9. Here another level of temporality which does not appear in Figure 5 is introduced: the level of daily scheduling. Both in the radiology departments and Company A, temporalities of radiologists and assistant teams became monochronic as far as the activities examined (interaction with technicians and E/L tasks respectively) are concerned. However, when we extend our perspective to include the temporal organization of a working day, we see a different level of temporality as described below.

Before KTNET, applications for E/Ls arrived irregularly in the assisting teams, that is, whenever necessary for business departments. They occurred polychronically. The E/L workers in the assisting teams responded to them as they occurred, which is also polychronic. This polychronicity in dealing with E/L tasks meant for the E/L workers that they organised their daily schedule in a monchronic way. In other words, because E/L applications occurred irregularly, the E/L clerks’ work tempo was often interrupted by them, and they were not able to concentrate and deal with other tasks properly. As a result, they were not able to make a schedule for daily activities. During a given period of time, e.g. a working day, what they could perform properly was E/L procedures only. Their organization of work time from the viewpoint of the daily schedule was monochronic. They performed only E/L processing in most working hours.

KTNET has made a difference to this scene. E/L applications no longer come irregularly, but at specified times. KTNET has enabled the assisting teams to fix those times for E/L transmission. Some specific slots of time are allocated exclusively for them in E/L workers work time. It is monochronic. From the viewpoint of the daily schedule, they know the exact times for their major duties, e.g. the E/L processing, and can make a plan for the day. Consequently they can deal with other tasks properly during a given period of time, e.g. a working day. In other words, their organization of daily work time has become polychronic. The E/L worker in the assisting team can now deal with other jobs while waiting until the deadlines come. The major informant responsible for KTNET in the company agreed that KTNET applied to E/L procedures enabled E/L workers to utilise their time in a polychronic way. A similar analysis can be applied to radiologists’ temporality, too.

Finally, another important issue of discussion was raised by one of the anonymous reviewers of this paper. He/she commented that the effect of IT on temporality, despite being claimed to be paramount, seemed secondary in the trading companies. Many of the changes reported in the companies are general work organization changes which could have been achieved without the technology. Response to the criticism is half yes and half no. It is no in that technological changes bring in organizational changes in most cases. This is supported by the socio-technical systems perspective (Trist, 1982). Therefore, it is often difficult, or meaningless, to differentiate technological and organizational initiatives. Technology cannot be separated out ‘causally’ from other factors. IT implementation and organizational changes cannot be separated easily in the sense that they happen simultaneously or the one following the other or the other way around.

On the other hand, it is yes in that what is decisive is the organization’s decision. According to Barley (1988), technological changes ‘occasion’ a reallocation of activities or tasks. He used the verb ‘occasion’ deliberately because ‘although technologies influence the actual nature of work, the allocation of tasks to persons and persons to physical space are human choices made on grounds that are at least partially independent of technology’s dictates’ (p 159). Based on Barley’s view, the reviewer’s criticism is right; some temporal changes in the case can be attributed to organizational changes rather than to technological ones. Even in this case, however, the importance of technological effects on temporality cannot be completely dismissed.

## Conclusions

By the two studies described so far, we have shown that information technology transforms temporalities of work and also creates a temporal symmetry between work groups interacting with each other. This temporal transformation of work by information technology has rarely been explored in organizational studies as well as information systems research. Exactly speaking, it has been taken for granted, rather than considered as an issue which requires scholarly inquiry. To recognise its importance is one of the contributions that this study makes both in information systems research and organizational studies.

Although several results are shared by both the two cases, it may be hasty to generalise them into information technology as a whole. There are several limitations which restrict the extension of the results. First of all, the information technologies examined in the two studies are different in nature. While Barley dealt with computer-based radiological technologies, KTNET is an application of electronic data interchange, a type of interorganizational information system. Although the radiology equipment in Barley’s work is based on computers, it is closer to manufacturing technology than administrative and managerial technology which is more relevant when we refer to information systems. In addition, organizations where the case studies were conducted are fundamentally different; hospitals are characterised by complex composition of various professions, and the composition of trading companies is relatively homogeneous. Different professions—medical doctors and technicians on the one hand, and office workers— may also make a difference. For example, the relationships between doctors and technicians differ from those among office workers, say, in allocation of power. Temporal symmetry between groups may be influenced by power structures in organizations; they decide whose timing predominates and who have to follow it. In the findings of this study, the relationship between power and temporality did not show up (see note 1). It needs to be addressed in further research on time in organizations.

At this stage of research, it is difficult to decide which temporality, monochronicity or polychronicity, information technology facilitates, or whether information technology will always create temporal symmetry among work groups. More research on the relationship of time and information technology is required to generalise or make a contingent theory. Whatever temporal consequences of information technology are supported by future research, this consideration of temporality has value on its own in information systems development and implementation. By taking into account polychronicity, monochronicity and temporal symmetry, we will be able to develop information systems which better fit users’ temporal behaviour.
