# An AHP model of DSS effectiveness

GA Forgionne

Information Systems Department, University of Maryland Baltimore County, Catonsville, MD 21250, USA

Empirical studies have reported equivocal, or even dysfunctional, results from the use of decision support systems (DSS). Recent examples are the Davis, Kottemann, and Remus production planning experiments. According to the researchers, these experiments demonstrate that DSS what-if analysis creates an ‘illusion of control’ that causes users to overestimate its effectiveness. Such experimental findings are contrary to case-supported DSS theory. This paper examines the discrepancy. It first overviews the decision-making process, presents a generic DSS, identifies the theoretical role of the DSS in improving decision making, develops a multiple criteria model of DSS effectiveness, and gives a DSS for delivering the model to users. Illustrating with recent empirical investigations and the Davis, Kottemann, and Remus studies, the DSS-delivered model is used to reconcile the incongruity between the experimental findings and the casesupported theory. The paper concludes with a discussion of the article’s implications for information systems research and practice.

## Introduction

By coupling the intellectual resources of users with the capabilities of the computer, decision support systems (DSS) are expected to improve the quality of decisions. Many case studies support the theory by showing that decision support systems have improved the management of various public and private enterprises (Turban, 1993; Mallach, 1994). Yet, empirical studies have reported equivocal, and sometimes even dysfunctional, effects from using decision support systems in experimental situations (Benbasat & Nault, 1990). These results persist across decision situations and DSS-delivered decision-aiding techniques. Tables 1 and 2 summarise some of the experimental studies.

As Tables 1 and 2 illustrate, most empirical studies have focused on either outcome or process tests. For the dual-test experiments, the outcome measure typically was limited to organizational performance. This varied focus reflects the diversity of research orientations, issues addressed, problems supported, and tools used during the experiments. More importantly, these studies demonstrate that no single DSS evaluation model has been applied consistently across the experiments.

The lack of a comprehensive and uniformly-used DSS evaluation model may account for the incongruity between case-supported DSS theory and experimental findings. This paper addresses this issue. It first overviews the decision-making process, presents a generic DSS, identifies the theoretical role of the DSS in improving decision making, develops a multiple criteria model of DSS effectiveness, and gives a DSS for delivering the model to users. Illustrating with recent empirical investigations and the Davis, Kottemann, and Remus studies, the DSS-delivered model is used to reconcile the incongruity between the experimental findings and the casesupported theory. The paper concludes with a discussion of the article’s implications for information systems research and practice.

## Decision making support

Human decision making involves the four-phase process of intelligence, design, choice, and implementation (Young, 1989; Turban, 1993; Mallach, 1994). During the intelligence phase, the decision maker gains a fundamental understanding of, and acquires the general information needed to address, the organization’s problems or opportunities. In the design phase, the decision maker develops a specific and precise model that can be used to systematically examine the discovered problem or opportunity. This model will consist of decision alternatives, uncontrollable events, criteria, and the symbolic or numerical relationships between these variables. Using the explicit models to logically evaluate the specified alternatives and to generate recommended actions constitute the ensuing choice phase. During the subsequent implementation phase, the decision maker ponders the analyses and recommendations, weighs the consequences, gains sufficient confidence in the decision, and implements a final decision.

This decision-making process is continuous. After the final choice is implemented, the decision maker should observe the new reality and, where appropriate, follow through with intelligence, design, choice, and implementation. While some phases, or some steps within phases, may be performed concurrently, decision making fundamentally is a sequential process. Design will require intelligence. Choice should not precede without design. Implementation follows choice. An outcome from the decision can occur only after the final choice has been implemented.

## Decision support systems

From the pioneering work of Keen, Scott Morton, and Gorry through the present, there have been many definitions of DSS. Tools and applications have also varied widely. The various representations have suggested many ways in which such systems can support decision making (Young, 1989; Turban, 1993; Mallach, 1994). The generic architecture shown in Figure 1 encompasses this variety.

As Figure 1 illustrates, computer hardware and software are used interactively to perform analyses and evaluations with data and models that result in status reports, forecasts, and recommended actions. Applications software deliver accounting, economic, and information science constructs and systems, operations research/management science (OR/MS) models, and statistical methodologies that help users formulate models. The formulated models, augmented by the managers’ insights and judgments, are used to evaluate alternatives in a systematic and analytic fashion and to recommend alternatives (Fripp, 1985). These evaluations typically involve: (a) simulating performance outcomes from stipulated actions and policies under specified internal and external conditions, and (b) solving specified models for the most preferable actions and policies. The results, which can be provided in vivid detail with tables, graphs, and other supporting material, are used to explain, justify, and communicate the decisions during implementation.

Feedback from the processing provides additional data and models that may be useful for future decision making. Output feedback (which can include outcomes, cognitive information, task models, and what-if, goal-seeking, and other types of sensitivity analyses) is used to extend or modify the original analyses and evaluations (Young, 1989; Songupta & Abdel-Hamid, 1993; Turban, 1993; Mallach, 1994). These interactive feedback loops make it relatively easy for management to support the decision-making process in a continuous and dynamic manner. Along with the original analyses and evaluations, the feedback loops also increase the users’ confidence in the recommendations and enable the decision maker to better explain, justify, and communicate the decisions during implementation (Liang, 1986; Todd & Benbasat, 1990; Sengupta & Abdel-Hamid, 1993).

## DSS effectiveness

When implemented successfully, DSS can improve the outcomes from, and the process of decision making. Outcome improvements from using the DSS include (Liang, 1986; Benbasat & Nault, 1990; Adelman, 1992; Udo, 1992; Turban, 1993):

(a) gains in organization performance, such as increases in returns, reductions in costs, or boosts in information flows; and

(b) the maturation of the user as a decision maker, as would occur when there is progress in the person’s understanding of the current problem and solution or

a gain in the person’s general problem-solving skills. In some decision situations, competition, external constraints, internal restrictions, and other factors may make it impossible to improve organizational performance. Under these circumstances, the learning, training, and other beneficial side effects involved in user maturation will be the main outcome improvements from the use of the decision support system (Fripp, 1985; Young, 1989; Tan & Benbasat, 1993; Turban, 1993; Mallach, 1994).

Process improvements involve enhancements in the users’ ability to perform the phases and steps of decision making. Besides outright gains in phase and step proficiency, these process enhancements may be manifested, among other ways, through (Young, 1989; Sethi & King, 1991; Adelman, 1992; DeLone & McLean, 1992; Tan & Benbasat, 1993; Turban, 1993; West & Courtney, 1993; Hochman et al, 1994; Mallach, 1994):

(a) a hike in personal efficiency, such as reducing the time needed by the user to structure the problem or increasing the number of alternatives evaluated by the user in a given time period; and

(b) an improvement in personal productivity, such as reducing the time needed for decision making or increasing the amount of pertinent information, knowledge, and wisdom from decision making. knowledge, and wisdom from decision making.

In any particular management situation, support for some phases may be more important than support for others. A given DSS should improve the process for at least the relevant phases of decision making.

Outcome and process are interrelated. Enhanced outcomes will be the result of improvements in the process of decision making. Indeed, outcome enhancements cannot be reliably credited to DSS usage without explaining how the system improved the decision-making process. Assessing DSS effectiveness, then, will be a multiple criteria problem that should include valid process, as well as outcome, measures.

## Decision value

Some assessment measures (such as organization performance and personal efficiency) can be expressed in absolute numerical terms, while others are subjective in nature (such as decision maker maturity and phase proficiency). The measures also can conflict. For example, an increase in decision maker maturity may come at the expense of personal efficiency.

Under these circumstances, a multiple criteria decision making (MCDM) methodology will be useful in arriving at a composite measure of a DSS’s decision value. One of the most popular MCDM methodologies is the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) (Saaty, 1994). In AHP, the problem first is separated into a hierarchy of interrelated decision elements. The overall decision criterion is placed at the top, variables in the middle, and alternatives at the bottom of the hierarchy. Next, elements (alternatives or variables) at one level are compared pairwise for their relative importance in attaining the elements (variables or decision criterion) at the next higher level. These data are used to estimate the relative weights of the variables. Then, the weights are aggregated into a set of ratings for the decision alternatives.

Figure 2 gives the decision hierarchy for the problem of assessing DSS effectiveness. As this figure illustrates, the overall criterion, composite decision value, is determined by the process support offered by, and the outcome from using, the system. Outcome, in turn, is set by organization performance and decision maker maturity, and process support is prescribed by phase and step proficiency, personal efficiency, and personal productivity. There may be several measures that fall within these general categories. The alternatives will be the list of provided support tools (none, DSS, or other).

Figure 2 DSS effectiveness decision hierarchy.

Equal or different weights can be assigned to each assessment variable. Variables not included in the analysis would receive weights of zero. Similarly, each support tool can receive an equal or disparate weight in the overall evaluation. In a completely impartial scheme, each variable would receive an equal weight, and each tool would have the same weight. By aggregating the estimated weights through the hierarchy, the evaluator will obtain an overall rating for each provided support tool. The overall criterion ratings then provide a basis for identifying decision value from the provided support tool. Highly effective tools will receive the largest decision values.

## Evaluation DSS

A DSS can deliver the AHP assessment model to the decision maker in an effective and completely transparent manner. Figure 3 presents a conceptual architecture for this evaluation DSS.

## Inputs

As Figure 3 demonstrates, the evaluation DSS has two major inputs. There is a database and a model-base.

## Database

The database contains the outcome and process measures relevant for the decision situation and user-specified weights for the variables and support tools. Outcome measures will include pertinent organization performance (for example, return, cost, or net income) and decision maker maturity (such as the effort expended on, and capability in, decision making and problem learning) assessments. Process measures can include relevant personal efficiency and productivity appraisals, expert or self ratings on the users’ ability to perform the phases and steps of decision making, or combinations of these gauges.

## Model-base

The model-base contains the decision hierarchy as set out in Figure 2 and the AHP methodology. There will also be a methodology for assigning the variable values and user-specified weights to the decision hierarchy. Such an assignment will, in effect, make the AHP model operational for the particular decision situation.

Figure 3 Evaluation DSS.

Decision maker and computer technology

Decision makers (or their staff) typically will be performing the DSS effectiveness analysis. Since these people may have weak computer programming skills, the required computer technology should be readily implemented by non-technical persons with a minimum of training.

To meet user requirements, the evaluation DSS can be made available through easy-to-use, ready-to-run computer programmes that execute on a microcomputer available to decision makers (and their staffs). Several software options are available, including the use of ‘canned’ AHP packages (such as EXPERT CHOICE) or a spreadsheet with an AHP template. The configuration of software can be made available through a graphic user interface running under a windowed operating system.

## Processing

Users would initiate the processing by pointing and clicking with the computer’s mouse on a screen-displayed push-button that calls the generic decision hierarchy. The evaluation DSS would respond by performing three distinct processing tasks in sequence—data organization, model structuring, and simulation. Execution would be realised in a completely interactive manner that makes the processing relatively transparent to the user.

## Data organization

After the decision hierarchy has been called, the DSS would automatically access the database. Outcome (organization performance and decision maker maturity) and process (phase and step ratings and personal efficiency and productivity) statistics then would be organised in the form needed for the AHP methodology.

## Model structuring

The AHP model and decision hierarchy would be stored as programme files within the DSS’s model-base. After the relevant outcome and process statistics have been organised, the DSS would automatically retrieve the appropriate programme files from the model-base. The DSS then would use the data and pertinent value and weight assignment methodologies to operationalise (attach parameters to) the generic decision hierarchy model.

## Simulation

The DSS would use the operational model to automatically perform the AHP computations needed to simulate DSS effectiveness. Results would then be displayed on preprogrammed forms desired by the decision maker.

## Outputs

Two major evaluation-specific outputs would be produced through DSS processing. There would be DSS effectiveness forecasts and reports, synthesised into a main document that enumerates: (a) outcome projections decomposed into organization performance and decision maker maturity assessments, (b) process projections separated into phase and step ratings and personal efficiency and productivity appraisals, and (c) overall decision value from the use of the DSS. Such an enumeration would provide the detailed documentation needed to properly justify the evaluation.

Several output options would be available to the user. He, she, or they could display the desired section of the main output on the monitor and print the display. Alternatively, the user could save the entire document to a file and print a hard copy of the results. All options would be selected by point-and-click operations that make the processing transparent to the user.

## Feedback loops

The evaluation DSS would provide continuous decision support, and this support is indicated with the feedback loops in Figure 3. The top loop signifies that feedback from the decision maker’s actions (processing) can be used to update or revise database entries and the operationalised decision hierarchy model dynamically. The bottom loop shows that output feedback can be used to guide, extend, or modify the original analyses and evaluations. Important further evaluations include what-iftype sensitivity analyses. In these analyses, the decision maker can determine what will happen to DSS effectiveness if there were specified changes in outcome or process measures, components of these measures, or variable and tool weights.

## Experimental studies

Two new experiments have used the AHP-based system successfully to assess DSS effectiveness. The experiments involved different decision situations that were supported with distinct decision aids. This section summarises the new experiments and illustrates how the AHP-based system can reconcile the incongruity between earlier experimental findings and case-supported DSS theory.

## Health care experiment

One recent experiment tested whether an enhanced DSS (called a management support system or MSS) could improve the outcomes from, and the process of, strategic hospital decision making (Forgionne & Kohli, 1995). In this experiment, every subject was instructed to utilise available information with her/his experience, judgment, and knowledge in developing a hospital policy that would generate as much return on revenue (ROR) as possible. Control subjects received a basic DSS, while experimental subjects had the MSS, to support their decision making. By using these systems, competing subjects could input decisions and environmental forecasts and get an instant report on the resulting revenue, profit (or loss), return on revenue (ROR), and other financial outcomes. The MSS delivered more decision-specific information and knowledge to the user than the basic DSS.

## Outcome measures

Organization (hospital) performance was measured by the absolute difference between the observed return on revenue and the 6% midpoint of the industry standard (RORX). Decision maker maturity was assessed by the subject’s effort expended on, and capability in, defining the management problem, exploring the interpretation’s relationship to alternative views, and generating alternative clinical and administrative solution concepts. Effort was measured by the time (in minutes) spent on the simulated decision making (TIME). Capability was assessed with the numbers of opportunities (OPP) and problems (PROB) identified during the simulation, and the number of alternatives (ALT) generated during the simulation.

## Process measures

Experts used their experience, judgment, and knowledge to rate the subject’s ability to perform each of the four general phases of decision making—intelligence (PH1), design (PH2), choice (PH3), and implementation (PH4). Self-ratings assessed the usefulness of the system in supporting the subject’s ability to perform the identifying objectives (IDEN), recognising the problem or opportunity (REC), gathering qualitative data (QLD), gathering quantitative data (QND), generating alternatives (GEN), establishing criteria (EST), evaluating alternatives (EVA), choosing the final alternative (CFA), decision confidence (DC), system effectiveness (SE), and implementing the final choice (FC) steps of the decisionmaking process.

## Decision hierarchy

Statistical data analyses indicated that the provided information system (DSS versus MSS) had a significant effect on organization performance (RORX), some aspects of decision maturity (ALT and TIME), most decision-making phases (PH1, PH3, and PH4), and some decision-making steps (REC, GEN, EVA, CFA, and SE). Applying these significant measures to the general model in Figure 2 yields the specific health care decision hierarchy shown in Figure 4.

## Decision value

Using the numerical values for the variables, with equal weights for these variables and the tools, in the evaluation-DSS-delivered AHP yields a decision value of 0.38 for the DSS and 0.62 for the MSS. Moreover, the rating for each process and outcome variable was higher for the MSS than DSS. Such results support the general finding that the MSS results in better decision value (superior outcomes from, and an improved process of, simulated hospital decision making) than the basic DSS.

## Small business experiment

A second recent experiment tested whether a DSS and a creativity-enhancing DSS, called a qualitative-thinking support system (QTSS), could improve the outcomes from, and the process of, small business decision making (Forgionne et al, 1995). In this experiment, subjects were divided randomly into seven, five-person teams, and each team played a competitive management game called TEMPOMATIC. Each of the competing seven teams took over the management of a small corporation that produced and marketed a generic product similar to a small appliance. All corporations sold essentially the same product in the same three markets, and all seven corporations had the same starting position and available information. During the game, each team entered a set of 34 management decisions every week, and the game continued for 15 weeks. The decisions involved marketing, production, personnel, and finance. TEMPOMATIC used the entered data to simulate three months of competition and report quarterly operating statements to each team.

One team was designated as the control group; three were denoted as DSS groups; and three others were labelled as enhanced DSS groups. The control group was not given any decision aid. Each team in the DSS group was furnished with a decision support system that enabled the user to interactively perform rapid simulations and sensitivity (what-if and goal seeking) analyses outside the game prior to policy implementation. Each team in the enhanced DSS group was given the same decision support system embellished with a small-scale QTSS. One member of each enhanced DSS team used the system to: (a) access the QTSS while the DSS was running in the background, (b) utilise the QTSS’s creative thinking and qualitative decision aids to identify pertinent decision alternatives, and (c) transfer the results to the DSS for use by other team members.

## Outcome and process measures

Corporate performance and advancements in the participants’ level of decision-making maturity were used to measure the outcomes of the decision-making process. Corporate performance was appraised through net income after tax (NIAT), and decision-making maturity was assessed by the time (in minutes) spent on the simulated decision making (TIME) and the number of alternatives (ALT) considered. Since subjects were trained on the systems prior to the experiment, the time spent on decision making should reflect problem-solving effort rather than hardware, software, and model learning. Process improvement was measured through consistency (variance in net income VIN), the degree of creativity (CRE), and user confidence in decision making (CONF).

## Decision hierarchy

Statistical data analyses indicated that the provided information system (No DSS, DSS, or QTSS) had a significant effect on organization performance (NIAT), decision maturity (ALT and TIME), and decision-making process (VIN, CONF, and CREA). Applying these significant measures to Figure 2’s general model yields the specific small business decision hierarchy shown in Figure 5.

## Decision value

Using the numerical values for the variables, with equal weights for these variables and the tools, in the evaluation-DSS-delivered AHP yielded a decision value of 0.310 for the No DSS, 0.339 for the DSS, and 0.351 for the QTSS alternatives. Further, the rating for each process and outcome variable was higher with either the DSS or QTSS than with No DSS. Such discoveries support the general finding that the QTSS or DSS results in better decision value (superior outcomes from, and an improved process of, simulated small business decision making) than no decision aid.

## Other experiments

Obviously, previous empirical studies have not used the AHP-based system to evaluate DSS effectiveness. Would these studies’ conclusions change if such a system were employed? While space impedes an examination of all previous studies, some light can be shed on this question by examining some of the earlier investigations in detail. The most recent and illustrative of these investigations are the production planning experiments by Davis, Kottemann, and Remus (Davis & Kottemann, 1994; Kottemann et al, 1994).

The production planning experiments were designed to approximate the real-life situation in which a decision maker accustomed to making unaided decisions is offered the use of a decision support system on a trial basis. The person then forms an opinion about the system’s effectiveness. In the experiments, subjects were given a dynamic, complex, and semi-structured decision task. They faced future uncertain demand (the uncontrollable input) and decided on production and workforce levels (the controllable inputs or decision variables) over a series of periods. Production and workforce decisions were made one period at a time. The objective was to minimise cumulative costs over a simulated planning period, and the Holt, Modigliani, Muth, and Simon (HMMS) model was used to compute these costs.

## Decision support systems

Every subject was provided with a DSS, conceptually like the generic architecture shown in Figure 1, to support the simulated production planning. All of the decision support systems delivered, through spreadsheetbased software, an operationalised (parameter-specified) HMMS model. Each DSS received users’ inputs, updated the operationalised HMMS model, and generated reports on HMMS model-generated current simulated costs and cost-relevant parameters. The specific DSS architecture differed from group to group within the experiments.

## First and second experiments

In the first and second experiments, the DSSs supported policy simulation but did not recommend a production plan. The decision support systems differed mainly by the type of output feedback provided to the user. For the control (labelled the unaided) group, output feedback was limited to organizational outcomes. Subjects entered production and workforce levels for the current period on an input screen, and the DSS subsequently displayed the resulting cost reports on an output screen. For the experimental (labelled the what-if) group, output feedback included prominent what-if-type sensitivity analyses as well as organizational outcomes. Users tried production and workforce levels (and, in the second experiment, anticipated demand) for several periods and observed the simulated results on the input screen before entering the desired controllable inputs. The DSS then presented the cost reports resulting from the desired inputs on an output screen.

## Third experiment

In the third experiment, all subjects had the same whatif-type DSS as used by the experimental groups in the other experiments. These experiment three decision support systems also displayed on the input screens the recommendations (which subjects could follow or ignore) of a heuristic decision rule that yielded a good, though not necessarily optimal, production plan. The DSSs differed mainly by the type of output feedback provided to the user. For the control (labelled the non-feedback) group, the DSS did not display the heuristic decision rule’s period-to-date performance on the output screen. The experimental (labelled the feedback) group had a DSS that displayed the heuristic performance feedback on the output screen.

## Outcome and process measures

None of the experiments explicitly measured the participants’ ability to perform the phases and steps of the decision-making process. Each experiment did gauge:

(a) the time spent on the decision task, (b) the number of alternatives considered during the simulated decision making, and (c) the subjects’ confidence in their decisions. These factors can be used to infer process improvement from the DSS-assisted decision making.

None of the experiments explicitly measured the maturation of the user as a decision maker. However, inferences about this maturation can be drawn from: (a) factors measured in the experiments (for example, experiment three subjects’ ability to discern the heuristic’s performance superiority) and (b) material reported in the Davis, Kottemann, and Remus articles. Organization performance was gauged explicitly in all three experiments by total production cost.

## Organization performance

Subjects in all of the Davis, Kottemann, and Remus experiments were required to perform a dynamic optimization task—finding the plan that would minimise cumulative production costs over a discrete horizon. Yet, they were not provided with a DSS that could deliver effective support for this optimisation.

In the first and second experiments, subjects searched for a problem solution by trying a single plan (non-whatif-type DSS users) or several plans (what-if-type DSS users) during each simulation run. The what-if-type DSS provided a more extensive trial and error search capability, but no more decision guidance, than the non-whatif-type DSS. Neither DSS guided the user toward the best (or even a good) plan. Since both systems delivered virtually the same type of decision support (trial and error search) for the identical (or statistically equivalent) subjects, there should have been no cost performance difference between non-what-if-type and what-if-type DSS users. Statistical tests by Davis, Kottemann, and Remus confirmed this expectation at the  = 0.05 level of significance.

In the third experiment, subjects had the option of following the advice of a system-delivered heuristic decision rule that would guide the user toward a good, though not necessarily optimal, production plan. The rule’s current period advice was displayed alongside the screen entry spaces reserved for what-if-type trial and error inputs. In any particular trial of the simulation, users could (and sometimes did) obtain lower current period costs with a trial and error search than with an adherence to the heuristic’s advice, but the rule yielded far lower cumulative costs over the twenty-four-period simulation run. While feedback-type DSS users received comparative (trial and error versus heuristic) period-todate cost output, they needed several trials to discern the heuristic’s superiority from this output. By then, these users would have incurred a substantial cost to switch from trial and error search to the rule’s advice. Given the relatively small number of simulation trials, subjects were unlikely to make the switch and incur the expense.

Since both systems delivered virtually the same type of decision support (trial and error search supplemented with a decision heuristic) for the identical (or statistically equivalent) subjects, there should have been no cost performance difference between non-feedback-type and feedback-type DSS users. In conformance with other recent empirical research (Sengupta & Abdel-Hamid, 1993), statistical tests confirmed this logic-based expectation at the = 0.05 level of significance.

In the Davis, Kottemann, and Davis studies, then, the experimental design and the nature of the provided DSS effectively precluded the evaluation of outcome effectiveness on the basis of organization performance. However, it was possible to determine the beneficial effects of the DSSs on the maturity of the user as a decision maker. Process improvements also were discernible.

## Decision-making maturity

In the first and second experiments, what-if-type DSS users processed more problem-focused information than non-what-if-type DSS users. It seems reasonable to assume that such focused processing would provide subjects with useful knowledge, and perhaps wisdom, about the structure of the problem and the effects of this structure’s components on organization performance. In the third experiment, there was substantial subject learning during the simulation. For example, feedback-type DSS users: (a) detected that it would be costly to switch in mid-simulation from a what-if-type analysis to decision rule usage, and (b) were better able to discern the heuristic’s performance superiority than non-feedback-type DSS users. While additional experiments would be needed to confirm the hypothesis, the training effects from DSS usage also could be expected to improve the subjects’ general problem-solving skills.

## Process Improvements

In the first and second experiments, what-if-type DSS users considered at least three times as many alternatives as non-what-if-type DSS users. Although what-if-type DSS users took longer to perform the decision task than non-what-if-type DSS users, the actual times were unreported. Unless there was at least a three-fold time difference (an unlikely event), then, what-if-type DSS users were more efficient (able to evaluate more alternatives in a given time period) than non-what-if-type DSS users. In the third experiment, the feedback-type DSS generated more pertinent information and knowledge from decision making (comparative performance feedback) than the non-feedback-type DSS. This additional information, which represented an improvement in personal productivity, increased the users’ beliefs in (acceptance of) the superiority of the heuristic decision rule. In the first and second experiments, what-if-type DSS users had more confidence in their decisions than non-what-if-type DSS users. Since a lack of confidence will inhibit the acceptance of a decision (Benbasat & Dexter, 1982; Silver, 1988; Sainfort et al, 1990) it seems reasonable to assume that the what-if-type DSS reduced the time needed for implementation by (improved the personal productivity of) the user.

## Decision value

Although the forms varied, each of the three experiments utilised a base DSS, which can be labelled as BDSS, and an enhanced DSS, which can be denoted as EDSS. BDSS was the non-what-if-type DSS in the first and second experiments and the non-feedback-type DSS in the third experiment. EDSS was the what-if-type DSS in the first and second experiments and the feedbacktype DSS in the third experiment. All three experiments also had assorted measures of decision maker maturity, personal efficiency, personal productivity, and the step proficiency gauge of decision confidence (which can be labelled as CONF). Applying these outcome and process measures to Figure 2’s general model yields the specific production planning decision hierarchy shown in Figure 6.

Using their own analyses of the experimental results, Davis, Kottemann, and Remus concluded that EDSS usage created an ‘illusion of control’. They suggested that the subsequent misjudgments could lead people to: (a) continue using the system even when it is not beneficial, and (b) avoid using potentially superior decision support technologies. The implications are that the EDSS had no more decision value than the BDSS, and this value was minimal or even negative.

In most cases, Figure 6’s measures were not expressed on a precise numerical scale. Consequently, system alternatives had to be compared on an ordinal basis. Using the resulting ordinal gauges, with equal weights for the assessment measures and system alternatives, in the evaluation-DSS-delivered AHP yielded a decision value of 0.379 for the BDSS and 0.621 for the EDSS alternatives. In addition, the rating for each process and outcome variable was higher for the EDSS than BDSS alternative. Contrary to the Davis, Kottemann, and Remus implications, then, such results support the general finding that the EDSS results in better decision value (superior outcomes from, and an improved process of, simulated production planning) than the BDSS.

## Conclusions

Decision support systems should be evaluated on the basis of their contributions to improving the outcomes from, and the process of, decision making. Since enhanced outcomes will be the result of betterments in process, a study will be incomplete without the inclusion of valid process, as well as outcome, improvement tests.

In short, evaluating DSS effectiveness is a multiple criteria decision-making problem.

As this paper has illustrated, such multiple criteria evaluations have not always been performed in empirical research. The incomplete nature of the empirical testing may account for the discrepancy between earlier experimental findings (which have equivocally supported the theory that decision support systems are effective in improving decision making) and cases and the two new (health care and small business) experiments (which have sustained the theory).

This paper’s examinations also suggest that a multiple criteria DSS evaluation model can be useful in effecting a reconciliation and in providing a consistent basis for future studies on DSS effectiveness. Such a model can be estimated through the analytic hierarchy process (AHP), as was done here, or with alternative methodologies (such as multi-attribute utility theory). Moreover, the DSS evaluation model can be delivered to the evaluator in a relatively transparent manner through a decision support system.

Further empirical studies will be needed to confirm or refute this paper’s findings. Additional research will be required to develop and test alternative: (a) evaluation outcome and process measures, (b) decision outcome and value functions, and (c) estimation methodologies for the measures and functions. Figure 3’s conceptual evaluation DSS also could use added development and empirical efficacy testing. A successfully tested evaluation model then can become a standard for DSS effectiveness studies, and a tested evaluation DSS can serve as the normal delivery mechanism for the model.
