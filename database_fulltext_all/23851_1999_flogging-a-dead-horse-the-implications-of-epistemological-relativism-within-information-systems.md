# Flogging a dead horse: the implications of epistemological relativism within information systems methodological practice

F Wilson

The Information Systems Research Centre, University of Salford, Salford, M5 4WT, UK

The supporters of the realist principles of information system methodological development argue, that what makes our theories and beliefs about information systems design true or false is the way they stand in relation to reality and not just the way reality is ‘constructed’ in accordance with a particular cultural world-view, descriptive scheme or belief system. In contrast are the views of the anti-realists and epistemological relativists for whom such an argument is nonsensical—involving as it does the appeal to truths outside or beyond our best current knowledge. In this paper, using recent work from within the area, the realist and relativist models of information systems research and methodological design are identified Following this an analysis is presented which debates whether relativism in information systems design, as a model of epistemology, provides practitioners and researchers with directions for achieving the epistemological state it describes. Finally, the tenet that practitioners and researchers are able to learn and adhere to the principles of relativism and from this will be able to provide an improved methodology for information systems design is questioned.

## Introduction

In examining information systems theory at the most general level it is possible to identify two basic forms which conform to the realist and relativist epistemologies. The success of realist practice as a basis for the development of information systems design methodologies may be seen to rest upon the development of positivistic, ‘hard’ methodologies over the last thirty years and the success of this project may be regarded as a microcosm of the enlightenment legacy and the claim of scientific enterprise to be able to emancipate humanity from the shackles of custom and dogma. The basis of the assumptions of hard methodological development is that an increase in scientific, natural and social knowledge would enable an increase of control by men and women over their own fate. Thus the basis of positivistic realism in information systems development is identifiable in the attempt to ground inquiry, communication and design in a manner which excludes any reliance upon mere belief or unexamined practice. The first twenty years of information systems design reflected this perspective in terms of the production of a number of structured design methodologies. In the last decade however, information systems theorists and practitioners have become aware of the failure of many of the social systems to which they apply their methodologies, to respond to the traditional positivist prescription: i.e. more knowledge equals more control. In this regard designer and theoretician interest has moved towards attempts to understand the new kinds of uncertainty resulting from human development, described by Beck (1992) as a ‘risk society’, which is inherently unamenable to ‘the Enlightenment prescription’ and may be classified into the category of relativism.

These new theoretical considerations have resulted in a change in the discourse of the computing and managerial sciences towards an increased interest and emphasis upon the ‘softer’, interpretivist and critical approaches to design and implementation methodology. The interest in soft systems has increased recently with the much promoted need for enterprises to consider their structures as an inter-linking of processes as opposed to a series of functions and thus to ‘transform’ themselves through the use of this knowledge coupled with the deployment of advanced information systems (Applegate, 1994). This interest has coincided with a debate among systems practitioners and theorists about whether the growth of technical expertise and the use of computer-based technologies will result in a rationality which places too much emphasis upon technocratic modes of organizational control (Wilson, 1994). An outcome of this debate has been a concern to develop theories which evaluate the effects of technology and organizational change in terms of the actions, reactions, and interactions of the interested parties. This concern within the information systems field has led to attempts to identify and categorise the nature of, and relationships between, the realist ‘hard’ and relativist interpretive forms of methodologies (Hirschheim et al, 1996; Fitzgerald & Howcroft, 1998). As academics and professionals have become more informed of the complexities of the organizational and social situations within which their theoretical and practical activities are located, so has this awareness led them to renewed efforts to develop relativist approaches to the understanding of information systems (Checkland & Holwell, 1998). However, despite the many recent presentations of information systems design taxonomies which emphasise the relativist approach, it is proposed that these developments of methodological theory still require further debate upon their philosophical value and the consequences of deploying these methodological approaches within the teaching and the practice of information systems design. In exploring this debate recent work in the field, most notably that of Checkland and Holwell (1998), is examined with the intent of analysing the epistemological assumptions of relativist information systems design methodologies.

## Realist information systems design

In realist information system design the dominant paradigm for the application of ‘systems-based’ knowledge to practical human problems has evolved from classical scientific method derived from the physical sciences. At the ontological level, as Fitzgerald and Howcroft (1998, p 319) observe, the realist perspective suggests that the external world consists of pre-existing hard, tangible structures which exist independently of an individual’s cognition. Realism emphasises techniques of observation, measurement and quantification, and requires scientists to ground their theories in overt behaviour which is observable (Hempel, 1965). Thus realism within information systems design becomes ‘an amplification of the idea that what goes on in organizations is rational decision making in pursuit of declared objectives’ (Checkland & Holwell, 1998, p 78). Realist methodologies can be understood as the product of a series of converging theoretical developments to form the general systems theory movement (Noble, 1997; Lilienfield, 1978). From this grounding information systems design within the realist model requires the assigning of ‘correct’ descriptions to entities, data flows etc, and the deployment of a structured design methodology whose repeatable outcome is certain to its users. This certainty of outcome is assured by promoters who consider that in its embodiment is the discovery of rules which are so fundamental as to be universal, rules, that if correctly followed will always lead directly to coherent intelligibility in the understanding and modelling of the problem area. Thus the premise that the realist perspective relies upon is the availability of a set of formal constraints which have the characteristics of abstractness, generality, invariance across contexts. Once these constraints have been discovered and put in place, the resulting system takes on an independence of action which is capable of regulating complex systems.

## Relativist information systems design

The ascendency of realist methodologies created concern as to whether the assumptions informing the work of realist theorists of information systems design failed to take into account that as information technology extends throughout an organization, becoming pervasive to the extent that numerous tasks and decisions are controlled by it, the conceptualisations and meaning of technical rationality may similarly become distorted. Thus the relativist ontology emerged which questioned the validity of singular external reality and posits the ‘belief that multiple realities exist as subjective constructions of the mind’ (Fitzgerald & Howcroft, 1998, p 319). In this concern relativist theories of knowledge typically oppose any views of a fixed human nature, a rationality that can transcend the contingent circumstances of a particular society, and an objective reality. The arguments may be traced back to Protagoras in his saying that: ‘Man is the measure of all things; of what is that is, and of what is not that is not’ (Coady, 1995, p 757). More recently the idea that humans continually create and recreate a shared reality through social action and the use of language has been given considerable support in the information systems field. As Boland (1987) notes, ‘We all know that information is the meaning or inward-forming of a person that results from the engagement with data. Yet we continually avoid the problem of meaning in our information systems research’ (p 36). As practitioners and theoreticians have become aware of this failure, over the last two decades there has emerged a debate concerning how new methodologies could recognise that ‘there is no permanent “social reality” except at the broadest possible level, immune from the events and ideas which, in the normal social process, continually change it’ (Checkland & Holwell, 1998, p 104). In this investigation researchers were seeking operational methodologies which would circumvent the criticism made of the realist project and provide ‘richer’ insights into the developing use of computer-based systems within organizations. The need to include the discovery that there is no universal truth and that situational understanding is dependent upon the values and beliefs of al participants within a context is one which is necessarily adhered to (in theory at least) in all the ‘soft’ system methodologies and arguably in the attempts to operationalise many of the ‘critical’ ones (Tsoukas, 1992; Wilson, 1997). Within this perspective the prescriptions of systems modellers and methodologists remain valid only if they can recognise the unique experience of the subject and from this recognition produce a means of better organising open and free debate about the value or otherwise of existing and proposed systems designs (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994).

## The relativist proposition

In reviewing the field of information systems design methodologies, elements of the relativist argument are to be found in recent derivatives which include institutional democracy design, rational argumentation design (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994), consensual communication development (Hirschheim & Newman, 1991), soft systems methodology and process for organizational meanings (Checkland & Holwell, 1998). Current developments which are of relevance to the relativist category may also be found in use of actor-network theory, based upon the work of Callon (1986) and Latour (1987), in the field of information systems design, which is also critical of the use of realist theory in methodological development (Willcocks & Grint, 1997).

The attraction of relativism for those information systems theoreticians, who promote it and find in it the basis for extensive development in method, follows directly from the demonstration that realist methodology is based upon a false picture of the human situation. Since it is not the case, as realists assume, that the scene of communication includes a free and independent self facing a similarly independent world to which it can be linked by the rules of a universal language, a methodology based on these assumptions will necessarily fail to achieve its goal. Thus Klein and Lyytinen (1985, p 143) suggest that ‘the separation of information systems goals from human purpose and the identification of data with measurable facts conceals the real nature of information systems as social communication systems’; and that, ‘under scientism, science, rather than being a critical conscience and teacher of practice, becomes its myopic servant’ (Klein & Lyytinen, 1985, p 151). Conversely, if the correct understanding of the human situation is, as relativist methodology presents it, one of individuals whose acts are socially constituted and who are embedded in a world which is a function of the historical and conventional forms of thought that bring it into being, then it becomes possible to extrapolate from this understanding a better set of methods for operating in the world we are constantly restructuring and reconstructing, a better set of rationales and procedures for making judgments, and a better set of solutions to the problems of information systems design. Checkland and Holwel (1998) present a recent example of the hope of relativist theorists and practitioners that their work will provide a ‘conceptual cleansing’ and where ‘the sense making models which emerge can encompass any particular assumptions about the nature of organizational reality and management, whether “hard” realist or “soft” interpretive ones, though the author’s sympathies are with the latter’ (back cover). In referring to Schon’s (1983) ideal of a ‘reflective practitioner’, Checkland and Holwell (1998) promote their model as a ‘high level tool for structuring reflection’ (p 237) and suggest that their methodology provides a ‘process outcome, an intellectual tool which helps furnish a process of inquiry for making sense of a field, a tool available not only to reflective practitioners but to reflective researchers and reflective students as well’ (p 238). In a similar vein Hirschheim and Klein’s (1994) proposal for rational argumentation design identifies that their ‘neohumanist paradigm seeks change, emancipation, and the realisation of human potential and stresses the role that different social and organizational forces play in understanding change. It focuses on barriers to emancipation—in particular ideology (distorted communication), power and psychological compulsions, and social constraints— and seeks ways to overcome them’ (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994, p 109).

From this relativist perspective systems are seen as the mental constructs of observers rather than as entities with a real, objective existence in the world. Multiple views of reality are admitted and their implications are examined. Values are included rather than excluded (in theory) from the methodological process (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990). The privileged role of experts is questioned and an attempt made to include problemowners and other concerned individuals in carrying out a study of a situation and finding possible ways forward. The relativist approach attempts to build our conceptions of truth and reality on the way human activity is integrated with a particular way of acting. Truth is constructed and not discovered and we build up our understanding of the nature of things from what we already take for granted. In summary, the very essentials of systems design that are assumed in realist discourse to be opposed to the local, the historical, the contingent, the variable, and the rhetorical, turn out to be irreducibly dependent on, and indeed to be functions of these contexts.

## The realist rejoinder

In reviewing the rise of relativism, however, it would be too premature to say that the realist argument lies in ruins. It is in fact remarkably resilient and resourceful in the face of attack. The resistance, or persistence, of realism usually takes the form of a counter-attack in which the supposedly disastrous consequences of relativism are expounded. The realist rejoinder posits that when everything depends on our frame of reference and there is no way of testing or reasoning about that frame, our most fundamental beliefs about reality will have an arbitrary starting point. Since the sum total of the traits of any empirical phenomenon is infinite, anybody attempting to describe it must decide (consciously or unconsciously) what to note and what to leave unrecorded, and how much attention and space to give to each mentioned item or aspect. Neither the canons of veracity and exactitude nor the distinctions of philosophical semantics, nor even a recourse to unemotional recondite words, can provide a line of escape from the necessity of making such choices. Even if our practices are based on certain general facts of human nature, these are irrelevant to the question of justification. This lack of an independent platform to ground veracity is cited as central problem of relativism. (As every journalist knows, even a picture of a simple event like an accident or brawl can be altered out of recognition by picking on one assortment of details rather than another; just as a speech can be utterly distorted by stringing together selected, though literally correct, quotations.) The consequences of the loss of foundation are usually said to extend to the loss of everything necessary to rational inquiry and successful communication.

This is a dark prophecy, a realist nightmare vision in which a liberated self goes its unconstrained way believing and doing whatever it likes; but it is also a misreading of the central principle of the relativist stance in information systems design, i.e. its insistence upon situatedness. A situated self is a self whose every operation is a function of the conventional possibilities built into an environmental context. Thus in using Vickers’ (1995) theory of ‘appreciative systems’ Checkland and Holwell (1998) identify that these operations ‘are themselves internally generated by the previous history of the system itself and its interactions with its environment’ (p 47, original emphasis). In anchoring the subject to the social situation, Checkland and Holwell’s relativist perspective reveals the subject to be always, and already, tethered by the local or community norms and standards that constitute it and which enable its rational acts. In deploying Vickers’ theories Checkland and Holwell propose that ‘we always see the world through a “filter” created by previous judgments about it. New judgments both reproduce the previous “settings” and create new ones, changing the filter in so doing: an appreciative system is always the product of the previous system itself and its interactions with its environment’ (p 73). Checkland and Holwell also note that ‘we always selectively perceive parts of the world, as a result of our interests and previous history’ and that ‘the act of attributing meaning and making judgments implies the existence of standards against which comparisons can be made, standards of good/bad, important/unimportant, etc’ (1998, pp 99–100). Thus such a subject can be many things: certain, confused, in turmoil, at rest, perplexed, etc and that while it may not be sensible to assume that this behaviour reflects a systemic real-world actuality there remains the opportunity to develop a ‘process of inquiry’ that ‘can be organised as a learning system’ (Checkland & Holwell, 1998, p 78) and which will allow the inquirer to understand these sets of beliefs within their situated, systemic constraints. However, because of this insistence upon the situatedness of the individual, it is later proposed that relativist practice cannot have the negative consequences outlined in the realist rejoinder.

## Relativism and the risk society

In examining the relativist interest in understanding the situatedness of the subject it is possible to note the relationship between relativism and the emergence of the so-called ‘risk society’ (Beck, 1992; Giddens, 1994). Giddens (1994) describes this as an increase in ‘manufactured uncertainty’ and ‘action at a distance’ resulting in a ‘post-traditional order’ of global societal risk and uncertainty. He identifies how single decisions [from interest rates to nuclear safety] may have dramatic and rapid effects upon the world economy. Within this expansion of social complexity notions of fixed chains of cause and effect together with the possibility of understanding them through reductionist methods become infeasible. The concern is now one of understanding how the subject is situated within this complexity and how subjective perceptions of this situatedness will in turn shape the unfolding of future events. The emphasis in this understanding is to regard complexity as an interconnection of processes where the use of relativist techniques may lead to the generation of hypotheses and new knowledge. Thus relativist information systems design methodologies with their promotion of change, transformation, contextual variability, social-reflexivity, etc, are seemingly suited to the new emphasis in business organization upon process over product, the replacement of particular standards of correctness by the fluid and dynamic standards of effectiveness and continuous improvement and the growing demand that people make more and better informed decisions for themselves on a range of decisions in their own work and in their personal lives.

This shift in orientation is not new since the admonition to examine organizational processes rather than functions is now conventional practice which is reflected in the many variations of the ‘excellent’ management and ‘reengineering’ genres. In relativist methodologies it seems that this conventional wisdom has found a new paradigm, one that systems designers can presumably draw upon in order to operationalise their knowledge. This possibility is suggested by Willcocks and Grint (1997, p 105) who note that actor-network theory may assist in the ‘synthetic analysis’ needed to ‘make concrete the repoliticized, re-engineered organization’. The prospect of relating anti-realist methods to these genres becomes even more attractive as it allows designers a way of explaining, what is at least perceived to be, the failure of many systems implementations. Thus in the use of action research techniques where organizational situations are recognised to consist of ‘systemic relationships’ a diagnostic methodology is described where a ‘continuous cyclic process in which the combination of developing theory from research and implicit pre-understanding informs action, and reflection upon action informs theory development’ (Eden & Huxman, 1996, p 532, emphasis in the original). It now becomes possible to view implementation failures as the result of the deployment of the wrong epistemological assumptions, and now that through the prescribed dialectic process, the right ones have been discovered, success or at least dramatic improvement should follow.

## Some methodological difficulties

The introduction, by epistemological relativists, of the claims of situation, history, politics and convention, in opposition to the more commonly successful claims of realist method, provides much of interest to practitioners and researchers in the field. However, the proposition that relativist arguments and the perspectives of systems design as process, in some sense ‘go together’ raises questions regarding the antecedental nature of whether the former can serve as a theoretical resource for the latter or that, conversely, the elaboration of the latter depends on a prior understanding of, and commitment to, the former. Thus the central questions are whether relativism in information system design as a model of epistemology provides practitioners and researchers with directions for achieving the epistemological state it describes and whether, if practitioners and researchers are able to learn and adhere to the principles of relativism, this will provide an improved methodology for information systems project development?

Here it is proposed that the answer to both these questions is no; moreover, it is an answer dictated by relativism itself. It is an answer implicit in one of Derrida’s (1976, p 9) characteristically enigmatic pronouncements: ‘This situation has always already been announced’. By ‘this situation’ Derrida means the situation in which knowledge is always and already mediated, the situation in which language, rather than being the mere medium or instrument with which we interrogate the world, is itself the origin of the world, of our modes of interrogation, and of ourselves. It is the situation that Derrida comes to announce in Of Grammatology and elsewhere; yet he says it ‘has always already been announced’. Derrida’s point is that since it has always been announced, it cannot be discovered, at least not in the sense that would allow information systems designers to gain a fulcrum that they did not previously have. That is, the designer realisation that something has always been the case does not make it more the case than it was before s/he realised that it was; the designer is still, epistemologically speaking, in the same position s/he was always in. The fundamental difficulty in being objective about situational subjectivity stems from the inescapable circumstances that neither the requirement of factual correctness nor the canon of semantic neutrality tells designers anything about what to include and what to leave out when they are describing a situation. The fact that they are now able to announce that all the systems participants (including the designer) are situated does not make them more situated, and even when they could not announce it, being situated was always their situation. However, the relativist proposition suggests that this ‘new’ self-consciousness is something the designer is able to achieve in a space apart from situations and to make this reflexive knowledge a knowledge which is firmer and more ‘true’ than the knowledge that was previously available without reflection. Thus in ‘reestablishing the human agent as an actual creator of social reality, through the attribution of meaning to observations and experience’, the provision of a set of methodological tools allows the relativist inquirer to ‘draw upon phenomenology, hermeneutics and interpretive sociology’ which will escape the strictures of ‘positivism, and lead to a variety of modes of inquiry other than hypothesis testing’ (Checkland & Holwell, 1998, p 78). However in deploying these ‘methodolog ical tools’ the inquirer is once again reflecting the aspir ations, ideals and assumptions of liberal thought in that valid knowledge may only be achieved disinterestedly in the manner of projecting observer impartiality on a particular situation, however it is an assumption wholly at odds with relativist principles of this form of information systems research and design. The contradiction is in the centre of the relativist argument. This is the argument that begins by denying the possibility of a knowledge that is independent of our beliefs and prac tices and ends by claiming as one of its consequences the achieving of just such an independence. This is the claim which relativist insight makes in its proposal of providing us with a perspective upon events which now places us in a position either to have a greater under standing of a situation or to achieve new objectives. Thus the claim in which the notion of situatedness is said to be a methodological tool which allows us to gain a purchase upon situations is finally a claim to have escaped situatedness, and is therefore nothing more or less than a reinvention of realism by the very form of thought which has supposedly denied its validity.

Thus, as an example of methodologies which promote the possibility of ‘richer concepts of “information system” and “information systems development”,’ Checkland and Holwell (1998) outline a series of proposals which will inform their relativist perspective where ‘the aim is to define plausible purposes and ways in which it would be possible to pursue them . . . what are sought are accommodations which enable energy to be enlisted in undertaking action relevant to plausible purposes (p 112, original emphasis). In defining ‘plausible purposes’ Checkland and Holwell redescribe relativism so that it becomes a new and more fashionable version of the realist systems approach, a political vision that has at its centre the goal of disinterestedly viewing contending partisan perspectives which are then either reconciled or subsumed in some higher or more general synthesis, in a larger and larger ‘accommodation’—a word Checkland and Holwell repeatedly use. In identifying this methodological sleight-of-hand it must be stated yet again that this aspiration is incompatible with relativism because it assumes the possibility of designers gaining a perspective upon their own beliefs, a perspective from which those beliefs can be evaluated and compared with the similarly evaluated beliefs of others. This is the central principle of relativism in its insistence upon the inescapability of situatedness, and if situatedness is inescapable, designers cannot possibly identify, in nonevaluative ways, their own beliefs, because as situated beings some set of beliefs of which they could not be aware would be enabling any identification they might make; and, therefore, the act of identification would from the very first be ‘evaluative’ throughout the design process. One could escape this logic only by saying that while the operations of the mind are always a function of context, in one operation, the identification of its own context and that of others, the mind is independent. Such an exemption is obviously contradictory and marks a return in Checkland and Holwell’s discourse of the realism they have supposedly banished.

## Escape attempts

The attempt to build a larger and larger consensus on the insight of relativism can only commence if the first tenet of relativism, the situatedness of all knowledge and of all acts of knowing, is overlooked. It is overlooked as a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for the development of a ‘high level tool for structuring reflection’ (p 237) and to allow the practitioner to construct information systems designs which reflect the currently fashionable concerns with the goals of reform and emancipation (Wilson, 1997). The reasoning is understandable: since the lesson of relativism is that the world and its facts are not given but made (Man is the measure of all things . . . etc), it follows that those who have learned the lesson will feel free to make them again. However in this reasoning the information systems designer once again makes the error previously identified, the error of thinking that a conviction as to the circumstantiality of everything we know can allow them a perspective upon their circumstantial knowledge and enable them to change it. The point is that circumstantiality (another name for situatedness) is not something that the designer can escape by recognising it, since the act of recognition will itself occur within circumstances that cannot be the object of self-conscious attention.

Thus reform and emancipation can take no particular warrant from relativism, and to think otherwise is once again to make the mistake of making relativism into a foundation.

In summary, the knowledge that the designer and the system participants are in a situation has no particular significance for any situation they happen to be in, because the constraints of that situation will not be relaxed by that knowledge. In the promotion of relativist methods, while it may provide academics and practitioners with the possession of a new philosophical perspective, it will not equip them with a tool for operating in the world they already inhabit. Being informed that they are in a situation will not help designers to dwell in it more perfectly nor to develop information systems within it more successfully.

When Checkland and Holwell (1998) state that ‘models or pictures . . . are never descriptive accounts of “what is the case”; rather they provide categories and language enabling us to describe what is perceived to be the case’, i.e. discourse conventions which may be identified by relativism, they are open to the same criticism that they level at realist theorists who assume that what counts as information is fixable and static and where the rules formulated to describe behaviour are the same rules that produce the behaviour. This is also their assumption in as much as they believe that a description of how we come to know what we know can be turned into a set of directions for knowing (i.e. soft systems methodology, process for organizational meanings etc) ‘in order that deliberate intentional action can be taken, including action in the name of the organization, or some part of it, as a whole’ (p 113). As a searching critique of method, soft systems methodology cannot itself be made the basis of a method without losing its relativist character.

However in countering these criticism proposals may be made which suggest that the link between relativism and information systems can be preserved by making it weaker. It may be argued that even if relativism cannot serve as a methodological tool with which to master situations, it can direct us to new areas of understanding and learning about information systems design. This is in effect what is urged by Checkland and Holwell (1998, p 97) when they quote the example of Stamper (1987) who, they say, ‘advocates a focus on the social process in which shared meanings are attributed to selected data, and would base development upon a study of the social norms which entail meaning attribution’. Thus all the developer requires is help in understanding the social norms themselves, in all their fullness, and then the staple activity of methodology instruction will be the analysis of the semantics and conventions of the particular target area. As Stamper suggests, ‘Meanings belong to human agents . . . If we can keep track of those agents and what they do, we shall devise a new semantics that can be applied successfully to business, legal and other social information systems’ (in Checkland & Holwell, 1998, p 97). Here the advice is, if all knowledge is the result of ‘social norms’, then it is necessary to develop a ‘new semantics’ to understand these situations. The trouble with this advice is that in its strongest sense it cannot be followed, and in its weaker sense it cannot help but be followed, even when it has not been given. It cannot be followed in the ‘strong’ sense because in doing so it would amount to understanding social norms as if they were a new kind of object to which the researcher could now analyse, and that it is possible to achieve a distance from them such that the designer/researcher’s accounts of them would be a form of ‘true’ knowledge. However this move once again reinvents realism by substituting, for the discredited notion of determinate facts, the finally indistinguishable notion of determinate ‘social norms’ and by rendering unproblematic the researchers relationship to these newly determining entities. The truth is that a situation is not an entity, but a bundle of tacit or unspoken assumptions that is simultaneously organising the world and changing in response to its own organising work. A situation is always on the wing, and any attempt to capture it will only succeed in fixing it in a shape it no longer has. Moreover, the recommended ‘action research’ attempt to capture knowledge of social norms must itself be mounted from within a situation, and therefore the knowledge afforded by such an exercise is not only out-of-date but disputable. In short, if the development of an information systems theory of situations is inefficacious, the development of a ‘new semantics’ of situations themselves is impossible and a contradiction in terms.

In another sense, however, the ‘weak’ form of relativism, the development of a ‘new semantics’ of situations is not only possible but inevitable, irrespective of one’s doctrinal position. For if relativism is correct and everything we know is always a function of situations, then every development of research methodology is always the result of, and the producer of, further situational knowledge, whether we label it so or not. That is to say, even if a designer is being presented with a methodology as if it were independent, detached, and transferable, it is not thereby rendered independent, detached, and transferable. Therefore promoters of all methodological perspectives are always developing ‘new semantics’ of situations because they can do nothing else.

In conclusion the argument can be reformulated in terms that might at first seem paradoxical, but are not: if all knowledge is situational and we are always and already in a situation, then we can never be at any distance from the knowledge we need. Relativist practice cannot present us with the knowledge we seek because its lesson is that we already have it. This is explicitly the lesson taught by Polanyi (1967) in the name of ‘tacit knowledge’. Tacit knowledge is knowledge already known or dwelt in; it cannot be handed over in the form of rules or maxims and theories; there is no transition from ‘knowing that’ to ‘knowing how’. The difficulty in the acceptance of this notion of tacit knowledge computer and information scientists is noted by Collins (1990). He identifies the continuing desire among scientists to describe tacit knowledge through the establishment of sets of ‘behavioural coordinates’ of skill, together with the dangers resulting from these attempts at creating formalisms.

As Checkland and Holwell observe ‘the act of creating information is a human act, not one which a machine can accomplish . . . (and) no designer can guarantee that his or her intended attributions of meaning will be universally acceptable’ (pp 91–92, emphasis in original). However, Checkland and Holwell (1998, p 219) apparently overlook this and devise their own curious epistemology which ‘requires a number of distinctions to be made which establish a language of: “data” (the factual invariances); “capta” (the data selected, created, or to which attention is paid); “information” (meaningful selected data in context); and “knowledge” (larger, longer-living structures of information)’ and thus provide a theoretical rationale for certain ways of information systems design as opposed to others. This epistemology is used to bolster their ‘process for organization meanings’ methodology, in ‘which any organization with aspirations to survive has to think about seriously’ and where ‘some version of the process will have to be institutionalised in an appropriate form which fits the organizational culture’. But this methodological sleight is used to turn the view that knowledge is tacit into a recipe for achieving it, when of course the lesson of tacit knowledge is that it cannot be achieved by recipe, by the handing over of an explicit maxim, even when the maxim is itself. Nevertheless Checkland and Holwell (1998, p 219) rather menacingly maintain that in organizations ‘it is a process which will need to be continuously managed . . . given its susceptibility . . . to “maverick” members’.

As an argument that denies the possibility of information systems design by rule, the theory of tacit knowledge cannot legitimate another set of rules for design. Neither can it rule out any. However, according to Checkland and Holwell ‘ “information systems development” must start by carefully defining the action to be served, in its specific context, and using that definition to decide what information is needed and how technology can help provide it’ (1998, p 219). The difficulty with this observation is that this is itself a maxim and one that is difficult to distinguish from the realist ‘intelligence, design, choice’ model of Herbert Simon (1960). In any information systems development the designs will be made in part because the maxims the designers are given are not explicit at all, in the sense of being detached from a tacitly known practice, but are the precipitations of a particular design practice, whether they are presented as such or not. The notion of tacit or situational knowledge is simply too powerful to be endangered by realist ‘hard’ methodologies that are ignorant of it and in the strongest sense realist methodologies cannot be ignorant of it, no matter how much they try. The lesson of tacit knowledge, the lesson that it cannot be the object or the beneficiary of self-consciousness, must be extended to the theory of its inclusion in the relativist approach. Knowing that knowledge is tacit cannot put designers in greater possession of it or enable them to possess it in a heightened way; and not knowing that knowledge is tacit cannot deprive them of it. To make the notion of tacit knowledge either into a recipe for methodological development or into a set of requirements for ‘good’ practice is to exempt it from its own insight.

## Conclusion

Whenever and wherever information systems design, under the guise of the various interpretivist, soft systems methodologies, is espoused the problem of reflexivity arises. Relativist claims can never be finally grounded in reality. However, by definition, they must always end with how we can envisage reality. This lack of grounding, however, produces the problem of how any claims about human reasoning are to be made. Thus if all reasoning only takes place within particular social practices (perceived systems), what is the status of any reasoning about social practices as such? Is it merely the product of another social practice? If it is, it must be of little interest, since whatever force it may seem to have, comes from its being accepted to be making a claim about what is the case. The fragmentation of reason alluded to would mean that wide claims about the relativist reasoning about information systems design, even those about its fragmentation, cannot be made. In exploring the possible outcomes of this realisation it may be observed that recent authors identify this problem as one of the limitation of method (Introna & Whitley, 1997; Fitzgerald & Howcroft, 1998). Noting that the debate between ‘soft’ and ‘hard’ methodologies can never be resolved Fitzgerald and Howcroft (1998, p 324) call for a ‘strategy of dissolution’ and pronounce that the whole debate between realism and relativism is ‘sterile and vacuous and no longer important’. However, while such announcements are laudable the commercial promotion and success of relativist methodologies is a strong testimony to the need felt by many academics and practitioners to believe that what they do can be justified or explained by a set of principles which stands apart from their practice, by a theory. One cannot argue against that need, nor can one dismiss the narrative of self-discovery it often produces, the narrative in which conversion to a theory in information systems development leads directly to a revolution in practice. But this is a narrative that belongs properly to a realist information systems hero, to someone who has just discovered a truth above the situational and now returns to implement it; it cannot, without contradiction, be the narrative of a relativist hero who can only enact his heroism by refusing to take either comfort or method from his creed. It is a hard move to make or not to make because it brings so little immediate satisfaction and leaves the would-be relativist theorist with so little to do. If information systems theorists renounce realist epistemology there remains two alternative courses of action: either theorists may assume that design practice has nothing to do with theory, or they can make the opposite assumption and continue to try to use relativist theory as the basis for further developments in information systems methodology. By choosing the latter theorists give themselves the incalculable advantage of having something to say and something to sell. This advantage to theorists and philosophers is noted in the Ambrose Bierce (1989) definition of the noumenon in his work, The Devil’s Dictionary:

‘Noumenon, n. That which exists as distinguished from that which merely seems to exist, the latter being a phenomenon. The noumenon is a bit difficult to locate; it can be apprehended only by a process of reasoning—which is a phenomenon. Nevertheless, the discovery and exposition of noumena offer a rich field for what Lewes calls “the endless variety and excitement of philosophic thought”. Hurrah (therefore) for the noumenon!’ (p 231)
