# The status of women in the UK IT industry: an empirical study

A Panteli<sup>1</sup>, J Stack<sup>2</sup>, M Atkinson<sup>2</sup> and H Ramsay<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Management Studies, University of Aberdeen, Edward Wright Building, Dunbar Street, Aberdeen AB24 3QY; <sup>2</sup>University of Glasgow; <sup>3</sup>Strathclyde University, Scotland

In this paper we analyse the state of women in IT occupations in the UK. Although the growing UK IT industry is experiencing skills shortages, there is a declining trend in the representation of women. Using data from nationwide surveys and case study research, the paper aims to increase our understanding of gender occupational segregation in the UK IT industry. Our findings suggest that even though the IT industry does not exclude women, it does little to promote them or even to retain them in the field.

## Introduction

This paper derives from the need to gain a comprehensive understanding of the gender composition, work and career patterns of women in the UK information technology (IT) industry. Given the explosive growth of the IT market over the last decades and the industry’s skills shortage, software employees constitute an increasingly vital component of the labour force. It is therefore of significant importance for organizations in this industry to use all potential resources in the best possible way.

Although there has been an increase in the number of women entering IT occupations since the seventies, women are under-represented in all member states of the European Union. This is an industry that has been predominantly white, middle-class and male-dominated. Men in general are more likely to be seen as the designers, developers and managers of systems whereas women are seen as the users of these systems (Grundy, 1996). In no member state of the European Union is the proportion of women in the IT workforce estimated to be above 30% and in most cases the proportion is closer to 20%. While all European Union member states report an under-representation of women, Spain and France consider that women are increasing their presence in these jobs and will continue to do so in the future, while Germany, Italy, Belgium and the UK see a declining trend in the share of women among software developers. What is most significant for our study is that, out of all member states of the European Union, the UK has been experiencing the worst decline in the proportion of women into the IT sector (Social Europe, 1993).

During the 1980s, women accounted for a quarter of the workforce in the IT industry in the UK (Orldroyd, 1996). Since the early 1990s, during a period when women accounted for some 45% of the UK labour force (UK Labour Force Statistics, 1991) the average proportion of females in IT has ranged between 20% to 22%, falling to 19% in 1993. Similarly, Wright (1996) has identified similar rising and then declining trends in the IT industry in the US, and talks of a remasculinisation of the field after two decades of feminisation.

During this same period, the IT industry is growing faster in the UK than in the rest of Europe. In 1995, the UK market for software and computing services increased by 18%, experiencing its fastest growth in real terms for a decade (Financial Times, The Holway Report, 1996). The overall turnover of the companies in this sector has grown by 22% and further growth is expected. This growth is also evident in the fact that there is an increasing demand for IT skills. According to a recent survey by the Higher Education Careers Services Unit, which contains an analysis of about 8000 graduate vacancies over a two-year period, more than a fifth of all the advertised posts were for work in IT and management information (The Scotsman, Recruitment, June 13, 1997, p 45). Also, according to a recent report commissioned jointly by the Scottish Software Federation and the government, there are 33,000 unfilled IT jobs in the UK, with around 10% of these in Scotland<sup>1</sup> (The Herald, July 16, 1997, p 24).

A possible explanation for the low representation of women in IT occupations is the decreasing number of female students who graduate with a computer science degree. Indeed, women’s share of computer science courses fell from around a quarter in the 1970s to 12– 13% in the late 1980s (see Rubery et al, 1992 in Social Europe, 1993). Recent evidence also shows that only 12% of full-time undergraduates on British computer science courses are women (Education News, 1996). According to researchers in this field, it is the image of computing as technical and male-dominated, the perception that computing work is a field for antisocial individuals, and the socialisation and identification of women themselves away from maths and science and thus not suited for such work which filter out the majority of females from pursuing computing as a career (Decker, 1986; Newton, 1991; Grundy, 1996; Siann, 1997; Wilson, 1997). Drawing on such findings, a number of organizations and associations target the promotion of countervailing views as the chosen means to change the status quo for the better (e.g. Women in Science and Engineering, Women into Computing, etc.).

Although the declining number of female students who graduate with computer science degrees should be given serious attention, this should not be seen as the sole reason for the low representation of women in IT occupations. Due to the diverse nature of IT occupations there are in fact increasing opportunities to enter the industry from a wide range of backgrounds. During the late forties and fifties programming was the major type of computing work, but in the nineties IT also includes jobs in the areas of systems management, communications and networks, systems development, technical support, operations, user support and help-desk operations (NCC, 1992, 1997). Efforts to establish more communication and collaboration with users have resulted in the creation of yet more job specialties (Beirne et al, 1998). The diversification of specialties and skills in this industry offer prospects not only for computing qualifications but also for those with business-like qualifications (Tijdens, 1991) or post-graduate IT degrees to enter IT occupations. Thus, women should theoretically at least, be increasingly involved in IT, especially in an age of increasing equality of education and opportunity. Furthermore, a study by Carey (1991/92) shows that 47% of all new entrants to computing work in the US were from other occupations within their employing organizations. Despite these opportunities, however, women are also more likely to leave IT work than are men (Wright, 1997). In this paper, our intention is to examine the current status and state of women in the IT industry and identify why a diversified IT industry may operate to the disadvantage of women even after they have entered IT occupations.

## Ghettoisation and masculinity in IT

In many occupations, the sexual division of labour has been established through an historical sedimentation of role allocation such as the clerical (female-dominated) and engineering sectors (male-dominated). Computing, which has only come into existence during World War II (Kraft, 1977), might be seen as potentially less bound by traditions, for example, as men’s or women’s work, and therefore as a gender-neutral occupation offering opportunities to both men and women to enter and progress. Despite its newness, however, several research studies (Kraft & Dubnoff, 1983; Donato, 1990; Baroudi & Igbaria, 1995), mainly based in the US, found gender segregation in this sector as much as in other longer-established technically-oriented and male-dominated professions. Nonetheless, due to its recent history, it may be argued that this sector allows us to examine the working through of gender issues more clearly, since these issues have only come to the fore in the recent past. This in turn may allow clearer testing of theories about the nature and resilience of gendered divisions, and the construction of gendered organizational cultures. In this section, we present the occupational culture of computing.

According to existing research, there is growing evidence of segregation of male and female work in IT occupations (Strober & Arnold, 1987; Donato, 1990). Women are still found mostly in the non-managerial group of IT personnel, such as analysts or programmers, while men are predominant in managerial positions, such as project leaders or IT managers (Baroudi & Igbaria, 1995). Significant salary differences have also been reported between males and females (Truman & Baroudi, 1994). In Reskin and Roos’ (1990) terms there are signs of ghettoisation in this industry: women are increasingly concentrated in specialities that are becoming female-dominated but are at the same time of lower status than other IT jobs. Meanwhile, the emergence and progressive reinforcement of a masculine culture in the IT field (Tierney, 1995; Wright 1996) is argued to compound the ghettoisation of the sub-professions and to make women more likely to leave computing jobs than men.

In computing, a particularly resilient and prevalent version of masculinity is argued to be especially prominent—that which attaches to a putative male superiority in handling ‘technology’ (Faulkner & Arnold, 1985; Wajcman, 1991; Henwood, 1993; Grundy, 1996). Hemenway (1995) describes four ‘forces’ which make for discrimination and pressure for women in industries like computing. The first is a catch-22: women with traditional feminine work styles, seeking consensus and team effort, are seen as less confident and capable than men; while those women who emulate masculine behaviour are seen as aggressive and disliked by men for not conforming to feminine stereotypes. A second force is the tendency for employees to cluster with others like themselves. This can work to the disadvantage of women and the advantage of men, because males associate with, take advice from, and establish trust relations creating a network more likely to supply key information and contacts relevant to promotion opportunities. Similarly, Tierney (1995) reports networking advantages for ‘the lads’ specifically in the computing sector. Hemenway’s third force is the reproduction of ‘mental roles’ of success in the image of senior males. This will also tend to lead to the potential of female candidates to be devalued through such comparison. Fourth, male culture behaviours (‘harmless’ joking, ‘laddish’ conversation, discussion of male interests, and in extreme form, harassment) prove stressful and, not so much individually as by sheer attrition over time, can erode women’s self-confidence and ability. These gendered processes are confirmed in Acker (1992).

Other accounts develop this image of masculinity in computing and its effect on women (Kiesler et al, 1985; Henwood, 1993; Wright, 1996). Wright (1966) reports research which shows how stereotypes about computing jobs as ‘male’ are held by both men and women at work. Turkle (1984, 1995) argues that men are more likely to be comfortable with a ‘hard’ computing culture that emphasises mathematical calculation and working with source code, while women are more comfortable working through interfaces that permit a ‘soft’, flexible approach, allowing experimentation, a non-sequential and a non-hierarchical approach. Mcllwee and Robinson (1992) suggest that computing is dominated by an ideology of technological solutions to problems, with technology as the field of male engineers. This generates an interactive style, acting out this image of engineering rationality, privileging ‘tinkering’ with hardware as a display of masculine dominance and control over the machine. Women may play this role, they argue, but will be disadvantaged by their discomfort with it.

On the other hand, Henwood (1993) suggests that this dichotomy between masculine, technical competence and feminine incompetence is not as stark as these accounts imply, nor does it accord with the experience of many people. The discourses of computing technology and power were contested in Henwood’s assessment. Women were ambivalent in the face of images of masculine work conveying status, and of traditional feminine work which might ghettoise them. Meanwhile, Gill and Grint (1995) warn against the drift to essentialism in the criticisms of ‘masculine’ technological styles, the danger being that women are branded as innately collaborative or consensual in the celebration of skills which tend to be devalued, but that in the process they are nonetheless presumed different. This tension within feminism and gender studies is not a new one, of course, but is no less of a dilemma for that.

What emerges from this literature is that computing may be particularly vulnerable to stereotyping of female and male roles through the ideologies of respective styles and competencies, whether these are accurate or not. If they are descriptors of real differences, then they are accompanied by a devaluation and marginalisation of feminine skills and styles; if they are misplaced, they still brand and help to herd women into ghettoised tasks and positions. With our present study we hope to clarify some of these issues.

## Our research study

Much of the analysis of gender issues in computing has concentrated in the US, and there is thus a need for a comparable depth of knowledge on the specifics of the UK. Moreover, there has been insufficient research on gender and IT in the UK context, which may differ from the US situation (see Panteli et al, 1997). Although several surveys are published on an annual basis on IT skills and other employment issues in the IT industry in the UK (such as the NCC Survey on Staff Issues in Computing, Computer Weekly Survey on IT skills and employment), these remain ‘gender-free’ as the results are not analysed by the sex of respondents. We believe it is important to give voice to gender-sensitive research in order to compare job roles and status of males and females in this industry and thus provide statistical indications of the situation of women in the UK IT industry.

Indeed, the UK situation as described earlier in this paper has given particular cause for concern among those hoping for computing to exhibit the erosion of gendered inequality. There is an urgent need for research on gender and IT in the UK in order to understand the causes for and the consequences of these contradictory trends: the growing IT industry on the one hand and on the other the declining representation of women at a time when the overall female workforce has been increasing (Employment Gazette, 1992). In this paper our objectives are: first, to map the representation and distribution of women employed in different types of positions in the IT industry; second, to examine gender similarities and differences in attitudes in career in IT; and finally, to study ways IT organizations structure and define occupations and to examine the extent to which these contribute to gender segregation and gendered structures in IT workplaces. Quantitative and qualitative approaches will be used to meet these objectives. These are described in the following section.

## Research sources and methods

This is an interdisciplinary research project between social scientists (Panteli and Ramsay) and computing scientists (Stack and Atkinson), designed specifically to link the human and technical resources in the IT field, thus contributing to the production of meaningful research that is of both theoretical and practical significance. The study was carried out using a combination of quantitative and qualitative approaches. Our initial objective was to obtain nationwide statistical information on the representation of women in the IT industry, and information on the degree of industry segregation by both specialty and hierarchy. At this stage, we secured close collaboration with major industrial associations that have shown support and willingness to help at various stages of the research. Computer Weekly kindly agreed to analyse by gender the results of their 1996 Survey on IT Skills and Employment Issues (Computer Weekly, 1996). This survey was sent to individuals who held IT occupations in the summer of 1996. The questionnaire was mailed to 10 000 IT staff, randomly selected from the Computer Weekly database. There were 2236 returned questionnaires that were analysed, representing a response rate of 22%. There were 301 female participants representing 13.5% of the sample. We particularly asked that the following be analysed by gender: ‘reasons for leaving previous employer’, ‘factors that are important in choosing a new job’, ‘training opportunities provided by employer organizations’, ‘age’ and ‘current salary’.

Furthermore, the Institute of Data Processing Management or IDPM (recently renamed as the Institute for the Management of Information Systems), had given us access to their own 1996 annual report on IT Skills in the UK. The report uses a variety of sources, including among others ‘The Computer Economics Limited Computer Staff Salary Survey’. This survey “has provided a uniquely authoritative base (usually around 35 000 staff in around 500 companies) for analysing the in-post workforce, since it was launched in 1968” (IDPM Report, 1996, p 3). We found that the IDPM report provided the most comprehensive segregation of IT occupations in the UK by gender.

The obvious limitation of this part of our study is the absence of raw data which would enable cross-tabulations and detailed analysis of results. We readily acknowledge this as an area for future research. Nonetheless, we believe that the data made accessible to us by Computer Weekly and IDPM do provide some urgently needed illumination of the nature of gender segregation in the UK IT industry. Due to the different data that they provide, the two surveys are used complementarily in this study.

Furthermore, this statistical presentation, which raises many questions but does not provide explanations, is supplemented for this reason with case study research in four organizations. These involved the management information department of a public organization (Case A), a regional software development centre of a large organization (Case B), the software division of an engineering organization (Case C) and an independent software organization (Case D). These organizations were chosen to provide a variety of experiences. Data were collected through semi-structured interviewing and documentation review in order to reveal background information about the organizations. Interviewing aimed to understand the organizations’ account of how they view women’s employment and career progress in the area of software development. By talking to both managers and employees/developers, we were interested in gathering information about organizational policies such as recruitment processes, human resource management policies and training procedures. Though the interview questions were pre-determined to ensure that the needed information was collected, these remained open-ended in an attempt to record the views and perceptions of the respondents. This allowed questions and issues that were revealed during the interview to be explored further. Interviews lasted from 40 to 90 minutes and were taperecorded when permission was given by the interviewees. Having explained the methods used, the following sections report the findings of our research.

## Results based on nationwide statistics

## The gender segregation of the UK IT industry

The growth experienced by the IT industry has led to an occupational expansion both in terms of specialty and hierarchy (see Tijdens, 1997). As a result, a wide range of different job titles are found in this industry with Computer Weekly and IDPM identifying 43 and 76 different job titles respectively. User support and networks have been the fastest growing specialties in this sector in the UK during the last ten years (NCC 1987, 1997). Networks and communications have also been cited as vital for the future (IDPM, 1996; NCC, 1997). Titles that have emerged associated with networks are those of network services supervisor, senior network engineer, network engineer and junior network engineer. In communications, emerging titles are those of chief communications analyst, senior communications analysts, communications analysts and junior communications analyst (IDPM, 1996). However, it is difficult to relate with certainty this increasing number of job titles to the industry’s growth due to the lack of standardisation of job titles in IT. Different organizations use a variety of titles to describe the same job. Nevertheless, this continuous emergence of titles in the IT industry further supports evidence presented earlier that IT is a fast developing industry, producing an increasing demand for different skills to meet the advancements of technology and the changing needs of the business community.

Unlike previous studies based in the US (Donato, 1990; Reskin & Roos, 1990), which found that women are increasingly concentrated in a limited number of specialties such as systems analysis, programming and operations, our data shows that although the proportion of women is relatively higher in these areas, they are visible across all specialties. Women are also found in the expanding areas such as networks and communications (see Table 1). The level of their representation across the different specialties, however, varies.

Interestingly, the proportion of women reaches 40% and above only for those specialties that are related to administrative and support functions: e.g. help desk assistant 62.4%, help desk analyst 47.3%, help desk supervisor 47.1%, assistant user support analyst 44.2% etc. The proportion of women in the programming and the more technical-related occupations, reaches 25% or more in mainly junior positions (e.g. junior analyst programmer 26.2%), traineeships (e.g. trainee analyst programmer 29.4%), or assistantship posts (assistant technical analyst 25%).

In contrast, the presence of women declines on moving up the hierarchy. For instance, although in 1996, women constitute 11.7% of management in all areas, only 7.3% of heads of IT departments are women. This is an increase from only 4% in 1994 (Orldroyd, 1996).

Women are not only under-represented at the supervisory and managerial level but they are also underrepresented in the higher ranks of the technical track (or the ‘professional track’ as in Igbaria et al, 1997). For example, although they constitute 26.9% of the total number of trainee programmers, only 8.3% of principal programmers are females. Similarly, although there are 25.5% female assistant technical analysts, only 6.2% of the senior technical consultants are female. Clearly, women are disadvantaged in both the management and technical career development tracks even though their presence is evident across the various IT occupations.

The low representation of women in IT managementrelated occupations might be because managerial jobs are often associated with years of experience (Tijdens, 1997). This is clearly shown in the Computer Weekly survey where 63.4% of all those (males and females) who reported IT (and data processing) management as their main job function are above the age of 35. However, while the proportion of the female workforce under the age of 29 is 24%, this drops to 21% for women over 35, which shows that women are opting out earlier.

Furthermore, women who have reached the senior ranks are also disadvantaged in terms of salary. The average salary for females in the IT industry is £26,026 and for men is £29,870. Only 27% of women but 39% of men have a salary of £30,000 and above (Computer Weekly). Salary differentials are also beginning to rise as women get older. Earnings of females under the age of 24 correspond to 95% of the average earnings for that age-group in this industry, but this falls to only 85% for those aged 40–44. It is hard to deduce the reasons for this from the statistics, but this rising earning differential might be due to the fact that women in general get a lower salary or that they often sacrifice a full-time career for their family. Some may also decide to leave the industry altogether for these reasons, which might explain the declining number of women in the workforce over 35. Career breaks and part-time work also appear to counteract career development in both the technical and the management track. Due to career breaks women take longer to move up the hierarchy because of the need to keep up with technological advancement. Also, supervisory responsibilities are often associated only with fulltime work, marginalising women who work on a parttime basis.

A comprehensive analysis of women’s position in the IT industry requires an understanding of gender differences in attitudes to careers in IT. It is to this that we now turn.

## Gender attitudes towards job selection and career criteria

Participants in the Computer Weekly survey were asked to indicate the level of importance (very important, quite important, not very important, not at all important) for several factors (i.e. job status, responsibility, job interest, work environment, employer reputation, job security, job type, sector, salary, fringe benefits, location, career prospects, challenging, ongoing training) when choosing jobs in the IT industry. The proportion of males and females who rated these factors ‘very important’ in job selection is shown in Table 2.

The most striking finding from Table 2 is that a high percentage of both sexes rated interest and challenge in their job as very important; however, it is worth noting that in both cases the percentage of females rating this as very important is even higher than the males. A greater percentage of women than men also rated responsibility and ongoing training as being very important when choosing a job. Interestingly, a low proportion of both males and females rated job status as very important. Figure 1 (which extracts statistics from Table 2) illustrates these differences more clearly.

The attitudes of men and women show similar levels of importance attached to factors such as work environment, employer’s reputation, job type and career prospects; but a slightly higher proportion of women than men rated these as very important.

A greater proportion of men rated salary as being very important whereas a greater proportion of women placed importance on location. Salary and location are the factors which show clear distinctions in gender attitudes. These findings are also supported by the results shown in Figure 2 on reasons for leaving a previous employer. Salary and location are again the factors that appear quite distinct for men and women.

## Results based on case-study research

## Gendering IT occupations within software development organizations

The nationwide statistics raise questions that warrant further examination. Case-study research can provide insights into the experiences of specific organizations, and so we attempt in this section to develop a contextbased explanation of gender segregation in IT. In this section, we examine the factors that contribute to the gendering of occupations in the IT industry by considering occupational segregation and its relation to gender in the cases of four software organizations. The organizations concerned recognised this situation and have cooperated willingly in this research. The low proportion of women and their under-representation at the higher ranks of both the technical and management career paths were reflected in all four organizations that participated in our study.

Case A is a department which provides management information to a public organization. It has 20 members of technical staff. The proportion of female technical staff is 45%. Out of nine analysts/programmers six (66.6%) are females, but out of six project leaders only one (16.6%) is female. Of the three operations staff, two are females. Both development and operations managers are males.

Case B refers to a regional software development centre of a large nationwide organization. This centre employs 190 development staff and is supported by administrative staff and some contract workers. The proportion of permanent female staff is 17.4% (33 out of 190) of the total number of permanent development staff in the centre. The proportions of female staff at the various hierarchical levels are as follows: engineers: 18.6% (26 out of 140), team leaders: 13.5% (Five out of 37), group leaders: 11.1% (one out of nine), centre managers 0% out of three. According to a centre manager: ‘although these numbers seem low, they have to be compared with the past. In the past we had a difficulty attracting female graduates to come for interviews. Currently . . . we use recent female graduates who have joined the company as role models to attract new female staff. The centre had 42 new staff last year and eight of them females. From these, six came as graduate trainees’.

Case C is a software division in an engineering organization. This division consists of 33 staff, including some contract staff. There are 10 females (30.3% of the workforce), making the proportion of female staff in Case C higher than the average percentage of women in this industry. Women are found across all levels apart from management. The only divisional manager is male. Also none of the contract staff at the time of the research were female. At other levels, women constitute 67% of graduate trainee software engineers (four of six), 33% of software engineers (two of six), 33% of senior engineers (one of three), 22% of principal software engineers (two of nine) and 25% of senior principal software engineers (one of four).

Case D is an independent software organization. This family organization, founded in 1986, has gained international reputation since the launch in 1992 of its core product, a customer management system for mail orders. This has resulted in a dramatic expansion. The company currently has over 80 employees split into self-managing teams. Employees are multi-skilled and teams are crossfunctional. The majority of the staff work in deployment which deals with implementation of the company’s core product to client organizations. Other functions of the company are development (that maintains and introduces new features on the product), marketing, training and administration. There are 14 female staff in the company and the majority of them are in administration. There is no female in deployment even though this is the biggest function, but the company has recently recruited the first female developer. The use of job titles is restricted in this company. According to a member of staff: ‘if you give people a job title, you limit them as to what they can do’; everyone therefore in Case D is encouraged to move around and experience different aspects of the job in order to ‘learn new things and have a challenge’.

Cases A, B, C and D support the argument made earlier that due to the lack of standardisation of job titles even in a particular specialty, a wide range of titles may emerge describing similar functions. At a first glance then, Cases B and C may appear more hierarchical than Cases A and D where many of their staff have the same job title and are called analysts/programmers or developers respectively. Out of all four organizations, it is only in Case B that job titles can identify core functions. Here, a team leader has the responsibility for a small group of four to five people who work on the same project; this person is also responsible for the planning and organization of the project and reports to the group leader. A group leader has the responsibility for about 30 people who are allocated into different projects.

In contrast to Case B, in the other hierarchical workplace, Case C, promotion and seniority in title do not necessarily secure higher status. As a female principal software engineer puts it: ‘[even after a promotion] you don’t actually see any change in your job. Maybe through payment but it is not related to the actual job title. During the last two years I got responsibilities for other people but it is not actually due to [my] job title. You don’t necessarily have a team as a senior engineer. You get to the ‘Principal’ and you find that there is nowhere to go, so they create new job titles’. Therefore, even though in this organization there is a higher than average proportion of women in senior positions and thus, women are seen to be moving up the hierarchy, the seniority in their job title does not necessarily secure an involvement in decision making, team leadership and organization.

Having the same job title does not secure similar responsibilities. For instance, in Case A, one analyst/programmer is mainly a programmer with some supervisory responsibilities, while another is mainly providing user support. There is also one (the only one) female project manager. She wants to move to a higher salary grade which is a post for managerial staff. ‘The promotion will be more for recognition. The last three years I have undertaken a lot of responsibilities’. She was told that the post needs to be advertised first. The promotion procedures of the organization (there are only 15 promotions every year on the administration side) act as a barrier to her advancement. As she puts it: ‘in large institutions, management information departments can be invisible and the efforts that staff make are not recognised; anything to do with computing is blank’. Her view is reinforced in a way by a statement made by a male colleague from another department in the organization: ‘I am not surprised at the high proportion of female staff in the management information department; it looks like a clerical job, doesn’t it?’.

The lack of part-time scheme could be a serious hurdle to women’s retention in the IT industry. In Case B, a female team leader decided to leave the organization when part-time work was not available when she returned from maternity leave. Case A also abolished its part-time policy two years ago because it was thought by the department’s management that such a policy hinders productivity and the development of staff since they are not available for training. As a female project manager puts it: ‘From an employee’s point of view, it shouldn’t be the case; from a manager’s point of view, I can understand it. When your staff are on a part-time or half-day work, you don’t have the resources to meet users requirements such as to answer their urgent queries’. The same person also added: ‘flexibility to work from home was an issue that was discussed, but never put into practice’. Out of the four organizations, only in Case D was there a part-time scheme operating successfully. As a manager put it: ‘All we want to see at the end of the day is that the job is done. So, we encourage all our people, we try to keep good staff by inventing good ways for them to work . . . technology can help you do this’. Case D uses voice-mail and e-mail to be in contact with employees who work away from the office either in clients’ sites or at home.

Clearly even though these organizations make conscious efforts to recruit women we cannot identify IT as promoting gender equality in any of the four organizations. Interestingly, employers/managers in all four organizations claimed that the under-representation of women in this industry is due to the small number of female students studying computing science. Despite the fact that these organizations have been supportive of initiatives that encourage female students to enter computing science degrees, these cases together present a variety of constraints for women within the IT industry. Stereotypes such as ‘males have a more burning interest in technology’ and ‘women are better at customerfocus’, ‘women don’t complain as much about the boring tasks assigned to them’ and ‘part-time work makes it difficult for supervisory responsibilities’ hinder women’s progression in this fast-changing industry. Also, lack of part-time and flexible work schemes and reluctance to innovatively use technology, all combine to deter retention.

## Discussion

We began this study by noting the contrasting trends of the UK IT industry. While the UK has the fastest growing software market in Europe and demand for new recruits is bigger than in any other industry, at the same time the UK is experiencing a declining proportion of women in these occupations. The findings from both nationwide statistics and the case-study research dispel arguments made by the participating companies that IT workplaces are gender neutral and that the problem is an educational one only. Our study suggests that women in the UK IT industry do not receive equal pay for equal work nor do they share equally in the opportunities for advancement. Salary differences, limited career development opportunities due to career breaks and part-time and flexible work are factors that retain the disadvantaged position of women in this area as it is the case with other traditionally male-dominated industries. These findings also confirm the results of similar studies carried out in The Netherlands (Tijdens, 1997), Ireland (Rafferty, 1997), Switzerland and Germany (Sonnentag, 1994), and the US (Baroudi & Igbaria, 1995; Truman & Baroudi, 1994; Wrights, 1996).

In this study we have identified the growing market and diversification of the IT industry as creating opportunities for women to enter and advance in this field. The industry does not exclude women directly. In fact the participating companies make conscious efforts to recruit female staff by for instance using female IT staff as role models. Further, the divisional manager of Case C gave examples of the achievements made by female developers in his department during his invited lecture on ‘software development’ to first-year computing science students. During an interview, he later emphasised not only the degree of organization and planning required in software development but also the repetitive nature of some of the tasks: ‘I certainly find that some of the tasks that we are having to address as engineers. females are better at addressing them’.

Should we, however, accept the view that the IT industry needs women because women are carriers of those attributes which the industry currently requires? As suggested earlier (see Figure 1) a higher percentage of women than men rated responsibility, interest and challenge as being very important in selecting a new job, whilst when asked (in the Computer Weekly survey) ‘what was the main reason you left your previous employer?’, 30% of the female respondents referred to the need for a fresh challenge (see Figure 2). Employers may indeed prefer women to enter the field but they also tend to reserve certain positions (e.g. managerial and senior technical positions) for men perpetuating sex segregation within the IT industry (Donato, 1990). As a female participant in the study recognised: ‘Characteristics that are admired such as open communication and collaborative approaches are not necessarily promoted’.

Clearly also, even though the expanding and developing IT industry creates opportunities for women to enter these occupations, it may also constrain their career development. The speed of change makes it difficult to catch up with technological advancements for those who take a career break, notably women who take maternity leave, and for those who can only work on a part-time basis, again mainly women with family responsibilities. Current policies and practices in the IT industry indirectly constrain women’s development in these occupations. Male dominating attitudes and perpetuating stereotypes seem to predetermine the positions that should be held by women who have managed to enter such occupations. Lack of innovative work practices and a career development path also hinder promotion. Further, we are finding that retaining women in the workforce, in addition to their recruitment is an area of concern (Cockburn, 1984). There is also a wider implication that the opportunity for women to influence the situation remains at a minimum because women are excluded from those higher-paid managerial positions where decisions are taken regarding the corporations IT strategy.

The industry certainly is not seen to be doing enough to utilise effectively either its human or technical resources. The under-representation of women who have managed to enter IT but who are persistently excluded from the higher echelons do little to change the image of the computing culture. Hacker (1989), for example, suggests that women’s decisions to exclude themselves from technical fields may be related in part to a dislike of the field’s ‘culture’. As discussed earlier, this has been described as an ‘engineering’ culture (Strober & Arnold, 1987), a ‘masculine’ culture (Knights & Murray, 1994; Wright, 1996) or even ‘lads’ culture (Tierney, 1995). There has also been research on women reported to enjoy their computing work but eventually leaving their employer organizations due to dislike of the gendered structures of their workplace (Ramsay et al, 1997). What emerges from our study is that lower occupational positions and also less challenging and less responsible work assignments do not only affect opportunities for professional growth and promotion but also distance women from the exercise of authority and from positions of power. In this situation, their efforts are likely to go unrecognised, a situation found in an earlier study of women end-users (Ramsay et al, 1997; see also Pain et al, 1993) reinforcing the vicious circle.

To conclude, the growth in IT should have opened up new possibilities for women to enter these occupations. However, the result has been used to construct and maintain gender differences and to create male hierarchies. Until women are more visible in all ranks of the IT industry, their current predicament will act as a future deterrent to further recruitment so perpetuating the status-quo. If the IT industry wants to increase women’s participation and influence in the development process, rather than simply to increase the total number of female recruits, then the issues that need to be addressed are much wider.

## Implications for practice

From our findings, it seems that women require a better deal in IT employment. A way to help them achieve this is to ensure that training and career programmes are more accessible to them and more adaptable to their needs. We particularly make three sets of recommendations in order to improve this situation:

The first group of recommendations include broadening career opportunities. Kunda (1992) found that considerable corporate efforts are made in high-tech organizations into designing a dual-career path that incorporates both technical and managerial career orientations. In our study, this dual ladder, which requires organizations to provide career paths for technically inclined and managerially inclined staff (Igbaria et al, 1995), is identified as ineffective for female IT staff. According to a senior female developer: ‘As I wish to remain in a technical role rather than a management role, I do not see much progression in my career, as far as promotions and the like go. I would hope to continue to learn and work on new projects using new technologies, if I remain in software’. This supports the findings of Igbaria et al (1995) who also criticised the dual career path as ineffective for IT identifying instead a rich diversity of career orientations held by information systems personnel.

A second set of recommendations concern strengthening training programmes. Training and development schemes should be comprehensive enough to incorporate the needs of female staff with family responsibilities. They should therefore provide training opportunities for women returning from maternity leave, as well as for staff, primarily women, who work on a part-time or flexi-time scheme. Using distance learning could be another way for training staff who have to work remotely or from home. Technological advancements open up new opportunities to employer organizations for innovation in their training courses. As IT is becoming one of the growing job markets, the industry has an obligation to accommodate more effectively females among the workforce by rethinking career development schemes, as well as training programmes, and thus building a healthier and supportive work environment for all staff.

Finally, a third set of recommendations concern changing the attitudes of employers, male colleagues and managers. Male-perpertuated attitudes and dominating stereotypes seem to pre-determine the positions that should be held by women who have managed to enter such occupations. Indeed, women themselves may come to believe the stereotypes.

Changes to traditional attitudes to women’s employment are vital. This requires a long-term vision and is the most challenging task which can only be implemented gradually with joint efforts between individuals, organizations, associations and government units. It demands a social and ethical responsibility to work together in order to tackle this issue and so prevent this recently formed and fast growing industry from falling further into the traditional route of a male-dominated environment.

## Implications for research

The companies that participated in our study have not been selected randomly but rather for their conscious efforts to employ female computing science graduates. Yet these organizations do not employ women in a gender-neutral way. Indeed, the situation may be worse, considering that the majority of employer-organizations do not take their responsibility seriously. Further casestudy research is therefore required for a more comprehensive analysis of the gender-based patterns found in IT occupations.

Due to the increasing demands for skills, organizations have a responsibility not simply towards providing access for women to enter IT but also, and most importantly, to secure their progression and even retention in this industry. The IT industry has therefore an obligation not only to innovate in product development but also in work and organizational arrangements. We suggest that this is placed in the agenda for future research in the area of gender and occupation segregation in the IT industry.<sup>2</sup>

There is a growing literature on the importance of diversity in the workforce (e.g. Watson et al, 1993). Bulkeley (1995) found that IT organizations with a workforce diverse in sex, race and age are more progressive in terms of innovative ideas and skills than those without. A diverse workforce introduces ideas and experiences into the workplace that may never be considered in a more traditional white male-dominated environment. Furthermore, it was found in Bulkeley’s study that one of the benefits of a diverse workforce is that it improves the social skills of IT staff enabling them to work more effectively within the user community. Also, according to the IDPM report: “. . . the fact that many of those organizations which are most imaginative in their use of IT to meet commercial objectives and/or consumer needs have a higher than average proportion of women in post suggests that the others are seriously losing out” (IDPM, 1996, p 50). More systematic research is required to understand the value of gender diversity (and other forms of diversity, e.g. racial diversity) in IT. This can be done, for example, through a longitudinal study of an IT organization with initially a male-dominated workforce and which is moving towards gender equality.

The study reported in this paper derives from the efforts of an inter-disciplinary team. We feel that close collaboration between computing scientists and social scientists is essential for the appropriate methodology to be developed and results to be interpreted with understanding from both disciplines. The software development process, for instance, needs examination and interpretation from technical as well as organizational aspects, but these aspects are also interdependent and need collaborative analysis. If change is to occur in the gender composition and career patterns in the UK IT industry, then findings from this research must inform the IT community through relevant publications and organizations. Further research of this nature should take place we believe, as many questions have been raised through our findings. It is necessary for both disciplines to recognise the validity of this type of research, and to ensure that both the terminology and the theory employed are not mutually exclusive.
