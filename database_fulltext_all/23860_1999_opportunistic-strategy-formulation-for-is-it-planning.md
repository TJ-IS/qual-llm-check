# Opportunistic strategy formulation for IS/IT planning

R Hackney and S Little

Manchester Metropolitan University, Faculty of Management and Business, Department of Business Information Technology, Aytoun Street, Manchester, M1 3GH, UK

Existing models and frameworks for information systems and information technology (IS/IT) planning are not robust enough to explain or predict strategic gains and many organizations are wary of investing further in their information infrastructure. Because technology is not the only ingredient of success, a complete and rigorous reconsideration of the techniques, options and approaches to IS/IT planning linked to business strategy is needed. It is argued that this can only be achieved through consideration of the recent organizational processes involved for creating a successful IS/IT strategy. This paper reviews aspects of both the literature on business strategy and IS/IT planning and considers case examples from two organizations in which the contrasting outcomes from the same technical innovation illustrate the dynamics of opportunistic strategy formulation.

## Introduction

There has been considerable academic and commercial interest in IS/IT in recent years abetted by the perceived benefits that could be derived from the technology and a dramatic increase in its use. In the UK, by 1993 the overall company expenditure on IT exceeded £15.5 billion (Willcocks & Lester, 1993). However, existing models and frameworks for IS/IT planning are not robust enough to explain or predict strategic gains. They are focused on measures reflecting the shorter-term effects of methodologies for technical efficiency derived from technology rather than the longer term gains in effectiveness which give rise to strategic opportunities (Swanson, 1987). Much research, reliant on survey data, obscures differences in the performance achieved by adopters because enterprises are aggregated by industry or size of budget rather than by strategy or tactics. Consequently, the evidence suggests that many organizations are wary of making investments into their information infrastructure.

It has been contended that an innovative use of technology may help in achieving sustainable advantage but it has been noted that technological ‘wizardry’ and innovating first may not necessarily be the complete path to competitive success (Earl, 1996). This is because whenever one company achieves a significant market position, competitors seem to ‘catch up’ even before the investment begins to pay back. Since the lessons of an expensive learning process may be assimilated rapidly by competitors and early adopters lack the guidance of the de facto standards evident to later movers, the cost of technical leadership can be substantial. Too often companies pursuing massive, integrated technical visions have faltered in the implementation stage while the reactive, incremental and opportunistic policies of competitors have delivered success in the market-place.

Two premises for the creative consideration of IS/IT planning are proposed which are based on recent literature in the field and expanded fully within the paper, as follows:

(1) It is important to consider the informal social relations, for example through coalition formulation, which are common in organizations to help in developing a good understanding of the complex managerial situations arising with an increased use of IT.

(2) There is a need for more interpretative case analysis in an attempt to reflect upon surfacing the important managerial issues and the development of more appropriate social and professional actions of the managers involved before success in systems exploitation can be found.

This paper will illustrate these issues with data from two longitudinal studies of IT innovation. Each study tracked a highly technical initiative promoted by the information systems staff. A specific class of system was involved called ‘expert systems’ which are a prime example of the ‘wizardry’ against which Earl (1996) cautions. The widespread enthusiasm for such systems during the eighties reflected both the intrinsic attraction of managerial workers to technical fixes, and the growing desire of IS/IT staff to impact directly on key processes in their organizations. However, the contingent and opportunistic nature of strategy formulation meant that the consequences in each organization (Railco and

Steelco) were strikingly different. One system was marginalised before completion while the other provided the basis for a new IT strategy for the support of core processes. These outcomes reflect the differential ability of the actors involved to respond creatively to strategic opportunities arising from both the innovative technology itself and the development processes it required.

## Towards business strategies

The unique challenge for linking an organization’s business strategy to its IS/IT strategy is the recent evidence that the strategy process generally is diverse, unpredictable and changing. This realisation is a significant issue for analysts who argue that strategy is both emergent (Mintzberg & Waters, 1985) and even serendipitous (Hamel & Prahalad, 1994). Developments in the field have been usefully classified into two distinct categories as either formal-rational or power-behavioural approaches (Waema & Walsham, 1990). The former considers strategy formulation as a series of prescriptive techniques which can be applied to obtain a level of fit between an organization’s resources, distinctive competencies and its environmental opportunities. The latter, however, contends that strategy formulation is not a rational process but requires a consideration of the specific context of the organization in terms of its culture and political structures. These two views on strategy are dominant, through a variety of forms, in the literature where proponents of both approaches seek to establish their respective validity. It is useful, therefore, to outline elements of each to enable a deeper understanding and to challenge some of their underlying assumptions for the needs of strategic IS/IT planning.

The origin of contemporary analysis into approaches to strategy formulation can be found in the field of thought commonly termed the ‘design school’. Here the essential components are the extent of congruence or fit between an organization’s internal structure and its external situation. Mintzberg (1990), in particular, notes that the design school ‘. . .places primary emphasis on the appraisal of the external and internal situations, the former uncovering threats and opportunities in the environment, the later revealing strengths and weaknesses of the organization’. The objective is to determine what a firm believes to be its main commercial strengths and then to match these to its wider competitive environment. Proponents of the design school essentially seek a strategic match between internal resource capability and external opportunity. This, it is argued, can enable an organization to achieve its competitive aims, following its strategy and structure, towards superior performance in terms of profitability and increased market share (Ansoff, 1965; Chandler, 1962; Andrews, 1971; Hofer & Schendel, 1978; Porter, 1980, 1985). For the purposes of this paper the main criticism of formal-rational views of strategy formulation is their failure to recognise and acknowledge the diversity and complexity of organizational realities (Franz & Robey, 1987).

More recent views on strategy formulation, however, suggest that organizational decisions are significantly influenced by cultural, political and power-behavioural situations (Mintzberg, 1987, Pettigrew, 1987; Prahalad & Hamel, 1990; Pettigrew & Whipp, 1991). Braybrooke and Lindblom (1963), for example, describe the notion of ‘muddling’ through the strategy process where outcomes are uncertain and unpredictable. This is in line with the later observations of Quinn (1980) who proposed that strategy develops incrementally through logical decision making and where organizations experience ‘strategic drift’, i.e. consequences which differ significantly from initial formal planning. Mintzberg and Waters (1985) further suggest that such strategy formulations will emerge over time and may not represent what was originally intended. There is also some evidence that managers within organizations establish small informal groups to aid their pursuit of objectives. These associations or coalitions provide the opportunity to secure more resources and to gain influence where there is mutuality of interest. Waema and Walsham (1990) note that ‘Increasingly, psychological, social and political behavioural processes have been found to be important in influencing the effectiveness of strategy formulation’.

Thus, in this view there may be a complete lack of managerial consensus towards organizational decision making which is a major assumption of the previously described formal-rational approaches. Theoretical underpinning and empirical evidence is growing that supports an analysis which considers power-behavioural perspectives. Pettigrew (1985), for example, adopts a useful approach which relates to the context, process and content of strategic decisions. This offers the added value of considering both the formal components (situational factors in context) and the informal components (political factors over time) relating to the processes involved in strategy formulation. Other analysts argue that the study of informal processes in organizations also provide valuable insights into managerial interdependencies and interaction (Tyre & Orlikowski, 1993; Hackney, 1996).

Stacey (1993) also observes, ‘. . .shared mental models determine how managers together construct their perceptions of reality and therefore what they agree to attend to’. Alternative paradigms have therefore been suggested which urge organizations to think differently from conventional formal leadership, restructuring and re-engineering approaches (Whittington, 1993). Clearly, there are a number of challenges for IS professionals if their current views relating to strategy are to include such a variety and complexity of assumptions, as Burnes (1992) observes, ‘. . .the case for how strategy is determined appears overdue’. Strategy formulation, therefore, involves an ability to articulate and capture a diverse, fluid and informal set of organizational characteristics which, to date, IS professionals regard as functional, quantifiable and certain. The challenge of strategic planning and its alignment to IS/IT planning is evident. This paper considers the consequences of these assumptions and subsequently offers useful insights into the basic beliefs relating to such planning.

## Strategic IS/IT planning

There is also a proliferation of literature which argues the concept of linking an IS/IT strategy with a business strategy. Much of it offers a range of prescriptive methods and frameworks designed to support the strategic objectives of organizations. Many of these frameworks present a top–down approach at variance with the diffuse, user-oriented nature of current IS/IT (Buchanan & Boddy, 1992). Researchers and consultants have consistently endeavoured to develop an appropriate use of IS/IT within organizations such that a sustainable competitive advantage could be achieved (Boynton & Zmud, 1987; Ward & Griffiths, 1996). However, this has been done with a formal-rational understanding of organizations and a lack of creative approaches to formulation. Some analysts have criticised the non-historical perception of such perspectives on the organizational planning for IS/IT. Others have provoked management to restructure work practices, cognitive aspects and organizational contexts and have searched for alternative models of economic competition on which to base their strategic models of IS/IT use.

Earlier works in the field include the IT Strategic Grid (McFarlan & McKenny, 1983) which helped managers to position their organization in relation to the strategic advantages they could obtain from IT. The concept proved to be widely adopted for matching the potential of IT with an organization’s strategy, the choices affordable to senior managers and the recognition of the changes in the organization’s external environment. Porter and Millar (1985) proposed the idea of a value chain which concentrated on the role of technology and an organization’s competitiveness. The usefulness of this framework is its conceptualisation of the added value from the technology itself. There is a proliferation of literature which argues the concept of linking an IS strategy with a business strategy. Earl (1989) provided, however, the first comprehensive review of IS through a range of prescriptive methods and frameworks designed to support the strategic objectives of organizations.

The views of Earl and other recent IS/IT management theorists (Wiseman, 1985; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1992; Burn, 1993) represent the mainstream literature for aiding the formulation and implementation of an IS/IT strategy. However, they mainly adopt, as noted, a formal-rational perspective where the managers in an organization are believed to have shared goals and a logical approach to decision making. Walsham (1993) suggests that much of this literature is related to the notion of logical incrementalism proposed by Quinn (1980). In this respect the predominance of IS/IT literature does not support an analysis of organizational realities. There is a social context for the development of IS which extends beyond prescriptive management frameworks and the functional design of technical systems (Waema & Walsham, 1990). Attempts to articulate the social influences upon IS/IT development have been proposed by Kling and Scacchi (1982). They argued that much of the analysis of computing (IT) in organizations is too narrow. The boundaries which analysts place around organizational studies often fail to capture the important social relationships which are critical for the successful development of these systems. They do not generally consider the social context in which IS/IT are developed and concentrate on the economic, physical and component parts of the technology. Consequently, the social, political and historic context of the technology is not made explicit.

The IS/IT strategy literature generally records the economic potential of IS/IT without reference to the necessary social relations and organizational innovations for its development. Analysts study the defined and official roles specified through job descriptions, etc, and assume a rational approach to decision making. They do not consider, for example, coalition formulations which are common in complex organizations (Kling, 1987, 1991). The prevalence of coalitions is an important organizational reality where resources are limited. It is the social context of organizations which identifies what options are available and what constraints are imposed upon managers. In addition the interactions between key groups, their values, beliefs and common practices are also critical (Orlikowski & Robey, 1991). It is argued that it is these interactions which influence creative IS/IT planning in organizations generally. The paper now considers case examples of this approach through two organizations.

## Opportunistic IS/IT planning

The stable framework of postwar international trade has been unravelling since the early 1970s (Dicken, 1992). The emergence of the dynamic globalised economy which has replaced it coincided with dramatic developments in information and communication technologies. A re-assessment of the role of the state in developed economies has accompanied these changes. The resulting global relationships have become increasingly dependent on these technologies. Consequently both publicly owned and private sector organizations have had to cope with turbulent new conditions to which the so called ‘top–down’ framework of mainstream strategic planning is increasingly irrelevant. Some commentators stress continuities in the present situation (Hirst & Thompson, 1996; Krugman, 1996) while others stress discontinuities (Ohmae, 1985, 1990). Many organizations have responded to the challenge of globalisation by emulating ‘world best practice’ wherever possible, particularly in their use of information systems and information technology. The succession of solutions which have been promoted combine good practice, genuine innovation and ‘snake oil’ in varying proportions.

The relationship between innovation in technical practice and the emergence of strategy is illustrated through two organizations representing the public and corporate sector. These organizations referred to here as Railco and Steelco, were the subject of a longitudinal study into the evolution of IS/IT strategies between the late eighties and the mid nineties. Both organizations were active in the investigation and promotion of expert systems within their business community. These systems represent a sub-set of knowledge-based computer applications developed from early artificial intelligence research. The simplest definition of an expert system is a computer program which attempts to augment or support expert decision making, indeed, they were initially promoted as direct replacements for human cognition.

Organizations prominent in the expert systems user community during the mid eighties can be regarded as ‘early adopters’ among their peers (Rogers, 1983), sharing a generally innovative orientation and likely to be equally innovative in other aspects of their operations. They can therefore be regarded as genuinely innovative.

The case studies focused on IS/IT innovation in Steelco, the steel-making division of a multi-divisional private corporation, and Railco, a public sector rail transport undertaking which has subsequently been substantially re-organised into smaller specialised companies. Both organizations monitored comparable overseas companies for policy ideas. Their desire for a strategic framework was evident in the strategic planning exercises for IS/IT conducted by each organization in the early nineties. In Railco this was conducted by external consultants whose proposals aimed at recovering from the consequences of uncontrolled end-user activity through the development of an enterprise database accessible from all platforms. The corresponding exercise in Steelco created a ten-year horizon for IS/IT planning within its steel-making division and was used to identify a set of priorities and necessary resources. Such exercises must necessarily bridge between the technical and institutional levels of organizational decision making (Little, 1990). In particular, they are vulnerable to the different time-frames affecting decision making at these levels (Little, 1987). The case studies consider the responses of Railco and Steelco to the opportunity to engage in IS/IT strategic planning provided by the interaction between technical and organizational imperatives. The very different outcomes serve to emphasise the opportunistic and contingent nature of such interactions.

## Technical strategies

Both organizations initiated expert systems development with existing mainstream applications. These fitted the classic view of expert systems as a replacement of the expertise of a highly specialised individual with a computer-based model. Railco made a successful proposal to the vendors of their mainframe computer to act as a trial site for the development of a knowledge-based application. The computer vendor provided hardware, software and personnel support for the company. Railco’s proposal was for an expert system applied to the rostering of operations staff, but this was changed to locomotive rostering on further consideration of the sensitivities surrounding such systems. Utilising a rapid prototyping methodology developed by the vendor an initial demonstration system for mainline locomotives was completed and presented after some eight weeks of work. However, over a year of development for operational systems followed. The overheads of familiarisation with both a novel software environment and a development task supporting a sophisticated process meant that this first component took longer than expected. In addition, during development, working practices were altered in ways that reduced the scale of the rostering task itself, with branch workings reducing, and shunt workings being re-allocated to mainline locomotives.

The size of the development team varied between one and four analysts but implementation required a considerable input from a ‘domain expert’, someone with deep knowledge of the activity supported by the system, who was not available for normal duties during that time. The trial implementation of the first module did provide him with more time but after a year of development work the system was abandoned.

Steelco enjoyed a clear advantage of size. It was able to support a research section charged with the evaluation of new technology using earmarked resources which did not have to be withdrawn or diverted from routine operations. Several laboratories were operated by the company and these dealt with the full range of production technologies in use. Individual plants retained their own technical development staff for operational development, but longer term issues which may become common to a number of sites were dealt with by the appropriate research laboratory. One laboratory also provided research and innovation support for IS/IT. Although the data processing departments of the entire organization were merged in 1990 to form an information technology division, innovative IT applications remained the responsibility of research laboratories which are able to acquire and evaluate new technology. Prototype applications were then offered to potential user divisions on a trial basis. Such a relationship with end-users ensured that the proponents of expert systems at Steelco developed networking and alliance building skills to complement their technical skills.

Despite the clear division between strategic and operational levels the laboratory’s resources were limited, and in effect reflected its success in diffusing technologies in terms of ‘sales’ to operating divisions. A key concern of laboratory staff was to identify potential clients who were not risk averse and who would serve as the ‘early adopters’ within the internal community of users.

Because of its smaller size, Railco had to balance resources for the development of innovative technologies against the continuing support of key existing applications. Here the perception of relative resource allocation seems to have been as important as the actual allocation in determining support for innovation. Railco had to develop the skills required to utilise a specialised expert systems ‘shell’ which forms the template for the applications. This was an expensive and time consuming process, even with a substantial contribution in personnel from the vendor.

Since the laboratories at Steelco only undertook to initiate developments in new technology, considerable work was put into the creation of software architectures that would enable users to maintain these systems. For example, the company developed its own software to translate the knowledge bases of expert systems into the more familiar form of database tables for on-site maintenance. This placed some restrictions on the range of potential solutions they could offer to the user divisions, but reflected a core concern with the issue of cost versus benefit in staff time, at both training and operating levels of the implementation process.

Once a client division was satisfied with a prototype, it took on the responsibility for making a case for resources, in conjunction with the IT division. The laboratories performed only a supporting role at this stage. The IT division was responsible for the implementation of the system in its operating environment and for its subsequent support. Prototypes were often made available on a hardware platform loaned by the laboratories, and budgetary and other restrictions became the key determinant of the ultimate software environment and hardware platform of the production system. However, on at least one occasion, a user-site retained its field prototype as a production system; resources were not forthcoming for the conversion from prototype to production system, nor for the consequent re-implementation on different equipment. In such cases, the flexibility which allows targeted innovation of technology with strategic potential creates problems of control. Despite such pitfalls, the IS/IT strategy developed by

Steelco allowed the evaluation of exotic technologies and their controlled introduction, albeit sometimes in a less exotic final form. This clear separation of identification, evaluation and implementation of new technologies gave Steelco a decisive advantage over Railco.

## Organizational context

Changes in the practice of IS/IT development and the limited resources for the provision of technical services within many organizations have produced conditions which are essentially those of an internal consultancy for developers. Interviewees in both organizations mentioned the need to negotiate projects with user groups. The impact of strategic planning exercises and structural and financial changes increased consciousness among these technical developers of the need to sell the advantage of any new system over current practices.

The IT staff in both organizations revealed a sensitivity to the organizational context of novel projects, distinguishing between the varying requirements of clients and potential users and the broader differences in outlook and priorities across the organizations. Broad organizational strategies utilising the technology to promote the role of IS/IT in high value, low volume transactions within an increasingly dynamic business environment were described and the sensitivity of such applications was acknowledged. Several comments indicated an appreciation of the linkage between institutional and technical frames of reference, previously noted, were discussed (Little, 1990).

The creative strategy most evident in Steelco reflected the needs of the research laboratories themselves. To develop and maintain a strategic role within the wider organization they sought entry to the core technical process of steel-making. They sought a self-contained entry point where resistance would be minimal, in order to establish credibility before approaching more critical operations. This resulted in a classic expert systems project to capture the knowlege of a retiring expert with a wealth of experience in the management of an installation for the production of sinter, a feedstock for steel production. This successful demonstration of the technology was followed by the development of a system to control the critical process of the continuous casting of steel. In Railco the pilot and proposed expert systems components became peripheral to the organization’s strategies. The developers were less insulated from day to day operational support but involved in a learning curve at least as severe as that at Steelco.

The notion of the need to educate the user in the new technology, and to communicate in meaningful terms, appeared in interviews in both organizations. The objective was to ensure that clients appreciated the time and cost implications of prospective developments. The difficulty that users faced in appreciating the resource overheads involved in producing robust production systems from demonstration prototypes was a concern. The need to establish an appreciation among users of the connection between input and output of the systems was also understood. If a system created extra work for a user group who did not see any direct benefit, it could be expected to enjoy little support, and would be vulnerable to any shift in resources or priorities. In particular, data which required demanding input could be jeopardised and systems which placed the burdens of maintenance on one group of users while delivering their benefits to another group were considered particularly vulnerable and therefore a lower planning priority.

Such concerns rightly apply to any substantial IS/IT development, however, the introduction of expert systems also implied a learning effort required on the part of the professional developers themselves, in addition to the wider processes of organizational learning identified by Sproull and Kiesler (1991). Expert system tools and development procedures differed from the established development methods used in the organizations. In particular, the need to use a prototyping approach for these novel applications was mentioned, along with the need to absorb the overheads involved with relatively unfamiliar software technology, and in some instances for some of the staff involved to gain more general computing experience. Ultimately the strategic success of expert systems technology at Steelco was achieved only at the expense of much of the potential originally perceived by its proponents, and abandoned with some reluctance. This reciprocal learning on the part of the innovators allowed the novel technology to be simplified and harnessed to routine processes which were themselves innovative. As a result the adoption of a total quality management (TQM) orientation for these processes finally provided the means of entry to core processes for the laboratory.

The Railco project was unable to undertake such a realignment. The pilot application was intended to make a substantial contribution to a core activity, but continuing rationalisation of operations impacted directly on the operations targeted. Revised operating and maintenance procedures reduced or removed the task of rostering locomotives for shunt and local trip workings. This change was a symptom of a substantial re-institutionalisation of the organization (Jepperson, 1991), connected to a federal government national freight strategy which was to transform the organization over the three years following initial contact with the organization and to lead eventually to its break-up. Interviewees at Railco divided into those who regarded the expert systems project as an expensive diversion and those who regarded it as a technical success denied full development.

By contrast, at Steelco, the advent of TQM practices and a corresponding awareness of the need to reduce variability of performance was used to promote the diffusion of expert systems technology, but at the cost of a radical simplication of the original objectives. The technical compromises were regarded as a necessary evil because of a shared view of corporate objectives and relevant technical support lacking at Railco. While the pilot expert system implementation was chosen with a view to gaining access to the more central steel-making processes, the goals of the system were revised from the classic one of the expert system replacing key human expertise to one of decision support facilitating consistency of performance among a range of competent performers. This consistency is a key component of the statistical approach to quality measurement incorporated into TQM. Almost a decade on, expert systems technology forms a key component of a strategy for the alignment of process control systems across the production process through intelligent scheduling.

## Internal markets

The strategic framework established by the formal reviews conducted at the beginning of the nineties had slightly different consequences for each organization. For Steelco it established the ground-rules of an emerging internal market-place which took the place of more formal planning mechanisms. In Railco the technical constituency was dominated by a traditional view of railways and their social role in national development, the operating division was already aligning with world best prctice, anticipating the forthcoming changes and promoting a cultural shift to a streamlined and focused bulk transportation service. In such an environment, a technical project based on established and traditional practices was doomed.

The processes observed in both organizations correspond to those noted in several Australian organizations where strategic fit emerged from adjustment between technology, available skills and management processes rather than from the top–down determination of strategy and appropriate structure (Yetton et al, 1994). At Steelco the growing capabilities in knowledge-based systems influenced the adaptation of total quality management practices to the company’s needs. In effect the emergent principles of strategy formulation could be observed where the benefits of an internal focus are apparent through attention to capabilities and resources (Hamel & Prahalad, 1990).

Some interviewees felt that the very success of rapid prototyping in promoting new technologies in the internal market-place was problematic in this context. The early demonstration of even partially functional systems created unrealistic expectations for the delivery of a production system. Such raised expectations were a critical component of the failure of expert systems innovations in Railco. In Steelco developments in the organization based around the diffusion of TQM methods led to a major re-assessment of the innovative technology. In Railco a similar institutional shift led to the re-definition of the role of the organization. This in turn led to the abandonment of the very activities supported by the innovation. Sauer (1993) suggests that the adjustment of both function and performance criteria for IS/IT needs to be continuous through the development and life of systems in order to maintain the support of users. The final routinisation or institutionalisation of innovations described by Rogers (1983) was not achieved by the systems examined by Sauer (1993), nor by Railco, but may yet be achieved at Steelco.

## Conclusion

This paper has presented an overview of the main features of business and IS/IT planning in organizations. The opportunities for IS/IT to be linked with an organization’s business strategy are clearly more complex than generally perceived. It was noted that descriptions are highly prescriptive and often represent a formal-rational approach to systems development. Most importantly organizational analysis of IS/IT should not be confined within a narrow boundary. The value of more holistic models are considered critical which include the social interactions and coalition building of the participants who influence the exploitation of both the systems and technology.

The basic premise within the paper is to demonstrate the value of an interpretive case analysis which exposes the complexity of IS/IT strategy and explains two distinct organizational outcomes. The introduction of an expert system is clearly not just a functional issue relating to the technical capabilities of the system. From the perspective of the technical constituencies involved, the work practices necessary to innovations such as expert systems and end-user applications provide a set of skills equally useful in the wider negotiation of support among other constituencies within complex organizations. This paper sought to address this social and political element in the planned adoption of these systems which is commonly ignored in the mainstream IS/IT literature.

This was further exemplified in the approaches adopted in the respective case studies where Steelco achieved some additional success through a creative IS/IT strategy which included prototyping systems through departmental capabilities and resources outside of the influence of formal organizational control. The broader context of a Zeitgeist emphasising downsizing and streamlining of core activities was coupled with outsourcing of contingent activities. At Railco this led to a redefinition of the core business which eliminated the activities supported by the innovation. The success at Steelco reflected a consensual understanding of both technical and organizational capabilities and objectives which may be difficult to achieve where even critical IT applications are outsourced. Further case analyses are needed to generate the qualitative and longitudinal data needed to determine whether more robust IS/IT planning in future business strategy models is achievable in prevailing business conditions.

Dr Ray Hackney is Director of Research within the Department of Business Information Technology (BIT) at Manchester Metropolitan University. His main professional research interests are defined within the identified areas of strategic management of information technology, information systems and organizational development, and innovation and technological change within a variety of private and public sector context. He has contributed to research in the field of information systems with presentations and publications in numerous national and international conferences and journals. He has a wide experience of management development through his teaching on a number of MBA programmes including MMU, Manchester Business School and the Open University. He also leads the organising committee for the annual BIT Conference series and serves on the Board of the UK Academy for Information Systems. Dr Hackney is the Vice President Research for Infor mation Resource Management Association (USA) and Associate Editor of the Journal of Global Information Management and Annals of Cases in IT Management.
