# Software reusability as synchronous innovation: a test of four theoretical models

T Ravichandran

Lally School of Management & Technology, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, NY 12180, USA

Information systems departments are being challenged to improve systems delivery performance. Software reuse is a potential strategy to address recurring systems development problems such as high development cost, low programmer productivity and long systems delivery lead time. However, the barriers associated with reuse implementation are significant. A better understanding of the administrative changes that facilitate reuse implementation is required to further research and practice in this area. We conceptualise reusability as a software process innovation that constitutes administrative and technological dimensions. Drawing from the synchronous innovation concepts, four theoretical models of the interrelationships between these dimensions of reusability and systems delivery performance are developed. Using the data collected through a postal survey these models are empirically evaluated. The results strongly suggest that adminis trative innovations intervene in the relationship between technological innovations and systems delivery performance and vice versa. This implies that synergies exists between administrative and technological dimensions of reusability which are more important in explaining variance in systems delivery performance than these dimensions individually. Implications of the study for IS theory, future research and practice are identified and discussed.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) managers are exhorted to adopt reusability in their efforts to address recurring systems development problems such as high development cost, low programmer productivity and long systems delivery lead time (Bassett, 1987; Boehm, 1987; Moad, 1989; Cusumano, 1991). Estimates suggest that more than half of the design, code and documentation for a new system already exists in some form, usually within the same organization (Jones, 1986; Anthes, 1993). If organizations could effectively tap into these ‘software assets significant cost savings and productivity gains can be achieved (Apte et al, 1990; Karimi, 1990; Banker & Kauffman, 1991). Furthermore, reusing well-tested software components is likely to reduce errors and improve quality (Bassett, 1987; Ince, 1988).

While reusability is a strategy of great promise, its promise has largely been unfulfilled (Biggerstaff & Richter, 1987; Parker & Hendley, 1988; Moad, 1989). Lack of a clear understanding of how reusability should be implemented is a major factor inhibiting reuse initiatives in organizations (Biggerstaff & Richter, 1987; Apte et al, 1990). Software reuse has been viewed as a technical innovation that involves code and design parameterisation, standardisation of data definitions and creation of repositories. Traditional recommendations for implementing reuse emphasise the use of standardised systems design methods and tools, disciplined planning, analysis and design processes and automation support for design and coding (Swanson et al, 1991). In contrast to the technical focus of these recommendations, recent studies attribute the success or failure of reuse initiatives to organizational factors. For example, Apte et al (1990) found that changes in reward structures were necessary for the success of reuse initiatives. Other factors identified to influence reuse implementation include top management support for reuse, organization wide policies that mandate reuse and changes in project cost structures (Biggerstaff & Richter, 1987; Tracz, 1987; Moad, 1989; Karim, 1990).

Literature in diverse fields such as manufacturing, technology management and information systems suggest that successful implementation of technological innovations depends on the simultaneous adoption of administrative innovations (Ettlie, 1988; Leonard-Barton, 1988; Dean & Snell, 1991). In fact, administrative and technological innovations are interdependent and could have synergistic effects on each other and on performance outcomes (Kimberly, 1981). Thus, it is necessary to focus on the interrelationship between administrative innovations and technological innovations to better understand their effects on performance (Collins et al, 1988; Markus & Robey, 1988).

Deep understanding of the processes through which organizations realise tangible performance improvements from reuse is required to identify organizationa levers that could be manipulated for successful implementation of reusability. In this paper, we conceptualise reusability as a software process innovation comprising of both technological and administrative dimensions and develop the theoretical relationships between these two dimensions of reusability and systems delivery performance. Specifically, we draw from the synchronous innovation literature to put forth four alternative models of how the technological and administrative dimensions of reusability interact to impact systems delivery performance. We also empirically test these models using the data collected from IS units in Fortune 1000 firms and large government agencies in the United States.

Three basic issues in previous work helped to frame the research reported in this paper. First, past research in IS innovations has focused on either technological innovations or on administrative innovations (Zmud, 1982; Swanson, 1994) but not much on the interrelationships between them. This study, by explicitly focusing on the interrelationships between administrative innovations and technological innovations complements past research on IS innovations. Second, reuse is considered an initial step towards a manufacturing approach to systems development (Apte et al, 1990; Cusumano, 1991). Hence, it is appropriate that research in manufacturing management inform studies on reusability. Specifically, we draw from the ‘synchronous innovation’ concepts reported in the manufacturing literature (Ettlie, 1988; Georgantzas & Shapiro, 1993) to develop the theoretica relationships of interest in this study. Third, past studies on reusability have largely used the case methodology to examine reuse in specific contexts. Examining reusability across a larger sample of organizations would complement and add to existing knowledge in this area. Hence, a survey methodology is used in this study.

The paper is organised into the following sections. Section 2 presents the theoretical foundations of the study followed by section 3 that presents a synchronous innovation perspective of software reusability and discusses four theoretical models that interrelate the administrative and technological dimensions of reusability and systems delivery performance. The execution of the empirical study is described in detail in section 4. Section 5 describes our analytical approach and presents the results of the statistical analyses. Finally, in section 6 the results are interpreted and their implications for IS research and practice are discussed.

## Theoretical foundations

Organizational innovation combines the development and implementation of new ideas, systems, products or technologies (Damanpour, 1991). Administrative innovations embody the adoption of administrative programmes, processes or techniques new to the adopting organization. New reward structure, autonomous work groups and policy deployment are examples of administrative innovations. Technological innovations incorporate adoption of production processing systems new to the adopting organization. Examples of technological innovations in the context of systems development include software process technologies such as CASE tools, object-oriented methods and information repositories.

Researchers have examined the effects of innovation on performance from administrative and technological perspectives. Typically, they treat administrative and technological innovations separately and emphasise the distinctions between the two (Daft, 1978). The technological perspective views “technology as an exogenous force which determines or strongly constrains the behaviour of individuals and organizations”. (Markus & Robey, 1988, p 585). The organizational perspective argues that whether and how a technology gets used are determined by people. Hence, administrative factors such as reward schemes and policies determine technology use and thus have a more significant influence on organizational performance.

In reality, implementation of technological innovations is a mutual adaptation process that involves both the reinvention of the technology and the simultaneous adaptation of the organization to the technology (Leonard-Barton, 1988). New technologies often require changes in work methods, outcome expectations and social relationships among organizational members. To accommodate these changes managers should alter administrative practices. On the other hand, the technology itself has to be adapted to fit the organizational setting in which it is used.

A recommended practice to facilitate such mutual adaptation is concurrent adoption of technological and administrative innovations (Ettlie, 1988; Leonard-Barton, 1988; Orlikowski, 1993). Ettlie (1988) refers to such overlapping adoption of technological and administrative innovations as ‘synchronous innovations’. He notes that while synchronous means coincident in time, synchronous does not refer to some random or chance coincidence of events. Rather, “it is the planned, simultaneous adoption of congruent technological and administrative innovations” (Ettlie, 1988, p 2). In several cases of advanced manufacturing systems deployment, he found that administrative practices were adopted specifically to facilitate the introduction of new technology. Examples include quality circles that were started specifically to configure and design flexible manufacturing systems and, new organizational structure that consolidates process engineering and database management to deploy computer-integrated manufacturing systems. He goes on to suggest that the synchronous principle holds regardless of whether new technology or new administrative practices is adopted first. Furthermore, since the two types of innovations are causally connected and overlap in time, they must be compatible to contribute to effectiveness.

## A synchronous innovation perspective of software reusability

We conceptualise software reusability as a synchronous innovation that encompasses both infusion of new technology and adoption of compatible administrative innovations. Implementing reusability requires adaptations to the development infrastructure to enable codification, storage, indexing, retrieval and use of software artifacts such as design models, code and programme documentation within and across systems development projects. From a technical standpoint, reusability involves implementing an information repository, populating the repository with parameterised design and code and constructing systems from these software components. The repository is at the heart of component-based system building because it contains information on and provides links between data resources, offers a means to store, index and access design models and code, and provides a means to integrate software components to construct a functional system. Thus, the extent to which an information repository has been implemented represents the technological innovation in the context of software reuse.

The administrative dimensions of reusability includes changes to the policies and structures within the IS unit that are specifically adopted to facilitate reuse implementation. Anecdotal evidence in the IS literature suggest that reuse implementation requires formalisation of the development approach through explicit policies that promote construction of reusable components and mandate component usage within and across systems development projects (Karimi, 1990; Cusumano, 1991). Specific actions taken by organizations to formalise reusability include recognition of reusability as a corporate objective, institutionalisation of company-wide efforts to plan for reuse and establishment of incentives linked to the extent of reuse (Karimi, 1990). Organizations such as Hitachi have successfully used reuse-linked rewards to overcome resistance from programmers to reuse software artifacts (Cusumano, 1991). Other studies have found that reward schemes linked to systems quality and programmer productivity could be effective in facilitating reuse adoption by programmers and analysts (Swanson et al, 1991). Thus, the extent to which formal policies about reuse and reuse-oriented reward schemes have been implemented represents administrative innovations in the context of software reuse.

Building systems from reusable components impacts systems delivery performance in dimensions such as cost, schedule adherence, systems delivery lead time and programmer productivity. Field studies indicate that both the Japanese and US firms have realised significant improvements in programmer productivity and systems delivery lead time through reusability (Jones, 1986; Joyce, 1988; Apte et al, 1990; Cusumano, 1991; Swanson et al, 1991). For example, GTE data services found that the productivity of its programmers increased by 20 to 30% after they started using a repository of COBOL, C and assembler codes (Anthes, 1993). Similarly, Swanson et al (1991) reported that adopting a reuse strategy at Celite Sales Corporation resulted in significant lead time reduction for development of program modules and, Canadian Airlines reported a 50% reduction in lead time in developing a frequent-flyer application by reusing design models (Anthes, 1993). In addition to improvements in programmer productivity and systems delivery lead time, software reuse can reduce variability in the development process since system construction is reduced to assembly of reusable components. This enhanced control over the development process that can result with software reuse should enable IS units to complete projects on time and within budget. Thus, we define systems delivery performance in terms of the following four dimensions: (1) adherence to time schedules, (2) adherence to cost budgets, (3) systems delivery lead time and (4) programmer/analyst productivity.

## Synchronous innovation models of reusability

Four descriptive models of the causal connections between administrative and technological innovations and organizational performance have been put forth in the operations management literature (Georgantzas & Shapiro, 1993). These models represent alternate forms of synchronous innovation and constitute the most rudimentary relationships between administrative innovation, technological innovation and organizational performance. These relationships include the independent, intervening, moderating and interaction effects between administrative innovation and technological innovation and, the impact of these interrelationships on organizational performance.

We adapted the four models of synchronous innovation put forth by Georgantzas and Shapiro (1993) to the software reuse context (Figure 1). These models depict the interrelationships between the technological innovations and administrative innovations in the context of reusability and their impact on systems delivery performance. These four models allow examination of both complementary and conflicting effects of administrative and technological dimensions of reusability on each other and on systems delivery performance. We discuss below each of the four synchronous innovation models of reusability and the theoretical rationale underlying the relationships between technological innovation, administrative innovation and systems delivery performance depicted by these models.

## Independent effects model

In the independent effects model, administrative and technological innovations affect performance independently. This model implies that while both administrative and technological innovations impact performance, the two may not influence each other. The technological and organizational imperatives discussed by Markus and Robey (1988) support this model as they attribute the responsibility for the outcomes observed to either the technology or the administrative practices. According to the technological imperative, information technology is responsible for ‘impacts’ on organizational forms and performance outcomes. Policy implications of this perspective include selection of information technology with particular features that are expected to have maximal impact on performance. According to the organizational imperative, the consequences of introducing information technology into a work setting are a function of the choices and behaviours of managers, systems designers and users. Thus, implementation strategies determine the nature of use and the impact an information technology has on performance.

## Intervening effects model

Two versions of the intervening effects model have been proposed (Georgantzas & Shapiro, 1993). In the first version, technological innovation affects administrative innovation, which in turn affects performance. Often the mere preparation for a technological innovation creates the need for the adoption of administrative innovations (Gerwin, 1988; Georgantzas & Shapiro, 1993). Typically, during technology implementation the innovation is tried out and evaluated. Discrepancies between the technology and the work setting are attacked through adjustments to the technology, the organization and in performance expectations. At the same time organizational conflicts caused by the changes in work setting and outcome expectations must be handled. These organizational adjustments require adoption of new administrative practices. For example, combining the role of programmers and analysts were required when CASE tools are implemented (Rai & Howard, 1993) and, changes in progress monitoring methods and staffing patterns become necessary when object-oriented methodologies are used for systems development (Fayed et al, 1996). These changes are necessitated by the nature of the technology and are required to facilitate the use of the technology. While a technological innovation may have been triggered by factors external to an IS unit, changes within the unit are required for it to utilise the technology and realise performance gains from it.

The second version of the intervening effects model depicts that administrative innovations affect technological innovations which in turn affects organizational performance. An appropriate innovation context within the organization increases a firm’s attention to the external environment, its awareness of the need for technological innovation and the likelihood of adopting novel technology-based solutions (Collins et al, 1988; Georgantzas & Shapiro, 1993). This rationale is generally supported in the management literature and is exemplified by the recommendations for the creation of organic structures to foster innovation (Burns & Stalker, 1961). Within the IS context, Zmud’s (1982) findings that organic overlays facilitated the adoption of modern software practices by IS units support this version of the intervening effects model.

## Moderating effects model

The moderating effects model also has two versions. In the first version, administrative innovation moderates the effect of technological innovation on performance. In the second version, technological innovation moderates the effect of administrative innovation on performance. Both models suggest that technological innovations and administrative innovations augment or suppress each other’s impact on performance.

Technology implementation is often characterised by misalignment between the technology and the organization at the time of initial introduction. These misalignments must be recognised and appropriate adjustments to the organization and the technology must be made for effective technology assimilation. How effectively these adjustments are made is largely determined by existing administrative arrangements. Different administrative arrangements vary in their capacity for change because of the patterns of social interaction that those arrangements encourage. The major causal mechanisms proposed are the impact of administrative arrangements on the amount and quality of information in the organization, the variety of perspectives involved in decision making and the extent to which members of the organization monitor the external environment (Collins et al, 1988). For example, administrative arrangements characterised by high complexity, decentralisation and low formalisation promote technology assimilation through flexible problem solving. On the other hand, rigid structures inhibit organizational adaptation to the technology and thus hamper technology assimilation. Thus, an IS unit’s existing administrative structures could augment or suppress the performance implications of new software process technologies such as reusability.

The techno-economic perspective of innovation supports the second version of the moderating effects model. This perspective examines how characteristics of production technologies themselves—that is, the physical processes, methods, techniques and tools affect organizational capacity for subsequent change (Skinner, 1978; Collins et al, 1988). Technological advances depend upon a minimal level of certainty in the systems development process. Process certainty is accomplished as processes mature and organizations develop insights about how to manage the processes (Humphrey, 1988). As an organization gains experience with systems development, it finds ways to further improve the development process thus increasing the operational complexity. To cope with the problems of complexity, the organization adopts more and newer technologies. Thus, a firm’s existing technology sophistication in effect determines its innovation potential, at least in the short run.

## Interaction effects model

In the interaction effects model, neither administrative innovation nor technological innovation has a direct effect on performance. Rather, they impact performance through their interaction. Ordinarily, we perceive technology as something physical because it is directly observable. Broader definitions of technology encompass the complex network of physical, informational and socioeconomic transformation that enable the physical components towards stated goals and objectives (Zeleny, 1986). Thus, technology is embodied in the hardware and software and in the knowledge and skills of organizational members (Ettlie, 1998; Gerwin, 1988).

This broader definition of technology supports the interaction effects model (Georgantzas & Shapiro, 1993). The support network is a component of administrative innovation that is conceptually distinct from the technological innovation it supports. Yet, it interacts with the technological innovation in practice to affect performance. Adopting a technological innovation as a composite of hardware, software and the support infrastructure constitutes a social transformation.

## Empirical study

Data to test the theoretical models put forth here was collected as part of a larger study on systems development quality management. The population of interest are IS units that develop application systems in-house. We limited our sampling frame to IS organizations in Fortune 1000 companies and large government agencies in the United States. We followed a systematic approach in constructing the mailing list for the survey. First, the Fortune 1000 organizations were identified through a search of the Compustat corporate database. Organizations such as holding companies, conglomerates and trusts were dropped from the mailing list. This yielded a set of 700 organizations. Next, the mailing addresses for these organizations were obtained from the Directory of Top Computer Executives (1994). Organizations not listed in the directory were dropped resulting in a set of 605 Fortune 1000 companies. Finally, 105 federal and state government agencies were randomly chosen from the same directory to construct the total sample for the study.

Senior IS executives were chosen as the respondents as they are likely to be most informed about both technological and administrative innovations in IS units. The names of senior IS executives in the sampled organizations were identified from the Directory of Top Computer Executives (1994). Where multiple names were found, the most senior person was chosen as the respondent. A total of 710 questionnaires were mailed. The questionnaires were coded to allow us to identify nonrespondents for follow-up mailings. A total of four mailings, each spaced apart by three weeks, were undertaken (the survey was conducted in 1995). The three followup mailings were targeted only at the non-respondents. A total of 123 usable responses were received resulting in a response rate of 17.32% (Table 1).

The response rate is modest but close to the minimum recommended level of 20% for organizational surveys (Yu & Cooper, 1983; Grover, 1997) and similar to those obtained in many IS surveys (Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 1993). Nevertheless, it is recommended that all efforts be made to maximise response rates and reduce the chances of sampling error (Yu & Cooper, 1983). We systematically checked for non-response bias by comparing respondents with non-respondents and late respondents with early respondents.

Proportionate industry classification (SIC codes) of respondents and non-respondents were compared. The chi-square analysis (x<sup>2</sup> = 5.59, df = 8, P = 0.69) provided evidence of the absence of response bias. Moreover, the response rate did not vary much across industry segments providing further evidence for the absence of response bias (Table 1). The respondents and the nonrespondents were also compared on organizational size and ISD size using one way ANOVA. No significant differences in the mean values of the two demographic variables were detected between the respondents and non-respondents.

## Measures

Multi-item scales were used to measure the implementation of technological and administrative innovations and single item scales were used to measure each of the four systems delivery performance dimensions. A fivepoint scale with values ranging from ‘null’ to ‘very high’ was used to measure the implementation of repository and the implementation of formal reuse policies. (The responses were normalised to a seven-point scale to maintain uniform scale width for all constructs.) A seven-point Likert scale with values ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ was used to measure the implementation of reuse-oriented rewards and each of the four systems delivery performance variables. Appendix 1 depicts the scales used to measure the various research constructs.

Implementation of a repository was measured using a five-item scale adapted from Cusumano (1991). Principal component analysis resulted in a one-factor solution that accounted for 53.7% of the variance represented by the five items. The item loadings for all five items were above 0.57. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the scale was 0.78 which is higher than the minimum recommended in the literature for reliable scales (Nunnally, 1988).

Implementation of formal reuse policy was measured using a four-item scale that was also adapted from Cusumano (1991). Principal component analysis resulted in a one-factor solution that accounted for 70.7% of the variance represented by the four items. The item loadings for all four items were above 0.65. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the scale was 0.85 indicating that the scale reliability is adequate.

Recent case studies on reusability (Apte et al, 1990; Karimi, 1990; Cusumano, 1991) indicate that IS units have re-oriented the reward structures of analysts and programmers to promote reuse adoption. Based on the anecdotal evidence presented in these case studies, we developed a two-item scale for reuse-oriented rewards. This scale measures the extent to which the rewards of IS personnel are tied to the levels of reuse, development cost, systems delivery lead time and programmer productivity. Principal component analysis resulted in a one-factor solution that accounted for 67.4% of the variance represented by the two items. The item loadings for both items were above 0.82. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the scale was 0.69.

The above analyses indicate that all three scales are unidimensional and reliable. In order to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of these scales, we pooled the items constituting the three scales and factor analysed these items. Three factors were extracted representing each of the three scales. All items loaded significantly on the factors representing their respective scales. Furthermore, the cross loadings of all items on other factors were below the cut-off level of 0.30 recommended in the literature (Kerlinger, 1986). Cumulatively, the three factors extracted 64.6% of the variance in the data. These results suggest that the scales have acceptable levels of convergent and discriminant validity. ## Statistical analyses and results

The focus of our statistical analyses is to determine which of the four theoretical models put forth best fit the data. In order to accomplish this objective, we adopted a systematic analytical approach that is schematically depicted in Figure 2. The statistical tests depicted in the figure and discussed in detail below are appropriate for cross-sectional data such as the one used in this study.

Simple zero-order bivariate correlation reveals the relationships between technological and administrative innovations and systems delivery performance. In order to test the first version of the intervening effects model, we compared the zero-order bivariate correlation of technological innovation with systems delivery performance with their first-order partial correlation, while controlling for administrative innovations. If the zero-order correlation are significant and the partial correlation are not significant, then the dimensions of administrative innovation intervenes between those of technological innovation and systems delivery performance. If the zeroorder correlation are not significant, but the partials are significant, then the dimensions of administrative innovation act as suppressor or distorter variables that mask the actual relationships between technological innovations and systems delivery performance. If both the bivariate and partial correlation are either significant or not significant, then administrative innovations and technological innovations have either independent effect or no effect on systems delivery performance, respectively. A similar analysis controlling for technological innovation can be carried out to test the second version of the intervening effects model.

The above procedure suggests whether the intervening or independent effects model is more descriptive of the data. However, this procedure is not a sufficient test for the independent effects model because the bivariate correlation do not reveal if dimensions of both administrative and technological innovation explain variance in systems delivery performance independent of each other. To test whether the independent effects model is descriptive of the data, it is necessary to use stepwise multiple regression analysis. If one or more independent variables enter the equation and no interaction terms enter the equation, then the independent effects model is descriptive of the data. If the interaction terms enter the regression equation either singly or along with the main effects, it suggests that the independent effects model is not descriptive of the data.

To test the moderating effects model we used hierarchical moderated regression analysis. Moderated regression is similar to multiple regression except that cross-product terms for independent variables thought to interact are entered into the equation after the main effects are determined. The moderating effect is examined by comparing the multiple correlation coefficient (R<sup>2</sup>) of the equation without the cross-product terms to the equation with the cross-product terms. The increment in R<sup>2</sup> is then tested for statistical significance. Since both versions of the moderating effects model rely on the same statistical model, differentiation between the two models must be made on theoretical grounds.

There are no straightforward procedures for differentiating between the moderating and interaction effects models. Boal and Bryson (1987) recommend that the results of the hierarchical regression should be compared with those of free-entry stepwise regression to determine whether the interaction effects model fits the data better that the moderating effects model. They reasoned that if the interaction effects model best describes the data the following should occur. First, in the free entry stepwise regression analysis only the interaction term should enter the equation. Second, in the hierarchical analysis the interaction term should always enter in the equation. Third, with all the main effects and the interactions entered, only the beta weight of the interaction terms should be statistically significant. Only if all three conditions are satisfied, one could conclude that the interaction effects model best fits the data.

In this section we present the results of the analyses carried out to test the various models. Table 3 presents the descriptive statistics and the zero-order bivariate correlation matrix of the dimensions of administrative innovation, technological innovation and systems delivery performance. It is seen from this table that all dimensions of both administrative innovation and technological innovation correlate positively with all systems delivery performance dimensions. Of the twelve bivariate correlation between systems delivery performance dimensions and administrative and technological innovations, nine are statistically significant $( P \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ Implementation of reuse policies does not have a significant relationship with both budget and schedule adherence and, implementation of repository does not have a significant relationship with systems delivery lead time. Except for these three relationships, all dimensions of administrative innovation and those of technological innovation are significantly related with all dimensions of systems delivery performance.

Testing the first version of the intervening effects model entails comparing the bivariate correlation of technological innovation and systems delivery performance with their partial correlation (Table 4, Matrices 1 & 2). Of the eight correlation pairs examined, six support this version of the model and two show no effects. Reuse based rewards intervenes in the relationship between repository implementation and (1) adherence to project cost budgets, (2) adherence to time schedules and (3) programmer productivity. Similarly, reuse policy intervenes in the relationship between repository implementation and (4) adherence to cost budgets, (5) adherence to time schedules and (6) programmer productivity. Interestingly, while repositories appear to have no effect in reducing systems delivery lead time, implementing repositories without appropriate reuse policies and reward schemes seem to have a negative impact on lead time (albeit, not statistically significant).

Of the eight pairs of correlation coefficients examined (Table 4, Matrix 3), four support the second version of the intervening effects model, two show apparent independent effects and two show no effects. It is found that in the absence of a repository, implementation of reuse policies may not improve (1) programmer productivity and implementation of reuse-based rewards may not improve (2) adherence to cost budgets, (3) adherence to time schedules and (4) programmer productivity. Interestingly, it is seen that mandating reuse without developing a supporting infrastructure in terms of a centralised repository has a negative impact on adherence to time schedules (albeit, not statistically significant).

Two bivariate and partial correlation pairs show apparent independent effects. ## Discussion

Organizations adopt innovations to improve performance. However, the relationship between innovation adoption and performance is anything but automatic. This study provides a base to better understand the relationships underlying the implementation of software process innovations and systems delivery performance. A basic premise of the paper is that synergies exist between administrative innovation and technological innovation that might be more important in explaining variance in performance than the innovations individually. To examine the effects of administrative and technological dimensions of reusability on systems delivery performance, four descriptive models of the interrelationships between them were put forth and tested. The results strongly suggest that administrative innovations intervene in the performance effects of technological innovations and vice versa.

Our results indicate that in order to realise performance gains from reusability, IS units might have to implement an information repository, institute policies that mandate reuse and put in place reward schemes that provide incentives for both creation and reuse of software components. Innovations such as reusability adopted with the intent of improving systems delivery performance might be well aligned with the objectives and goals of the IS unit. Such goals include cost and lead time reduction, productivity improvement and ontime delivery of systems. However, individual programmer/analysts may have little incentives to adopt reusability if they perceive that software reuse might have a deleterious impact on their jobs. Programmer/analysts could feel threatened that software reuse might undermine their role in developing, implementing and maintaining ‘custom-built’ software applications (Williamson, 1997). They might also believe that their primary role is to develop systems that meet requirements of current projects. Consequently, they could perceive parameterising design and code to facilitate future reuse a relatively non-significant task. This perception could be reinforced by performance evaluation schemes that emphasise on-time and within budget completion of assigned tasks.

Thus, software reuse conflicts with the (1) the traditional performance criteria used by management to judge the work of programmers/analysts, and (2) programmers/analysts’ perceptions about their role in systems development. Such misalignments could lead to the rejection of the technological innovation if not countered through changes in administrative practices. Policies that support reuse signal the importance attached to reusability by senior IS management and thus provide the rational for IS personnel to experiment and learn reuse methods and techniques. Rewards linked to reuse encourage programmers and analysts to invest time and effort in developing components for reuse. Together these administrative changes could alter programmer/analysts’ perceptions about reusability, encourage information sharing about reusable components, promote learning and experimentation and thus facilitate implementation of information repositories and component-based system construction.

While the need to adopt congruous administrative practices to facilitate reuse implementation is evident from the results, it is necessary to understand the temporal order in which administrative and technological

## Theoretical and practical implications

Recent research has called for studies aimed at understanding the administrative accommodations that IS units make to facilitate reuse implementation. Apte et al (1990) observed that reusability efforts are “more likely to fail from a lack of management commitment and programmer interest than from any technical reason” (p 430). Similarly, Karimi (1990) points out the need to address non-technical issues associated with software reuse for successful implementation of reusability. This study addresses these issues by developing theory-based models of the reuse implementation process and empirically testing these models. A broad definition of reusability encompassing its administrative and technological dimensions was adopted. Innovation management, manufacturing and information systems literatures were integrated to understand the interrelationships between these dimensions of reusability and systems delivery performance. The results are generally consistent with anecdotal evidence in the IS literature that success of reuse initiatives requires changes in administrative practices.

Our findings lead us to question deterministic theories about innovation adoption and performance based on technological or organizational imperatives and, provide evidence that an emergent perspective (Markus & Robey, 1988) is a more useful theoretical lens to understand innovation-performance relationships. According to this perspective, performance improvements should not be attributed to actor intent or the exogenous technology. Rather, it should be attributed to the interaction between the two. Moreover, our findings support the general theme expressed by IS researchers (Zmud, 1982; Orlikowski, 1993) that implementation of software process technologies involves substantial social change and cannot be achieved without adaptations in administrative practices. A closer understanding of the process of organizational adaptation is critical for shaping further research in this area and for building more adequate theories of technological change in organizations. This study adds to our existing knowledge about technology implementation by focusing on the process through which IS units could implement and realise performance gains from a specific software process technology, i.e, software reusability. However, we believe that these results can be generalised to other software process technologies with similar characteristics.

The results of this study should be viewed as initial evidence of the synergistic effects of administrative and technological dimensions of reusability on systems delivery performance. Among the organizations we studied, significant support was found for the intervening effects model. Our confidence in these findings will be enhanced if similar results are obtained with different data sets. Hence, future research should be directed at confirming the findings reported in this study. A useful extension to this research would be conducting in-depth longitudinal studies which investigate the dynamic interplay between repository adoption, consequent administrative changes and systems delivery performance improvements. Longitudinal studies should extend the simple non-recursive models put forth in this study to include feed back loops between systems delivery performance and innovation adoption. Such in-depth studies are required to develop a better understanding of the intervention strategies that could lead to successful implementation of reusability.

Recent literature in software engineering suggests that the tasks, skills and tools required for creating reusable software components differ fundamentally from those required for developing systems from software components (Mili et al, 1995). In fact, some on-going research programmes are experimenting with the idea that these two fundamentally distinct tasks should be handled by different groups within the IS unit (Mili et al, 1995).

Such separation of tasks constitutes an administrative innovation that could facilitate reuse implementation. It is necessary to carefully evaluate the impact of such administrative innovations on the success of reuse implementation. Two frequently emphasised administrative innovations in the context of reusability (reuse policies and reuse-based rewards) were considered in this study. In addition to these, future studies should include innovations such as separation of componentdevelopment and component-usage tasks discussed in the software engineering literature. Researchers are also encouraged to look at the broader organizational innovation literature to identify administrative innovations that were found to facilitate implementation of other technologies and adapt these to the IS context.

From a practical standpoint, the results of this study provide guidelines for IS managers on software process technology implementation. In organizations where the first version of the intervening effects model (administrative innovation is the intervening variable) holds true, IS managers seeking to improve systems delivery performance may monitor market-driven technological changes and proactively invest in technologies with desired capabilities and features. This will trigger the process of organizational accommodations that need to occur to assimilate the technology and these changes eventually could results in performance improvements. In organizations where the second version of the intervening effects model holds true (technology innovation is the intervening variable), IS managers could look inward to understand their organization and seek technologies compatible with the culture, norms and work methods in their organization. However, such inward focused approach to technology adoption is seldom practiced and perhaps explains why some organizations fail in their attempt to implement software process technologies.

## Conclusion

This study adds to IS research by integrating concepts and theories from manufacturing and organizational innovation with those in the information systems. Synchronous innovation concepts provided the theoretical foundation to understand how IS units could manage software process technology implementation. The theoretical models developed and tested here are useful starting points to further explore how software process technology implementation occurs in organizations. The results of the study are interesting and add to existing knowledge in this area. Furthermore, the results provide a basis to develop guidelines for IS managers on how implementation of software process technologies such as reusability could be managed.
