# The illusion of ‘best practice’ in information systems for operations management

J Swan<sup>1</sup>, S Newell<sup>2</sup> and M Robertson<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL; <sup>2</sup>Nottingham Business School, Nottingham Trent University, Burton Street, Nottingham, NG1 4BU; <sup>3</sup>Coventry Business School, Coventry University, Priory Street, Coventry, CV1 5EB, UK

This paper explores the adoption of information systems (IS) for operations management by manufacturing firms in four European countries. The notion of ‘best practice’ in information systems for operations management, as presented by technology suppliers (i.e. plain vanilla implementation of standard packages) is shown to be both illusory and potentially disruptive. In reality, firms usually reconfigure systems in order to use them effectively within their unique contexts. It is argued that the reason for this difference between technology supplier prescription of ‘best practice’ and user adoption of IS is because of the different interests of these two groups. Tensions exist at the interface between users who have unique manufacturing problems and demand flexible or customised information systems, and technology suppliers who have interests in promoting and supporting standardised solutions or methodologies. User firms need to recognise and address these tensions early when thinking about IS design.

## Theoretical background

Ideas relevant to the design of new technologies can be diffused in a variety of ways via different social networks. For example, technology suppliers such as software vendors and consultants play a role in diffusing particular technological solutions to firms in industry by marketing them as the latest ‘best practice’ (Abrahamson, 1991). Also, institutions such as educational or professional bodies see as their primary purpose the development of professionals in particular fields. They aim to keep professionals up to date with technological developments through training and education in the latest ‘best practice’ tools and techniques (Lynch, 1989; Robertson et al, 1996).

The ways in which ideas relating to technology design are diffused also vary across nations (Clark, 1987). Exist ing research has illustrated how the design and diffusion of new products and processes have been influenced by social institutions and by the national context surrounding adopting organizations (e.g. Bijker et al, 1987; Clark, 1987; King et al, 1994; Rogers, 1995). Clark (1987), for example, described how the UK game of rugby diffused to the US where it was redesigned and developed into the game of American football. National characteristics (such as patterns of media coverage and constraints imposed by advertising slots) led to pivotal changes in the design of the game (such as the introduction of frequent ‘timeouts’). Information systems (IS) may be particularly susceptible to this kind of social reconstruction (Coombes et al, 1992; Fulk, 1993). Yet detailed empirical accounts comparing the ways in which IS design is socially shaped across nations are relatively few.

This paper aims to provide a comparison of the adoption and design of IS for operations management by manufacturing firms across four European countries (UK, Netherlands, Sweden, and France). Previous work has identified IS suppliers (software/hardware suppliers and specialist consultants) and manufacturing firms, or ‘users’ (Clark, 1987; Fleck, 1987; Clark & Newell, 1993) as key social groups involved in the diffusion and design of IS for operations management, at least in the UK and US. The paper will therefore focus on the relationships between IS suppliers and users in each country and will discuss how these have influenced the diffusion of a distinct design variant of IS for operations management known as ‘manufacturing resources planning’ (MRP2).

Over the past three decades advances in computing have led to major developments in IS for the management of production planning and inventory control within manufacturing organizations. Among the earliest computerised IS promoted as ‘best practice’ was material requirements planning (MRP). MRP systems were developed mainly in the USA and sought to automate existing manual procedures for planning and controlling production schedules. These manual systems were often complex and typically relied heavily on informal and tacit knowledge and decision-making by operational staff on the shop-floor. This presented a challenge to management control of production processes. MRP was designed in the 1960s as a way of automating this process by working back from sales orders to determine the materials that would be required for production. In this way, materials requirements orders could be kept in balance with inventory depletions and reliance on decision-making at the level of the shop-floor could be minimised. However, implementation of MRP proved extremely difficult and in many firms the technology failed to live up to expectations (Belt, 1979; Miller, 1981). This was in large part because MRP was an open loop system which relied on managers’ forecasts of sales orders and planned inventory depletions rather than actual depletions. These estimates were often inaccurate. Systems designers therefore sought to provide a closed loop system which would be able to take into account actual shop-floor capacity when producing production schedules. This technology became known as manufacturing resources planning (MRP2). Like MRP, MRP2 is based on a philosophy of centralised planning and management control but integrates information from across different aspects of the production process with broader operations management strategy.

MRP2 diffused rapidly during the 1980s in North America as the definitive ‘best practice’ IS design for operations management. The MRP2 design philosophy was accompanied both by the development of increasingly sophisticated software tools and also by methodologies and education to assist with implementation. What emerged was an extensive set of computer software modules for managing the different functional aspects of the production process (such as material requirements planning, shop-floor scheduling, capacity planning). In order to achieve ‘best practice’ with MRP2, adopting firms were required to integrate these different modules in the particular ways specified by systems designers. A typical MRP2 ‘best practice’ solution is shown in Figure 1. In addition, ‘best practice’ methodologies for implementing MRP2 systems were developed and marketed successfully, the entire implementation process being estimated to take around 18 months (Wight, 1984).

Central social networks in the diffusion of MRP2 concepts and tools in North America were software and hardware suppliers (originally IBM) and manufacturing consultants (in particular, Oliver Wight—Wight, 1981, 1984; Wilson et al, 1994). These IS suppliers used a variety of social networks to disseminate the ‘vision’ of MRP2 ‘best practice’ to firms in manufacturing. This included recruiting members of the US professional association focusing on operations management—the American Production and Inventory Control Society (APICS)—to act as agents in the diffusion process (Clark & Newell, 1993). Most APICS members worked in the operations function within manufacturing firms and therefore APICS was targeted by IS suppliers as a useful network through which to market their ideas, albeit indirectly, to potential users.

Now in the 1990s many firms in Europe are still trying to introduce new MRP2 systems or upgrade existing systems and look to specialist IS software suppliers and consultants for assistance (Wilson et al, 1994). Recently the market for MRP2 software has been buoyed by the lack of Year 2000 capacity in legacy systems. This widespread diffusion of MRP2 across Europe has occurred despite a literature on MRP2 implementation which points to many examples of failure or partial failure, especially when firms attempt to integrate systems (Waterlow & Monniot, 1986).

The notion of ‘best practice’ that underpins the discussion so far can be described as a technology supplier ‘best practice’ perspective. It is predicated on the assumption that ‘one size fits all’. This assumes that IS have definable parameters that can be slotted into any organization that chooses to adopt the system. This supplier notion of ‘best practice’ is also evident in the innovation diffusion literature, which has been heavily influenced by a communications perspective which has generated ‘supply-push’ models of diffusion (Clark, 1987). Rogers (1962, 1983, 1995), for example, developed a widely cited model of diffusion aimed at enabling technology suppliers to get potential users to become aware of, and to adopt, prescriptive ‘best practice’ technologies. This technology supplier perspective of ‘best practice’ presumes that packaged software solutions embody universally applicable best practices, which should be implemented as far as possible without modification, i.e. the aim should be for ‘plain vanilla’ implementations of standard packages. The assumption is that the most effective way to implement such systems is in the way their developers intended, since this allows the adopting firm to ‘leverage the best practices embedded in the software’ (Smith, 1999). Software developers have spent huge amounts of resources, so the argument goes, on identifying the most streamlined way to structure business processes and have developed tools for configuring their applications to a variety of business types and sizes, without modifications to the software. Smith (1999) concludes: ‘Given the fact that an organization typically goes through an exhaustive evaluation to select the right package, it makes good business sense for that company to use the software the way it was designed, and avoid the increased costs of excessive modifications and longer implementations’ (p 83).

While this view of a fixed ‘best practice’ solution appears to continue to underpin the activities of software suppliers and many consultants, it can be criticised in a number of ways. Rogers himself recognised that inherent in such approaches is a ‘pro-innovation’ bias whereby the assumption is that a predefined solution is universally beneficial to all organizations regardless of context. Whilst this may be true of some new solutions (for example, Rogers cites the diffusion of improved diseaseresistant seeds to farmers via agricultural suppliers), it is not necessarily true of complex IS innovation which is usually context-dependent (Ettlie & Bridges, 1987; Swanson, 1994). Thus the assumption of a singular ‘best practice’ IS raises potential problems for users because it de-emphasises the importance of designing IS solutions so that they can be used appropriately within particular organizational and societal contexts.

This tension between the desire of users to modify standard packages to suit their particular context and the desire of technology suppliers for ‘plain vanilla’ implementations, can be related to the divergent interests of different social groups in relation to adoption and design of IS. For example, it is in the interests of MRP2 software suppliers to sell tightly packaged, standardised systems that have wide market potential and can be easily supported and upgraded. Thus suppliers primarily focus on encouraging diffusion and adoption of generic, standardised software. However, as research on MRP2 implementation shows, attempts to implement bought IS often fail (Swan & Clark, 1992; Wilson et al, 1994). As seen, user firms may find that they need to modify, or even fundamentally redesign, bought MRP2 systems so that they can be implemented within their particular manufacturing context (Robertson et al, 1996). Users need to focus, then, on technology design. But, if users modify bought IS this can create difficulties for technology suppliers who then find it harder to support systems where they have been heavily customised by adopting firms (Wilson et al, 1994). Thus tensions exist between the respective interests of technology suppliers and users which may make relationships between these social groups difficult (Scarbrough, 1995). This paper considers the processes that influence diffusion and design, focusing on the interface between technology suppliers and users in different national contexts.

The technology supplier perspective on ‘best practice’ also tends to emphasise technical artefacts whereby technology is objectified and discussed as a physical entity to be adopted by a community of firms. The idea of a technical ‘fix’ is easy for technology suppliers to communicate and perhaps be attractive to managers looking for quick solutions. However, this treats technical and organizational systems as if they are separable entities (Hosking & Morley, 1992). Similarly a major theme in the literature on technology and organization (for example, the early socio-technical approaches) has been on establishing the degree of ‘fit’ between separable technological (i.e. technical) and organizational structures. The idea of a ‘technical’ fix’ might be quite appropriate for technologies which do have parameters that can be defined and are therefore easily packaged and codified (such as a new machine tool)—the design of such technologies may be relatively independent of the context in which they are used. However, IS are not so easily accommodated. This is because IS, by definition, have properties which are informational and subjective as well as physical or technical (Swanson, 1994). They are thus embedded in, and inseparable from, the knowledge, social structures and social processes that exist in user organizations (Coombes et al, 1992). With respect to IS, organizational processes form a central part of the technology itself and therefore the extent to which IS can be objectified into generic, portable solutions is probably limited.

Other theoretical approaches to innovation have attempted to reconcile the dualism between technology and organization. These approaches recognise that IS are contextually embedded and that their design and use is shaped both by pre-existing social structures and processes in adopting organizations and also, more widely, by the societal context of the adopting organization (Leonard Barton, 1988; Weick, 1990; Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). These approaches thus emphasise the importance of understanding the design of IS and the roles of different social groups that shape processes of diffusion and design. They also implicitly or explicitly, question the validity of a notion of a definable ‘best practice’ which is equally applicable in any organizational context. Instead, ‘best practice’ would imply that user firms redesign the particular packages presented by IS suppliers, blending them with existing organizational practices, in order to develop them appropriately within their own organizations (Clark & Staunton, 1989; Fleck, 1994). This paper provides an empirical test of this prediction by assessing the ways in which supplier prescriptions of ‘best practice’ MRP2 have been redesigned within user firms. In essence then, the paper compares two opposing notions of ‘best practice’: the technology supplier ‘best practice’ prescription of ‘plain vanilla’ implementation and the contextual embedding ‘best practice’ prescription which advocates the need to modify the technology to suit the particular context of application. Indeed, ‘best practice’ is probably not a concept which would be readily used from this latter perspective, since no definable ‘best’ practice would be assumed.

## Methodologies

This paper draws upon detailed surveys and interviews to compare the diffusion and design of MRP2 in four European countries: UK, Netherlands (ND), Sweden and France. (The data presented here were collected as part of a wider study of the roles of professional associations in technology diffusion funded by the Economic and Social Research Council, Swindon, UK.) Professional operations management associations in each of the four countries participated in the research by providing a list of their members so that these could be surveyed. The majority of these members in each country worked in manufacturing firms usually as operations or production managers. This resulted in data on a total of 1046 manufacturing firms (626 in UK, 100 in ND, 167 in Sweden, 153 in France). The UK sample was larger than those in the other countries because the professional association here had more members. It should be noted that the sample of firms represented in the survey is not entirely random. For example, there may have been an overrepresentation of larger firms since these are more likely to have employees who are members of professional associations (Swan & Newell, 1995). However the distribution of the size of firms represented in the survey was found to be similar across countries and did cover the full range from very small firms (i.e. less than 50 employees) to very large firms (greater than 5000 employees).

The survey provided detailed information on the design of IS for operations management in members’ firms. The vision of MRP2 ‘best practice’ as prescribed by IS suppliers is of a ‘total company wide system. It is a way to get all the people in the company working to the same game plan; to the same set of numbers. A company can now plan material, capacity, finance, marketing strategy etc. all with the same system’ (Wight, 1984). Questions were asked about which of a list of different functional aspects of the operations management process members’ firms had adopted computerised systems for and for how long these had been in place (where 1 = not adopted; 2 = manual system; 3 = adopted for less than 2 years; 4 = adopted for 2–5 years; 5 = adopted for more than 5 years). This list, shown in Table 1, was drawn from the list of functionalities that, if computerised and integrated, comprise a ‘best practice’ MRP2 design from an IS supply perspective (Wight,

1984; Corke, 1985). Thus, according to IS suppliers, all of these systems must be adopted and integrated for the firm to be classed as an MRP2 user. Integration is also said to be more likely if users adopt standardised MRP2 software packages as these are developed specifically to be integrated in particular ways. Thus questions were also asked about the extent to which each computerised system had been modified (where 1 = standard package, 2 = modified package, 3 = bespoke system) and the extent to which the various systems in use had been integrated (from 1 = no integration to 4 = full integration of manufacturing systems). Finally, respondents were asked to indicate how ‘successful’ they perceived their computerised systems to be. Different firms are likely to possess different criteria for ‘success’ so this was assessed in terms of the extent to which the various systems implemented had lived up to respondents firms’ own expectations (where 1 = lived up to expectations in no cases; and 5 = lived up to expectations in all cases). Whilst being a fairly crude indicator, this does at least provide some comparability across firms, albeit based on respondents’ subjective judgements of ‘success’. In a different area of the questionnaire respondents were asked to indicate whether or not their firms were using ‘MRP2’ and, if so, for how long. Thus the questionnaire provided detailed information on ways in which computerised IS for operations management had actually been implemented in firms that claimed to have adopted the ‘MRP2’ design philosophy.

Questionnaires were translated into the appropriate language in each country and piloted to make sure that the correct terms were used to describe the IS. ‘MRP2’ was the recognised acronym in all countries. Software suppliers differ in the exact names they gave to the different systems that comprise MRP2 but those listed were found, during pilot work with the surveys, to be representative.

In addition, interviews (around 20 in each country) were carried out with a matched sample of operations management practitioners, organisers of the professional operations management associations, and manufacturing consultants and software suppliers specialising in the provision of operations management technologies and concepts, particularly MRP2. Interviews addressed the roles of these different social groups and national differences in the diffusion process. These data were important for explaining the findings of the survey and are used to inform the discussion of those findings presented next.

## IS design among ‘MRP2’ users and non-users

Table 1 compares for the entire sample the systems that had been adopted by firms who classed themselves as MRP2 users with those who did not claim to be using MRP2 and illustrates the extent to which these different groups of users had customised their IS. Of the total sample, 599 (57%) claimed that their firms had adopted MRP2 and 447 (43%) claimed that their firms had not adopted MRP2. The rather high percentage of firms that claimed to have adopted MRP2 may reflect an overrepresentation of larger firms in the sample. These are more likely to have employees who are members of professional associations and who therefore had the opportunity to respond to the survey (Swan & Newell, 1995). They are also more likely to have the resources needed to invest in expensive IS such as MRP2.

Importantly Table 1 demonstrates that the concept of MRP2 ‘best practice’ design as prescribed by technology suppliers occurs in name only. Firms that called themselves ‘MRP2’ users did use more computerised systems than those that did not claim to be using MRP2 (t = 13.70; P , 0.0001). However, these were not designed according to supplier specifications of MRP2 ‘best practice’. Few firms which claimed to be using MRP2 had the full range of specified systems, nor had they integrated these fully, both of which are fundamental to supplier prescriptions of MRP2 ‘best practice’. For example, around 20% of firms claiming to have adopted MRP2 had not implemented a computerised master production schedule (MPS) which is central to supplier prescribed MRP2 ‘best practice’. Further, around 50% of those who claimed to have MRP2 did not have shop-floor data collection systems and around 30% did not have a capacity requirements planning system or a rough cut capacity planning system. These firms were not operating a closed loop IS and were therefore not using MRP2 as prescribed by technology suppliers. This finding concurs with other case study work which has found that firms may selectively appropriate and redesign MRP2 ‘best practice’. For example, they may use computerised IS for planning and forecasting but alternative technologies which are not necessarily computerised (such as Justin-Time) for shopfloor control (Sillince & Sykes, 1993; Robertson et al, 1996).

Of those firms claiming to have adopted MRP2 many were using bespoke or modified systems for different aspects of the manufacturing process. For example, although firms that claimed to be using MRP2 had modified their solutions significantly less than those that did not claim to use MRP2 (t = 5.00; P , 0.001), still less than 40% had unmodified packages and the mean modification score for this group was 1.93 (on the three-point scale where scale of 1 = unmodified to 3 = bespoke). Around 25% of systems used by firms claiming to have adopted MRP2 were in fact entirely bespoke systems. These do not fit comfortably with the technology supplier MRP2 ‘best practice’ design philosophy.

Despite this high level of modification of standard MRP2 packages in practice, the technology suppliers interviewed continued to explicitly discourage modifications to their software, even if they realised that the system they were supplying was not exactly appropriate for a particular user. Customised systems were seen as much more difficult to integrate, to support and to upgrade. In support of this claim there was a significant negative correlation between the extent to which systems had been modified and the degree of integration that was achieved $( r = 0 . 1 4 6 ; P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , although this was perhaps not as high as would be expected based on the claims made by suppliers of MRP2 software. From these suppliers’ point of view it was better if users modified their organization or conducted ‘work arounds’ to fit the technical requirements of the software system and not vice-versa. One supplier did acknowledge that there was an opportunity for them to develop the systems they sold to incorporate modifications added by their customers but suggested that this was rare because usually these modifications lacked general applicability.

According to technology suppliers, to achieve successful best practice with MRP2, the systems adopted must be fully integrated. In our sample, those who claimed to have MRP2 did report significantly higher levels of integration that those who did not claim to have MRP2 $( t = 8 . 9 1 ; P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). However, still 46% of those that claimed to be MRP2 users had managed only partial integration of their systems and only 16% had achieved the kind of ‘total, company wide’ integrated system prescribed by technology suppliers. Those that claimed to have MRP2 did report higher levels of success with their systems overall than those that did not claim to be using MRP2 (t = 3.20; P , 0.01), though the difference in the means was rather marginal (Have MRP2 = 3.62; Don’t have MRP2 = 3.47 on the fivepoint scale).

These data suggest that integration was more likely among firms that claimed to be using MRP2 because these firms had implemented more computerised systems than those that did not claim to have MRP2 and had modified these to less of an extent. They also imply that achieving the kind of full company-wide integration promised by technology suppliers may be problematic (cf. Waterlow & Monniot, 1986). This is perhaps because so many MRP2 users modify their systems and this makes integration more difficult. However the fact that users see a need to modify their systems suggests difficulties in practice in using the tightly prescribed, universally applicable ‘best practice’ designs promoted by technology suppliers. Users need, instead, to redesign these solutions so that they are appropriate for their specific context. That there is little difference in terms of perceived success between those that claim to be using MRP2 and those that do not claim to be using MRP2 further suggests that customised IS, though more difficult to integrate, nonetheless meet firms’ requirements adequately in many cases. Taken together, these data show that there was little evidence of a definable MRP2 ‘best practice’ as prescribed by technology suppliers among those firms who saw themselves as using MRP2. The main difference between MRP2 users and non-users is that users had implemented more computerised systems overall, but these were configured in a variety of ways.

## Country differences in IS design

To the extent that diffusion and design of IS is shaped by societal and institutional contexts, we would expect to see differences in the diffusion and design of MRP2 across the different European countries in our study. Country differences in IS design were revealed by the survey and interviews suggested explanations for these. These differences are considered next.

Table 2 shows the differences between companies in the four countries in terms of respondents claims that their firms had adopted the MRP2 design philosophy. These differences were significant, with Swedish respondents least likely and French respondents most likely to make this claim (Chi-square = 25.37; P , 0.001).

This suggests that the diffusion of the MRP2 design concept had occurred differently across countries with the concept having diffused less widely in Sweden. Interviews confirmed this and suggested that in fact the MRP2 design philosophy had been recognised in Sweden considerably later than in the other countries. For example, interviewees in Sweden suggested that the MRP2 design concept had not really been known about in Sweden until the end of the 1980s, several years after its introduction in the other countries. One of the earliest implementations of standardised MRP2 software in Sweden was not started until 1989.

In terms of the actual systems implemented for different aspects of operations management, there were significant differences across countries in the extent to which firms had developed computerised systems, whether or not these were referred to as ‘MRP2’ (F = 28.14; P , 0.0001). There were also country differences both in how these systems were designed within firms and in the extent to which they were seen to be successful. These differences are summarised in Table 3.

Again the biggest contrast was seen between Sweden and the other countries. Interestingly, whilst Swedish firms were slower to have adopted the ‘MRP2’ label, this was not because their computerised IS for operations management were less well developed. In fact pairwise comparisons indicated that Swedish firms had implemented significantly more computerised systems for different aspects of operations management and had developed these earlier than firms in any other country. They were also more likely to have developed these as bespoke systems than firms in the other countries but were less likely to have integrated these systems. Again, this lower level of integration in Sweden is consistent with the claims by technology suppliers that bespoke, customised systems are more difficult to integrate than standardised software packages. However, despite this lack of integration Swedish respondents saw their systems as relatively successful. Interviewees suggested that this was because their systems had been developed over a number of years mostly inhouse and were tailor-made to fit their particular organizational context and manufacturing problems.

UK firms were more likely than Swedish firms to claim to be MRP2 users. They also reported the highest levels of integration of their computerised IS as compared with the other countries surveyed. UK firms were more likely than Swedish firms to have adopted standardised software packages which were unmodified or slightly modified. Interestingly, despite the relatively high level of integration achieved with these systems in the UK, they were seen to be significantly less successful here than in any other country (F = 22.09; P , 0.001).

Together with the interviews these data suggested that UK firms were more likely than Swedish firms to have pursued the IS supplier ‘best practice’ MRP2 design philosophy, this being reflected in the greater use of standard software and higher integration of systems (though with considerable variation across firms in system design as seen earlier). However, the apparent lack of satisfaction suggests that they may have experienced some difficulties in actually achieving this MRP2 ‘best practice’ design philosophy.

French firms who were the most likely to claim to be using MRP2 had, like UK firms, achieved high levels of integration and had tended to customise their systems less than firms in Sweden. However, French firms had implemented more of the functionality needed to run an MRP2 system than UK firms and saw their systems as relatively more successful. This again suggests the pursuit of an MRP2 design philosophy among French firms but also suggests that the systems they were using in practice actually mirrored this philosophy more closely than the systems used in the UK.

Dutch firms were just as likely as UK firms to report using MRP2 and had adopted a similar number of computerised systems. However, their systems tended to be more customised than those in France or the UK (though less customised than those in Sweden) and were rated as more successful than those in the UK. During interviews respondents suggested that there was widespread awareness of the IS supplier ‘best practice’ MRP2 philosophy in the Netherlands but that this had been received quite critically by the user community. This is perhaps because there was a clearer education route into developing careers in logistics here than in the UK, with the result that senior managers making choices about information systems design had relatively sophisticated understandings of MRP2 concepts, principles and limitations. As a result, whilst they were prepared to use the MRP2 label, they recognised that the notion of a universally applicable MRP2 ‘best practice’ was limited and that some degree of customisation might be needed in order to appropriate MRP2 technologies within their own firms.

Technology supplier-user interface and MRP2 design Interview data suggested that country differences could be explained, at least in part, by examining the interface between technology suppliers and users in each country. A major difference revealed in this study was that the MRP2 design philosophy had been adopted relatively late in Sweden as compared to the other countries studied. In Rogers’ (1983, 1995) terms Swedish firms could be described as ‘laggards’. However, this categorisation implies lack of innovation or slow technological development among Swedish firms. This research showed that, although ‘MRP2’ had diffused more slowly in Sweden, technological development, in terms of implementing computerised IS for operations management, had occurred earlier in Sweden than in the other countries. However, Swedish firms had opted for mostly tailor-made in-house systems instead of standardised MRP2 ‘best practice’ technologies and software.

Interviews suggested explanations for this, a major one being that there had been a weaker technology ‘push’ for this particular MRP2 design from technology suppliers in Sweden than in the other countries. Technology suppliers had been much less active in promoting MRP2 concepts, education and software in Sweden. For example, the Oliver Wight Consultancy, originally established in the US, has played a leading role in the diffusion of MRP2 design concepts (Fleck, 1994; Wilson et al, 1994). Oliver Wight, its creator, is widely recognised among industrialists and academics as was one of the ‘founding fathers’ of the notion of a fixed MRP2 ‘best practice’. This consultancy has played a central role in diffusing American ideas about MRP2 ‘best practice’ to manufacturing industry worldwide. This consultancy had ‘partners’ (i.e. consultants) in each of the UK, France and the Netherlands but it did not have any partners in Sweden. Swedish consultants suggested further that the consultancy industry in general had been less well developed in Sweden than, for example, in the UK. This suggests that in Sweden, firms had traditionally relied on intra-firm IS knowledge and expertise to drive the development of their systems rather than buying standardised packaged solutions from suppliers. This had advantages in that firms retained IS knowledge in-house and were less likely to become dependent on external technology suppliers for systems expertise (Scarbrough, 1996). They were also able to tailor solutions to their particular organizational context. However the disadvantages were that these systems were more difficult to integrate and upgrade. These disadvantages, together with increasing globalisation and a perceived need to develop common systems in global companies, were leading some firms to change to more standardised systems.

Software suppliers specialising in MRP2 software packages had also been slower to penetrate the Swedish market. In the early 1980s IBM dominated in the supply of MRP2 software. Their package (MAPICS) was widely adopted both by firms in the US and in Europe, especially the UK (Vollman & Berry, 1985). However, interviewees commented that this was ‘never really a hit’ in Sweden. There are currently around twenty different software packages sold as MRP2 packages, although probably only about four or five of these actually support the fully integrated MRP2 ‘best practice’ philosophy. The Netherlands and UK both house market leaders in MRP2 software supply (e.g. Bahn, in the Netherlands, SSA and Minerva in the UK). Most packages were developed using English as their user-interface language.

This was seen as another reason why Swedish firms had been slower to take them on board, being reluctant to abandon their in-house systems which were written in Swedish for systems written in English. There is now one major MRP2 software supplier in Sweden (Intentia) supplying a package called MOVEX. During interviews this supplier acknowledged that they had themselves had been quite late in fully understanding the MRP2 design philosophy. For example, it was not until 1992, when working in the US on a joint project with a US firm, that they realised that MOVEX did not fully support an MRP2 environment. They then began to re-educate themselves in MRP2 concepts so that they could sell MOVEX in the US.

Technology suppliers diffuse ideas about ‘best practice’ technologies directly to potential users but also indirectly via a number of different networking activities (Abrahamson, 1996; Robertson et al, 1996). For example, Oliver Wight hosts a user group where examples of MRP2 ‘best practice’ are presented by their ‘Class A’ users to other practitioners who may be thinking about introducing MRP2 systems. Professional operations management associations comprise mainly members working in manufacturing and technology supply industries and therefore provide another network through which technology suppliers can disseminate ideas to practitioners in industry (Swan & Newell, 1995). For example, during the early 1980s, IBM used the American Production and Inventory Control Society, as a forum through which to promote MRP2 ‘best practice education and software (Vollman & Berry, 1985).

The interview data suggested country differences in the extent to which professional associations were used actively by technology suppliers to promote MRP2 ‘best practice’ concepts. For example, the associations in the UK, France and Netherlands had for some time been offering a qualification called the Certificate of Production and Inventory Management (CPIM) and disseminate a journal to members, both of which have focused on MRP2. Many of the supporting materials and courses for the CPIM and the articles in journals have been written by technology suppliers who have both the time and motivation to get involved in MRP2 education. Because suppliers are interested in increasing adoption of the concepts, these materials tend to promote MRP2 in an overly positive way (Newell et al, 1997). Thus the professional associations have provided another route through which ideas about MRP2 ‘best practice’ can be disseminated to potential users from technology suppliers and may also have generated pro-innovation bias for this particular design. This network was used less actively by technology suppliers in Sweden where, for example, the CPIM had only been available to members for about two years and there was no journal. The French association had the highest proportion of members who were taking the CPIM. These members also met regularly in study groups from which technology suppliers were deliberately excluded. In this way practitionermembers working in manufacturing could discuss with each other the various problems as well as advantages associated with the introduction of fixed ‘best practice’ MRP2 systems. It is feasible that this helped these members to develop a better understanding of what changes would actually be needed in order to implement MRP2. This could help to explain why the systems implemented in members’ firms in France generally mirrored more closely technology supplier prescriptions of MRP2 ‘best practice’ (though again, with wide inter-firm variation). In contrast, in the UK there was also a strong technology ‘push’ for standardised MRP2 systems both directly and via the association. However, there were not the same opportunities for practitioner-members to discuss the relative merits of MRP2 and the perceived success of these systems in general in the UK was lower.

In the UK, relationships between technology suppliers and users were often reported as being acrimonious. Suppliers of MRP2 software reported feeling frustrated that some users did not take the time to fully understand the systems they were implementing. For example, users would sometimes insist on software that the supplier knew was not appropriate (if this was the case the supplier would usually sell it). Others saw users as unwilling to resolve fundamental problems in their own organization in order to accommodate their systems and attain the advantages that standardisation could offer. Instead users would, it was claimed, expect the suppliers to provide a technical ‘fix’ to all their problems. Users were often equally unhappy with the information they received from technology suppliers, particularly software suppliers, seeing it as partial and in some cases misleading. Some felt they were persuaded to adopt MRP2 systems that could, it was promised, be introduced without major organizational change or disruption. They then found that the systems they had bought failed to live up to expectations and needed major modifications in order to be implemented. These problems were compounded when the suppliers were unwilling to support these modifications because, for example, they were more interested in working on a new release of their standard package (see also Robertson et al, 1996). This highlights the need for users to manage their relationships with IS technology suppliers such that they can learn about new IS developments whilst recognising the potential for ‘pro-innovation bias’ for particular forms of ‘best practice’.

## Conclusions

The data presented above suggest that the technology supply notion of a definable ‘best practice’ MRP2 system is illusory. Rather, the data indicate that users redesign the systems presented by IS suppliers. Thus, there were very few examples of ‘vanilla’ implementations of standard packages. The data also provides empirical evidence that the design of IS is shaped by the societal and institutional context surrounding adopting organizations (King et al, 1994). The institutional context including, importantly, the technology supply industry and professional association networks, created a stronger technology ‘push’ for standardised MRP2 solutions in some countries in the study than in others. Together, these findings show little support for the technology supply notion of a fixed ‘best practice’, but rather support the contextual embedding view that ‘best’ is situationally specific.

Nevertheless, it must be recognised that the departure from the standardised systems developed by software suppliers can make upgrading and integrating systems difficult. This study highlights the need to manage tensions between the interests of IS suppliers in selling generic ‘best practice’ solutions and the interests of users in designing solutions that can be used appropriately within their specific organizational contexts. Informational and organizational implications of MRP2 systems tend to be de-emphasised by IS suppliers but these can be problematic for users attempting to implement MRP2 within their own organizations. Discrepancies in the legitimate business interests of IS suppliers and users need to be recognised so that user-supplier relationships can be collaborative rather than combative. An example from the research of a collaborative relationship was where a firm providing servicing and rentals for trucks was working closely with an MRP2 system supplier to design a new version of their MRP2 system especially for service and rental operations. This new system was to be tailor-made for the user but would also become part of a new standard MRP2 package that the supplier could then market to other service firms. Thus both supplier and user-interests were being met and the relationship was co-operative even to the extent that suppliers committed time to the project over and above contracted consultant hours. This is an example of ‘innofusion’ whereby modifications in systems design introduced by users were to be taken up by the IS suppliers and further diffused to the wider user community (Fleck, 1987). Fleck argues that innofusion is an important element of the innovation diffusion process. However, innofusion is not likely to occur unless supplier-user relationships are managed effectively and knowledge is shared openly among the two parties. The results presented in this paper suggest that such open relationships will be difficult to develop as long as users and suppliers maintain their very different views about what constitutes ‘best practice’.
