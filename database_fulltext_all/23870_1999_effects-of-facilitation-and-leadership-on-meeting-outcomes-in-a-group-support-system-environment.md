# Effects of facilitation and leadership on meeting outcomes in a group support system environment

BCY Tan<sup>1</sup>, Kwok-Kee Wei<sup>1</sup> and J-E Lee-Partridge<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Systems, <sup>2</sup>Department of Decision Sciences, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 119260, Republic of Singapore

This study investigates the effects of facilitation and leadership on outcomes of meetings involving group support systems (GSS). It extends existing GSS literature by examining the critical roles of facilitation and leadership simultaneously within a single study. A 2×2 factorial controlled laboratory experiment was conducted. A preference task, which had no objective correct answers, was employed. Ten five-person groups completed each treatment. Independent variables were facilitation (with vs without) and leadership (with vs without). During their meetings, facilitators and leaders carried out task and interactional interventions respectively. Dependent variables were measures of meeting success (post-meeting consensus, decision confidence, discussion quality, and process satisfaction). Results reveal that: (1) facilitation appears to raise decision confidence; (2) leadership appears to enhance discussion quality and process satisfaction; (3) in the absence of leadership, facilitation seems to lower post-meeting consensus; and (4) in the absence of facilitation, leadership seems to raise post-meeting consensus. These results are compared with findings from prior related studies. Implications of these results for practice are discussed.

## Introduction

Group meetings are a critical aspect of organizational communication in spite of the fact that they are often time-consuming and potentially unproductive (Mosvick & Nelson, 1987). Group support systems (GSS), which incorporate electronic tools to support group work, have been proposed as a means to raise productivity of group meetings (Dennis & Gallupe, 1993). However, empirical findings have revealed that GSS are not a panacea for such productivity problems (Benbasat & Lim, 1993; Dennis et al, 1996). In general, field studies tend to report more consistent and positive GSS impact than laboratory studies (Dennis & Gallupe, 1993). Scholars have observed that groups may sometimes not benefit from GSS because they lack the ability to use GSS tools appropriately during their meetings (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994).

GSS impact on group meetings can be enhanced through facilitation (George et al, 1992; Dickson et al, 1993; Anson et al, 1995), a set of activities carried out to help groups achieve better outcomes before, during, or after their meetings (Bostrom et al, 1993). Task and interactional interventions form the core of such activities. Task interventions are strategies that assist groups to resolve substantive and procedural problems arising from their tasks, thereby carrying out their tasks more effectively (Lee-Partridge, 1992). Interactional interventions are strategies that assist groups to manage relational and maintenance problems, thereby improving the relationships among group members (Lee-Partridge, 1992).

In GSS-supported meetings, these facilitative activities can be carried out by external facilitators or group leaders (Bostrom et al, 1993), or sometimes using automated tools that embed decision heuristics (Limayem, 1996). GSS empirical studies have investigated how external facilitators (e.g. Dickson et al, 1993; Anson et al, 1995), group leaders (e.g. George et al, 1990; Ho & Raman, 1991), or automated decision heuristics (e.g. Limayem, 1996) individually affected meeting outcomes. However, these sources of facilitation may function together in unanticipated ways to alter meeting outcomes (Reagan-Cirincione, 1994). This study extends existing GSS literature by investigating the critical roles of facilitation and leadership simultaneously within a single study. Specifically, it examines how external facilitators (who carry out task interventions) and group leaders (who carry out interactional interventions) may individually and jointly affect meeting outcomes.

Sections 2 and 3 of this paper review pertinent facilitation and leadership literature respectively. Section 4 provides the arguments behind research hypotheses while Section 5 covers critical aspects of the research methodology. Following this, Section 6 presents the results of data analyses while Section 7 interprets and discusses the implications of these results. Section 8 concludes this paper by offering avenues for further research.

## Facilitation

Argyris (1970) defines facilitative activities as delicate interventions that come between people, in an ongoing system of relationships, with the objective of helping them. The role of facilitators is paradoxical in several ways (Griffith et al, 1998). First, facilitators have to function as experts in the decision domain while remaining neutral towards the decision. Second, facilitators have to play a central role while helping group members to rely on their own resources. Given their complex role, there is a need for extensive facilitation training (Clawson et al, 1993; Niederman et al, 1996; Griffith et al, 1998).

In GSS-supported meetings, the availability of a variety of electronic tools adds further complexity to the role of facilitators. Facilitators need to manage the relationships among group members, group tasks, and GSS tools, in their efforts to help groups attain meeting objectives (George et al, 1992; Bostrom et al, 1993; Niederman et al, 1996). Dickson et al (1993) distinguish between the roles of facilitators and chauffeurs. Chauffeurs provide solely operational facilitative support to groups by assisting them in technical matters arising from the use of GSS tools. In addition to such a role, facilitators provide process facilitative support to groups by helping them to manage their meeting process so as to enhance their meeting outcomes. In terms of consensus, chauffeur-driven groups were reported to have the best performance, followed by facilitator-driven groups, and finally user-driven groups who had no support at all (Dickson et al, 1993). Dickson et al (1993) attribute the inferior performance of facilitator-driven groups, compared to chauffeur-driven groups, to the use of a rigid facilitation approach. Nevertheless, the results suggest that groups unfamiliar with GSS technology can benefit from the assistance of an external technical expert (Griffith et al, 1998) in the form of a chauffeur or facilitator.

Lee-Partridge (1992) refines the notion of process facilitative support by separating its activities into task and interactional interventions, as defined earlier. Task interventions encompass activities such as managing group emotional climate, encouraging broad participation, resolving conflicts, and relieving tension. Interactional interventions include activities such as structuring of tasks, clarifying agenda items, summarising information, testing for agreement, and identifying group decisions. Compared to unsupported groups, groups that were given interactional or task interventions were not found to attain better consensus. Moreover, though the provision of task interventions helped to raise perceptual measures such as decision quality and process satisfaction, the provision of interactional on top of task inventions had the opposite effect of reducing these perceptual measures (Lee-Partridge, 1992). In this study, the external facilitator had some freedom to use appropriate task and interactional interventions whenever necessary. A plausible reason for the negative impact of interactional interventions is the inability of the external facilitator to handle relational and maintenance issues in a group. A group member, serving as an internal facilitator, may be more suited to carry out interactional interventions (Lee-Partridge, 1992).

To circumvent problems arising from a rigid or semiflexible facilitation approach, Anson et al (1995) conducted a study using a flexible facilitation approach. When applying a rigid facilitation approach, facilitators adhere to pre-determined procedural structures for problem solving by leading groups strictly along such directions. When using a flexible facilitation approach, facilitators are given the freedom to help groups select and adapt procedural structures depending on their specific needs. Results of Anson et al (1995) showed that facilitation had a marginally positive impact on group cohesion and group perception of its meeting process. In addition, the combined effects of facilitation and GSS on both these variables seemed to be additive. However, both facilitation and GSS did not affect task performance (Anson et al, 1995). The value of a flexible facilitation approach was also shown by Reagan-Cirincione (1994). An intervention combining a flexible facilitation approach, the social judgement analysis technique, and information technology was conceived. Groups given such an intervention were found to outperform their most capable member consistently on two tasks (Reagan-Cirincione, 1994).

To understand what effective facilitators really do, Clawson et al (1993) carried out an extensive study on facilitation. Using the critical incidents approach, they examined the work of 50 facilitators to identify critical role behaviour (specific actions taken by these facilitators to fulfil their role requirements). The 146 critical role behaviour that emerged were then mapped into 16 critical role dimensions (broad-based requirements of the role of facilitators). Through in-depth interviews with 37 practicing facilitators, Niederman et al (1996) created a list of factors that were important to the success of facilitation efforts. Besides reinforcing the critical role dimensions of facilitation identified by Clawson et al (1993), the results of Niederman et al (1996) extended existing knowledge on facilitation by providing specific suggestions on appropriate facilitator behaviour. Congruent findings from two such extensive studies provide an excellent basis for subsequent empirical tests and theoretical development (the objective of this study).

As a step towards theoretical development, Table 1 relates the critical role dimensions from Clawson et al (1993) to important concepts in the facilitation literature. First, role dimensions are classified as activities to be carried out either before or during meetings (Bostrom et al, 1993; Niederman et al, 1996). Second, role dimensions pertaining to meeting activities are separated into those pertaining to operational support and those pertaining to process support (Dickson et al, 1993). Third, role dimensions that provide process support during meetings are further subdivided into interaction interventions, task interventions, or both (Lee-Partridge, 1992). From such a framework, this study builds and tests a set of hypotheses that potentially contribute towards theoretical development on facilitation in a GSS environment.<sup>1</sup>

## Leadership

Stogdill (1974) defines leadership as an exercise of influence, a focus of group processes, and an instrument of goal achievement. In exercising influence, leaders engage in efforts to change the opinions of group members and move them towards consensus (Chemers, 1987). As a focus of group processes, leaders channel the energy of group members in desired directions and serve as agents in determining group activities and working atmosphere (O’Reilly & Roberts, 1978). As an instrument of goal achievement, leaders arrange the working situation to allow group members to work together towards common goals with maximal effectiveness and minimal efforts (Fiedler et al, 1979).

When fulfilling their functions, leaders seek to achieve both task and relational goals (McGrath, 1984). Task goals are achieved when leaders successfully guide their groups to complete their tasks or missions. The probability of attaining these goals is enhanced if leaders are able to establish operating procedures, define problems, generate solutions, evaluate solutions, and plan for implementation of solutions (Poole, 1983). Relational goals are achieved when leaders successfully establish and maintain healthy interpersonal relationships. The likelihood of accomplishing these goals is raised if leaders are able to manage conflicts, motivate group members, create affection, foster loyalty, and promote contributions (Poole, 1983). Hence, in pursuing task and relational goals, leaders take on roles resembling those of facilitators who carry out task and interactional interventions respectively. Leaders tend to have positive effects on group performance regardless of whether they pursue solely task goals, solely relational goals, or both (Palazzolo, 1981).

Hiltz et al (1991) investigated the effects of designated leadership in a distributed computerised conferencing environment. In terms of consensus, groups without leaders benefited from statistical feedback (tables that periodically displayed the degree of consensus) by the system. Conversely, groups with leaders had a reduction in consensus when statistical feedback was provided. George et al (1990) examined the effects of assigned leadership in GSS-supported meetings. Leadership was found to have no effects on consensus and decision quality in both unsupported and GSS groups, and both identified and anonymous groups. This lack of effects has been attributed to possible confusion arising from the overlapping missions of facilitators and leaders (George et al, 1990). Ho and Raman (1991) studied the effects of elected leadership in unsupported and GSS groups. Leadership had no effects on consensus under both treatments. This result could be due to the inability of leaders to cope with demands of both task and relational goals (Ho & Raman, 1991). Lim et al (1994) explored the effects of elected leadership on the group meeting process. Leadership resulted in an increase in amount of influence behaviour for unsupported and GSS groups.

## Research hypotheses

The independent variables are facilitation (with vs without) and leadership (with vs without). Groups under the facilitation treatment have an external facilitator who performs task interventions, aimed at attaining task goals, during their meetings. The facilitator does not carry out interactional interventions because prior research has found that facilitators may not be able to cope simultaneously with both task and interactional interventions without extensive experience (e.g. Lee-Partridge, 1992). Groups under the leadership treatment elect an internal leader who carries out interactional inventions, aimed at achieving relational goals, during their meetings. Prior research has suggested that an internal leader may be more capable than an external facilitator at carrying out interactional interventions (e.g. Lee-Partridge, 1992). The leader does not perform task interventions because prior studies have found that leaders may not be able to deal simultaneously with the demands of both task and relational goals (e.g., Ho & Raman, 1991). All groups have a chauffeur who assists when group members have difficulties using GSS tools (Griffith et al, 1998). This clear separation of roles among the facilitator, leader, and chauffeur helps to reduce confusion arising from overlap in roles (George et al, 1990; Dickson et al, 1993).

The dependent variables reflect key aspects of meeting success. Successful meetings are characterised by task effectiveness and participant satisfaction (Niederman et al, 1996). All groups complete a preference task, which has no objective correct answers (McGrath, 1984). Effective completion of such a task is often measured using group consensus and decision confidence (Watson et al, 1988; Sambamurthy, 1989). Post-meeting consensus is the level of agreement among group members after their meeting. This measure tends to be high when group members complete their task effectively and agree wholeheartedly with the group decision (Dess & Orieger, 1987). Decision confidence is the degree to which group members are sure that they have arrived at an appropriate group decision. When their task has been completed effectively, group members tend to have greater confidence with the group decision (Sambamurthy, 1989). Discussion quality and process satisfaction are facets of participant satisfaction (Niederman et al, 1996). Discussion quality is the extent to which group members feel that the information exchanged during their meeting is meaningful. Process satisfaction is the degree to which group members are happy with the way they arrived at their group decision. Group members tend to be more satisfied with their meeting if they are able to exchange meaningful information and have a good process of making decisions (Gouran et al, 1978; Green & Taber, 1980).

The adaptive structuration theory posits that the manner with which groups appropriate a GSS can affect their meeting success (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Through task and interactional interventions, facilitators and leaders can alter the way groups appropriate a GSS, thereby influencing meeting success.

## Facilitation effects

Task interventions can be effectively carried out by a facilitator who promotes group ownership and responsibility for its decision, clarifies and integrates information, develops and asks appropriate task questions, keeps the group focused on its meeting outcomes, presents relevant information to the group, and directs and manages the meeting (Clawson et al, 1993) (see Table 1). These interventions can be done with the aid of GSS tools. For instance, information and opinions can be solicited from the group and integrated using the idea generation tool. This information can then be displayed on the public screen so that the facilitator can discuss and clarify it with the group. Using GSS tools to solicit opinions helps the facilitator to promote group responsibility for its decisions. As another example, the facilitator can use the public screen to pose task questions and present relevant information to the group. Moreover, a proper combination of the idea generation and evaluation tools can assist the facilitator to keep the group focused on steps (e.g., generation and evaluation of decision criteria followed by generation and evaluation of decision alternatives) that lead to task accomplishment.

By systematically guiding the group to its decision through proper task interventions and use of GSS tools, facilitation can alter meeting outcomes (Bostrom et al, 1993). When the facilitator encourages the group to follow a decision approach that permits all group members to contribute, group members are likely to agree with the final decision because inputs from everyone have been incorporated (Watson et al, 1988). To the extent that such inputs represent the collective knowledge and wisdom of the group, group members are likely to be confident with a final decision that captures the essence of all such inputs (Janis & Mann, 1977).

Hypothesis 1a: GSS groups with facilitation will have higher post-meeting consensus than GSS groups without facilitation, controlling for pre-meeting consensus.

Hypothesis 1b: GSS groups with facilitation will have higher decision confidence than GSS groups without facilitation.

If the facilitator pursues a decision strategy that actively elicits inputs from everyone, group members will be able to contribute according to their knowledge and experience. The collection of useful perspectives from all group members is likely to enhance the overall quality of group discussion (Janis & Mann, 1977). Moreover, the assurance of having used a systematic decision approach, where all group members can participate during decision-making, is likely to be translated into increased satisfaction of group members for their decision process (Green & Taber, 1980).

Hypothesis 1c: GSS groups with facilitation will have higher discussion quality than GSS groups without facilitation.

Hypothesis 1d: GSS groups with facilitation will have higher process satisfaction than GSS groups without facilitation.

## Leadership effects

A leader can carry out interactional interventions effectively by creating an open and positive meeting environment, building good rapport and relationships in the group, managing and controlling conflicts and emotions, and encouraging multiple perspectives (Clawson et al, 1993) (see Table 1). If the group meets with an open mind, group members are more likely to share their true opinions. If the group adopts a positive attitude, group members are more likely to resolve their disagreements constructively and react favourably to the use of GSS tools. Good rapport and relationships in the group will enable it to function cohesively and efficiently. When conflicts and emotions are regulated at healthy levels, the group is less likely to be distracted by destructive disputes and criticisms, or personal dislikes for GSS tools. Instead, it can channel its energy to task accomplishment. When multiple perspectives are solicited from the group, a more complete process of decision-making results. The final decision is more likely to reflect the collective interests of all group members.

When a leader applies appropriate interactional interventions and use of GSS tools, the group can use its time and resources more constructively for task completion during its meeting (Bostrom et al, 1993). With an effective decision approach that elicits a wide range of perspectives and stresses constructive resolution of conflicts, group members can better understand mutual viewpoints and how these viewpoints lead to the final decision. This way, group agreement with the final decision is likely to be high (Watson et al, 1988). If group members are convinced that their conflicts have been constructively reconciled and their differing perspectives have been incorporated into the final decision, they are likely to have greater confidence for their final decision (Janis & Mann, 1977).

Hypothesis 2a: GSS groups with leadership will have higher post-meeting consensus than GSS groups without leadership, controlling for pre-meeting consensus.

Hypothesis 2b: GSS groups with leadership will have higher decision confidence than GSS groups without leadership.

When a leader guides a group along a constructive decision approach, group members may be more willing to contribute opinions and information if their personal interests are compatible with the group goal. To the extent that the greater contribution of all group members reflects their collective knowledge and wisdom, the quality of group discussion is likely to be high (Janis & Mann, 1977). Moreover, a constructive conflict resolution strategy, where destructive disputes and criticisms are controlled, is likely to increase the satisfaction of group members for their decision process (Green & Taber, 1980).

Hypothesis 2c: GSS groups with leadership will have higher discussion quality than GSS groups without leadership.

Hypothesis 2d: GSS groups with leadership will have higher process satisfaction than GSS groups without leadership.

## Combined effects

The substitutes for leadership framework (Kerr & Jermier, 1978) can be used to predict the combined effects of facilitation and leadership on meeting outcomes (Anson et al, 1995). This framework posits that structures of work processes, such as facilitation and leadership, can interact in constructive, neutral, or destructive ways. Constructive interactions occur when structures supplement each other. For example, the facilitator displays collective information on the public screen and guides the group in discussing this information while the leader encourages group members to view opinion differences positively. Neutral interactions occur when structures substitute each other. For instance, the facilitator solicits multiple perspectives using the idea generation tool while the leader requests group members to challenge current perspectives. Destructive interactions occur when structures interfere with each other. For example, the facilitator suggests using specific GSS tools but the leader douses group enthusiasm by highlighting the technical difficulties of using these GSS tools.

Both task and interactional interventions serve to enhance different aspects of group meetings. When used conjointly, they constitute an effective intervention into the group meeting process (Hackman & Kaplan, 1974; Chilberg, 1989). This simultaneous pursuit of task and relational goals, through task and interactional interventions respectively, has been strongly advocated by the leadership literature (e.g. Palazzolo, 1981). Empirical GSS literature provides some indication that constructive interactions can occur in GSS-supported meetings. Anson et al (1995) found that the combined effects of facilitation and GSS on group cohesion and group perception of its meeting process were additive. Reagan-Cirincione (1994) reported that facilitation, group process technique, and information technology complemented each other to produce an additive impact on group task performance.

The discussion on facilitation and leadership suggests that each role, on its own, is likely to impact meeting outcomes positively. Given that the facilitator and leader in this study play complementary roles by focusing exclusively on task and interactional interventions respectively, constructive interactions are likely to result with facilitation and leadership. Therefore, the combined effects of facilitation and leadership are likely to be additive.

Hypothesis 3a: The combined effects of facilitation and leadership on post-meeting consensus will be additive, controlling for pre-meeting consensus.

Hypothesis 3b: The combined effects of facilitation and leadership on decision confidence will be additive.

Hypothesis 3c: The combined effects of facilitation and leadership on discussion quality will be additive.

Hypothesis 3d: The combined effects of facilitation and leadership on process satisfaction will be additive.

## Research methodology

The laboratory experiment had a 2×2 factorial design, manipulating facilitation and leadership. Each of the four treatments had 10 groups. All groups had five members.

## Operationalization of independent variables

The benefits of a chauffeur had been shown by Dickson et al (1993) and articulated by Griffith et al (1998). In this study, all groups had a chauffeur whose role was to help group members understand GSS tools and make them comfortable when using these tools (Clawson et al, 1993) (see Table 1). Prior to this study, the chauffeur received training in the use of GSS tools. During the meetings, the chauffeur explained the proper use of GSS tools to group members, helped them resolve all technical difficulties that arose, and addressed all inconveniences caused by the use of GSS tools. For experimental control purpose, the same chauffeur participated in all the 40 groups.

The task interventions carried out by the facilitator during the meetings are summarised in Table 2. Such interventions have been found to contribute towards meeting success (Clawson et al, 1993; Niederman et al, 1996). Prior to this study, the facilitator was thoroughly instructed on the nature of the experimental task and an appropriate agenda to complete this task. The main agenda items were criteria definition, criteria evaluation, alternatives definition, alternatives evaluation, and decision definition (Watson et al, 1988). Besides these instructions, the facilitator was thoroughly trained in carrying out task interventions in a GSS environment. All groups received a printed explanation of agenda items for their meetings. This helped to prevent effects of the agenda (Niederman et al, 1996) from confounding with facilitation effects.

However, this approach was taken to ensure consistency in facilitation abilities across all groups under the facilitation treatment. A t-test showed that the number of task interventions did not differ significantly for treatments with and without leadership.

## Measurement of dependent variables

Both pre-meeting and post-meeting consensus were measured using a fuzzy set method based on matrix algebra (Spillman et al, 1980). The input to this method was a matrix containing the decisions of all group members for all alternatives considered. This method assessed the agreement level in the group by comparing decision of all group members over all alternatives considered. The output of this method was a score ranging from 0 (total disagreement) to 1 (total agreement), with a higher score indicating better agreement in the group. Premeeting and post-meeting consensus were computed by supplying, as input to this method, the decisions of all group members before and after the meeting respectively. This method of measuring consensus has been used in prior GSS empirical studies (e.g. Poole et al, 1991; Dickson et al, 1993; Tan et al, 1994).

Decision confidence, discussion quality, and process satisfaction were perceptual variables (see Table 4). Decision confidence was assessed based on three questions taken from Sambamurthy (1989). Each question was anchored on a seven-point Likert scale. Discussion quality was measured using four questions taken from Gouran et al (1978). All these questions were also anchored on a seven-point Likert scale. Process satisfaction was gauged using five questions taken from Green and Taber (1980). All these questions were anchored on the original five-point Likert scale that accompanied this instrument. The score of each group member for each perceptual variable was obtained by averaging the responses of that group member to all questions measuring that variable. The score of each group for each perceptual variable was computed by averaging the scores of all group members for that variable. A higher score indicated a better measure for all three perceptual variables. These measures have been used extensively in prior GSS empirical research (e.g. Gopal et al, 1993; Sambamurthy & Chin, 1994; Anson et al, 1995).

A factor analysis, specifying a three-factor solution, on responses to questions in Table 4 showed that all the questions measuring each perceptual variable loaded onto a separate factor. These loadings ranged from 0.41 to 0.82, indicating strong correlation between each question and the variable it measured. These results were evidence of construct validity for the three perceptual variables (Johnson & Wichern, 1992). Cronbach’s alpha for decision confidence, discussion quality, and process satisfaction were 0.70, 0.80, and 0.85 respectively, satisfying Nunnally’s (1978) criteria for reliability.

## The procedure

Each group began by solving a warm-up task in a faceto-face setting with neither GSS support, nor facilitation, nor leadership. This no support setting prevented introducing effects that could confound with treatment effects. Besides alleviating problems of zero-history groups (McGrath, 1984), this warm-up task gave each group under the leadership treatment a basis to elect their leader (Ho & Raman, 1991). Each group was given 30 minutes to complete the warm-up task. After this, each group under the leadership treatment was asked to elect a leader among the group members. Following the election, the leader was thoroughly instructed on the role of carrying out interactional interventions and given a printed explanation of this role for reference purpose. Each group under the no leadership treatment skipped this stage.

Group members completed the experimental task individually, without communication. This information allowed level of pre-meeting consensus in each group to be computed. There was no time limit for this stage. After this, the chauffeur trained each group in the use of GSS tools. The training session for each group was conducted using the same training script so that training effects would not confound with treatment effects (Watson et al, 1998; Tan et al, 1994). This stage lasted about 45 minutes. After the training, group members were given about 15 minutes to read a printed explanation of agenda items that could help them complete the experimental task (Watson et al, 1988). Group members kept this list of agenda items for reference purpose.

Each group met to perform the experimental task under its own treatment conditions. No time limit was imposed for the group meeting. After this, group members individually responded to a questionnaire that collected demographic information and measured their levels of decision confidence, discussion quality, and process satisfaction (see Table 4). Finally, group members completed the experimental task individually again, without communication. This information allowed level of post-meeting consensus in each group to be computed. There was no time limit for the last two stages.

## The tasks

The warm-up task was the Desert Survival Task (Watson, 1987). Group members were given a crisis scenario, which depicted them as survivors of a plane crash. Trapped in the middle of a large desert, they had to rank 15 items in order of importance for survival purpose. This task had been shown to elicit considerable debate (Watson, 1987), which should provide ample opportunities for a leader to emerge.

The experimental task was the Personal Trust Foundation Task (Watson, 1987). Group members had to allocate a specific amount of funds to six competing projects. These projects were anchored on the six basic motives in personality (theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, and religious). During task validation, people were found to complete this task based on their unique personality profiles (Watson, 1987). Since a wide range of personality profiles existed among people, there was no objective basis for establishing a correct answer for this task. Hence, it could be considered a preference task (McGrath, 1984).

## The subjects

A total of 200 subjects (40 groups) completed this study. Each subject took part in only one group. Subjects were information systems undergraduates from a large university. They were randomly assigned to groups, which were randomly assigned to treatments to control for confounding effects due to differences in subject characteristics. Subjects were given course credit to encourage them to take this study seriously. ANOVA tests found no significant differences for subjects in different treatments in terms of age, experience working in groups, and experience working with computers. Mann–Whitney tests found no significant differences in the proportion of male to female subjects across treatments. Controls over subject characteristics, through randomisation, appeared to be successful.

## The setting and meeting

All meetings took place in the same place and same time setting, where group members could communicate verbally or electronically. Group members were seated around a U-shape conference table facing a public screen. For groups with leadership, the leader took the most prominent centre seat at the conference table. The chauffeur and facilitator were not given seats at the conference table because they were considered external parties rather than an internal part of the group. The same meeting room and GSS facility was used by all the 40 groups.

The GSS used was Software Aided Group Environment (SAGE). During meetings, a private terminal allowed each group member, including the leader, to invoke capabilities of the GSS. For groups with facilitation, the facilitator was served by another similar private terminal. A public terminal contained a shared database that collected the details of group meetings. It was connected to a projector that displayed collective group information onto a large screen. In all groups, the chauffeur worked on the public terminal, which could be used to start up or shut down the entire GSS facility, or diagnose technical problems. GSS tools used in this study were idea gathering, idea evaluation, meeting thoughts, and meeting utilities. All the 40 groups relied extensively on GSS tools and electronic communication to conduct their meetings.

The meetings typically started with the group using the idea gathering tool to generate decision criteria. A consolidated list of such criteria would be displayed on the large screen. The group would then debate on these criteria verbally or electronically using the meeting thoughts tool. Criteria deemed irrevelant would be discarded while new criteria deemed relevant would be added. Next, the group would proceed to rate, rank, or vote on criteria using the idea evaluation tool. Results of criteria evaluation would be displayed on the large screen. Verbally or electronically, the group would discuss these results, focusing their debate on more contentious criteria. After discussion, another round of criteria evaluation might be conducted. This process of criteria evaluation and discussion would proceed until the group was comfortable with the results of criteria evaluation. The final outcome of criteria evaluation would then be displayed, using the meeting utilities tool, on a group scratchpad visible to everyone at their private terminals.

Since the six alternatives (projects) had been defined in the experimental task, the group would proceed to evaluate these alternatives. Taking into consideration results of criteria evaluation, group members would invoke the idea evaluation tool to rate, rank, or vote on alternatives to establish their relative importance. Results of alternatives evaluation would be projected on the large screen for everyone to see. Again, the group would discuss these results verbal or electronically, focusing on more contentious alternatives. Further rounds of alternatives evaluation and discussion could be carried out if necessary. This process would continue until the group was satisfied with the results of alternatives evaluation. Using the meeting utilities tool, the final outcome of alternatives evaluation would be displayed on a group scratchpad visible to everyone at their private terminals.

Finally, based on the results of alternative evaluation, group members would use the idea evaluation tool to allocate funds to the alternatives (projects). As before, results of fund allocation would be displayed on the large screen for everyone to see. If there were substantial disagreements on any project, these issues would be debated verbally or electronically by the group. If necessary, further rounds of fund allocation and debate could be carried out. The meeting ended when all group members agreed on the amounts of funds to be allocated to each of the six projects.

## Results for post-meeting consensus

The level of pre-meeting consensus in a group had been found to significantly affect the level of post-meeting consensus (Watson et al, 1988; Ho & Raman, 1991). Hence, the data for post-meeting consensus were analysed using an ANCOVA test with pre-meeting consensus as a covariate. A logarithm transformation (Weisberg, 1985) was performed on the data for postmeeting consensus to allow it to meet ANCOVA requirements of homogeneity of variances and normality of residuals (Neter et al, 1990). ANCOVA also required the dependent variable and covariate to be related by linearity and homogeneity of regressions (Kufs, 1983). But violations of these requirements would not be critical if the covariate had a normal distribution (Elashoff, 1969). A Shapiro–Wilk test showed that the data for premeeting consensus was normally distributed.

## Discussion and implications

The results of this study are summarised in Table 8. These results are discussed and compared with prior findings, and implications for practice are suggested.

## Discussion on post-meeting consensus

In the absence of a leader who performs interactional interventions, the presence of a facilitator who carries out task interventions appears to lead to a reduction in consensus. The roles of the facilitator include assisting the group in using GSS tools to elicit a wide range of perspectives from all group members and guiding them in subsequent discussion on perspectives generated. This may trigger a substantial amount of disagreements in the group. The leader can contribute by encouraging respect for and consideration of all perspectives, and promoting constructive resolution of disagreements through consensus. Without a leader who plays such roles, the group may have a reduction in consensus. Similar observations on facilitation effects have been reported in other studies. Dickson et al (1993) reported that groups supported by rigid facilitation had lower consensus than groups supported by a chauffeur, in the absence of leadership. Lee-Partridge (1992) found that provision of semiflexible task or interactional interventions by a facilitator did not help to raise group consensus in a no leadership treatment.<sup>3</sup>

In the absence of a facilitator who performs task interventions, a leader who carries out interactional interventions appears to help a group to attain marginally higher consensus. Without the facilitator, there is no one to ensure that the group will employ GSS tools in support of the agenda items. For instance, instead of using the idea evaluation tool to elicit diverse opinions from group members and then resolve differences constructively, the group may use this tool to identify dissenting views and then suppress them with negative remarks. In such instances, it is valuable to have a leader who promotes mutual respect among group members, encourages them to participate constructively, and helps them to resolve conflicting opinions through consensus. In this way, the diverse opinions of group members can be incorporated into the group decision. When this happens, group members may agree more with each other and with the group decision (Watson et al, 1988; Lee-Partridge, 1992). Other studies manipulating leadership have yielded consistent results. In their GSS treatment with no facilitation, Lim et al (1994) observed that a leader can help to raise the amount and evenness of contributions from group members. Hiltz et al (1991) found that, in the absence of statistical feedback and facilitation, a leader can help to raise group consensus. The study by Hiltz et al (1991) was conducted in a same time but different place setting. Thus, its results add some generalisability to the observation that a leader can contribute to consensus attainment when facilitation is absent.

## Discussion on perceptual variables

A facilitator who carries out task interventions (as defined in Table 2) appears to raise decision confidence in a group. For example, efforts by the facilitator to direct and manage the meeting may have helped group members to arrive at their final decision systematically. Efforts by the facilitator to clarify and integrate information may have encouraged all group members to contribute so that the group benefits from the collective knowledge and wisdom of everyone. Therefore, with task interventions, group members have a better perception for the product of their meetings.

A leader who carries out interactional interventions (as defined in Table 3) appears to enhance discussion quality and process satisfaction. For example, efforts by the leader to encourage multiple perspectives may have helped group members to better understand mutual viewpoints and engage in richer discussion of issues. Efforts by the leader to manage conflicts and emotions may have allowed group members to resolve disputes constructively during the meeting process rather than allowing disputes to escalate. Thus, with interactional interventions, group members have a better impression for the process of their meetings.

Earlier studies (e.g. Clawson et al, 1993; Niederman et al, 1996) have elicited the list of task and interactional interventions by interviewing facilitators and observing their work. This study formally tests the list of interventions in an experiment and demonstrates that task and interactional interventions play different roles during meetings. While the former contributes to meeting success by enhancing meeting the product, the latter contributes by improving the meeting process. Moreover, the combined effects seem to be additive. Hence, both task and interactional interventions, when exercised by a facilitator and a leader respectively, can be used conjointly to raise group perception for the product and process of their meetings. Raising group perception is critical in organizational groups because this affects the level of support by group members for the implementation of the group decision.

Lee-Partridge (1992) found that provision of interactional on top of task interventions by a facilitator lowered perceptual measures like decision quality and process satisfaction. Among their GSS group, George et al (1990) did not observe any effects of leadership on decision quality. They attributed this lack of effects to the possible confusion among roles. With clear and mutually exclusive roles for the facilitator and leader, this study shows that both facilitation and leadership can have a positive impact on perceptual variables. These findings support the argument by George et al (1990) for a clearer separation of facilitation and leadership roles during meetings. They also reinforce the observation by Lee-Partridge (1992) that interactional interventions may be better carried out by an internal leader rather than an external facilitator.

Earlier studies conducted in traditional meetings without GSS support suggest that task and interactional interventions serve to enhance different aspects of group meetings and can be used conjointly as an effective intervention (Hackman & Kaplan, 1974; Chilberg, 1989). The findings of this study extend that suggestion by illustrating that both types of interventions can also be used together to produce a positive impact in GSSsupported meetings. Moreover, these findings reinforce the results of prior GSS research (e.g. Reagan-Cirincione, 1994; Anson et al, 1995) showing that constructive interactions of interventions can occur in GSSsupported meetings.

## Implications for practice

Prior experimental studies have demonstrated that meeting outcomes tend to be adversely affected when internal leaders had to cope with both task and interactional interventions (Ho & Raman, 1991), when external facilitators carried out interactional interventions (Lee-Partridge, 1992), or when there was no clear separation of roles between external facilitators and internal leaders (George et al, 1990). As a rare study which manipulated both facilitation and leadership simultaneously, this study shows that meeting outcomes can be positively affected when external facilitators performed solely task interventions, internal leaders carried out solely interactional interventions, and this separation of roles for external facilitators and internal leaders was clear. By formally assessing the impact of task and interactional interventions, that have been consolidated based on the work of facilitators (Clawson et al, 1993; Niederman et al, 1996), this study offers precise guidelines on task and interactional interventions (see Tables 2 and 3) that can be applied during meetings. Niederman et al (1996) provide additional details on interventions that may be useful before and after meetings.

The role of an experienced professional facilitator is to effectively carry out both task and interactional interventions. Results of earlier studies have revealed that it is challenging for facilitators to cope with such combined roles and that professional facilitators can benefit from extensive training (Bostrom et al, 1993; Griffith et al, 1998). Nevertheless, when such an experienced professional facilitator is unavailable, the findings of this study provide useful suggestions for practice. First, leaders can be trained in the application of interactional interventions and allowed to solely concentrate on such interventions during meetings. Second, external facilitators can be restricted to applying task interventions during meetings. Third, when both external facilitators and internal leaders are exercising such interventions, their respective roles should be mutually exclusive and made clear to the entire group.

## Conclusion

This study employed experimental groups who met in a laboratory setting. Attempts to generalise its findings to organizational groups who meet in real settings must be done with caution. Nevertheless, the generalisability of these findings can be extended with further research in several directions. First, this study can be replicated with groups of larger sizes. Effects of facilitation and GSS have been shown to be stronger with larger groups (Valacich et al, 1994). Second, this study can be replicated with other task types (McGrath, 1984) since task type is a critical factor moderating the impact of GSS during meetings (Benbasat & Lim, 1993; Tan et al, 1994). Third, the feasibility of conducting task and interactional interventions using automated decision heuristics (Limayem, 1996) instead of human facilitators and leaders can be explored. If proven feasible, GSS tools that embed such heuristics can help to reduce the cost of human facilitation and do away with the need to expose external facilitators to sensitive information during meetings.

In an information age, groups have to meet to solve increasingly complex problems, requiring them to interpret increasingly voluminous information in progressively shorter time spans (Huber, 1984). Application of appropriate task and interactional interventions during meetings can help groups to better cope with their challenges (Chilberg, 1989). Means of enforcing these interventions include facilitation, leadership, and automated decision heuristics. But these different means have produced different effects on meeting outcomes, depending on the situation. Research along the direction of this study can contribute to a contingency theory on what interventions and means of application are appropriate under what circumstances.
