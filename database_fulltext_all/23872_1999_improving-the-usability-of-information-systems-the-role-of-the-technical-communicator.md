# Improving the usability of information systems: the role of the technical communicator

J Fisher

Victoria University, PO Box 14428, MCMC, Melbourne, 8001, Australia

A successful information system implementation requires effective logical analysis, sound technical construction, effective design for users and successful change management during implementation. If these conditions are not present then in all probability the system will be a failure. Ensuring a system is successful from a user’s perspective is primarily about usability. There are many aspects of information system design that impact on usability including: online help, the design of the user interface, system and error messages. These aspects need consideration early in the development cycle. This paper discusses the need for improved usability design in information systems and presents results of recent research which examined the role of technical communicators in systems development and explored the extent to which their work improved system usability. The study found that from a user’s perspective an improved system result when technical communicators are involved particularly in the early stages of the development process.

## Introduction

Failures in implementation of information systems, and the reasons and explanations behind them are well documented (Grudin, 1991; Myers, 1993; Bussen & Myers, 1997). One common reason given is a lack of communication between users and developers leading to deficient analysis and poor usability. Shand (1994, p 76) suggests, ‘. . . the users fail to communicate to the technologists what it is they really want the system to do, and the technologists fail to communicate to the users what they intend the system to look like and how it will work’. Bresko (1995) also cites communication problems as a major cause of system failure and argues that development methods and management techniques are the cause of most of the problems.

Communication problems between system developers and users particularly result in the failure of the human factors aspects of the system; that is, the user is unable to effectively use the system. The system may meet the functional and design requirements but it may not meet user expectations and therefore fails (Shand, 1994, p 76).

This is a significant problem for the information technology (IT) industry and must be addressed if costly rework and inefficient and ineffective systems are to be avoided.

Part of the problem, the literature argues, is that programmers and developers who are not the most appropriate people, are responsible for the usability aspects of systems (Nelson, 1990; Grudin, 1991). Nelson puts it bluntly when he says:

Historical accident has kept programmers in control of a field in which most of them have no aptitude: the artistic integration of the mechanisms they work with. It is nice that engineers and programmers and software executives have found a new form of creativity in which to find a sense of personal fulfillment. It is just unfortunate that they have to inflict the results on users. (Nelson, 1990, p 243)

One approach to improving the communication between users and IT developers has been the employment of specialist skilled technical communicators in information systems development projects. This paper argues that the inclusion of a technical communicator in the development process improves the usability of a system.

## The technical communication profession

The origins of technical communication go back to the 1950s in the United States, and the 1960s in Australia, where technical writers were employed by the manufacturing and electronics industries principally to write manuals and instructions for the use of complex mechanical equipment (Brockmann, 1989; Brady, 1996).

Today technical communicators are employed in many industries writing a range of material. The majority of technical communicators in the United States and Australia work in computer related areas (Fisher, 1994; STC, 1995). Although the term technical writer is still used to describe someone performing this work, the term technical communicator is a more appropriate descriptor because today the work of a technical communicator extends well beyond that of just writing (Haselkorn, 1996), particularly in the context of systems development.

## Skill set of technical communicators

The basic task of a technical communicator is to present and explain complex technologies in a simple and relevant way for end-users. Technical authoring is the most widely recognised skill of a technical communicator, however this skill involves a particular style of writing. A Canadian study (STC-Manitoba, 1994) recognised that technical communications professionals have the skill of writing, however, with important and specific differences. Technical communicators must also be able to ‘. . . adopt the user’s perspective, and may be able to suggest changes that make a product or process easier to use’ (STC-Manitoba, 1994, p 12). The study suggests the writing must be clear and precise with no judgments attached.

The literature describes other skills technical communicators must necessarily have to produce all forms of effective user documentation. These include:

I organization of information

I the design and layout of information

I editing and revising

graphics and illustrating (STC-Manitoba, 1994; Green & Nolan, 1995).

Skills such as layout of information, graphics and illustration creation are important for interface design and again the literature suggests that technical communicators have skills which allow them to participate in this area.

## System usability and technical communicators

System usability broadly encompasses those factors that affect how easily a user can use a system, or, how user friendly it seems from the end-users’ perspective.

Usability refers to how people work with the product . . . Usability means that the people who use the product can do so quickly and easily to accomplish their own tasks. (Dumas & Redish, 1994, p 4).

Attention to the usability aspects of technology can be the difference between market success and failure. The quality of user information in its various forms has a direct impact on the application of the system and user productivity. The provision of hard-copy user manuals, online help, intuitive interface design, meaningful system and error messages are a necessity if users are to make the best use of a system.

There is much literature to suggest that when professionals, such as technical communicators, are involved early in the development process there are significant improvements to the usability elements of systems (STC-Manitoba, 1994; Shand, 1994; Redish, 1995). Recent research in the United States, aimed at quantifying the value of technical communicators, identified a number of benefits including increased productivity of users and more of the users problems being identified earlier in the process because of the work of a technical communicator. In addition there were cost savings including:

Fewer support calls; lower support costs.

Less need for training; lower training costs.

Less downtime for workers.

Lower costs for writing paper, printing, etc, because technical communicators convinced developers that they did not need all the documentation they were planning . . . Fewer errors by users . . . (Redish, 1995, p 29).

The aspects of systems, important from a usability perspective, that are the focus of this paper are online help, the design of the interface, and system and error messages.

## Research method

The findings described in this paper are derived from data gathered from a national survey and a structured case study.

## The survey

At the end of 1996 a survey was sent to all 458 members of the Australian Society for Technical Communication (ASTC). The response rate was 46% (210). Bryman and Cramer (1992, p 67) argue that members of a professional body are a legitimate group to select as they are representative of the whole profession.

The survey sought to determine the roles technical communicators were playing in the development of information systems in Australia, at what stage they became involved in the process and to establish if their contribution improved the final product from the users perspective. Of those who responded, 73% (154) indicated they had recently worked on a computer system. The data, from those 154 respondents, was analysed using SPSS. Frequency analysis, comparison of means, cross-tabulations and a factor analysis were undertaken.

## The case study

The survey results describe only part of the overall picture: that is, the perspective of one group of people involved in the development of information systems. To be able to draw conclusions as to the contribution technical communicators make to usability, an investigation of the views of the other stakeholders, system developers and users, was necessary. The survey results were used to frame a case study investigation of the development of 20 information systems. Ten systems involved a technical communicator and 10 did not. The first 10 systems detailed in the appendix used a technical communicator. Greater detail of the 20 systems can be found in Appendix 1.

The case study method involved structured interviews with participants and stake-holders for each system investigated. Interviews were held with a developer, between three and five users, and the technical communicator, where appropriate. A total of 22 developers, 11 technical communicators and 66 users were interviewed. The majority of users interviewed were unaware of the involvement or otherwise of a technical communicator in their system. Both qualitative and quantitative data was collected through the case study. Other similar research reported in the literature has used as few as 58 interviews to generate meaningful, reliable data (Nord & Nord, 1994; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994).

A research protocol, in the form of a booklet, was developed. Interviewees were asked to complete some questions in the booklet and were encouraged to verbally qualify their answers. Some questions required only a verbal response from the interviewees. All interviews were tape recorded. The use of a research protocol, Yin (1989) argues, increases the reliability of case study results. The interviews were semi-structured allowing analysis of quantitative data. The qualitative data was analysed using a meta-matrix as described by Miles and Huberman (1994). A matrix ‘. . . is essentially the “crossing” of two lists, set up as rows and columns’ (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p 93). ‘Meta-matrices are master charts assembling descriptive data from each of several cases in a standard format’ (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p 178).

The use of a meta-matrix allows data to be analysed in a number of ways. Miles and Huberman (1994, pp 246–253) argue that conclusions that generate meaning (making and interpreting findings at different levels of inference) can be drawn using these techniques. Both Miles and Huberman (1994) and Yin (1989) argue that results from multiple cases analysed appropriately can be generalised.

The technical communicators who completed the survey and those involved in the case study are all members of the professional body of technical communicators. The 10 development projects that used a technical communicator employed that person in a way consistent with the accepted role of a technical communicator.

## Australian research results

The following sections summarise the results of both the survey and the case study relating to respondents’ work on those aspects of systems that impact on usability. Other aspects were also investigated but are not reported in this paper.

## Skills of technical communicators

Qualifications and experience in the field are important indicators of professional performance. The survey determined that technical communicators in Australia are highly qualified with 84% holding a tertiary qualification. Few Australian respondents have a qualification in technical communication, since (unlike the United States) there are few tertiary level courses (Fisher, 1995). Respondents have extensive experience in the field with 57% having worked in the field for more than six years.

## Roles performed by technical communicators

A number of questions and statements were put to respondents relating to different usability aspects of systems. A factor analysis demonstrated that there was a correlation between these items, that is they tapped into the same concept and respondents understood what those concepts were. The survey therefore had content and construct validity.

Survey respondents were presented with a list of roles and asked to indicate which of those roles they had performed recently. Table 1 presents those results relevant to this paper.

These results indicate that the most frequently performed role was writing online help, however this was still performed by little more than half the respondents. More than one-third of respondents have had some involvement in interface design. Writing system and error messages was the task least performed with less than a third of respondents undertaking work in this area.

## Who else was involved?

Survey respondents were asked to provide the title of the person who performed the roles of writer of online help, interface designer, and writer of error messages, if that person was not a technical communicator. During the interviews the developers were also asked who performed these roles. The respondents and the interviewees predominantly said that programmers, developers, analysts or the development team performed these roles.

## Online help

It could be assumed that because technical communicators are recognised primarily as the writers of paperbased user documentation they would also be heavily involved in the writing of online help. Online help also uses the written word to convey information to users, to expedite their use of a computer system. The literature, however, suggests that online help is often of poor quality and not written by technical communicators (STC-Manitoba, 1994; Bresko, 1995; Gillmor, 1995). Where this is the case users are limited in their effective use of a system, on the other hand when technical communicators are involved a better online help product results (Blackwell, 1995).

The current literature strongly argues that technical communicators should be writing online help (Horton, 1993; Humphreys, 1994). Despite this there is little research that demonstrates the level of involvement of technical communicators. In 1989 Kirsch found that while 77% of technical communicators he surveyed had worked on software manuals only 15% had written online help (Kirsch, 1989, p 219). This finding was supported by more recent Canadian research (STC-Manitoba, 1994). Interviews were conducted with employers of technical communicators, one question they were asked was what types of material technical communicators frequently worked on. Of a list of nine, based on frequency, online documentation was listed eighth suggesting employers are not using technical communicators for this work.

Online help is not always provided with a system. The survey results indicated that 74% of systems have online help. Of the 20 systems investigated during the case study, 15 (75%) had online help. Although approximately three-quarters of all systems have online help only 59% of survey respondents indicated they were involved recently in writing this material. This was consistent with the case study findings. Although nine out of the 10 systems where a technical communicator was involved had online help, the technical communicator was responsible for writing it for only six of those systems.

Technical communicators and the quality of online help If online help is provided, and is not written by a qualified person such as a technical communicator, does this have an impact on the quality of the online help?

To explore this issue, survey respondents were asked to indicate on a five-point scale the percentage of time they spent writing online help. They were also asked to indicate on a five-point scale the level to which they agreed or disagreed with a statement relating to quality. The question and statement were cross-tabulated and the results are presented in Figure 1. The actual table of figures can be found in Table 8 in Appendix 2.

The results indicate that if the respondent spent time writing online help then they were likely to agree strongly or agree with the statement that the quality of the online help was effective for users. All respondents who disagreed strongly with the statement spent no time working on online help. Further statistical analysis of this data (using a Chi-square test $P < 0 . 0 5 )$ indicates these findings are statistically significant (0.00009). It could be argued that these results are to be expected, that if someone is performing the task then they will rank the quality of the work high. The best judges of online help are the users.

There were more negative comments of online help written by programmers.

## User interface design

The literature provides some evidence of technical communicators participating in interface design, however their contribution is quite subtle, with the role tending to be an advisory one (Pieratti, 1995). Authors such as Velte (1990) claim technical communicators are interface designers on the basis that they are responsible for aspects of the interface such as online help. Others such as Shirk and Smith (1994) and Zimmerman (1989) suggest technical communicators are part of a team making decisions about user interfaces and the distinction between writer and programmer is harder to draw.

Whilst there is little research in the area there is some evidence that technical communicators are moving into the field and making a valuable contribution (Zimmerman, 1989; Roberts, 1995).

To explore the question of the extent of the involvement of Australian technical communicators in the design of user interfaces, survey respondents were presented with three statements. Respondents were asked to indicate on a five-point scale the level to which they agreed with each statement. The results are presented in Table 5.

The results indicate that the majority of technical communicators will tell developers if they believe the user interface is not appropriate for users and 40% will convey the views of the users on the interface to developers. One-quarter of respondents are consulted by developers on the design of screen icons.

The case study results supported the survey findings that the role of the technical communicator in the design of the user interface tends to be a subtle, advisory role. Developers of systems where a technical communicator was involved indicated the technical communicator in most cases did contribute significantly to the design of the user interface (see Table 3).

## Users’ views on user interfaces

Users were asked to indicate on a five-point scale the level to which they agreed with the statement: ‘I am comfortable with the design of the interface’. There were five systems where the developer rated the technical communicator’s contribution to the design of the user interface, to be seven or greater, out of a possible 10 (see section above). The Mann–Whitney U test for two unrelated samples was applied comparing the mean rating given by users of those five systems (group 1) with the users of the other systems (group 2). The test was used to determine if the contribution of the technical communicator did have an impact on the quality of the interface from the users’ perspective. Table 6 presents the results.

This result strongly indicates that there is a measurable difference in the quality of the user interface where a technical communicator has been involved. It is statistically significant that the users of systems where a technical communicator contributed to the design of the user interface are more comfortable with that interface than the users of systems where the interface has been designed by a programmer.

## System and error messages

Although technical communicators are not commonly used for writing system error messages, it is a role that primarily requires communication skills and an understanding of users. It is in this context that it would seem appropriate that technical communicators be involved. System and error messages occur unexpectedly and often relate to technically complex situations that users do not specifically understand.

The message: ‘Do you wish to continue? (Y/N)’, is often a critical decision.

System error messages convey to users timely information about the system, hence it would be expected that technical communicators would play a role in their design (Rubens & Krull, 1988). Again there is little research into the extent to which technical communicators are writing these messages. A limited study of technical communicators conducted by Kirsch (1989) in 1988 found 19% of respondents had written system messages.

There is, however, evidence from a number of case studies that when technical communicators are consulted or involved that there is an improvement in the quality of those messages (Killingsworth & Jones, 1995; Pieratti, 1995). Further, a number of articles directed towards technical communicators, raise the issue of the quality of system error messages and the impact of these on user satisfaction, performance and success with a system (Horton, 1990; Roush, 1992; Heneghan, 1996).

Although all surveyed technical communicators saw the writing of system and error messages as a role they should perform, only half of the system developers agreed. ## Users’ views

Users do care about the quality of system messages. A simple count of the number of specific comments users made regarding system and error messages found that half of all users commented. Of these comments 72% were negative. Users made more negative comments about the quality of the system and error messages than for any other aspect of the system. The results suggest that users are generally unhappy with error message text and this would further indicate that it is not meeting their needs.

A comparison of systems where a technical communicator was used to write system and error messages and systems where this was undertaken by someone else is not easy to make. Few systems used the technical communicator in this role and in many cases the technical communicator performed only an editing role. Although no conclusions can be drawn regarding the involvement of the technical communicator and the quality of the system and error message text in the systems where the technical communicator did contribute, the users rated the quality of those messages highly.

## Conclusion

To improve the usability of information systems the current literature suggests there is a need for system development teams to include other experts such as technical communicators in the development process. The literature argues that technical communicators have the necessary skills to contribute particularly to the usability aspects of information systems. The research reported in this paper found that technical communicators are highly qualified and skilled for the work described. Their key skill, writing, however is not being fully utilised. The developers acknowledged that technical communicators can, and do contribute significantly to the writing of online help and the design of the user interface. However, developers appear to overlook the contribution technical communicators might make to the writing of system and error messages. The most significant findings from this research though is that users find the quality of online help to be higher when a technical communicator writes it and are more likely to use it. Further the users also indicated that the interfaces they preferred were those where a technical communicator had been involved. Users overwhelmingly are frustrated by the poor quality of system and error message text. Developers write the majority of this information.

The research results suggest that technical communicators play a much greater role in systems development than may have previously been recognised. From the research findings it can be concluded that technical communicators are involved with, and do make a contribution to, the three human factors aspects of systems discussed. They have the skills, as professional communicators, and should be consulted to a much greater extent. There is clear evidence that their contribution does improve the usability of the system from the perspective of both users and developers.
