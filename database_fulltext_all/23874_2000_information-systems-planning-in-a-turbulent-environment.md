# Information systems planning in a turbulent environment

H Salmela<sup>1</sup>, AL Lederer<sup>2</sup> and T Reponen<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Turku School of Economics and Business Administration, Department of Management/Information Systems Science, PO Box 110, 20521 Turku, Finland; <sup>2</sup>Carol M. Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, Kentucky 40506–0034, USA; <sup>3</sup>Turku School of Economics and Business Administration, Department of Management/Information Systems Science, Rehtorinpellonkatu 3, 20500 Turku, Finland

The information systems (IS) planning literature suggests two competing theories of effective planning in a turbulent environment. One predicts that organizations using a formal, comprehensive planning approach will be more successful. The other predicts that organizations using an informal, incremental approach wil be more successful in such an environment. The action research described here in two real-world organizations examined actual IS planning practices in a turbulent environment. One practised formal and compre hensive IS planning, while the other relied more on an informal and incremental approach. Their experi ences suggest that in a turbulent environment, comprehensive IS planning may be more successful than incremental planning. Such a conclusion perhaps appears surprising. Nevertheless, it provides contributions for practice and implications for future research. It contributes to practice by showing IS planners the importance of understanding their environment and also the potential of comprehensive planning practices. It contributes to research by demonstrating how comprehensive practices can contribute to planning in a turbulent environment. However, it is only one study and of merely two organizations. Thus, it should also be interpreted as motivating future research to examine the theories from different perspectives.

## Introduction

The extent of frequent and unpredictable environmental change can dramatically affect both business and information systems (IS) planning (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978; Pyburn, 1983; Vitale et al, 1986; Bergeron et al, 1991; Earl, 1993). In such environmental turbulence, organizations more than ever need an IS planning process to help them choose the most appropriate IS and successfully manage their implementation.

Environmental turbulence is a state characterised by “the frequency and unpredictability of changes in stakeholder expectations” (Sambamurthy et al, 1994, p 3). In a turbulent environment, the expectations of customers, suppliers, competitors, and governments change rapidly and unpredictably (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990; Raghunathan & Raghunathan, 1991; Sambamurthy et al, 1994). In response, organizations revise their business plans and then re-evaluate their existing IS plans and ongoing projects.

Environmental turbulence increases the risk of IS investment failure. Without appropriate planning, organizations may fail to realise the anticipated benefits of their IS investments (Clemons & Weber, 1990). Changes in business direction and the resulting changes in IS project priorities may produce excessive delays in the implementation of IS investments (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990). Users may lose interest in a system designed to satisfy their old needs and then drop their support (Lederer & Mendelow, 1993).

While IS planning is generally assumed to assist organizations in coping with a turbulent environment, competing theories consider the most appropriate planning approaches in such an environment (Faludi, 1976; Fredrickson & Mitchell, 1984; Fredrickson, 1984; Minzberg, 1990; Ansoff, 1991; Boyd, 1991). The traditional planning theory predicts that organizations using comprehensive, formal planning will be more successful in coping with a turbulent environment (Ansoff, 1965; Andrews, 1971; Janis & Mann, 1977; Hofer & Schendel, 1978; Ansoff, 1984; Ansoff & Sullivan, 1993; Rowe et al, 1996). The critics of this view have proposed an alternative theory which predicts that organizations using an informal, incremental approach will be more successful in that environment (Lindblom, 1959; Minzberg, 1979, 1994; Quinn, 1980; Minzberg & Quinn, 1996).

This paper describes the use of a qualitative research method—namely, action research—to compare the two planning approaches (Lewin, 1946; Clark, 1972; Susman & Evered, 1978; Argyris, 1982; Benbasat et al, 1987; Argyris et al, 1987; Checkland, 1991; Jo¨nsson, 1991; Gummesson, 1988; Reponen, 1992, Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). The study described here in two real-world organizations examined actual IS planning practices in a turbulent environment. One organization practised a comprehensive and formal planning process, while the other relied more on an informal and incremental approach.

## Success in information systems planning

IS planning is the process of identifying the computerbased applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and realising its business goals (Lederer & Sethi, 1988). The process is characterised by large-scale comprehensive studies or by ongoing, smaller-scale incremental ones. (This section provides a broad description of IS planning activities, although the cases described later focus on project selection, senior management commitment, and implementation.)

Organizations thus conduct IS planning to identify the most valuable IS projects. They seek applications that provide higher payback (Galliers, 1987), strategic significance (Sullivan, 1985; Choe et al, 1998) and congruence with the organization’s competitive needs (Galliers, 1987; Segars & Grover, 1998).

IS planning is also concerned with the sequencing and implementation of IS applications, as well as the examination of existing and proposed IS applications (Sambamurthy et al, 1994). Hence, an important objective in IS planning is to ensure senior management commitment for IS projects (McLean & Soden, 1977; Earl, 1993) and to create a partnership between IS and user groups for successful implementation efforts (McLean & Soden, 1977; Segars & Grover, 1998). Insufficient commitment results in problems in the analysis, design and implementation of the selected IS projects. Hence, planning failures are often manifested as development failures.

In addition, IS planning forecasts resource and skill requirements and defines improvements in the IT infrastructure and IS organization to support both existing and future IS (McLean & Soden, 1977; Bowman et al, 1983; Galliers, 1991).

The extent to which IS planning meets its objectives offers a means of assessing its success (Raghunathan & Raghunathan, 1994). One way to do this is to assess the accomplishments of individual IS projects initiated on the basis of planning (King, 1988; Parker et al, 1988; Bergeron et al, 1991; Fitzgerald, 1993; Raghunathan & Raghunathan, 1994). King (1988, p 108) observed that “a formal assessment of IS development projects provides insights into the worth of past decisions that are direct, objective, and useful in profiling the overall worth of the IS planning system”.

From this perspective, IS planning is successful when it identifies valuable IS projects and ensures sufficient organizational support for their implementation. It is less successful when it promotes IS projects with only marginal business value and when it identifies valuable projects but fails to inspire sufficient support for their implementation.

In a turbulent environment, achieving IS planning success becomes more critical but also more difficult. Changes in business objectives and priorities threaten the business value of IS projects (Clemons & Weber, 1990), as well as the commitment for their implementation (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990, 1993). Thus, IS planning must be rigid enough to permit the completion of large projects yet flexible enough to adjust to environmental change (Lederer & Mendelow, 1993).

## Comprehensive information systems planning

A key choice for IS planners is the extent to which they attempt to be exhaustive in making and integrating strategic decisions (Faludi, 1976; Fredrickson & Mitchell, 1984; Sambamurthy et al, 1994). Planners who are comprehensive believe that such planning best achieves their objectives. They integrate key IS and business decisions into comprehensive, formal IS plans. To manage their diverse analyses, they choose an existing IS planning methodology or develop their own. Because they make organization-wide decisions, they use documented criteria.

Comprehensive planners rely on several practices expected to help them cope with a turbulent environment. These practices—explicitly recommended for a turbulent environment (many others are recommended regardless of the environment)—are summarised in Table 1 and are now elucidated.

As part of the comprehensive analyses, planners make a formal assessment of the competitive environment (Earl, 1988; Raghunathan & Raghunathan, 1991; Bergeron et al, 1991). They involve people from different parts of the organization to collect multiple views. For instance, workshops can be used to promote senior executive participation and mutual learning (Galliers et al, 1994). Thus, the analysis incorporates alternative futures and scenarios and does not rely on one particular future (Galliers, 1987; Earl, 1988; Ang & Teo, 1997). The objective is to bring the differing views and assumptions of key players on the surface, rather than to enforce a single unified view (Galliers, 1993).

Decision making is based on formal criteria and methods. Such methods are expected to guarantee that environmental trends and risks are considered (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978). Organizations in turbulent industries are also advised to choose a methodology which emphasises external analyses (Bergeron et al, 1991).

All key decisions are documented in a written plan (Pyburn, 1983). Written plans are seen as frameworks for managing change rather than as blueprints of the future (McFarlan, 1971). Written plans provide flexible frameworks wherein the implications of changed circumstances can easily be identified and managed (Galliers, 1987). Periodic planning meetings review the implementation of the plans and revise them as needed (Galliers, 1987).

Although the advocates of comprehensive planning believe they will more likely succeed in a turbulent environment by using these practices, their critics have argued why they would fail. These critics have suggested that the need to rely on formal planning methodologies and predefined criteria can trivialise planning so much that it becomes merely a simple top-down exercise (Vitale et al, 1986; Sambamurthy et al, 1994). They have noted that formal planning has also been shown to replace, rather than support informal communication (Pyburn, 1983). They have also pointed out that users and managers consider comprehensive and formal analyses as costly and remote (Earl, 1993).

The critics warn that change in a turbulent environment may be so rapid that plans are obsolete before they can be implemented (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990). This can make comprehensive planning susceptible to wasted efforts, misdirected investments, and low morale (Vitale et al, 1986). When the business environment is volatile, comprehensive planning simply may not provide the necessary flexibility to be effective (Pyburn, 1983; Ciborra, 1994). In effect, the critics suggest that a less comprehensive approach may prove more useful.

## Incremental information systems planning

In contrast to comprehensive planning, some organizations practice a more incremental approach. For them, planning focuses on a few or perhaps just one theme and IS decisions are made on a one-by-one basis (Earl, 1993). This focused agenda keeps the planning team small and enables planning to be based on informal contacts among team members (Pyburn, 1983).

Incremental planners perceive organizations as a collection of independent planning groups (Faludi, 1976).

No general scheme or model is available. Instead, people close to the operational level are allowed and even encouraged to tinker using local cues and heuristics, and to construct using whatever tools and routines are at hand (bricolage) in order to formulate ad hoc decisions (Ciborra, 1994). Decision making relies heavily on personal experiences and judgment (Sambamurthy et al, 1994).

In the minds of most planners, planning is firmly associated with rational and ipso facto comprehensive approaches (Faludi, 1976). Thus, it may sometimes be difficult to see that a piecemeal selection of IS projects is actually IS planning. It is easily confused with mere project planning. And yet, every time one project is selected, it is sequenced with other projects. The competition for project selection continues even during project development and implementation. Hence, in incremental planning, managers must be prepared to make IS planning decisions all the time and at any time.

Incremental planners also follow practices recommended specifically for coping with a turbulent environment. Such practices are summarised in Table 1 and now elucidated.

Incremental planners formulate initial interpretations of the planning issues and then share them with each other to develop a common understanding of them (Sambamurthy et al, 1994). By thus sharing and focusing the planning agenda, planning can be based on informal contacts and face-to-face communication (Pyburn, 1983). This is seen as facilitating the interpretation and sense-making behaviour required to understand environmental risks and opportunities fully (Sambamurthy et al, 1994).

Furthermore, incremental planners search for satisfactory solutions that deal with environmental threats and opportunities yet remain within the organization’s current resources (Vitale et al, 1986). Decisions thus rely on personal experiences and judgement (Sambamurthy et al, 1994). The individualised nature of planning allows decision-makers to choose a time horizon appropriate to current circumstances (Pyburn, 1983).

In general, the initial planning process is kept highly efficient, and implementation is emphasised (Vitale et al, 1986; Earl, 1993). Through informal contacts, planners are able to remain continually appraised of ongoing problems and opportunities (Pyburn, 1983). Decisions can be made at any time (Earl, 1993). Because planning is highly efficient, it is considered less vulnerable to resource cuts during tight budgets.

Thus, through such practices, incremental planners believe that they might more likely succeed in a turbulent environment. However, the risks of their approach have not been widely examined (Earl, 1993). Nevertheless, some reported difficulties illustrate why incremental planning might fail in a turbulent environment.

In particular, senior managers have expressed concern that incremental planning fails to address critical needs (Earl, 1993). For example, from their perspective developing new systems locally can mean that potentially strategic applications can be dismissed as ‘just enduser hacking’ and never get sufficient resources to become organization-wide innovations (Ciborra, 1994). Some IS managers have warned about poor definition of IS requirements and infrastructures (Pyburn, 1983; Earl, 1993). Others have worried about how future planning themes would be generated. A new chief executive officer (CEO), management team, or management style could erode the incremental planning process (Earl, 1993). Rapid responses to changing needs have also been seen as increasing the risk of incomplete projects (Pyburn, 1983).

## Two theories of information systems planning in a turbulent environment

A contingency view of IS planning suggests that its success depends on contextual factors (Pyburn, 1983; Bergeron et al, 1991; Earl, 1993). A single planning approach would not likely be universally successful.

Environmental turbulence is a widely discussed IS planning contextual factor. Environmental conditions are classified as: (1) static, simple and predictable, (2) dynamic and/or complex, or (3) fundamentally unpredictable and thus turbulent or hypercompetitive (Volberda, 1996). In turbulent environments, changes are not only frequent and unpredictable, but the environment is also seen as hostile. Firms, whose responses are inappropriate, incomplete, or too slow are severely penalised, and their ability to experiment, learn and adapt is reduced (Hanssen-Bauer & Snow, 1996).

Two competing theories (although not previously called IS planning theories) predict IS planning success in a turbulent environment.

One theory predicts that comprehensive IS planning will be more successful in a turbulent environment (McFarlan, 1971; McLean & Soden, 1977; Premkumar & King, 1991; Raghunathan & Raghunathan, 1991). In summary, its rationale is that meticulous analysis produces greater knowledge and more appropriate commitment and thus a better plan.

Another theory predicts that incremental IS planning will be more successful (Pyburn, 1983; Vitale et al, 1986; Earl, 1993; Sambamurthy et al, 1994). In summary, its rationale is that meticulous analysis requires so much time that the environment changes and renders the plan obsolete. Planning in small steps, however, will facilitate satisfactory choices and implementation.

In general, a stable environment is considered as less challenging for IS planning. The proponents of incremental planning consider that even comprehensive approaches can work in a stable environment (Pyburn, 1983; Vitale et al, 1986; Sambamurthy et al, 1994) and vice versa (McFarlan, 1971).

Research has not, however, empirically validated either theory. The results from previous, comparative studies are few and inconclusive. For example, in one study the great majority of interviewees expressed that comprehensive planning with multiple year horizons was more effective than trying to manage without them even though plans had to be revised unexpectedly from time to time (McFarlan, 1971). In another study managers were less satisfied with a comprehensive approach even if the environment was turbulent (Vitale et al, 1986). In a third study, managers of two organizations which practised an informal planning approach considered their planning successful (Pyburn, 1983). IS planning was considered a failure in an organization which practised a somewhat more formal approach in a turbulent environment.

Despite these findings, a recent study failed to confirm the hypothesis that less comprehensive planning leads to higher performance in a turbulent environment (Sambamurthy et al, 1994). Also, a large survey study found no relationship between environmental variables (dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility) and the sophistication of methods used for integrating IS with business (Teo & King, 1997).

To conclude, survey studies using multiple item instruments have not been able to establish a statistically significant relationship between environmental conditions and IS planning effectiveness. Perhaps organizational variables have stronger influence on IS planning than environmental conditions (Teo & King, 1997).

Perhaps the problem is more complex than assumed and there is no linear relationship between the two variables. In any case, the inconclusive results from previous studies are the main argument for selecting a qualitative research method for this study. More needs to be learned about the planning approaches and how they influence planning success in a turbulent environment. Thus, a more detailed investigation of the relationship between IS planning and its success is needed.

## Methodology

Action research was the investigative methodology in this study. It “aims to contribute both to the practical concerns of people in an immediate problematic situation and to the goals of social science by joint collaboration within a mutually acceptable ethical framework” (Rapoport, 1970, p 499). This twofold view of the objectives of action research—to solve a problem for a client and to advance science—is, perhaps, the most fundamental feature of action research (Lewin, 1946; Clark, 1972; Susman & Evered, 1978; Argyris, 1982; Benbasat et al, 1987; Argyris et al, 1987; Checkland, 1991; Jo¨nsson, 1991; Gummesson, 1988; Reponen, 1992; Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998).

Action research was selected because of the in-depth and firsthand understanding that the researcher can obtain from organizational change processes and strategies by using it (Benbasat et al, 1987; Reponen, 1992). Furthermore, action research provides an opportunity to learn about practice and alternative ways of carrying it out (Argyris et al, 1987; Wood-Harper, 1992). Hence, action research has been used in developing IS planning methods such as Evolutionary Model for Information Systems Strategy (Reponen, 1993) and a large number of systems development methods such as ETHICS (Mumford, 1979), Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland, 1991) and Multiview (Wood-Harper, 1992), to mention only a few.

Thus, the senior author participated as an external advisor in two IS projects during 1988 and 1992. His role was to interview users and managers and assist in the planning and implementation of an IS project. The particular form of action research used in the projects would best be described as clinical field work, where the researcher, as a trained professional, facilitates in finding a normative solution in a problematic situation (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). Personal involvement in the planning process allowed data collection through direct observations, interviews, and the review of company documents.

Thus, the researcher had the two main action research objectives: (1) to assist in solving a planning problem for the client, and (2) to contribute to the ongoing study of IS planning practices.

After the initial planning process, the researcher monitored environmental changes and reviewed the success of the project longitudinally. Data collection continued via personal visits to the research sites, telephone interviews, and conversations. The last contacts with the client organizations concluded seven to nine years after the initial project proposals.

## The workplace information systems case: comprehensive planning

The National Board for Labour Protection (NBLP; also referred to as the central administration) was a large public sector organization that supervised occupational safety in all Finnish organizations and workplaces. Although the actual inspection work was done by inspectors in fourteen regional offices, the NBLP was responsible for labour protection (LP) activities on the national level.

NBLP managers were well aware of the difficulty in using their workplace information system (WIS). The regional inspectors were making huge efforts to key information into the existing WIS database. Still, no one knew how to use this database very well. WIS maintenance was also a considerable NBLP expense. Thus, both the regions and the NBLP agreed on the need to enhance the WIS.

However, they disagreed about the objectives of an improved WIS. NBLP managers wanted better performance evaluation and reporting capabilities but the regions wanted a new WIS to be a tool to help them in their inspections.

The difficulties in improving the WIS reflected a more general political tension between the regions and NBLP. With the organization’s growth in the 1980s, the regions and NBLP had become more distant. One manager described the evolution of this distance as follows:

In the beginning of NBLP, the organizational climate was very positive. Everybody wanted to make contributions to improving working conditions in this country. There was no gap between regions and central administration, we were on the same boat so to say. Then something happened and the situation got worse. Nowadays, the meetings are of no use. Both sides come to them to express the same basic ideas. After the meetings, when travelling back home, we discuss the problem of won’t those idiots ever learn. I suppose the other side is doing the same.

To get a more balanced view of NBLP and region needs, NBLP managers sought external consultants. In August of 1990, they contracted with a Finnish business school to conduct interviews in the NBLP and two regions.

## The planning process

The NBLP made a clear attempt to be comprehensive in planning. The WIS project competed with other ongoing or planned IS projects for approval and funding in several consecutive stages. For example, a mediumrange plan formulated in the year 1989 contained the six most significant IS projects and the WIS was one of them. An annual IS plan for the year 1990 considered 20 different proposed applications. Yet, only six actual IS projects were chosen in it and WIS was one of them. Competing projects included a dangerous chemicals registry, administrative IS, a security database, and office teamwork systems. Both in the medium-range plan and the annual plan, WIS was considered as one of the most important IS projects for the organization. Thus, the WIS project was part of the organization-wide plans (thus following comprehensive practice no. 1 in Table 1).

Hence, many efforts typical of comprehensive planning had already been completed at the time when the researcher began to work on the project in the Fall 1990. Despite these efforts, controversy over the project was prevalent. For instance, some regions seriously considered developing their own systems. Thus, the planning of WIS continued and it followed the comprehensive practices. The researcher’s investigations in 1990– 1991 were part of this planning. Table 2 summarises the key planning and development milestones in the WIS project.

A characteristic feature in the planning of the WIS project was the attempt to involve groups from different organisational levels (comprehensiveness practice no. 3 in Table 1). A committee was formed to analyse the need for the new WIS and suggest guidelines for its development. The chair and secretary of the committee were from the NBLP. Its four other members represented different regions.

The analyses made during different planning stages were extensive with specific emphasis on the assessment of external environment (comprehensiveness practice no. 2 in Table 1). Environmental trends had already been assessed while developing IS strategy so changes in the external environment were a key issue in the analyses made by the researcher. In fact, the researcher’s interviews emphasised the external factors likely to change inspection work.

A special report based on these interviews was formulated as a framework for managing change (comprehensiveness practice no. 5 in Table 1). The report identified three main external trends. First, attitudes and expectations towards LP in companies were changing. Second, new ergonomic and psychological problem areas were emerging in LP. Third, the NBLP faced increased cost effectiveness demands due to a recession and government budget constraints. The functions proposed for the new WIS were carefully designed to help the organization adapt to these external expectations.

Formal methods and predefined deadlines were used to ensure the comprehensiveness of analyses prior to making decisions (comprehensiveness practice no. 4 in Table 1). Thus, planning followed the generally recommended methodology for IS planning for government organizations. An exact date nine months hence was set for the committee to complete a proposal for WIS development.

The WIS committee completed its official report in April 1991. An extended WIS (or ‘WIS+’) would be built as a temporary solution so that a working IS was always available. A new control information system (CIS) would then replace the current WIS.

The official report was perhaps even more a model for managing change than the interview report (comprehensiveness practice no. 5 in Table 1). The report said very little about the exact WIS development process or the new functions. Instead, it listed various organizational changes upon which the WIS+/CIS development was seen as contingent. It emphasised the integration of CIS development with the development of other regional IS. The report also said that the final CIS design could be determined only after more experience with ongoing projects.

The CIS plan precisely described how the CIS project would be organised. The new CIS development committee would start its work in September 1991, and the CIS would be implemented during 1993–1995. Thus, the formal plan was important in that it made a clear organizational commitment to the ongoing planning of the CIS. It also made a clear commitment to the periodic review of plan implementation (comprehensiveness practice no. 5 in Table 1).

## Environmental changes during the IS project

The NBLP systems development staff implemented the temporary extensions to the WIS system in 1991. These extensions included the addition of some new fields to the old screens and reports. The additions permitted the WIS+ to support the classification of workplaces, the employment of cost-effective interaction methods, and the adoption of inspections for specific health hazards.

During 1991 to 1993, inspection work was difficult. A government budget crisis showed no sign of abatement. Government cost savings did not cover the deficit created by unemployment fees, bank support, and decreased tax income. The deficit rose annually. The need for even more radical cost cutting in government agencies continued. Hence, the steady growth in the LP appropriation that was characteristic of the 1980s also declined.

Concerns grew not merely about cost reductions but also about the need for LP itself. Rumours circulated about the future of the NBLP and the regions. These created uncertainty and job insecurity. In the spring of 1993, a regional manager refused to be interviewed but instead said:

The current situation in the LP regions is quite difficult. We have not had the resources to increase the use of WIS. The main use of WIS is still to accumulate a large body of labour protection data. On the other hand, we are attending to negotiations where the future of the whole labour protection work is questioned. I’m not very keen on talking about WIS, perhaps people in the central administration can give you more information.

A major organizational change occurred in March 1993, when the NBLP merged into Ministry of Labour. The status of NBLP fell from that of an independent national board to a Ministry department. Afterwards, a former NBLP employee, who continued in the Ministry, described the decision with:

It was the politicians who wanted it. They felt that there was one organizational level too much. I don’t know, now it seems that we are even more distant from the field.

The NBLP’s diminished importance meant more independence for the regions. The committee chair thus said:

In many ways, the regions are now more independent. They can now choose their own working methods, and they have their own budgets. We (central administration) can no longer send circulars or otherwise influence their activities. The central administration sends one memorandum each year, which describes the objectives for the year, and that’s all.

Interestingly, the major changes in the organization and its environment did not quash the CIS planning efforts. Instead, the members of the former WIS+ committee continued in a newly formed CIS committee. The CIS replaced the WIS+ in the beginning of 1995 and the project met its original deadline. Updates were implemented at year end.

In summary, for WIS planners (and NBLP managers in general) the environmental changes and demands originated from the government budget crisis and related political negotiations and thus were largely unpredictable. Because the NBLP and the regions were dependent on the annual labour protection appropriations from the Ministry, the environment was also uncertain and hostile. Inside the organization, the regions gained more power when the NBLP was merged to the Ministry. Thus new external pressures and the need to agree on major changes in the organization’s strategy and structure made comprehensive planning difficult. Due to the long tradition of comprehensive planning in the public sector, the planning approach was, however, not changed. Although the public generally views government agencies as enduring and stable, the environmental turbulence was so severe that it resulted in the demise of the NBLP.

## Evaluation of the IS planning success

The IS planning process succeeded in identifying a strategically significant, high pay-off IS project and laid the groundwork for its implementation. The regions saw it as a success because its new features supported inspection and its elimination of extensive keying saved time. The NBLP saw the system as a success because it provided direct operating and maintenance cost savings. The development committee head affirmed this with:

Because of CIS we are saving millions (of Finnish marks or FIMs) each year in EDP service costs.

The comprehensive approach to planning succeeded because it ensured the organizational commitment and resources for the completion of the WIS+/CIS. In fact, considering the uncertainty and turmoil, the planning and implementation of the WIS+/CIS was surprisingly smooth. Later on, the development committee head could not imagine any impacts of organizational restructuring on the CIS project. Also, though the resources for WIS+/CIS planning were great, they were not cut despite other budget reductions.

The project succeeded probably because the comprehensive, formal analyses (comprehensive practices nos 2 and 4) made the potential benefits of the WIS+/CIS evident throughout the organization. High level commitment to project execution was ensured through its selection as part of overall strategy. Planning had shown how the CIS could assist inspectors in facing the new challenges. When visiting a region in Fall 1995, the region’s EDP co-ordinator could still recall the researcher’s report although it was written in 1990.

The project also probably succeeded because its comprehensive planning involved many people from different organizational units (comprehensive practice no. 3) and thus helped commit different organizational groups to planning. The development committee head considered the democratic nature of decision making as the primary reason why organizational changes did not halt CIS/WIS+ project:

It is true that after the restructuring and decentralisation, the regions became more independent in IS decisions as well. They have now their own IS appropriate that they can use the way they want to. But this had little impact on the WIS/CIS project. Regions have had wide representation and a lot of influence in the development committees. Thus, the WIS/CIS project has reflected their views as well.

In other words, the project probably succeeded because comprehensive planning ensured that it reflected the views of all groups, not just a small, influential one. Thus, the plan was less vulnerable to unpredicted changes in organizational power structures.

## Investplan: incremental planning

A major supplier of construction materials in Scandinavia and Northern Europe began expanding its operations in the late 1970s, first to Scandinavia and later to Europe, the US, and Far East. As it grew, formal means of communication began replacing informal channels. By the end of the 1980s, the company had a full-scale IS for budget planning and control.

Its corporate financial controller was relatively satisfied with existing corporate reporting systems. However, the information provided by divisions and strategic business units did not describe major investment proposals or the progress of investments in execution. He considered this a weakness. Some of the five division controllers agreed with him. They had expressed dissatisfaction about the ambiguousness of investment proposal evaluation and selection.

Still others felt that the idea of more clearly defining investment practices with a new IS was an unnecessary formalisation and an attempt to implement an unneeded IS. No one had clearly explained the benefits of formalising and automating investment reporting. In fact, some managers saw the project proposal as a finance department ploy to increase its power.

These conflicting views convinced the corporate financial controller to obtain an outsider’s independent view. Thus, the IS planning process started as a joint effort of the company and a Finnish business school. The senior author served as a consultant and began by interviewing the division controllers.

## The planning process

At the time when the researcher began work on the project, it was in planning stage and no commitment to implement Investplan had been made. In fact, the first contract between the researcher and the company was limited to a feasibility study, because the company could not guarantee the implementation of the project. Table 3 summarises the subsequent key planning and development milestones in the WIS project.

The planning of the Investplan project deliberately followed the incremental approach. The project had not been officially accepted within the organization’s formal planning processes (following incremental practice No. 1 in Table 1). In retrospect, the corporate financial controller described the rationale behind the approach by saying:

We had an immediate problem and we wanted a solution to it. The company was investing huge sums of money and we needed tools for the planning and control of the investments. We didn’t want to waste time by conducting detailed analyses or by going through a multi-stage, formal approval process. The use of a light and efficient planning process was a conscious and deliberate choice.

To assess the need for the project, a pre-study was done. The senior researcher interviewed division controllers in October, 1988 for it. These controllers represented the views of the strategic business units. Thus, representatives from units did not participate in this planning. Instead, the organization kept the planning team small so it could rely on informal communications and networks (incremental practice no. 3 in Table 1).

To execute the project, a shared understanding of its need was promoted among the division controllers, group’s financial controller, and the chief financial officer (incremental practice no. 4 in Table 1). The interviews focused on three questions: What procedures existed for investment planning? What procedures existed for investment follow-up? Could an IS be used to support these procedures?

The findings from the interviews were summarised into a fourteen page report. It mainly described the investment planning practices in different divisions. It briefly argued the need to develop these practices on the basis of such past organizational changes as growth, internationalisation, and decentralisation. It also listed some investment planning problems and paradoxes. It was distributed to the interviewees.

A controllers’ meeting in November, 1988 made the decision to develop investment planning and control procedures with a system called Investplan. Such meetings of the chief financial officer, corporate financial controller, and division controllers permitted discussion of problems and development needs in a fairly informal and leisurely manner. The report was credited with providing new insights to the planning issues but decision making was largely based on informal discussions and the experience and judgment of the chief financial officer, corporate financial controller, and division controllers (incremental practice no. 2 in Table 1).

Following the principles of incremental approach, the review of the implementation of the plan relied heavily on informal contacts and networks. The controllers had many meetings where questions related to Investplan could be discussed if necessary. The process was highly flexible, and decisions about the project could be made at any time (incremental practice no. 5 in Table 1).

Thus, the initial analyses were limited. In general, the initial planning efforts were highly efficient (incremental practice no. 1 in Table 1). No specific emphasis was placed on reviewing future business changes or environmental risks. Instead, it was assumed that growth and internationalisation would continue into the future. If unexpected changes would occur during project implementation, it was expected that informal networks and situational judgment would be sufficient for responding to them.

## Environmental changes during the project

Project implementation started in the beginning of 1989. Although the controllers had accepted the project, it was not a top priority. One programmer from the software company was assigned to develop the investment planning and reporting systems. He was, however, simultaneously responsible for making modifications to the existing corporate budgeting system. Because the budgeting system was already in use, controllers typically placed higher priority for requests to improve budgeting system functionality. Investplan development was thus delayed because of them. Preparing the system for testing required over a year. The initial system tests took place in the Fall, 1990.

Soon after these tests, the signs of a general recession in the main market areas of the company appeared. It soon became obvious that the level of investments in all divisions would fall considerably. In all of Scandinavia, the construction industry suffered seriously from the recession. The total size of the Finnish markets plunged by over 50% from 1990 to 1993. The corporate financial controller described this rapid change in economic conditions as follows:

It was within one month that we learned that the accumulation of new projects and orders had stopped completely.

The company’s profitability and annual investments changed from 1985 to 1994. Investment activity had peaked from 1988 through 1990 when the annual net exceeded 1 billion FIMs. By 1991, however, net investment fell to only a third of the 1989 amount.

The dramatic change in economic conditions did not provide the expected growth and internationalisation and thus had a direct impact on the Investplan project. The new environment provided little need for IS support of investment planning. Personnel reductions also forced the company to adopt a conservative policy in developing new systems. The implementation of Investplan’s reporting functions would have violated it.

Thus management had little reason to complete the Investplan implementation and it chose to delay it temporarily. Later, when the full impact of the recession became more evident, it cancelled the project. Investplan was never used and provided no benefits.

In summary, for Investplan planners (and other managers), the environmental changes were unanticipated. The recession started unexpectedly and its impact on the construction industry was pervasive. The environmental hostility was evident in the large number of bankruptcies, mergers, and acquisitions in the industry. The company survived but only after a series of alliances, divestments and acquisitions through which it could move to more viable business areas.

## Evaluation of the planning success

The incremental planning process identified a strategically significant, high pay-off IS project. However, it failed to produce an understanding of the potential business effects of environmental turbulence, namely, the firm’s poor investment decisions in 1989 and 1990. Investplan likely would have prevented many of those decisions. The corporate financial controller described the major IS planning error by saying:

We should have emphasised more the business side of the problem. We analysed it too much as a problem in our financial reporting systems. Had we better understood the significance of the project for our business, we would certainly have organised it in a different way.

The failure of incremental planning to furnish an understanding of the impact of the environmental turbulence produced less top management comprehension of the need for Investplan and less support for it. The corporate financial officer articulated this shortcoming by saying that more emphasis should have been placed on communicating the project’s significance to senior and division management:

When we started the project, there were only a few managers who considered this as a truly serious problem. We should have used more time to communicate the significance of the problems, particularly to the senior managers. We should have tried to ensure more management attention to it.

Because top management did not understand the significance of Investplan, it did not provide a top priority and the necessary resources. Had it done so, Investplan could have easily been operational by 1989.

However, implementation was much too slow and Investplan was never used. In effect, the incremental planning process was not successful in terms of providing no foundation for implementation.

It probably failed for several reasons. Analysis based on controllers’ shared understanding, personal experiences, and judgment (incremental practices nos 2 and 4 in Table 1) was insufficient to permit an understanding of the impact of the turbulent environment. Instead, it led to a single view of the future—that growth and internationalisation would continue—which turned out to be false. Its reliance on informal networks (incremental practice no. 3 in Table 1) did not produce an organizational coalition to support the project.

While hindsight can provide a perfect vision, had meticulous analysis of comprehensive IS planning been conducted, the impact of the environment on the business would have been evident and Investplan expeditiously implemented.

## Contributions and implications

This research described two competing theories of IS planning. One predicted that comprehensive planning would be more successful in a turbulent environment while the other predicted that incremental planning would be more successful.

Action research was conducted on planning two IS projects in a turbulent environment. One followed comprehensive IS planning practices and the other followed those of incremental IS planning. Comprehensive planning successfully identified a significant project and facilitated its implementation. Incremental planning also identified a significant project but failed to facilitate its implementation because it did not provide an understanding of the environment’s impact and the project’s significance and hence it failed to produce management support for the project. Table 4 summarises the planning practices and several other key characteristics that distinguish the projects.

The analysis of the first case suggests that in a turbulent environment, the use of a large planning team and meticulous analysis becomes more important. The WIS planners used these practices to successfully identify a significant project. They succeeded in implementing the project because, according to the WIS development committee head quoted above, comprehensive planning ensured that it reflected the views of all groups, not just a single, small influential one. They used several committees and careful deliberation to involve many diverse groups from different organizational levels in planning, despite their conflicting interests. The resulting plan was a compromise and each group had its own reasons to support it. The wide support for the plan made it possible to preserve sufficient resources for the analysis, design and implementation of the system, despite external pressures to cut costs and major changes in organization’s power structure.

The analysis of the second case further confirms the need for a large planning team and meticulous analysis when planning IS investments in a turbulent environment. This is because although Investplan planners also identified a significant project, the incremental planning practices did not facilitate any project implementation. According to the Investplan corporate financial controller quoted above, analysis based on controllers’ shared understanding, personal experiences, and judgment was insufficient to permit an understanding of the impact of the turbulent environment. More meticulous analysis would have been needed. The reliance on a small planning team and informal networks did not produce an organizational coalition to support the project and thus sufficient resources for expeditious analysis, design and implementation of Investplan. The beginning of recession and its severe impact on the company’s business made the systems obsolete before implementation.

## Contributions to practice

Perhaps the most important contribution of this research is that it serves as a reminder of the possibility of environmental turbulence. Most IS planners are probably fortunate enough not to have endured such turbulence very often. However, they must be cognisant of their environment because turbulence is always possible and threatening, and the failure to understand it can be potentially very damaging.

If IS planners do work in a turbulent environment— and this research should prompt them to realise they do—then they can use the research described here by comparing their own projects to WIS and Investplan. They can thus compare their environmental conditions to those in the projects. They can use Table 1 to help them compare their practices to those used for WIS and Investplan. They can reassess the IS planning outcomes that they currently seek. They can ask which project their own resembles more: WIS or Investplan. Such assessment can help them adjust their IS planning practices to improve the chances that their IS planning will succeed. Table 1 thus contributes a checklist for IS planners who are trying to understand or alter the planning process in their organization.

Certainly no examination of just two projects— regardless of its thoroughness—can produce definitive prescriptions for practice. Nevertheless, this research confirms the usefulness of extensive environmental analysis and management support. In particular, IS planners should probably ensure that they conduct at least some significant level of environmental analysis and that they cultivate at least some significant degree of management support so that they can achieve successful IS planning outcomes. This research warns IS planners of the potentially disastrous consequences of failing to do so.

## Implications for future research

This study illustrates how action research can investigate competing theories of IS planning by comparing practices and outcomes in different organizations. It also shows how IS planning can be studied through the longitudinal analysis of the implementation of proposed IS projects.

In doing so, it supports the theory that comprehensive IS planning will be more successful in a turbulent environment (McFarlan, 1971; McLean & Soden, 1977; Premkumar & King, 1991; Raghunathan & Raghunathan, 1991). However, it is only one study and of merely two organizations. Thus perhaps most importantly, it should be interpreted as motivating future research to examine the theories from different perspectives.

For example, different criteria for IS planning success could provide different results. The current research evaluated IS planning on the basis of whether it identified valuable IS projects and ensured sufficient organizational support for their implementation. However, future research should consider more applications and the information architecture as well as the IS organization resulting from the planning process. Future research might also study many other outcomes (e.g., top management commitment to and knowledge about IS, top management’s satisfaction with the planning process, extent of implementation, return on investment for partially implemented projects, improved organizational structure, etc).

Intensive, qualitative research with small numbers of organizations might continue to be a useful approach. In particular, it might be especially interesting to seek an example of a successful project that used incremental planning (assuming one existed). An analysis of why it succeeded might reveal a great deal about the possible workings of IS planning. Naturally, a research method in which the researcher is an independent observer (instead of an active participant) could be used to confirm that the findings are truly objective and that they can be generalised to other settings (Benbasat et al, 1987).

Also, a field study would allow hypothesis testing with a larger number of organizations and yet preserve possibilities to investigate the relationships with qualitative methods and a survey instrument of specific questions about practices and outcomes. Such an instrument might be used first to validate the existence of the two countervailing IS planning approaches. Also, possibly extraneous variables (such as differences in industry) played a role in the findings here. A large sample might identify such variables.

To validate the existence of two IS planning approaches and to investigate the role of extraneous variables, future research might also study a much larger number of organizations with a survey instrument of specific questions about practices and outcomes.

## Conclusion

Some experienced IS planners and researchers may find the results of this study very surprising. For years, such planners and researchers have warned of the problems of comprehensive IS planning. Such planning costs too much and takes too long. The environment changes and management loses interest in implementing plans. They have hopefully viewed incremental IS planning as a potential, reasonable alternative. The contradictory finding herein—that comprehensive planning succeeded whereas incremental planning failed—should stimulate the imaginations of IS planners and researchers alike.
