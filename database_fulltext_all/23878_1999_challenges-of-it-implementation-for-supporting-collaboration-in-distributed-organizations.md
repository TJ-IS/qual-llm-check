# Challenges of IT implementation for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations

BE Munkvold

Department of Information Systems, Agder College, Serviceboks 422, 4604 Kristiansand, Norway

The study focuses on the implementation of information technology (IT) for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations, i.e. organizations consisting of two or more semi-autonomous units in different geographical locations. Six case studies of IT implementation projects have been conducted, focusing on the challenges in the implementation process as seen from the perspective of the implementation team. Being of an exploratory nature, the study has identified a large number of issues that have influenced the different stages in the implementation process. The implementation context studied is found to imply several additional challenges for the implementation team, compared to ‘traditional’ intra-organizational IT implementation. This is a result of greater autonomy among the participating units, heterogeneous tech nological platforms and logistical barriers in the implementation activities. Resistance from the organiza tional units, technological incompatibility and lack of incentives for adopting new work practices, are identified as major barriers in the implementation. In general, the implementation is found to require a process of maturation, where implementers and adopters gradually develop an understanding of how the technology can support the organizational needs.

## Introduction

Today there is a continuous focus on how organizations need to transform to adapt to current changes in their operating environments, due to increasing global competition, deregulation of markets, increasing customer sel ectivity on price, quality and service, and the rapid technological development. Team-based structures, intraand interorganizational collaboration and a focus on business processes, are key elements of this organizational transformation. Information technology (IT) is often emphasized as an important enabler for this restructuring. Although there is a tendency to overplay the role of IT in organizational transformation (Baskerville & Smithson, 1995), there is no question that IT enables new ways of working, by offering the possibility for collaboration across geographical and organizational boundaries. Successful implementation of a new technology is critical for obtaining effective use. However, despite the great interest in how IT can be used for supporting new organizational forms, there exists few empirical studies on the process of IT implementation in this type of organizational arrangement.

This paper reports a longitudinal study of the process of IT implementation for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations. Implementation here refers to the entire process from needs analysis and choice of technological solution, to the realisation of the full benefits from the technology. The technology in focus is IT supporting communication and information sharing, including both groupware and communications technologies. The term distributed organization refers to organizations consisting of two or more semi-autonomous units in different geographical locations. The autonomy here lies in that decisions regarding technology implementation and use are decentralised to each unit. This type of organization may include both organizational networks of independent organizations, and units in a single organization.

The research strategy applied is multiple-case studies, with the IT implementation process as unit of analysis (Yin, 1989). Six IT implementation projects in distributed organizations have been studied. The main perspective applied has been that of the implementation team, responsible for the activities in the implementation project. Building on the participants’ interpretations of the challenges experienced in the implementation process, the aim of this research has been to increase the understanding of this form of IT implementation process, and how it can be managed. In this sense, the study falls into the interpretive research tradition (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1993).

Being of an exploratory nature, the study has identified a large number of issues that have influenced the different stages in the implementation process. The format of this paper does not allow for a complete presentation of these. Instead, the paper concentrates on the main challenges identified in the implementation projects studied. The question addressed in this paper is thus: What are the challenges in the process of implementing IT for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations?

The findings from the study illustrate how this implementation context may imply additional complexity compared to intra-organizational IT implementation, as a result of a greater level of autonomy among the participating units, heterogeneous technological platforms and geographical barriers in the implementation process. Further, several barriers in the process of establishing collaborative work practices have been identified.

The paper is structured as follows. The second section presents an overview of related research on IT implementation. This is followed by an overview of the cases. The fourth section describes the methodological approach. The main findings identified in each stage of the implementation process are presented in the fifth section. The final section presents the conclusions from this study, and implications for practice and further research.

## Previous research

IT implementation has been a central topic in information systems research for more than three decades (Kwon & Zmud, 1987). In an attempt to unify different perspectives in this research, Kwon and Zmud (op. cit.) argued for using a technological innovation perspective on the IT implementation process. They have developed a framework for studying IT implementation, involving a six-stage model of the implementation process. This model has later been refined by Cooper and Zmud (1990) as shown in Table 1.

According to this framework, different factors can be expected to influence each stage of the process, making it necessary to “explore the impact of multiple contextual factors on multiple implementation stages” (ibid., p 125). However, the sequential nature of the model should not be taken too literally. Rather, the stages should be thought of as activities that may occur in parallel (Cooper & Zmud, op. cit.). The model has been adopted for structuring the data from this study.

The diffusion of innovation perspective on IT implementation has been widely accepted as a basis for research in this area (Fichman, 1992; Prescott & Conger, 1995; Kautz & Pries-Heje, 1996; Larsen & McGuire, 1998). Despite this common foundation, the research is still characterised by a wide variety regarding focus and level of analysis. This has led to the argument that it is necessary to focus on specific implementation contexts, rather than trying to develop general models of innovation processes (Fichman & Kemerer, 1994). Following this, two areas of the IT implementation research are considered to be of special relevance for the form of IT implementation studied in this project: research on the implementation of interorganizational systems and groupware implementation research. As suggested by Strauss and Corbin (1990), the literature review in this study has been conducted in an iterative way, being guided by the findings from the empirical part of this study.

Few empirical studies have been found that focuses on the type of IT implementation context studied here, i.e. the implementation of IT for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations. Still, the empirical research in the two research areas includes results that may be of relevance to this adoption context. In the following, a brief summary of these findings is presented.

## Research on interorganizational systems

An interorganizational system (IOS) can be defined as “an automated information system shared by two or more companies” (Cash & Konsynski, 1985, p 134). As defined in the previous section, the distributed organization does not necessarily include interoganizational collaboration. However, the research on IOS implementation is also believed to be of relevance for intra-organizational IT implementation involving semiautonomous units in different locations. The research on interorganizational IT implementation is largely focusing on the adoption and diffusion of IOS for linking organizations with their business partners, such as customers and suppliers, with special emphasis on EDI systems (e.g. Cox & Ghoneim, 1996; Bensaou, 1997).

At the industry level, competition intensity and environmental uncertainty have been identified as drivers for adoption of IOS (Grover, 1993; Cavaye, 1996). At the level of each organization, large firm size, decentralised decision making and internal IT expertise have been identified as factors promoting IOS adoption (Grover, 1993; Vijayasarathy, 1994). Technological factors identified are the possibility for integration with internal technologies and applications in each organization (Cox & Ghoneim, 1996), the existence of technology standards and the IT infrastructure in each organization (Grover, 1993).

Related to the conduct of the implementation project, many of the same factors that have been identified in intra-organizational implementation are found to be relevant here (Vijayasarathy, 1994). This includes top management support and participation, champions, user involvement and participation, training and adaptation of existing practices. In addition, the marketing of the technology in the participating organizations is found to be important for adoption of IOS (Cavaye, 1996), as are the financial costs to the adopting organizations.

## Research on groupware implementation

The empirical research on groupware implementation includes a wide variety of technologies and applications. Several studies report problems of resistance and inertia in the implementation of these technologies (Grudin, 1989; Orlikowski, 1992; Bowers, 1994; Rogers, 1994; Ciborra, 1996). In the discussion of how these problems can be overcome, the following issues are recurring: the need for organizational changes in policy and reward systems, establishing incentives for collaboration and information sharing (Orlikowski, 1992); seamless integration with existing technologies (Bullen & Bennett, 1990); the existence of a supportive technological infrastructure (Bowers, 1994; Grudin & Palen, 1995); and providing training that also focuses on how the technology can be used for supporting collaboration (Bullen & Bennett, 1990; Orlikowski, 1992).

The last issue is especially important for systems based on groupware platforms like Lotus Notes. The combination of several features can here result in that users develop different mental models of the technology, leading to inefficient use (Orlikowski, 1992). Ciborra (1996) also discusses how groupware technologies may be ‘fragile’ in nature, in that users easily turn to substitute media (e.g. telephone and fax) in case of technical problems with the new media.

## Overview of cases

Table 2 provides an overview of the six cases, including type of organization, tasks supported and informants interviewed in each case. In addition, a brief description of each case is presented in the Appendix. The sampling strategy applied for the selection of cases can be seen as a combination of criterion based, snowball and opportunistic sampling (Miles & Huberman, 1994), with the aim of learning as much as possible about the phenomena studied (Stake, 1994). All six cases have involved implementation of IT for improving collaboration among organizational units in different geographical locations. The first two cases are networks of independent small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), while the four other cases are large companies that operate on an international scale. The technology supports different forms of work processes. However, in all cases the technology is used for making communication, coordination and information sharing among the geographically dispersed units more effective. Four of the cases involve implementation of Lotus Notes applications, illustrating the strong role of Lotus Notes as ‘the groupware standard’. However, the features and functionality of these applications vary. In the two other cases the solutions implemented is based on network technologies (ISDN, frame relay, WAN).

The use of the technology in each case spans different organizational levels, including management. The scope of the implementation is varying. In the SME networks (NNB and OFFCOM) the user groups have consisted of representatives from each member organization being involved in the operations of the organizational network. In Telenor and ABB Corporate Research, the intended users have involved all employees participating in the research process. In Statoil and Kvaerner, the implementation has been organization-wide, encompassing all users in the company. This implies a heterogeneous mix of users in all six cases.

In the first three cases in Table 2, Telenor Research and Development has been responsible for the implementation projects. The other three implementation projects have been handled by internal personnel, with some consulting aid. Apart from the Statoil case, the implementation teams have been small compared to the scope of the implementation projects. However, in the four large organizations IT coordinators in the local units have played an important role as extensions of the implementation teams.

In all six cases, the implementation teams have experienced various challenges, that have made the implementation more slow and resource-demanding than had originally been expected. Before presenting these challenges, a description of the methodological approach is provided.

## Methodological approach

A qualitative approach has been applied for collecting rich data on the different issues influencing the implementation process in the cases studied. Methods used for data collection include interviews, document analysis and observation. Where possible, a longitudinal design has been used, involving repeated rounds of data collection. Referring to the six-stage model of the implementation process (Table 1), the case studies have covered all stages from the initiation of the implementation project, until the stage of the project at the time of ending the data collection. Depending on the time of entrance in the case study, some of the data collection has been of a retrospective nature.

As shown in Table 2, the informants used have included members of the implementation teams and users in the organizational units. A total of 46 semistructured interviews have been conducted, involving 38 informants. In addition comes a number of unstructured interviews and e-mail communication. In three of the cases, periodic updates were also given from the project leader on the status of the implementation project.

The interview guides have focused on the background and current status of the implementation projects, and experiences from the process. Documents analysed include general company information and project documents (project plans, design specifications, meeting minutes). The observation conducted include project meetings, design meetings, mapping in organizational units and training sessions. The researcher’s role can here be characterised as that of an outside observer, not participating in the activities related to the implementation process (Walsham, 1995).

The data analysis has been conducted in two steps. First, data from each case study have been analysed for identifying issues related to the different stages of the implementation process in this case. This has involved the use of open coding, as specified in the grounded theory approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Second, a cross-case analysis has been conducted. This has included comparison of the categories identified in each case, resulting in similar categories being merged or grouped together for each stage in the six-stage model (Table 1). Finally, the issues identified from the crosscase analysis have been compared with contrasting and similar findings from previous research, following Eisenhardt (1989).

## Main findings

## A revised model of the implementation process

The data analysis has identified a need for modifying the six-stage model in Table 1 to represent the type of implementation process studied in this project. This includes both the definition of the stages in the model, the relationship between the different stages, and the issues and activities related to each stage. Figure 1 presents a refined stage model for the implementation of IT for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations, comprising five stages that have been identified from the data analysis. The figure also lists the major challenges identified related to each stage. The model is descriptive in nature in that it only attempts to represent the implementation process as observed in the cases studied in this project.

Figure 1 A stage model of the process of implementing IT for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations.

The initiation stage involves the needs analysis and specification of technological solution. Initiation of the implementation project implies a decision by the central management that the technology should be adopted by the organizational units. However, due to the decentralised nature of these organizations, the final decision to adopt the technology is made by each organizational unit. This adoption process can be seen to be dependent on the process of establishing connectivity, i.e. installing the technology and demonstrating its potential for supporting the business processes for each unit. Compared to the adaptation stage in the six-stage model, the activities related to the specification and development of the applications are here considered to be part of the initiation and adoption stages in the process, e.g. through the development of prototypes. Further, activities involving the individual users, such as training and development of new routines are included in the individual acceptance stage.

After the technology is operable, the individual members of the organizational units decide whether to adopt the technology. The acceptance and routinisation stages in the six-stage model have here been merged, as it has proved difficult to distinguish the findings from these stages. In the final stage of the project, full utilisation of the technology is pursued through establishing new collaborative work practices. In the following, the main challenges identified related to each stage in Figure 1 are presented.

## Initiation of the implementation project

Market deregulation and increasing competitive pressure have been driving forces in the initiation of the projects. In addition, the projects have also been partly technology-driven, triggered by the emergence of new technologies like Lotus Notes and ISDN. The main challenges identified related to this stage are the mapping of the local context in the organizational units, and the specification of the technological solution.

## Mapping the local context

Prior to the specification of the technological solution, a mapping of the technological infrastructure and work practices in each unit has been conducted. A problem in this has been that the organizational units often have not been able to state their future needs regarding IT support for collaboration, as this represents a new way of working. Further, the geographical distribution in these organizations has made the charting resourcedemanding.

## Specifying the technological solution

The organizations studied include a heterogeneous mix of units regarding technological platforms and IT experience and competence. This has made it challenging to specify a technological solution that is sufficiently flexible to accommodate all needs. This has been further complicated by the use of novel and immature technologies.

## Decentralised adoption

At this stage, each organizational unit decides whether to adopt the technology. The main challenges at this stage have been related to local resistance from the organizational units, and the alignment of technology and business processes.

## Local resistance

The overall goal of the IT implementation in all the cases studied has been to make the operations more effective, and to support increasing collaboration within and across the organizational units. However, despite that the implementation teams have been able to document large potential benefits in the form of reduced communication costs and more effective utilisation of resources, several of the organizational units have been reluctant to adopt the new technology. In trying to explain this seemingly irrational resistance, those in charge of the implementation projects have ascribed this to a lack of awareness of the communication costs of today’s operations, lack of understanding of the new technology, and a general focus on short-term goals. However, there also exist several possible alternative explanations for this resistance, that makes sense from the perspective of the local units. These will be discussed briefly in the following.

Reduced autonomy: From the perspective of the organizational units, a closer integration with the other units in the form of increasing collaboration and coordination may not be desirable. Prior to the implementation, the units have been more or less free to make their own decisions regarding choice of technology and suppliers. According to a resource-dependency perspective (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978), the organizational units may oppose against activities intended to support organizational integration, because they want to be in control of the resources by using local suppliers instead of remote internal suppliers.

The standardisation of technology and procedures implied in the implementation projects may also conflict with existing routines and technology, that often have been established for supporting the relations with existing customers and suppliers. Strategies considered to be beneficial for the organization as a whole may thus not be regarded as such by the individual units.

Lack of involvement: The involvement of user representatives from the organizational units in the planning and design of the technology has been fairly limited. As the projects have aimed at developing standardised procedures for communication and information exchange, user involvement has not been given priority. Other factors inhibiting user involvement are the geographical distance between developers and users, a large degree of heterogeneity among the users regarding IT experience and competence, lack of availability of key users because of hectic work schedules, and problems in motivating users to take part in the design of systems related to future-oriented work practices.

Not being included in the planning and discussion in the initiation stage, some of the decision-makers in the organizational units feel that the technology is being forced upon them. In the four large organizations, the local IT departments have also been identified as a source of resistance in the adoption process, questioning the benefit from the new technology. They have expressed concern over how the new technology will affect the IT infrastructure in their organization, for which they are responsible for operation and maintenance.

Lack of need felt: In general, decision-makers in organizational units already engaged in collaboration with other units were found to be more able to see the benefits from adopting technology intended for supporting collaboration, than in units with no previous experience in this. The increasing focus on inter-unit collaboration represents a new form of working, and several units have failed to see the benefits from this, being comfortable with the existing way of doing business.

Bonds to existing technologies: The existing use of technology was found to influence the adoption in the organizational units in several ways. In units already having invested resources in learning to use other technologies, they feared that the new technology would require extra work. Further, established relations with other vendors resulted in a general resistance towards the new technology. For example, in Telenor the standardisation on Microsoft products resulted in resistance to adopt Lotus Notes.

Adoption costs: The costs related to the implementation have been a major concern in many of the adopting organizational units, especially in the three cases comprising interorganizational collaboration (Kvaerner, NNB and OFFCOM). From a transaction cost perspective, investments in technology linking the organizational units may be regarded as having high asset specificity (Williamson, 1981), due to limited value of use outside this collaborative arrangement. For example, the investment in Lotus Notes licenses in the OFFCOM network had very limited value for the member organizations in their individual projects.

## Aligning technology and business processes

Being able to adapt the presentation of the technology to the local business needs has proved vital to adoption. This is found to require a process of maturation, where the implementers and the adopters gradually develop an understanding of how the features of the technology best can support the business processes. The development of prototypes is important here.

## Establishing connectivity

The main challenge in this stage is found to be that of technological integration among the organizational units. Other major issues related to this stage are logistical barriers, timing of the installation and training, and the organizational instability surrounding several of these projects.

## Technological incompatibility

The previous section discussed different barriers in the process of obtaining organizational integration. In all six cases there have also been problems with technological integration. Examples of this include incompatibility between different LANs, interoperability problems with different e-mail systems and performance problems with Lotus Notes on different platforms. These problems are found to be dependent on the level of heterogeneity in the technological platforms, and the level of maturity of the technology. Some of the applications implemented are relatively advanced and complex, even on a world scale (e.g. in ABB Corporate Research). The experience among both the internal IT departments and the external vendors for dealing with problems has thus been limited, resulting in delays in the process.

The issue of technological interoperability can be seen as a generic problem in groupware implementation, with technology intended for supporting tasks that may span organizational and geographical boundaries (Grudin, 1989; Bullen & Bennett, 1990). However, few studies have been found to address these problems in detail.

## Logistical barriers

The geographical dispersion in the cases has created logistical barriers in the initial charting, installation of the new technology, and training and user support. Apart from the Statoil case, the small size of the implementation teams has resulted in limited capacity for following up the implementation in the different organizational units. In the large organizations, the local IT coordinators have played an important role as a local extension of the implementation team, offering technical support on-site. In the SME networks, the internal IT resources in the member organizations are scarce, resulting in that the implementation teams have had to take on the local support activities themselves. Limited capacity has here resulted in delays in handling the problems with the technology.

## Timing of the implementation

Findings from the case studies illustrate the importance of timing the activities related to installation and training both according to the project schedule of the collaborative arrangement and the individual projects of each organizational unit. In the SME networks, delays in the implementation resulted in that installation and training became ‘out of phase’ with the collaborative projects. When the technology finally was ready for installation, the member organizations were engaged in other activities, and did not have time for learning to use the technology.

Previous research has to a little extent addressed the problem of deciding the optimal timing of the IT implementation activities, related to other organizational activities. In the NNB network, the findings indicate that the ideal timing for the installation and training would have been in the interim period between two activity peaks in the project. Then the member organizations still had time available for learning to use the technology, and would also have seen an explicit need for the technology in the near future. In general, this study exemplifies how the time frame for the implementation may be limited in dynamic, collaborative arrangements, leaving little slack in the process. If the technology is not ready according to the original schedule, this may be crucial for the whole project.

## Establishing functional pilot groups

In three of the cases, problems were identified related to establishing functional pilot groups. In both SME networks, different criteria were used in each member organization for selecting individuals to this group, e.g. previous IT experience or role in the collaborative arrangement. This resulted in pilot groups that did not have a real need for communicating. And in the Telenor case, involving management as a pilot group proved difficult due to their hectic work schedule. The importance of establishing functional pilot groups has also been addressed in previous research on groupware implementation (e.g. Francik et al, 1991).

## Organizational instability

In all six cases the implementation projects have been related to some form of organizational change process. This has been in the form of supporting new organizational procedures already established, or as an element in the transition toward a new form of organization. In either case, the lack of organizational stability surrounding the implementation activity has implied additional complexity in the implementation process. This is due to the lack of established new routines, and unclear roles and responsibilities in the new organizational arrangement. In the cases where the new organizational structure had not yet been firmly established (NNB, OFFCOM and Telenor), this resulted in uncertainty about the use of the technology and reduced ability for making decisions. In addition, lack of continuity of personnel has been a problem in several of the projects.

## Individual acceptance

Establishing trust in the technology among the users has been identified as an important prerequisite for acceptance of the technology. Further, variations in the acceptance of different technologies have imposed challenges in the process.

## Establishing trust in the technology

The many problems with interoperability, instability and performance of the technologies resulted in users developing mistrust in the technology and thus preferring to use substitute media, e.g. fax instead of e-mail. In none of the cases had use of the technology been made mandatory. The users were therefore still able to choose among new and old communication media. As discussed by Ciborra (1996), the availability of substitute media may actually constitute a major threat to the implementation, in that the users easily will return to previous practices in case of problems with the new technology.

These problems also made it difficult to establish a critical mass of users. In the NNB network the problems with the technology resulted in that early adopters rejected the technology due to lack of universal access to the other organizations in the network. This corroborates the findings from research on the adoption of interactive communications media and groupware (Markus, 1987; Bowers, 1994).

## Varying acceptance of different technologies

In the Statoil case, a ‘toolbox’ of different Lotus Notes applications was implemented. Findings from this case show that applications intended for communication and archiving have been widely adopted, while the adoption of the tools requiring users to share information in common databases has been more slow. This is in accordance with results from other studies of groupware implementation concluding that e-mail is the most successful technology regarding diffusion (Grudin, 1989; Bullen & Bennett, 1990), and that shared databases are more difficult to diffuse (Orlikowski, 1992; Ciborra, 1996).

The lacking adoption of the common databases has been linked to the transparency of the work process implied in the use of this technology, resulting in a possible fear of exposure among the users. The use of shared databases for supporting the workflow represents a new way of working, where users have to reveal their work to superiors and colleagues at an early stage. This form of exposure is felt strange and uncomfortable by some users, and has resulted in reluctance to use the new technology.

## Establishing collaborative work practices

In the final stages of the project, establishing new collaborative work practices was found to be important for being able to realise the benefits from the technology. As discussed in the previous section, some characteristics of collaboration technology like Lotus Notes may actually constitute a barrier in this process. Further, lack of explicit routines for using the new technology is also identified as a barrier in this process.

## Creating incentives for use

In the Statoil case, Lotus Notes has not been used to its full potential, in spite of extensive training in how to use the technology for supporting collaboration. With the increasing focus on the skills and knowledge of the employees as important organizational resources, information and knowledge become the main assets for the individual employees. As long as the reward systems in the organizations still are based on individual performance, the incentives for sharing information with others will be lacking (Orlikowski, 1992).

## Providing guidelines

Apart from in Statoil and ABB Corporate Research, detailed guidelines for how the technology should be used had not been developed at the time of this study. Such guidelines include definition of different roles and responsibilities in the workflow, for example who is to be responsible for initiating the various tasks in the project, and who should be responsible for maintaining the timeliness and accurateness of the various types of information in the system. Further, this also includes specifying routines related to information access. Tools like Lotus Notes offer possibilities for detailed restriction of the individual user access, and effective use of these features requires an understanding of how this will affect the possibility for collaborating. In the Statoil case, specification of user access according to formal organizational boundaries was found to impose hindrances in the cross-functional collaboration among departments.

## Conclusion and implications

The findings from this exploratory study contribute to an increased understanding of a type of IT implementation that has not been studied extensively in previous research. The study shows how different issues have influenced the different stages in the implementation process, thus supporting the argument of Kwon and Zmud (1987). A number of challenges in this process have been presented, related both to the organizational context, characteristics of the technology and conduct of the implementation activities. Although several of these issues have been addressed in previous research, few studies have applied a similar holistic approach, mapping the various challenges in the different stages of the implementation process.

The study has illustrated how the implementation of collaboration technology in interorganizational settings may imply additional complexity compared to ‘traditional’ intra-organizational implementation of IT. This is a result of greater autonomy among the participating units, heterogeneous technological platforms and logistical barriers in the implementation activities. Also, the transformation to new work practices has been slow, with the transparency of work inherent in the technologies acting as a barrier in this process. In general, the implementation is found to imply a process of maturation, where developers and users gradually develop an understanding of how the technology can support the organizational needs.

Some limitations of the study should be taken into account. The number of informants in some of these cases is limited, due to restrictions in field access and limited resources. It has not been possible to cover all stages of the implementation process, as four of the projects were still running at the end of this study. Some of the data collection has also been of a retrospective nature. Still, it is believed that the findings from this study make a valuable contribution to the IT implementation research.

## Implications for practice

Related to the initiation stage, this study has illustrated the importance of a thorough mapping of the implementation process. This should include the technological infrastructure of each unit, IT competence and experience among the intended users, and their expectations of the new technology. An important task in this mapping should be to identify potential local champions, and incentives for adopting the technology in each unit. The technological solution chosen needs to be sufficiently flexible for meeting the needs of the different units. In this process one should also be willing to reconsider the ‘high-level requirements’ of the project (King, 1995), i.e. whether the project really is addressing ‘the right problem’.

Being able to align the functionality of the technology with the business processes is found to be critical for facilitating adoption in the units. The implementation team therefore should secure involvement of representatives from the local units as early as possible in the project. In cases where adoption costs represent a barrier, sponsoring strategies should be considered for establishing a critical mass of adopting units.

The initial mapping of the technological infrastructure is also instrumental for establishing connectivity. As in the ABB case, conducting compatibility tests of different platforms and applications prior to installation can be of great help. Timing of the installation of the technology and the initial training needs to be coordinated with the schedule for the collaborative activities and the independent projects of the participating units. In time critical implementation projects, use of immature technologies should be avoided.

Establishing trust in the technology among the adopters is crucial for obtaining a critical mass of users. Sufficient resources in the implementation team are needed for providing follow-through support in each unit. Development of automated routines for systems administration is here found to reduce some of the workload for the implementation team.

In the final stages of establishing collaborative work practices, developing incentives and clear guidelines for use is important. This requires an in-depth understanding of how the features of the technology can support the business processes, implying that local management should play an active role. In the Statoil case, the implementation was stated to be most successful in the units where the management had a clear vision of how the technology should be used.

## Suggestions for further research

The study has identified several issues that influence the implementation of collaboration technology in distributed organisations. In general, all these need to be explored in further research. In particular, future research should focus on the influence of the local context of each organizational unit on the implementation process, and the role of different actors in this type of process. There are also several important issues that have not been treated in this study. This includes the influence of cultural diversity among the units on the different stages of the implementation, for example in global organizations. Another interesting question is the possible effect of the implementation and use of this technology on the power relations within and between the organizational units.

As for methodological implications, this study illustrates how longitudinal, in-depth case studies involving qualitative research are necessary for developing an understanding of the complex process of IT implementation for supporting collaboration in distributed organizations.

## Appendix: six case descriptions

This appendix contains brief descriptions of the six implementation projects studied, with main focus on the challenges experienced in each case.

## Implementation of an integrated communication system in NNB

The organizational network NNB (North-Norwegian Building Group) was formed in 1989 by four independent Norwegian companies in the building industry.

The major reason for forming the network was to compete for the contract of building the media village for the 1994 Winter Olympic Games at Lillehammer. Further expressed goals of the network were to exploit common resources and to compete on new, international markets. The process of developing tenders in this network was very resource demanding, and Telenor Research and Development was therefore asked to provide communication services that could support this work. They developed a communication system offering integrated e-mail, computer-supported telephony, file sharing and fax, with ISDN as the network infrastructure.

Problems with immature ISDN services and incompatibility between the NNB virtual LAN and the existing LANs in the member organizations caused major delays in the implementation. The result was that installation and training had to be postponed until after the completion of the Lillehammer project. However, without any new projects scheduled, activities in NNB in general were not given priority among the member organizations, thus resulting in few incentives for using the system. Besides, the members of the pilot user group had been appointed independently by each member organization, resulting in a pilot group without a real need for communicating. Together with personnel changes, this also hindered the project. After the Lillehammer project, a large demand in the local markets of the two largest organizations resulted in these two organizations becoming fully occupied with new individual projects. This eventually resulted in the entire network arrangement being ‘put on hold’.

## Implementation of a Lotus Notes document management system in OFFCOM

The OFFCOM network (a pseudonym) was established in 1993 by seven Norwegian companies producing different types of telecommunications equipment for the offshore industry. Similar to the NNB network, the major reason for forming this network was to compete with larger companies for major contracts. As part of a research project on developing IT support for virtual organizations, Telenor Research and Development developed a document administration system for supporting the tendering process in OFFCOM, with Lotus Notes as the network infrastructure.

The implementation took longer than expected, and several impediments were identified related to this. First, there were variations in commitment among the different organizations, resulting in low participation from some companies. This was found to depend on their relations to existing customers, and the conjuncture in the local markets. Further, several of the organizations expressed concern over having to invest time in learning a new technology. There were also problems with unclear responsibilities among the different actors in the project. For example, it proved difficult to develop a testing scheme suitable for all parties. Several membership changes during the implementation process, both at the organizational and individual level also resulted in challenges for the implementation team. Although they bid for several contracts OFFCOM did not succeed in winning any, and in 1996 the network collaboration terminated.

## Implementation of Lotus Notes in Telenor

Telenor AS (former Norwegian Telecom) is the Norwegian market leader in telecommunications, IT and media distribution. Preparing for the deregulation of the telecommunications market in Europe in 1998, Telenor has been undertaking a major organizational restructuring since 1993. This has involved a restructuring of the operations into core processes, with product development as one example. This process is ‘owned’ by the product managers in all major business areas, with Telenor Research and Development as the operating unit. The product development process involves collaboration between members from all different business areas, working together in cross-functional teams.

Originally started as an implementation project in one of the divisions in the ‘old’ organization, the implementation of Lotus Notes has continued throughout the restructuring of Telenor. The project leader here has played a key role in acquiring funding for the project. As part of the project, Lotus Notes applications have been developed for supporting the product innovation process, through transforming the process documentation and its structure into a project archive with workflow support that can be used in product development projects.

Those responsible for the implementation in Telenor have experienced that the process has been slow. A general problem in the adoption of the technology has been the uncertainty that followed from the restructuring in Telenor. This has resulted in uncertainty about the routines and responsibilities of the new R&D unit. In general, it proved difficult to argue for adoption of technology intended to support a form of work that had not yet been established. Local IT personnel have also resisted the technology, mainly because of bonds to other technologies and lack of experience with the new technology.

In general, the case illustrates how an increasing alignment of the technology and the business processes has emerged gradually. Those responsible for the implementation have learnt more about the business process that the technology is intended for supporting, and those responsible for making the adoption decisions have developed a better understanding of the possibilities offered by the technology. In addition, the project shows the importance of having a project champion for ensuring continuing support for the project in a turbulent organizational environment.

Implementation of a global area network in Kvaerner Kvaerner is an international technology group, consisting of more than 430 companies and branch offices in 45 countries. After the take-over of Trafalgar House in 1996, the number of employees has increased to more than 56 000, making this the largest engineering company outside the US. Because of the decentralised structure of the Kvaerner group, no common IT strategy has existed until recently. In 1995 Kvaerner signed a contract with Telenor as their vendor partner for developing KIGAN—Kvaerner Internal Global Area Network. The contract included the establishment of telecommunications services for voice, e-mail and data worldwide in the Kvaerner group, based on frame relay technology. The strategic importance and scope of the project has increased gradually, from originally only focusing on reducing the communication costs in Kvaerner to also focusing on how to establish more efficient collaboration and coordination between the Kvaerner companies. Examples of areas with great potential for better coordination are experience transfer, deployment of common resources and large-scale benefits in purchasing and service contracts.

The decision to adopt the system has been fully decentralised: each of the Kvaerner companies has been free to decide whether to become part of the network. The implementation has been contracted out to one of the Kvaerner companies. They have formed an implementation team responsible for the internal marketing of the system in the entire organization. The size of this team is very small compared to the scope of its operations. The adoption process proved to be slower and more resource-demanding than expected. Several of the Kvaerner companies did not see the need for adopting this technology, as the level of collaboration with other companies in the group at this stage was low. Instead of using remote internal suppliers, they preferred choosing partners in their local markets. Further, there were problems with the technological solution chosen, as this did not provide sufficient flexibility to support the needs for all types of units in the Kvaerner network. Technological incompatibility has also resulted in problems with stability and performance. Another challenge for the implementation team has been to adapt the marketing of the technology to the local culture of the different units in Kvaerner, regarding decision-structure and local practices.

However, after reaching a critical mass of adopting units the use of the technology has increased steadily, and the network is now characterised as ‘mission critical’ for the Kvaerner operations.

## Implementation of Lotus Notes in ABB Corporate Research

ABB Corporate Research is part of the global engineering group Asea Brown Boveri. The research organization comprises eight research laboratories, six in Europe and two in the US, with a total number of employees of about 800. The research operation is organised in fifteen programmes across the eight labs. In general, the organization of ABB consists of a complex matrix structure, comprising 1300 separate operating companies that are all legal entities, organised in a two-dimensional matrix. This structure creates a need for a standard communication-sharing platform for all units, and ABB has chosen Lotus Notes as their common infrastructure. ABB Corporate Research was one of the first units in the company to implement Lotus Notes. A project support application has been developed that supports all stages in the research process. As in the Kvaerner case, the operation related to the implementation of this system has been small, with the core implementation team consisting of only three persons.

The implementation team has experienced several challenges in this project. First, the small operation of the implementation team has resulted in a large workload on the team members. Further, the available experience for problem solving, both internal and external, has been limited due to the very advanced nature of the application. For example, there have been performance problems with running Lotus Notes on different platforms, and problems related to upgrading of the versions in the network. Due to the complexity of systems administration in a distributed environment like ABB Corporate Research, the implementation team has seen a need for investing a large amount of resources in creating ‘metaprocesses’ for systems administration. This has reduced the workload for the team, through supporting user administration, bug reports, roll-out of new versions, etc.

Other challenges in this process relate to dealing with cultural differences and adapting the work processes to these, and different user behaviour in an individualistic environment like ABB Corporate Research. The inherent possibility in Lotus Notes for logging user actions and thus measuring user performance has also created concern among the users, e.g. managers. However, the application has gradually become a vital tool for the conduct of research projects in ABB corporate research.

## Implementation of Lotus Notes in Statoil

Statoil is a Norwegian state-owned oil company, with 14 000 employees and operations in 21 countries. The implementation of Lotus Notes started in 1992, after an initiative from the managers in one of the units. After some initial pilots, a large-scale implementation project was launched in 1995 with Statoil Data, the group’s central IT unit, in charge. The implementation has comprised seven standard Lotus Notes applications supporting asynchronous collaboration and coordination (workflow, bulletin boards, discussion databases, archiving, document management, meeting scheduling and e-mail). The decision to adopt the system has been discretionary, with the management in each result area making the decision whether to adopt the system, and also which of the tools to adopt.

Statoil Data has a dedicated support staff of more than 60 persons located in the different units. A large number of these have been mobilised during the Lotus Notes implementation. Compared to the other cases studied, Statoil thus has applied a much more resourceful and organised operation for the implementation. However, despite this ‘implementation infrastructure’ there still have been several barriers in the process. For example, there have been many problems with integrating the new technology with the previous systems used, such as the e-mail system. This has resulted in the users developing mistrust in the new technology.
