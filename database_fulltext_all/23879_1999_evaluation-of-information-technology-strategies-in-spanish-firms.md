# Evaluation of information technology: strategies in Spanish firms

E Huerta<sup>1</sup> and PJ Sa´nchez<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Departamento de Gestion de Empresas, <sup>2</sup>Departamento de Matema´tica e Informa´tica, Universidad Pu´blica de Navarra, Campus Arrosadia s/n, 31006. Pamplona, Navarra, Spain

The aim of this article is to analyse the guidelines followed by Spanish companies when evaluating the investment in information technology, in order to establish different evaluation typologies. It will be a detailed description of four Spanish firms, chosen by their distinctive characteristics from a study of twenty, taking into account the various stages of development of information technology within each firm. The study will be done in the framework of the contextual interpretation of the evaluation where the context, process and content aspects deserve main considerations.

## Introduction

Investment in information technology (IT) is an important issue because its expenditure and capital outlay continue to grow. It spreads through all areas of the firm and it affects not only its efficiency but strategy and competitive position as well. For example, such expenditure has been estimated as making up 2% of turnover in England and the United Kingdom (Symons, 1990; Willcocks & Lester, 1994), around 1.75% with an annual growth rate of 8.5% in Italy (Francalanci & Maggiolini, 1995), and around 1.25% in Spain (Huerta & Sa´nchez, 1996).

However, there is no shortage of statistics which demonstrate that the evaluation of IT is flawed due to the problems it engenders. So, it is not surprising that executives are concerned both about the role which IT is playing in the company and how they can evaluate the results of its contribution (Benjamin & Blunt, 1992).

The aim of this article is to analyse how four Spanish companies carry out this evaluation in practice, identify both common and contrasting important factors and classify them into different typologies. The result is the description of four different models of investment decision making in practice with regard to evaluation context, process and content when investing in IT. These companies have been selected after a cluster analysis of a wider number of organizations and each one of the four belongs to each group resulting from the analysis. The case study approach has been used in order to get in-depth information and for a better understanding of factors affecting the decision to invest in IT.

The plan of the article is as follows. Firstly, the evaluation’s definition, difficulties and methods are reviewed. Secondly, the empirical research is explained. Thirdly, the four cases are described. Fourthly, the distinctive and common characteristics from the cases are analysed to conclude in real prototypes of evaluation. Finally, some conclusions are drawn.

## The evaluation: definition, difficulties and methods

The evaluation of IT is both difficult and hazardous (Keen & Scott Morton, 1978; Brown & Blackler, 1986; Cas et al, 1990; Symons, 1990; Bacon, 1992; Powell, 1996). The reasons behind this opinion stem from the particular characteristics of this technology. These are that IT offers substantial economies of scale and scope (Mendelson, 1987; Banker et al, 1991; Gabel, 1991; Dewan et al, 1995), imposes significant switching costs on its participants (Farrel & Shapiro, 1987; Saloner, 1989; Buxmann & Ko¨nig, 1997), produces network externalities (David, 1987; Nault, 1998) and multiple effects, so that both income and costs are hard to quantify, are prone to a number of errors and its uncertainty is very high.

Furthermore, the traditional concept of IT (Bakopoulos, 1985) has been overtaken and today IT is considered not only as an end but also a means to obtaining some wider organizational aim (Ballantine et al, 1995, 1998). It is accepted that the effects of IT are not merely technical nor 100% organizational, but that these are interrelated.

The IT evaluation is defined as those tasks which are designed to assess total investment in IT, the expected profits and its use in helping to make decisions concerning alternative investments and within a strategic framework. In order to carry out the evaluation, different methods and tools are being developed. Several authors have summarised these and we can refer among others to Mukhopadhyay (1987), Amos (1990), Lincoln and Shorrock (1990), Symons and Walsham (1991), Farbey et al (1994, 1995, 1998), and Willcocks (1994, 1996).

The conventional and narrow accounting appraisal must be overtaken and focus on business changes and business values that IT enables (Parker & Benson, 1988; Earl, 1992; Galliers, 1993; Brown, 1994). So, to gain a good understanding and interpretation of the evaluation and to expand the traditional view of tangible costs and benefits, the ‘contextualism’ framework (Davis & Hamann, 1988) and ‘interpretative’ approach (Walsham, 1993; Kanellis et al, 1999) have been proposed (Farbey et al, 1994, 1995, 1998; Smithson & Hirschheim 1998).

This framework is the most adequate because, firstly, it considers that the role of IT has changed from one of automation and support to one of strategic importance and that the benefits are not only quantitative in cash terms, but qualitative, intangibles and uncertain as well. Secondly, differences from rationalistic approaches which are limited by their narrow focus on the system itself and on the quantification of benefits, and by omitting consideration of the wider organizational issues, this underlines that the social contexts within which the system will be utilised are as important as the systems technical features. It addresses qualitative issues and is aimed at producing an understanding of the social contexts and the social processes of the organization into which the information system is to be introduced. Thirdly, and the most important, is the conclusion that there is not a best method for all situations and that the characteristic of a project and its organizational environment affect the way an investment decision is handled.

Evaluation-in-context therefore must be a process which is immersed into the company, inextricably linked by content, context and by the process of organizational change which accompanies the development of the project (Symons & Walsham, 1991; Farbey et al, 1993; Deitz, 1994; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 1994) (Table 1).

The context of evaluation may include internal and external factors, the competitive environment in which an organization works as well as the corporate culture and political context within the organization. The context intends to answer the questions of ‘who’ is involved in the evaluation and ‘why’.

The process of evaluation means the way in which it is carried out, its social role and the results of the evaluation. The process describes ‘how’ the evaluation is or should be done.

The content of the evaluation answers the question of ‘what’ is to be evaluated and refers to the values and criteria to be analysed and measured and the measures itself.

However, sometimes, the difficulty of evaluation is not in its theoretical elaboration but in its practice. We have verified that important conceptual tools exist (Brown & Remenyi, 1994) that can help to solve the problem of evaluation and yet, in spite of this, a gap continues to grow between the theory and practice (Blackler & Brown, 1988; Powell, 1996; Ballantine et al, 1998; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Tam, 1998).

## Empirical research

The object of our applied research is to analyse how companies carry out this evaluation in practice, identify both common and contrasting important factors and classify them into different typologies.

The study was initially focused on a selected group of twenty companies which were felt to be different from the overall group. These companies were chosen based on their bringing together characteristic features. These were the strategic use of IT via the creation of electronic products such as CD-ROM or on-line products; being pioneers in the introduction of modern technology such as just-in-time (JIT), computer-assisted-design and computer-assisted-manufacturing (CAD-CAM) or computerintegrated-manufacturing applications (CIM); owing a high percentage of turnover to the foreign market or being considered highly profitable companies whose products involve a substantial degree of R&D (see Huerta & Sa´nchez, 1999, for a wider explanation).

The information concerning these companies was principally obtained in the years 1995–1997 using open questionnaires as stipulated in case-study guidelines, and was completed using information gathered from written documents, both from internal company sources as well as published material. As for the interviewees and their corresponding hierarchical levels, people spoken to correspond to general manager, to head of computing to financial director, to personnel manager, to manufacturing manager and to other categories.

With the aid of cluster techniques, these twenty companies have been classified into four groups regarding evaluation typologies (Huerta & Sa´nchez, 1999). Five companies belong to group one and the main characteristics are the consideration of strategic values in the evaluation of IT, the involvement of IT in company strategy and the attention paid to one of the important competitive forces in this setting, the customer. The second group, made up of two companies, is distinguished by the enormous importance given to the use of capital budgetary techniques to evaluate IT investment, together with the quantifying of economic benefits. Seven companies are included in group three, where the investment in IT is not a significant relative sum and the budgetary techniques of evaluation are used sporadically or as they are required and not during the elaboration of budgets. Group four consists of six companies where no financial techniques are used in the evaluation of generic investment nor in that specifically of IT. Nor are the criteria financial: cost alone is considered, with no attention paid either to financial or qualitative benefits. Furthermore, evaluation is normally carried out by the general manager.

However, out of the twenty companies studied, we only present the four here (Table 2) which we consider to be distinctive, which belong to each one of these four groups and which allow us to arrive at four prototypes of evaluation methods. The four companies looked at have been analysed in-depth with the case study methodology and have provided both qualitative as well as quantitative information in the content, context and process evaluation aspects. It reveals the technical complexity of IT, its versatility in adapting to the different functions and aims of the company, and its diversity to both organizational forms and evaluation methods.

The case study method has been adopted to carry out the research (Galliers, 1991; McBride & Fidler, 1994; Remenyi & Williams, 1995) because this is considered to be the most adequate for a contextual interpretation, where both qualitative and quantitative information is needed.

In the choice of variables for the evaluation, a rational perspective can be used which considers the content of evaluation as being of greatest importance. This content refers to the criteria used in the decision making, whether this be of a financial character or a more abstract character such as the implicit and/or explicit objectives of those making the decisions.

However this rational perspective presents a series of limitations and other aspects must also be studied such as the organization itself, the process and framework for decision making, which are key points and fundamental to understanding the context in which decisions are made (Deitz, 1994; Mirtidis & Serafeimidis, 1994; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 1994; Jurison, 1994).

With this perspective, information has been compiled into three large sections. In the first, we study a group of contextual variables relating to organization of IT within the company and the technology itself. We look at the IT department to the planning and strategic use of IT and the effective management of the role of IT in organizational aspects. We take into account such aspects as who carries out the evaluation, who makes the decisions and the commitment of the general manager. We also describe the structure and kinds of hardware and applications together with the economic and financial importance of information technology.

In the second, we will analyse the process of evaluation. The critical question is how the agreement is achieved. The consensus must be on what attributes of interest are to be measured and on what the procedures will be by those who carry it out, who make the decisions and who are the promoters. Other aspects are the role of the evaluation and the consideration given to the evaluation itself.

## Case studies

## Company No. 1: Aranzadi

This case describes a company, legally denominated as Limited, but of a family nature with regards to the share of capital. It has a workforce of 250 and a turnover of around £25 million in 1994. Its product is basically information: the compilation of legal texts and jurisprudence. It has a high share in the domestic market, reaching 100% for certain products.

Although the company has a long tradition in these products, a change in management at the end of the 1980s provoked a significant restructuring of organization and strategy. One could talk about the ‘rebirth’ of the company in its new organizational form and its vision now encompasses technological leadership as well as leadership in the market place.

To achieve this aim, IT forms the basis of its new strategy. Together with marketing and publications, computer systems will form one of the most important departments. Its aim will not only be to obtain improvements in productivity but also, more importantly, to permit the emergence of new, technologically innovative products, such as the CD-ROM or on-line services.

As regards the degree of development of IT—the evaluation internal context—the computing department is made up of some 21 people. As for the structure of its hardware, the company possesses two medium-sized client-servers which connect all personal computers, around 150, in the same network. Regarding software applications, one can say that computers have been introduced into all areas of the company, with particular importance being given to their use in the area of production of photocomposition and also in the development of new products such as CD-ROM. The company also possesses documentary databases.

All this evidence leads us to the conclusion that investment in IT is going to figure among the top priorities of the company. The most important forms of IT are the setting up of documentary databases (£0.15 mill), the computerisation of photocomposition (£0.37 mill), that of management (£0.25 mill) and the introduction of computers to form the backbone which brings together all operations and personnel of the company (£0.6 mill) in order to distribute and manage the information. However, the department’s expenditure which represents about 3% of turnover, a substantial figure and one of the highest of the companies looked at, is not considered as investment, but as expenditure.

The procedure involves the board of directors setting out the long-term strategy. Each member of the board translates these objectives into concrete projects which affect their own area of operations. The IT director formulates the long term plan and the IT budget is in line with the general strategic plan of the company, coming under the direct responsibility of the general manager. This person is the great promoter and mentor of this new vision of the role which IT is to play within the company. Although the board of directors must give its approval, no limitations are usually imposed, at least not with respect to the funds available for investment. The formal financial evaluation is only a bureaucratic process. Given the healthy situation of the company, those restrictions which do exist are brought about by the administrative requirements of personnel and by the understanding that such operations are gradual, sequential, slow and costly, and as such cannot be speeded up by intensifying activities in the same time period.

In the evaluation content, the analysis of this investment always takes into account the strategy of the company and the medium and long-term objectives marked out by this strategy are given priority over all others whether financial, or otherwise. These are analysed both from a technical as well as financial perspective, always accompanied by a simple yet compulsory study of profitability.

## Company No. 2: Sarrio´

This company, with a turnover figure of around £80 million and a workforce of 320 in 1994, specialises in the manufacture of paper tissue principally for the export market. Contextual aspects such as changes in ownership (in 1989 and 1994) have caused financial repercussions as well as having a profound effect on the departmental and manufacturing structure of the company.

One of the departments which underwent the most significant modification was that of information systems, since with the new distribution of industrial plants and organizational operations, the new company found itself totally lacking in any computer department; both in hardware as well as applications although some of the personnel remained. The situation brought about a reconsideration of all computer needs with regard to hardware, software and personnel. It was almost as if they were beginning from zero.

The new computing department is made up of eight persons who work specifically in the development and implementation of software which is fundamentally tailor-made. Its manager is at the top level of the organization and works directly under the general management. The managing director plays a major role in the realisation of this operation although he believes that computerisation is not a strategic tool for the company but one which is useful to productivity and coordination.

The company owns two minicomputers, one in the factory and the other in their headquarters which are directly connected. All personal computers at the headquarters are linked up to the network; in the manufacturing plant there are three small networks, one in the offices, another in the technical offices for the CAD, and a third for stocktaking and shipments.

As for applications which rely on the use of databases, their use has spread to all elements of administration, including the management of stock, bar-codes, and the use of electronic data interchange (EDI) with customers. The two challenges still to come are to complete the operational network, and the development of an application for production management which is tailor-made, as in the case of the majority of company software.

Both expenditure and investment in IT are considered to be items of minimal importance with regard to the total figure. Thus, this department’s expenditure in 1994 was almost 0.7% of turnover, of which a third corresponded to outsourcing services. Similarly, investment in IT does not make up 1% of total investment.

As regards the evaluation process, first an evaluation of the plan is carried out and then an evaluation of each year’s budget. The development of the plan followed a rational line, involving a study of the actual situation of the company, the perceived need for information, and the necessary means by which this could be temporarily and adequately satisfied. This plan was, from the outset, coordinated and promoted by the general management; it was agreed by all involved areas of the organization which required information.

The most important, albeit obvious diagnosis was the confirmation of an imbalance between corporate computer technology and microcomputer and end-user technology, or independent operations, with a negative balance for the former.

The formal document, which must be sent to the parent company in Brussels to be approved, analyses increases in income according to sales figures, or to a drop in labour, energy or maintenance costs. The finance manager carries out these studies of profitability and finance.

Finally, as regards evaluation content, although the principal aim in elaborating a computerisation plan was to identify the information needs for the management of the enterprise, mainly it takes into consideration from the outset the financial repercussions and the possible economic benefit, formally quantifying them and subjecting them to the filters of a financial tool. To this end net present value (NPV) and internal rate of return (IRT)

methods are used, insisting that any investment in IT represent a minimum value of three years and 20%, respectively.

However in addition to these financial benefits, the intangible benefits are also identified such as improved quality and service. It is accepted that these benefits will not simply be achieved by the automation of activities, but also and importantly as a result of the coordination, distribution and extension of information.

As regards evaluation as a means of choosing between different applications, a number of attributes are taken into account given values of between 1 and 5, and then added up to obtain a final points total. The considered values are the installation of the package and the prestige of the marketing company at international level, the guarantees which it currently offers and the adaptability to Spanish legislation, the professional opinions based on the company’s own information or on reliable reports concerning the global introduction of the package and, finally, its translation into Spanish

## Company No. 3: S Andres

This case describes a stable company within its sector. Production involves the manufacturing and shaping of paper and card and in 1994 sales reached £16.75 million with a workforce of 158.

On the subject of evaluation context, the company has neither a strategic nor long-term formal plan. The usual management document is the budget and the monthly audits which are studied periodically by the executive committee. As regards computer technology, this is looked after by an internal department of the company, directly answerable to the general management. The latter is sufficiently involved in any decision making, albeit generically, and supports a global information service. There is a general degree of satisfaction regarding the situation and role of IT not only on the part of the general management but also of the head of the computing department as well as other department managers.

The company possesses a minicomputer which brings together ten terminals and seven PC’s, although there is still no network which is anticipated for the following years. Software is introduced according to the needs of the department in question albeit with help from outside, and a progressive evolution is seen to be taking place. An awareness of the experience being acquired through this evolution, as well as the languages being used, have led to decisions which, while not presenting significant economic advantages with respect to the alternatives and at times the opposite being true, have attempted to exploit this acquired knowledge. The structure of software used by the company is that traditional to management (accountancy, payroll and invoicing). On a higher level an integrated package of marketing, production, stocktaking and purchasing exists. Software specifically for executives is also used which permits a daily control of the part of the management. Software for everyday operations is in evidence in all departments and their degree of computerisation is similar regardless of which department first underwent this process.

In the industrial process software exists but this is not considered important and this information is still not integrated into the central computer.

Investment and expenditure on IT by this company is among the lowest of the sample. Investment constitutes 0.34% of the total turnover figure and 6.3% of total investment for 1994 and expenditure is less than 0.5% of turnover. However the company is aware of the intangible assets it possesses through the internal work of the tailor-made software and which has been translated into expenditure.

With regards to the evaluation process, investment in IT is in theory evaluated in a similar way to other investment although the decision making is to a large degree delegated to the computer department. The managers feel personnel dissatisfaction due to the lack of knowledge on the part of evaluating the IT investment and they think that they are not qualified to participate in the decision-making process. Investment in computers are seen as giving less tangible results and they are not fully understood. It is hoped to be profitable and therefore need to be subsequently analysed. The investment process is incremental every year and will not cause a radical change. The approval of investment decision is made by the board of directors when the total cost exceeds £0.5 million.

The criteria used for decision making in the past— evaluation content—was not strictly economic. The company invests in IT if funds are disposable. The aim is to identify both the potential economic benefit as well as the intangible, however in neither case to quantify them. The economic results of productivity are indicated but not quantified.

Intangible benefits are identified both externally to the company and internally. They are considered to be of significant importance to decision making but are difficult to evaluate. Among the external intangible benefits, those which are customer related are considered to be most important with particular emphasis on information to improve service, image and presentation, the fulfilling of delivery deadlines, knowledge of machine operating time, agility and a better relationship between marketingproduction and the customer. This information is of particular importance given the characteristic of the production system and of sales, in the case of the latter 80% is carried out to order and the production system does not allow for sudden changes in manufacturing given the high cost of change.

However, qualitative aspects are also considered in connection with internal personnel. Given that primitive systems were used, a range of 3000 articles were used together with a great number of tools. In addition customer specifications were different, all of which provoked cumbersome processing, a lot of red tape, an excess of information and errors. The expected internal benefits motivated positively personnel.

## Company No. 4: Viscofan

The company studied here is an innovative company with growing profits which specialises in the production of cellulose wrapping. It has acquired other companies and has become the parent company of a group. At the same time it has managed to maintain a family character. In 1994, the parent company had a workforce of 420 and turnover stood at £43 million of which only 3% was obtained from the domestic market.

The product has been characterised for its high quality, competitive price and perfect adaptability to the highly personal demands of the customer. The production process is always to order.

From an organizational point of view, the style of management has a personal stamp to it, dealings are carried out directly, free of bureaucracy.

Contracting models are born which, although seemingly contradictory, have as common objectives efficiency and the reduction of costs. On the one hand the company contracts outside suppliers for their tailormade software, or outsourcing, and yet at the same time an internal energy generating plant is created, otherwise known as insourcing.

A lack of coordination and relating of information is noted between different computers of the same company. Software for executive levels or to integrate among different companies is non-existent. Radically different computer structures exist within different companies of the same group. Some process an important centralised computer department while others, like this one, are completely without a computer department. All this is gradually giving way to a growing demand for information to coordinate, control and manage the different companies of the group.

The company is experiencing a rapid rate of growth, 40% in 1994, with productive investment increasing similarly. However, investment in IT is considered to be somewhat periphery. Thus in 1993, of the £10 million spent on investment, the annual report showed no mention of investment in IT; in 1994, £0.25 million was invested in IT representing 3% of the £8.38 million total figure.

In the evaluation process, the company’s policy of ‘flexibility, the maximum; bureaucracy, the essential’ produces a decision-making process which is absolutely flexible and in no way standardised. Informal dealings and informal flow of information mean that model situations are highly unusual.

The decision-making process regarding the acquisition of corporate hardware involves, in the same way as general investment, an informal chat between the heads of

IT, finance and plant organization, but always within the framework which the chief executive has marked out for IT.

The process regarding computerisation is determined by three major driving forces. The first is the absolute belief of the general director that the contracting of this outside service, outsourcing is the most efficient method of computer operations. The second is the computerisation of different functions has been motivated by the solution to pressing problems. This necessity is accentuated by the rate of exponential growth of the company both commercially as well as industrially. Thirdly, successive software has essentially developed at the spontaneous requests of the users, without any degree of financial limit or control nor any prior planning. In this sense, the company has pioneered software which has later become standard. They are pioneers on a local level due to their introduction of robotisation and innovative software: tailor-made materials requirements planning (MRP) applications, quoting foreign exchange rates so as to permit multinational and multicurrency invoicing.

In the evaluation content of investment, profit analyses are not normally carried out on customary productive investment; instead intuition and experience are the determining factors. In the other cases of substantial investment which are not directly related to industrial activity itself, such as the investment in a co-generator of energy, the corresponding financial studies are made with the period of recovery taking priority.

The evaluation of IT investment has not been carried out from a strategic perspective, the formal methods used have been worthless and very poorly developed or no profit studies are carried out. Such investment is considered to be secondary and would always take back stage if it clashed with productive investment, this regardless of the quantities involved. Thus, for instance, the new plan for information systems which involves around £0.5 million, would warrant no profit study as it is an unavoidable necessity.

New hardware is acquired when current capacity is insufficient and when further information is required at a rate which existing personnel cannot satisfy due to the increased workload. The comparative costs of maintenance or acquisition are also influencing factors. The same criteria of necessity, but not of control of expenditure is true in the development of software in external departments.

As for microcomputers, the users pass on their hardware requirement to the person in charge. Software users send their requirements directly to the developments department. No limitations are normally placed on requirements, neither of a budgetary nature nor regarding content and form. This has occasionally led to very similar applications being made.

## A comparative analysis

The study of these four cases have been carried out in the light of the distinctive characteristics which arrange them into different typologies. Comparison allows us to identify a number of both common and contrasting factors on the evaluation context, process and content (Tables 2 and 3).

Common factors are to be found principally in the area of evaluation context, the technology used in hardware and applications. The most important being that:

(a) The companies have gone beyond the stage of microcomputing or the computerisation of specific functions and have moved into corporate computing via minicomputers which link up PC’s or with client/server platforms. The stand-alone computer use and the only administrative tasks automation is over. There is, at least, a hardware infrastructure used both by administrative and by all other departments of the companies. What is most important is that information technologies are seen not only for automation of these different tasks but for getting better information and coordination.

(b) The evolutionary aspect predominates in software applications and change is incessant. The widespread use of computers throughout the company is a reality as almost all indirect posts possess one; applications are not only focused exclusively on administration but have also been introduced into areas such as marketing, production, design and/or quality.

(c) All but one of the companies, which is in the process of following suit, have their computers connected to intra-organizational networks, either local or corporate. There is a network hardware infrastructure which allows the interdependency and the coordination between all departments. The information system is integrated and the information may be shared by the on-line computers.

(d) It is worth pointing out however, that in cases one, two and three, although each operates different information systems, all three possess an IT department under the direct responsibility of top level general management. This means that the IT department is in the same and first hierarchy level from that of other departments. Before now, it was in a second level under the supervision of the administrative chief officer. Now, the IT chief officer belongs to the manager’s committee, he knows the strategic and long-term aims of the company and it is easier to fit the IT objectives with those of the company. In contrast, case four possesses no such department and contracts this service externally.

As for the more distinguishing aspects of the four enterprises, these (Table 3) are found in the aims of IT, the role played by the general management—the evaluation context; the importance of initiative given to IT projects—the evaluation process; the make-up of the different methods of evaluating IT and its similarity, or lack of it, with respect to other forms of investment—the evaluation context. ## Company No. 1

In relation to context, the aims which motivate the use of IT are to seek innovation by linking IT to company strategy. IT is viewed as a basis for the future development. The company focuses constantly on the world outside with regard to both competitors in the domestic and foreign market. The analysis of other pioneering producers has served to emphasise the importance of IT as one of the primary functions of the company in bringing to fruition strategic objectives. The company carries out strategic planning and uses budgets to manage ordinary activities in the short term. The long-term IT planning is linked to strategic planning. We can therefore regard IT as a strategic element.

About the process, there is a decreasing tendency regarding the setting up of IT projects from case one to others; in this, it is the general management’s responsibility. As regards the general management’s level of commitment towards the development of IT, interest diminishes progressively from this case to the rest. In this case, the general management actively participates in encouraging the strategic role of IT. The vision of the general manager is agreed by the chief executives and consensus is that otherwise the firm would be closed.

A great difference prevails in the area of evaluation content in this company. IT evaluation is considered to be different to the evaluation of other investments. The followed criteria is to align investment in IT with the overall strategy of the company; the financial criteria plays a more subordinate role.

An important observation can be drawn and checked from an economic perspective. The higher the company is situated in the evolutionary pyramid of IS—automation, aid to decision making and strategic—the greater are the hidden and intangible assets of IT. These are derived from internal resources dedicated to IT and from methods of depreciation.

## Company No. 2

In the context area, the aim of IT is to be employed as an element of interorganizational coordination. The small local networks, later integrated into a corporate network, are used for more effective management. IT is considered as an aid to decision making. The company carries out long-term planning and uses budgets in the short term, both for general purposes and for IT.

Regarding the setting up of IT projects, in the process aspect, similar to case one, it is the general management’s responsibility. In the level of commitment towards the development of IT, he displays a positive attitude but without taking responsibility for this strategic aim. The agreement about the needed information requirement is well noted. The evaluation is seen as a worthwhile activity.

In the area of evaluation content, case two employs standard financial criteria which is strictly adhered to and compulsory for investment both in IT as well as that of a general nature. IT evaluation is considered to be similar to general investment evaluation.

## Company No. 3

In the context aspect, case three is not far away with its desire for automation, although with a slight emphasis on achieving a value-added increase for clients and for workers of the company. The company uses budgets to manage ordinary and IT activities in the short term but it does not possess an IT long-term plan.

In the process aspect, regarding the setting up of IT projects, this is down to the head of the IT department while in case one and two it was the general management’s responsibility. In this case there is no active participation in development and evaluation of the IT by the general manager and chief executives because they are unable to anticipate the benefits. The evaluation of IT is viewed differently from all other investment.

In the content aspect, it is only used in financial criteria for the evaluation of large general projects; with regard to investment in IT no financial evaluation, strictly speaking, is carried out. Only external costs are considered and intangible benefits totalled up so that the decision made is based predominantly on costs and the need of financial resources.

## Company No. 4

This company is at the extreme from case one. Regarding context, investment in IT is principally aimed at the automation of tasks, both of a management as well as production nature. Applications have been developed primarily to solve the problems brought about by the expansion of industrial and commercial activity and also to carry out other complex tasks or those which are dangerous to humans. However the goal is always to achieve greater cost efficiency and this includes the human element. IT is considered as an automation tool. The structure of the IT department is different from cases one to three although in case three this is down to the head of the IT department while in one and two it is the general management’s responsibility; here a formal IT department does not exist. It uses budgets to manage ordinary activities in the short term but it does not possess an IT budget. The evaluation of IT is considered to be similar.

In the evaluation process, regarding the setting up of IT projects, the users are the instigators of an upward movement. The attitude of the general manager in the fourth case is clear. The employment of an external service is compulsory in line with the IT goal of automation and of achieving the highest levels of efficiency throughout the company. Unless unitary costs are getting lower, total expenditure in IT is growing and IT is consuming more financial resources. The executive chiefs are feeling uncomfortable because they need more information, quickly and timely. Nevertheless, they all consider the evaluation as unnecessary and costly.

In the area of evaluation, investment in IT is motivated by the principle of becoming more cost efficient in the face of the need to automate administrative and productive processes.

To sum up, case one uses a methodology which we can classify as strategic, case two as standard-financial, evaluation through necessity in case three and evaluation by costs in four.

Strategic evaluation takes into consideration that the objectives sought with IT are those which bring together the strategic consequences of IT with the strategy of the company. In such a way, the company obtains a competitive advantage. In this evaluation, the general management has an important role to play as promoter and motivator of the vision of IT. Finally, these medium and long-term values take priority over financial and shortterm values.

In standard financial evaluation, the objective sought with IT is that of facilitating the coordination of the company’s internal activities using IT. In this form of evaluation the management is keen that the data needed for decision making is provided by IT. However the same strategic value is not given to IT as in the previous case. The values considered are strictly financial, calculated using typical financial tools such as IRT or NPV.

Evaluation through necessity bases its decision to invest in IT on the unique aim of increasing efficiency. The management appears unconcerned, alleging an inability to evaluate and leaves the decision to be made by the head of computing. The criteria used is the level of finance available in the company.

Finally, with regard to cost evaluation, the aim of investing in IT is to improve cost efficiency significantly which entails the outside contracting of computer services, or outsourcing. The management ignores IT as a tool for obtaining effective information. The criteria is to consider IT as a way of achieving information most cheaply.

Case one is far away from case four. Case one represents a model where there is adequacy between the aims of the IT and the criteria used to measure them. The implementation of IT has been made by a radical change and people feel satisfaction with the results of the investment in IT. The evaluation itself is considered beneficial. At the other extreme is case four. There is no adequacy between aims and criteria. The investment in IT has been incremental and people do not feel comfortable with the consequences of IT. The evaluation is considered not useful nor necessary.

Cases two and three are next to one and four, respectively. The IT has been implemented radically in case two, this change is considered beneficial and the evaluation itself useful. Nevertheless, there is a dichotomy between the aims and the criteria used, coordination and financial measures. The investments in case three has been made incrementally, the evaluation is considered useful but not necessary and people feel comfortable with the results. Nevertheless, there is discordance between the aims and the criteria for measuring them.

Except for case one, the other three cases present a dichotomy between the aims and the criteria. This is surprising, even more so if it is said that, except for case four, they feel comfortable with the results of the investments in IT.

## Conclusions

In this paper a contextualism framework has been used to overtake the financial and narrow view of the evaluation and to understand it in a wider form.

This framework has been applied to the research of four cases and they have been studied deeply with quantitative and qualitative information.

It has been noted that different real ways to evaluate IT exist, each with very characteristic features. It has been described a strategic methodology in case one, the standard-financial in case two, evaluation through necessity in case three, and evaluation by costs in case four.

Each differs from other in context, process and content factors. But the context ones sometimes ‘entirely overwhelm the content’ (Deitz, 1994; Farbey et al, 1994).

The success of the evaluation will be where the aims will determine the criteria and measures to be used in order to decision make as well as the agreement process about them. Otherwise, they will be largely unsuccessful or if the success happens, it will be unexpected and without control.

For future works it is intended to study in-depth how and why the dichotomy between the aims of IT and the criteria used for evaluation are produced and to establish recommendations in order to overtake these problems.
