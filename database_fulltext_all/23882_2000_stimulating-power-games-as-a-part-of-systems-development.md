# Stimulating power games as a part of systems development

V Torvinen and K Jalonen

Turku Centre for Computer Science TUCS, Laboris Research Group, University of Turku, Lemminka¨isenkatu 14A, 20520 Turku, Finland

The biggest problems of information system development (ISD) are not in the construction of technica artefacts. The real challenges to development are the social and political aspects of change. Imagining new organizational structures is difficult because knowledge about existing structures is often objectified. Furthermore, the power structures of organizations make such imagination more difficult or even forbidden. This paper describes a study in which a prototype of an information systems (IS) development method, called the Labour Game, was evaluated in field experiments. The paper describes the method and demonstrates how it succeeded in shifting the focus of discussions from technical development to issues which typically increased the competence of employees and modified the power structures of the work processes. The paper advocates the use of ‘soft’ methods, which help users and managers to talk about the social structures of work and consequently about different IS requirements in the early phases of systems development.

## Introduction

One problem associated with information systems development (ISD) methodologies concerning the development of human activity may be due to too much attention being given at the beginning of the development project to information technology (IT). Instead, the main concern in the process of development should be the activity in which the IT is intended to be used. Even though almost all ISD methodologies stress the importance of the development of activity, it seems to be rare for methodologies to describe the development of activity in concrete terms.

According to Hirschheim and Klein (1992), the ontological and epistemological background of ISD methodologies often reflects the positivistic paradigm: the world is seen as an objective reality, and its nature is better described as order than as conflict. From this perspective, the social structures of reality are ‘found’, ‘observed’ or ‘modelled’ rather than ‘interpreted’ or ‘re-created’. However, the basic challenge of any development should not be the creation of solutions following existing organizational premises and norms. Rather, development is a learning process, in which organizational premises should also be questioned (Bateson, 1972; Argyris & Scho¨n, 1978; Argyris, 1982). Furthermore, conflicting interests and power structures of work should also be analysed when questioning the premises of work.

In this study, problems related to the analysis of user requirements were investigated following an experimental research strategy. The development activities of a case organization were observed for ten months. After this period, field experiments were organised in the same organization in order to study development activities in a more controllable setting. At first, the organization’s existing practices in carrying out organizational and IS development were documented. Secondly, a prototype of an alternative, ‘soft’ ISD method was introduced to the participants and the transfer effect of the intervention was measured. As a result, the method transformed the mental state of the participants, from the analysis of operational problems following existing organizational premises to the analysis of labour structures questioning existing power structures and norms. The study demonstrates how a ‘soft’ ISD method can help users and managers to talk about power-related elements of change in work structures, learn new ways of conceptualising existing activities, and identify new potential ISsupported work structures.

The paper is organised as follows: the next section describes the prototype of the method, followed by the case study and a discussion of the research method used. The subsequent section presents an evaluation of the research process and outlines the results, and the final section presents a discussion of the study.

## The Labour Game method

The Game of Division of Labour (briefly, the Labour Game) is a method enabling users and managers to study their future needs (Torvinen, 1998). The game is based on the study of labour structures as a method for learning. The game searches for optional perceptions of work structures, and in this way supports the participants’ learning. A new, purposeful labour structure has a value of its own as a potential new way of organising activities. Furthermore, a new labour structure creates new kinds of understanding of the weaknesses and strengths of the present situation, and of beliefs, contradictions and power tensions related to the proposed change. The game is intended to be used at the very beginning of the ISD process, in which the activity system is analysed and IS requirements are created. It is situated at the front end of the phase of IS requirement analysis because it focuses more on the analysis of human activity system than on the analysis of computer application (cf. Checkland, 1981). IT is an inseparable part of the analysis because it facilitates new ways of working by offering new possibilities for organising the work in time and space. However, technology has only a secondary place in the analysis.

The Labour Game is in many ways consistent with the design ideas usually shared in Scandinavian usercentred design. Full participation of users is expected, informal aspects of work are stressed (e.g. the employees’ skills, tacit knowledge and communication), computer systems are seen as tools, productivity and quality of the work are considered equally, the design process is seen as a political process with inner contradictions, and the use situation is taken as a fundamental starting point for the design (Greenbaum & Kyng, 1991). One difference compared to participatory methods is that IS designers are not expected to participate in the Labour Game. The playing board and the changes are supposed to be created by the employees. The process is supported by a game leader as well as by predefined moves.

The Labour Game prototype consists of playing cards, a description of the playing board, and the basic moves allowed in the game. The cards are used to model existing work processes to create the playing board. The moves are used to construct new labour structures, which in turn are also represented by the same playing cards as the existing process. The game facilitates users’ and managers’ debates about existing and new labour structures. Each new work structure may lead to different IS requirements. The process of requirement analysis can be continued after the Labour Game session by more formal methods of analysis and design.

## The playing cards and the board

There are two sets of cards in the game. The first set represents different aspects of work roles, the so-called ‘role-models’. The second set represents potential actors, and are referred to as ‘actor-instances’. An extra card type is reserved for the arguments which arise from the game itself.

The role model is a minimum definition of the work role: what the work involves, what is required of the actor, and what the role is called. Three playing cards are used to model role models. The first card, ‘the work role’, was chosen simply to provide a name for a work role. The work roles are always interpretations, and employees do not refer to them as roles. The work role card provides a name for a role and creates a common vocabulary for the players. The second card, ‘criterion of an actor’, makes some tacit knowledge of the employees more explicit. The card describes the characteristics required of the actor who takes on the role. The characteristics may include skills, the required level of education, information to be elicited or other assumptions relating to the characteristics of the actors. The purpose of the card is to bring these silent assumptions to the surface and under evaluation. The third card, ‘the outcome’, defines the role as a domain of responsibility. The outcome can be concrete (e.g. forms completed, products produced etc) or abstract (e.g. information created). It also describes the criteria whereby the actor decides whether or not the tasks were performed successfully, i.e. the outcomes that have to be achieved for a successful performance.

The actor-instance set also includes three cards: the actors card, the skills card and the work-tasks card. The actors card describes the kind of individual (or group) who may take on a work role. The work role refers to ‘doing something’, while the actor refers to ‘being something’. The skills card describes the proposed characteristics of an actor. This card is very similar to the criterion of the actor card. Here, however, the characteristics are not taken from the requirements of the activity but created from the general assumptions and attitudes of the players. Again the characteristics include skills, profession, education or other characteristics of the actor (e.g. gender). The work-tasks card describes the concrete work tasks which a specific actor is expected to perform when she takes on a specific role. The work task can be either a concrete action or a domain of responsibility. Communication, co-ordination and control achieved between work roles are also part of a work task. Information technology and information systems are conceptualised as tools in the Labour Game, and consequently a description of the existing technology is included in this card. The actor-instances have different types of actors and concrete work tasks, but at the analytical level the work roles are the same. In this case, the work role can be taken on by a collective actor, i.e. the customer and the librarian together, or by an individual actor, i.e. the librarian.

The cards are situated on a playing board (e.g. a large conference table) in order to represent the work process (Figure 1). There is no concrete ‘playing board’ other than the order of the cards on the table. The bottom of the board is reserved for the role models and the top for the actor instances. When the work roles and their actor instances are situated on the playing board, they should follow the logic of the actual work process.

The playing board is not a detailed description of work practices, although they are present in the game. Work practices within a work role are not modelled in detail because they are not a central part of the labour structure (cf. participatory and scenariobased design, which focus mostly on situations and work practices). The playing board is not a detailed description of the work process either, because several process structures are included in the same board (cf. work flows, process modelling and redesign, which mostly focus on work processes). The ‘real’ process structure could be marked in the board, but it is not the central issue in the Labour Game. Rather, the main focus is on labour structures, i.e. an interpretation of work roles and their possible actor types.

## Playing the game

A move in the game consists of any change in the playing board or the cards which creates a new structure of the division of labour. The order or content of the cards may be changed, or one or more cards may be added to or removed from the board. For example, a move may change the division of tasks among work roles, combine two specialised work roles, identify a new actor type for a work role or create completely new work roles. In the process of creating new labour structures, the social knowledge which supports or resists the proposed change is also analysed. All the arguments, counterarguments, assumptions, opinions, decisions and any other comments brought up in the game are documented. They are written onto the argument cards and placed in the appropriate place on the playing board. Writing the perceived problems of the work roles on the argument cards and placing them on the board is also useful.

The cards facilitate the analysis of work structures by offering a means for making comparisons. Each card in the first set has a corresponding card in the second set (Figure 2). The pair of the work role card is the actor type card. The comparison of these two cards brings habitualisation and institutional power structures to the surface; in other words, some types of actors may automatically be connected to some types of activities without considering other possible roles or actor (Berger & Luckmann, 1966). The Labour Game suggests that this connection needs to be analysed, and new possible roles and types of actors need to be considered and created.

The pair of the outcome card is the work tasks card. The outcome card defines what has to be accomplished in the role, while the work tasks card defines the means by which these goals are achieved. Technology, information systems and other tools are also part of the work tasks. However, writing down how things are done is much easier than writing down the motive of activity embodied in outcomes. The goals and motives of a given activity easily become part of the tacit knowledge of employees (Berger & Luckmann, 1966). The comparison of these two cards allows the players to evaluate how well existing goals are achieved by existing means. In addition, the relevance of existing goals (outcomes) can also be evaluated.

The third corresponding pairs of cards between the two sets are the card of criteria of an actor and the card of proposed skills and characteristics of an actor. The criteria of an actor defines what has to be known and what kind of skills are required for the actor who may take the role. Proposed skills of an actor define the characteristics which are assumed from a type of actor. Skill requirements are taken from activity while the skills of actors are based on stereotyping of actors. Writing down assumptions of skill requirements of work and characteristics of actors helps in critical evaluation of social knowledge concerning the existing labour structures.

Each change proposal can be supported or resisted. In fact, arguments for and against the change proposals are considered and analysed even if there is no-one among the players who agrees with them. There are two reasons for this. Firstly, this procedure guarantees that opinions can be presented as the point of view of ‘somebody’, for instance of a role. In this way, even difficult aspects of proposed changes can be handled without personal emotional strain. Secondly, the procedure highlights the voluntary and value-related nature of development. There is no such thing as a deterministic or preordained direction of change. Development is always a conscious, political activity, with potential winners and losers. The open analysis of possible conflicts can even be therapeutic, especially if criticism is directed towards labour structures, not towards individuals.

## Case and research design

Most studies of participatory design have used action research approaches in order to ensure access to the relevant information which employees already have about their own work (Clement & Van den Besselaar, 1993). A more formal research method, the field experiment, was chosen for our research for two reasons. Firstly, the author of the Labour Game was taking part in the research as researcher and as game leader. This double role of the researcher required careful monitoring of the research situation. Secondly, the experimental nature of the research guaranteed access to the organization. The Labour Game was only a prototype, and there was no experience of the game other than the theoretical and laboratory testing. The use of a prototypical method as an actual ISD method would also be too risky for any organization.

Generally speaking, field experiments differ from laboratory tests (e.g., Batra et al, 1990; Davis, 1990; Batra & Davis, 1992) in the sense that the experiment is conducted in a real-life setting (Siau & Rossi, 1998). On the other hand, field experiments differs from typical case studies in so far as the research setting is more controlled than in real life. The benefit of the field experiment is that the phenomenon can be studied in a reallife setting, while at the same time certain variables can be controlled. In this study, the researchers observed and evaluated the work and development activities of the case organization for ten months before the field experiments took place. This experience provided an additional frame of reference for the evaluation of changes occurring in development activities with and without the Labour Game.

The field experiment was carried out in a research library with a staff of 118 employees. The organization can be described as an IS-dependent expert organization. Half of the employees have a university degree; the organization is female-dominated, and salaries are relatively low considering the level of education. There is one central IS at the very core of the activity; it is used by 84% of the employees, and 55% of the time.

The researchers had little influence on the selection of work processes included in the experiments. The researchers asked for activities which had a clear process structure and which crossed several organizational unit boundaries. The three processes chosen were those of periodical acquisition and loans, general acquisitions and interlibrary loans. The researchers had no influence on the selection of participants either, although we asked for non-IT participants who had first-hand experience of the existing work processes. The researchers also suggested an optimal group size of three to five participants. One of the final groups had three members and two had five members each (total thirteen participants). The number of participants is on a par with similar studies evaluating methods in a real-life environment (e.g. six participants in Vassey & Conger, 1994; sixteen in Calloway & Ariav, 1995; sixteen in Flynn & Davarpanah, 1998). The organizational position of the participants varied from shop-floor employees (n = 9) to managers (n = 4).

The schedule of the experiment was withheld from the participants beforehand, as it might have affected their behaviour. The participants knew only that there would be several sessions in the experiment and that the first day would focus on modelling and the second on design. It was stressed that the purpose of the experiment was the evaluation of the Labour Game method—not an evaluation of participants’ skills or other characteristics.

The experiments reported in this paper consisted of three sessions and two briefings (Figure 3). The sessions were designed to evaluate the outcome of the Labour Game: i.e. how the game affected development discussions and what kinds of ideas the players produced. The political process of choosing those ideas to be implemented was excluded from the study. Each experiment was organised as two four-hour sessions, held over two days. There were two other sessions, in which the learning of participants about the ideology and basic concepts of the method was measured; the results of those sessions will be reported in another paper.

In the pre-game session, the participants’ own understanding of the problems and critical development areas of their own work activity was measured. We were interested in observing the kinds of problems on which the participants concentrated in a spontaneous development situation. The participants were asked to document the problems, development ideas and future scenarios of their own work. It was stressed that ideas could be situated at many levels: i.e. from ideas for improving the use of current IS to ideas for global change. The participants worked as a group, and were able to make use of documents and process descriptions produced by the organization’s earlier development projects.

Briefing 1 introduced the Labour Game cards, and was followed by the board session, during which the playing board was built up. Problems and critical areas of development identified in the pre-game session were written on the cards and placed on the playing board. Most of the cards could easily be allocated to the place where the problems occurred. Further problems were identified using the playing board.

Briefing 2 covered the development of activity as changes in labour structures and was given prior to the game session. Development was explained as a process of reflection, in which the premises of labour were identified, studied from a distance, and questioned. The second briefing and the game session followed the description of the Labour Game (previous section).

The persons present in the actual research setting were the participants (3–5), a game leader (researcher) and an observer (researcher). The game leader was active in the board and game sessions as well as in the briefings. Data were gathered by observation, videotaping, oral feedback from the participants given immediately after the experiments, and the participants’ written feedback, given later.

## Evaluation

## Evaluation of the game

In the pre-game sessions, the participants’ understanding of the nature of development stressed the improved achievement of existing organizational goals. The problems of the existing activity were collected, and development was understood to consist of finding solutions to these problems. Most of the problems identified were related to the pace of work, neglected work tasks and difficult working procedures, or to weaknesses, shortcomings and bugs in the IS. There were some suggestions concerning new organizational procedures, e.g. better co-operation between different committees and the integration of operational employees into the organizational decision making. However, by and large the analysis of the existing work activity took the organizational goals and work arrangements as given (cf. Kensing & Madsen, 1991).

The construction of the Labour Game playing boards was easy in all three experiments. The interpretation of work roles and actors modified the analysis situation in two ways compared to the pre-game sessions. First of all, the analysis was extended to some elements of work which were not visible in the organization’s previous development activities. The assumed performers of work, skill requirements, outcomes of work roles and shifts of time and/or place become more clearly visible. Secondly, the analysis was relieved by some elements of work which seemed to hinder the analysis previously. The complexity involved in the performance of a particular work role, in different work practices, and in the operational problems of work procedures and the use of information systems were encapsulated under different instances of work roles and actors. Further, the work roles which were most heavily burdened by operational problems were easily identified by the board.

The effect of the Labour Game method can be characterised by three main types of change in the discussions. Firstly, the Labour Game provided a better focus on those problems under the control of the organization (Table 2). During the pre-game session, the participants identified many problems which hindered their daily work but which they could not really solve. The source of these problems was usually outside the organization, e.g. in the customers or in the activities of other organizations. The development of more intelligent control procedures for such problems is generally worthwhile; however, these problems took up a considerable focus of the participants without any concrete development plans. The playing board, on the other hand, helped to identify similar problems even better than the pre-game session. The playing board facilitated the systematic comparison of goals and means, i.e. outcome and work task cards, while the pre-game session as well as development activities in general in the organization were unstructured.

During the game session, the problems were more concrete and operational in their nature than in the two previous sessions. The reason for this must be the fact that the game session was solution-oriented while the other two sessions were problem-oriented. The solutions suggested also led to an awareness of new problems, which had not been identified during the previous sessions.

Secondly, the connection between problems and power structures was more commonly in the foreground in the Labour Game than in the two first sessions (Table 3). In the organization studied, the existing power structures were not subject to discussion, and consequently the scope of development was very limited. In general, development which adheres to existing power structures can only create new work practices but never radically new process structures or labour structures.

The Labour Game supported the transformation of the development discussion into political, power-related issues. The discussion was not heated but took the form of calm, rational conversation. The pre-game session and the board session clearly missed some elements of development because of the absence of power-related issues. In other words, the set of relevant problems was broader in the Labour Game than in the pre-game and board sessions.

Thirdly, the question of job enlargement and enrichment was raised (cf. Buchanan, 1979). The pre-game development discussions in the organization concentrated on the operational problems of work roles; in other words, the boundaries of work roles and their actors were taken as given. Development was understood as a question, for instance, of ‘how can the person who does the cataloguing do the work more effectively and efficiently?’ On the other hand, problems of centralisation and decentralisation were acknowledged; they seemed, however, to be such sensitive questions that they were not really discussed. Furthermore, a common idea for improving the situation was total centralisation or decentralisation, which would have destroyed the existing, relatively flexible work practice.

The perspective on development in the pre-game session focused on solving problems within the existing labour structures, while the game session stressed new responsibilities and challenges of work by changing the labour structures. If the problems discussed in the pregame session or board session were solved, the work of the employees would be easier, and in most cases the employees would need fewer or similar skills (Table 4). The process of problem-solving lost many opportunities because job content was not really questioned. IS were also optimised to support existing jobs. A more reflective analysis of job structures would have found unnecessary fragmentation of jobs; however, the collectively accepted, objectivised knowledge about the content of jobs made this kind of analysis impossible. On the other hand, the solutions offered in the game session often increased work requirements.

The Labour Game had a minor effect on the nature of discussions, measured as direct links with IT. During the whole experiment, 30% of the problems were directly related to IT (Table 5). Some changes in work structures would only have been made possible by new kind of technology. This means that technology had an important role in work design. The experiment demonstrates, however, that the development of technology was only one part of the discussions relevant to the participants. Many other development ideas which would have improved the work of employees were also presented. These ideas are easily ignored in technologydriven development.

To conclude, the experiment demonstrated how ‘a soft’ ISD method broadened the scope of development discussions in one particular organization from minor operational problems to more general, structural problems. The social and power structures of work were reorganised and not merely ‘modelled’ or ‘identified’; in other words, both organizational goals and means were open to questioning and redefinition. All the ideas discussed during the game sessions would have led to different activity models and consequently to different IS requirements. Surprisingly, the ideas occurring in the earlier phases of the experiments reflected a positivistic paradigm similar to those seen in most technically oriented ISD methodologies (Hirschheim & Klein, 1992). This finding may explain the success of traditional ISD methodologies. At least in this study, the concept of development in peoples’ minds did not include radical changes in the premises of organizational activities. The concept of development was restricted to the solution of technical problems.

## Evaluation of the research setting

In the main, the participants’ feedback about the game was positive. The game was considered to be ‘an interesting brainstorming method’, making it easy to suggest changes in work processes. The game was thought to serve job and process re-design, although the importance of organising the games and training the players was stressed, as was the post-analysis of the ideas generated in the game. The most suitable place for the Labour Game in the development process was suggested to be in the initial phase. The perspective of the game to the activity was characterised as ‘novel’ and ‘open-minded’. One participant commented on the game as follows:

The Labour Game helped us to think of new, unprejudiced solutions and procedures because the topics under discussion were on the cards—in a way detached from reality. For the same reason the participants did not take an attitude towards the suggestions, as would have happened if we just sat around the table and ‘made it up’. (free translation)

The theoretical background of the Labour Game suggests that contradictions and power games are typical of the game. There were no visible power games in the field experiments. The discussion of radical change ideas was calm, even when the organizational position of one participant would have been weakened. One explanation for this is that the participants assumed that the experiment had no real effect on their work. An alternative explanation is that the game actually helped participants to discuss painful and even emotional changes in a peaceful way. A third explanation for the calm atmosphere of the games is the exclusion of many employees who would have been affected by the ideas. They did not, in the main, participate in the experiments because of the small size of the groups. In fact, the briefings where the results of the experiments were reported resembled power games a lot more than the experiments themselves. The ideas produced in the game were criticised and rejected as irrelevant by two managers. However, the calm nature of the games cannot be explained by the lack of middle management in general, because some managers participated in the experiments.

The influence of the game leader on the game was significant. The game leader steered the game and the meandering discussion. A game without a game leader familiar with the playing cards and the philosophy of the game would probably fail. The game leader encouraged the participants to present and clarify their own ideas. He also summarised the discussion, and translated the themes of discussion into the vocabulary of the Labour Game. The final ‘moves’ were created together. The game leader facilitated the game by asking ‘stupid questions’ which may have helped the questioning of the organizational premises. The game leader also presented some ideas for the game, while strictly following the rules of the game.

The participants saw the role of the game leader in different ways. One argued that the game leader steered the game too much, while another stressed the importance of the leader’s knowledge of the work process under discussion. Most positive feedback suggested that it was good that the game leader was an outsider because he could ‘steer the discussion to “unpleasant” issues without suppressing the conversation.

There are three issues which might have had some negative influence on the results: the short time spent on the experiments, the presence of the video camera, and the experimental nature of the research setting. At the end of the second day, the actual time spent playing the Labour Game was very brief. This clearly affected the results. Many ideas which could have been produced in the game are probably missing. The proposed changes were not simulated in the game, which may have given participants an incomplete picture of the game. On the other hand, the purpose of the experiments was not to measure the quantity of ideas but the change in their nature. Some participants were affected by the presence of the video camera. This can occasionally be seen on the videotapes, and is also included in one participant’s written feedback. The experimental nature of the research setting may also have influenced the results of the game. The participants were aware that the primary goal of the experiments was not to produce ideas for implementation but to evaluate the method. In a genuine situation, participants might have been motivated to discuss the ideas in more depth. Conversely, the thought of actual implementation of the ideas might also have censored the discussion.

The structure of the experiment (pre-game session, board session and game session) may have had a positive effect on the results. The most obvious problems of the work processes were discussed before the actual game session. These problems did not hinder the game session because they had already been discussed and were situated on the playing board. A game without some kind of preceding problem session would probably be more loaded with problems concerning everyday work practices.

## Discussion

In this paper, we have described some theoretical and some practical aspects of the Labour Game. Using the power of unpredictable changes, the players of the Labour Game bring the premises of the division of labour to the surface, question them, and create feasible new work structures. The new structures lead to different IS requirements in the later phase of the ISD process.

The study demonstrates that a ‘soft’ ISD method can help users and managers to talk about emotional and even painful elements of change in work structures, to learn new ways to conceptualise the existing work, and to design and identify new potential IS-supported work structures. In this particular study, the participants saw the method tested as a new and innovative way to debate process structures. The topics brought up during the game were perceived as important ones, which had not previously been discussed systematically in the organization. The study demonstrates that it is possible to organise the ISD process as a rational discussion of labour structures, and that such discussion is seen by participants as important. It also shows that discussion of labour structures helps to invent new work structures and IS requirements.

A successful ‘soft’ ISD method should increase the participants’ awareness of the social nature of their work. Development is a learning process, where new possible ways of conceptualising reality are created. The process can be deemed successful if the participants have

learnt something about their own perception of reality, even if the process has not brought about any concrete changes in the work processes. The process has failed if the debate has caused a situation in which the participants are in conflict, feel themselves threatened, or are unable to talk about the power structures of work. However, even in this case some benefit is derived from the procedure. The participants have found some aspects of work which are sensitive and which are likely to form a risk in the potential change process.
