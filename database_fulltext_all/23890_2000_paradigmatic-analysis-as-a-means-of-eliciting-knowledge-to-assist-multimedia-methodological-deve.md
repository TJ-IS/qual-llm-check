# Paradigmatic analysis as a means of eliciting knowledge to assist multimedia methodological development

S Gallagher and B Webb

Management and Information Systems Division, Queens School of Management and Economics, Queens University of Belfast Belfast, Co. Antrim, N. Ireland, BT7 1NN

We have identified two paradigms in multimedia systems design based on the software engineering and graphic design approaches. Our aim is to better inform methodological development in the field. We argue that unless and until the underlying paradigm of each community is understood, attempts to develop specific multimedia methods will be sub-optimal. ‘Paradigm’ is defined in the Kuhnian sense of a ‘disciplinary matrix’ that is composed of those shared beliefs and values that guide a ‘community’ of theorists and practitioners. This paper is based on analyses of several textbooks from both disciplines and also on inter views with software engineers and graphic designers who are actively engaged in the development of digital interactive multimedia (DIM). The results indicate that (a) Kuhn’s concept of paradigm is a useful mechanism through which to examine the knowledge and approaches of these two design disciplines visa\`-vis multimedia development; and (b) that the resulting paradigms provide a useful framework from which to inform methodological development within the multimedia field. The paper concludes with a discussion of how the paradigms can assist theorists and practitioners in developing a framework for method evaluation and integration in multimedia.

## Introduction

Previous research suggests that it is possible to identify at least two distinct approaches to multimedia systems development (Gallagher & Webb, 1997). The software engineering approach is encapsulated, to varying degrees, in a number of hypermedia models and approaches (Dospisil & Polgar, 1994; Halasz & Schwartz, 1994; Hardman et al, 1994; Garzotto et al, 1995; Gingie et al, 1995; Schwabe & Rossi, 1995; Fedchak & Duvall, 1996; Lux, 1996; Mere & Rossi, 1996). This is a structured design approach that separates information structure and behaviour (which together constitute the systems kernel) from presentation, effectively relegating graphic design to a subordinate role. In contrast, the graphic design approach, based on commercial multimedia product development, begins and ends with a consideration of presentation, emphasising the importance of interface and user input (Patterson, 1992; Cotton & Oliver, 1993; Kuo, 1993; Heller & Rivers, 1996; Koumpis et al, 1996).

Multimedia development, by definition, is an interdisciplinary activity. This is an issue on which many agree (Neophytou et al, 1995; Streitz, 1995a; Molina, 1996; Mallon, 1998; Powell, 1998). The interdisciplinary nature of the field is, paradoxically, both its strength and its weakness. The strength of interdisciplinary collaboration is evident—a group of professionals who are specialists in their particular field contribute their expert knowledge. Unfortunately, and for the same reason, interdisciplinary efforts can be quite problematic.

Software engineers and graphic designers are just two of the disciplines that may be required to collaborate in order to produce a multimedia product. This research has concentrated on these two design disciplines because they represent contrasting views and attitudes on design (Margolin, 1989; Craig, 1991; Vertelney et al, 1991; Swaine, 1992; Romiszowski, 1993). Nevertheless, some consensus is undeniably evident, amongst interviewees and in the literature, on the need for an agreed approach (Narasimhalu & Christodoulakis, 1991; Blattner, 1994); on current skills shortages within the field, and on the fact that both of these disciplines are in a transition phase (Richmond, 1989; Faison Smith, 1995; Muhlhauser, 1996a).

Unfortunately, there is no evidence that such consensus extends to fundamental issues within the field. Reeve’s comments that current development efforts are guided by ‘habit, intuition, prejudice, guesswork, or politics’ would appear to typify the current situation (Reeves, 1993, p 79). Other research (Latchem et al, 1993, p 34) has also expressed concern over the ‘current emergent state’ and difficulties in reaching ‘a mature stage of development’ in multimedia. More recently, writers such as Dospisil and Polgar (1994), Fisher (1994), Garzotto et al (1995), Muhlhauser and Effelsberg (1996), West and Norris (1997), and Powell (1998) have commented on multimedia’s ‘craft’ and ‘cottage industry’ status, expressing concern regarding the immature state of tools, methods and standards for multimedia development. Morris and Finkelstein (1996, p 98) also express concern over the difficulties involved in obtaining effective ‘%integration of divergent approaches%’ in multimedia development. They conclude ‘%that any useful model of the production process, and any practical guidance, must embrace this diversity.

Thus, the purpose of this research is to contribute to the ongoing development of the multimedia field by examining the knowledge of two key groups of participants. It is proposed that an examination of the underlying paradigms offers a sensible mechanism through which to contribute to methodology development. This is made increasingly important given the concern over the current state of multimedia design (Romiszowski, 1993; Fraisse et al, 1996; Harada et al, 1996), particularly in relation to the development of large, complex hypermedia applications (Lange, 1994; Garzotto et al, 1995; Ginige et al, 1995; Mere & Rossi, 1996), and also internet-based applications (Shneiderman, 1997; Thimbleby, 1997; Powell, 1998).

Coupled with this concern for the current state of multimedia design, many authors (Narasimhalu & Christodoulakis, 1991; Alty, 1993; Blattner, 1994; Neophytou et al, 1995; Streitz, 1995b; Muhlhauser, 1996b) also express concern about the current state (or lack) of multimedia methodologies. As the field progresses there will be an increasing need for methodologies specifically geared towards multimedia design which exploit all of the talents of the disciplines involved. (Latchem et al, 1993; Blattner, 1994; Bieber & Vitali, 1997).

The next section of this paper explains the importance of Kuhn’s concepts and illustrates how they have been employed throughout the study. Following this, issues relating to research methodology are briefly examined before the results of textbook and interview analyses are presented. Based on these results, the authors discuss how the paradigms can contribute to methodology development, specifically method evaluation and integration, in multimedia. Finally, possible directions for future research are presented.

## Kuhn’s concepts of ‘paradigm’ and ‘community’

Since the mid 1980s information systems (IS) researchers have demonstrated increased concern over the philosophical underpinnings of their field(s). This concern is evidenced, for example, by the efforts of Van Gigch and Pipino (1986). Further efforts to examine the epistemology of management information systems (MIS) are recorded in Farhoomand (1987) and Banville and Landry (1989). More recently, other researchers (Iivari, 1991; Wernick, 1996; Mingers, 1997; Winder et al,

1997; Iivari et al, 1998) have also attempted to explain the philosophical aspects of the wider IS field.

Fitzgerald (cited in Winder et al, 1997, p 9) proposes that one of the major reasons for this growing interest in philosophical issues stems from the fact that IS development is not getting any easier—‘Thus many people are looking to philosophy%to provide alternative thoughts and insights on contemporary issues of information systems’. It has previously been argued (Gallagher & Webb, 1997; Gallagher, 1999) that a parallel situation exists in multimedia—where development is also not getting any easier. Recent concern over designing multimedia applications and the current state of multimedia methodology development serve to make these difficulties more acute. Several authors (Paine & McAra, 1993; Romiszowski, 1993; Grosky, 1994; Benyon et al, 1997) have commented on the lack of a sound philosophical/epistemological basis within multimedia.

To help combat this situation the authors propose that Kuhn’s (1970) concepts of ‘community’ and ‘paradigm provide a valuable framework through which to conceptualise and compare the knowledge base and contributions of software engineers and graphic designers. A ‘scientific community’ is a distinct group of people composed of the practitioners of a scientific speciality who have undergone a similar education and have drawn the same lessons as a result (Kuhn, 1970, p 177). Typically, a community is characterised by the unified acceptance of a belief system framework (the paradigm) that guides the members in doing what they do. The institutionalisation of a ‘school of thought’ or ‘scientific community’, (Kuhn appears to use these two terms interchangeably) is evidenced by the existence of scientific journals, conferences, textbooks and professional societies. Informal and formal communication networks and citations are other means through which community structure can be identified. Interviews with members of these communities can also provide insights into community structure as well as the nature of the accepted paradigm.

For Kuhn (1977), a paradigm represents a belief system that encompasses those concepts, models, assumptions and metaphysical principles that are shared within each community. Specifically a paradigm (referred to as a disciplinary matrix, or DM) may be defined as:

symbolic generalisations (such as mathematical formulae or scientific laws which are widely used by group members and readily justified by them).

belief in heuristic and metaphysical models (preferred and permissible analogies and metaphors used to explain phenomena).

values which are more widely shared among different communities than symbolic generalisations and models. (for example a commitment to quantitative over qualitative methods).

I exemplars or ‘paradigm as shared example’, (examples of how theories are applied in practice; essential for group cohesiveness).

The researchers consider software engineering and graphic design as two communities within multimedia design and propose that the identification of their respective DMs can help inform theory and practice. The rationale for performing such a paradigmatic analysis is based on the work of Van Gigch and Pipino (1986), who employed Kuhn’s concept of paradigm to examine the epistemology and theory in the IS field. They argued that a discipline must articulate its paradigm(s) in order to progress. It is proposed that a similar situation exists in multimedia. Specifically, the reasons for undertaking a paradigmatic analysis are:

it facilitates the understanding of many perspectives associated with multimedia development,

I it generates viable integrated inter-disciplinary research directions,

I it provides a basis for the comparative analysis, and,

it helps to formulate a firm foundation for design (based on Van Gigch & Pipino, 1986, p 71).

Phase two introduced Kuhn’s concept of ‘paradigm’ as disciplinary matrix (DM) and involved the examination of four software engineering and eight graphic design textbooks (listed in Appendices). Since Wernick (1996) has effectively established the existence of a DM for software engineering this research could build on the results of his work. Therefore, only four software engineering texts are examined as part of this study. However, we are aware of no previous application of Kuhnian theory to the discipline of graphic design. For this reason, the graphic design trawl examined eight textbooks (as originally proposed by Wernick, 1996). The selection of textbooks is based on the repeated suggestions of theorists and practitioners in each discipline, and also on examinations of citations in published works such as theses, textbooks and journals.

Kuhn (1970, p 10) is clear about the important role of textbooks in the development of a discipline—‘textbooks expound the body of accepted theory’. It is through the study of textbooks that the scientist prepares for, and finally gains, acceptance to a particular school of thought. (Kuhn, 1970, p 177; 1977, p 296). Similarly, for a researcher to understand the knowledge of software engineers and graphic designers, the textbooks must be examined as part of the paradigmatic analysis. Naturally, this phase is based on the premise that the approaches to multimedia design are based on traditional theory and educational experiences (Faison Smith, 1995; Muhlhauser, 1996a).

The third phase—the main interview study—was carried out in an effort to further validate the preliminary paradigms that resulted from the textbook analyses, and is based on 26 interviews with respondents from 15 different organizations. In this way, it is a much more focused and detailed examination of the attempts of software engineers and graphic designers to develop multimedia. In this manner it provides more insight into the important issues faced by software engineers and graphic designers engaged in multimedia design.

## Results of the analyses

For the purposes of brevity the results of the textbook and interview analyses have been integrated and are presented together. The objective is to clarify the nature of the respective paradigms and to begin the process of determining how this information can be useful to theorists and practitioners from both disciplines who are involved in multimedia design. ## The software engineering paradigm:

In terms of symbolic generalisations (ie, ‘laws’ and ‘formulae’), the software engineering paradigm includes references to ‘Symbols that form components of notations (for example, DFDs, flowcharts, etc)’. This is hardly surprising since much of software engineering theory and education refers to (diagrammatic and textual) notations that (hopefully) assist in the communication of design ideas. The beliefs section of the paradigm concentrates on the nature of the software engineering ‘design space’, and how software engineers should best approach designing. For example, the software engineering beliefs include (the usual) references to issues such as the need for compromise and the value of abstraction and decomposition in coping with large, complex problems. Software engineering values meanwhile provide references to standards by which software engineers should measure their design efforts. Examples include aspirations towards consistency of design notation and approach, efficiency (of both process and product), and traceability of design decisions. Finally, the exemplars section of the paradigm refers to well-established process models and the application of such things as Miller’s Law (7 + or − 2) when designing. Further examples of each type of element may be found in the Appendices to this paper.

## The graphic design paradigm:

References to the use of various grid systems as possible ‘formulae’ to facilitate the construction of graphic design artefacts appears in the symbolic generalisations section of the paradigm. Graphic designers apply grids in order to assist them divide up the available real estate (a sheet of A4 paper, or a computer screen). The graphic design paradigm is also composed of beliefs about the nature of graphic design and how best to approach such problems. The value of experience is emphasised along with the firm belief that ‘Graphic design is not merely a matter of aesthetics’. Furthermore, this paradigm contradicts the perception of graphic design as some form of adhoc, mystical process stressing that one must adopt a methodical and logical approach to design. In terms of values, graphic designers strive to achieve clarity in the resulting designed solution, harmony among the various graphical elements, and simplicity. Further examples of each type of element may be found in the Appendices to this paper.

## Common elements across both paradigms:

Not surprisingly a certain percentage of elements are common to both communities. Of the total number of distinct elements identified in this study (179) 67 are common to both. This represents 37% of all 179 elements, 54% of the 125 software engineering elements, and 55% of the 121 graphic design elements. While the manifestation of their respective design processes may differ—software engineers typically (claim to) use standard notations (for example, DFD’s) and models; graphic designers rely on sketches, thumbnails and storyboards—they both experience a transition from a highlevel view to a lower-level view of design. The software engineers’ lower-level is implemented in code and the graphic designers’ is implemented in icons, images and colours. However, the researchers acknowledge that many designers adopt a variety of strategies—including getting stuck into the detail straight away.

The presence of commonalities may appear to detract from any evidence supporting the existence of two distinct communities and associated paradigms. However, the level of commonality does not mean that it is possible to take a designer from one field and ‘drop’ them into another design context and expect them to immediately start designing. The existence of common design elements may be taken for granted (especially in practice), and is hardly surprising given that design activity can be considered as a spectrum that ranges from artistic/visual design to technological/engineering design (Margolin, 1989; Potter, 1989). This notion of a design spectrum implies a degree of gradual variation and overlap. Wernick (1996), in his efforts to articulate a software engineering paradigm, also noted a hierarchical relationship as well as common and dividing paradigm elements.

Having established (and accepted) that there is a degree of commonality across paradigms, the next (and more) important question relates to the fundamental differences between the paradigms. This is particularly important since it is the differences that make collaborative efforts problematic for practitioners. Ignoring the 67 common elements leaves 58 non-common software engineering elements and 54 non-common graphic design elements. On examining the respective non-common elements (NCEs) it appears that there are fifteen elements within each paradigm (paradigm-specific elements (PSEs)) that attained a relatively high (at least 50%) level of consensus within the respective paradigms (see Appendix). Additional statistical analysis of the NCEs within each community supports this argument (for a full discussion, see Gallagher, 1999, pp 194–199). ## Informing methodological development in multimedia

Jayaratna (1994, p 42), proposes the establishment of a conceptual framework, or meta-method, that facilitates the clarification, comparison, categorisation, evaluation and/or integration of many IS methods. Thus, the researchers’ goal is to examine the paradigms in order to develop a meta-method specific to multimedia. It is therefore proposed that such a meta-method be founded upon two fundamental assumptions that have emerged as a result of examining the paradigms. A metamethod should:

incorporate a common core that both communities can naturally relate to, and,

simultaneously embrace the inherent diversity (and strength) of the SEDM and GDDM.

Morris and Finkelstein’s (1996, p 98) stipulation that any model of the development process, and any other practical advice, should embrace the inherent diversity of the ‘divergent approaches’ in multimedia production and offers support for this approach. ## Method evaluation

Sixty-seven common elements have been identified which suggest that, at a certain level, software engineers and graphic designers share similarities in design approach. The researchers propose that these common elements can act as the basis of common criteria by which to judge methods. A multimedia method engineer (MME) could use the common elements as a list of common ‘method requirements’ that both communities deem mandatory. Thus, when evaluating methods an MME will look for these desirable features in a particular method or set of methods.

Kitchenham (1996), Kitchenham and Jones (1997), and Kitchenham and Pickard’s (1998) discussion of the DESMET meta-method for method evaluation lends support to the above proposal. This meta-method ‘%is concerned with the evaluation of methods or tools within a particular organization%[and] is intended to help an evaluator%to plan and execute an evaluation exercise that is unbiased and reliable’ (Kitchenham, 1996, p 11). Kitchenham claims that there are several different evaluation methods—depending on factors such as time available, previous experience of methods and evaluations, associated cost, and whether the evaluator seeks qualitative or quantitative data.

Of particular relevance to this discussion is a specific form of evaluation known as ‘feature analysis’. The simplest version of a feature analysis provides a list of ‘yes/no’ responses to the existence of a particular property. Thus, one examines particular methods in order to determine whether they possess specific properties, or provide support for particular features. Having evaluated several methods, an evaluator (the MME) could then count the number of ticks that each candidate method received. The scoring mechanism employed throughout the evaluation could range from a simple yes/no to some form of numerical ranking system (Kitchenham & Jones, 1997, p 14).

So, how can the common elements influence such an evaluation? The researchers propose that the common elements represent ‘features’ (or method requirements) that are acceptable to members of both communities. Thus, the common elements help to solve the problem of identifying which features to examine. If one is attempting to find a method that supports both communities then it is logical to choose a method that incorporates the common elements. Feature analysis, therefore, provides the mechanism to determine which method offers greatest support for these mandatory features.

Just as the common elements suggest desirable features, the NCEs, particularly the PSEs, can be used to derive features/method requirements that are specific to each community. When evaluating methods an MME could begin by examining methods to determine whether, and to what extent, they support the common features. Having completed this stage of the evaluation, the MME could then examine the short-listed methods vis-a\`-vis the paradigm-specific features (particularly the paradigm-specific elements). Such an evaluation obviously depends on factors other than the elements contained in the paradigms. The priority attached to a particular paradigm (and thus features) could depend on the culture of the organization and/or on the type of application. The organization’s previous experiences in developing multimedia may also influence the decision of the evaluator.

Thus, it is proposed that the common and non-common elements of the paradigms can assist method evaluation by suggesting (a) features of a method acceptable to both communities, and (b) paradigm-specific features. The former helps short-list methods while the latter adds a further level of granularity to the evaluation depending on the culture of the organization, the nature of the proposed application and the organization’s previous experiences. It should be noted that the evaluation might apply only to specific aspects of methods (for example, notations) as well as to entire methods.

## Method integration

Two generic approaches are available to a method engineer who wishes to combine several methods in order to overcome their inherent weaknesses. These include the development of:

I a meta-model which acts as a bridging mechanism between two methods, or

a method-base that consists of method fragments that are combined to produce a single method for a particular context.

The researchers believe that the paradigms can contribute to the development of each of these options, and thus facilitate members of both communities in combining their respective talents (and methods).

Saeki (1998, p 925) proposes the development of a meta-model as a useful mechanism through which to ‘%provide a framework within which individual methods can be integrated in a coherent manner’. This can be achieved by focusing on the document products produced by individual methods. In this manner, a method engineer attempts to develop ‘%a common underlying model for the individual methods’. Saeki proposes that in developing a common model the method engineer should concentrate on those concepts that appear in the various modelling notations used in different methods. Following Saeki, it is proposed that it would be beneficial to develop a meta-model to act as a bridge between members of these two paradigms. This meta-model would have to be based on the common elements since they indicate those concepts and working practices that are acceptable to both communities.

What might such a meta-model look like? The benefits of storyboarding (as a form of graphical design notation) have been widely acknowledged by interviewees and in the literature (Fisher, 1994; Morris & Finkelstein, 1996; Mallon, 1998). Flowcharting permits designers a ‘preview of the interactivity’, while storyboarding acts as a structural blueprint for the proposed product. Many of the interviewees (software engineers and graphic designers) claimed that a storyboard is often the first design document to be produced. According to Fisher (1994, p 268), storyboards are refined as design and development proceeds and thus offer an excellent means of tracking micro and macro design issues. For this reason the researcher proposes that storyboarding be considered as the common denominator across both communities.

As an alternative to developing such a meta-model a method engineer may decide to construct a situationspecific method from a variety of methods held in a method base. This approach involves disaggregating several methods, evaluating them and then combining various method fragments depending on the prevailing circumstances (Brinkkemper, 1996; Nuseibeh et al, 1996; Punter & Lemmen, 1996). In constructing the situation-specific method the method engineer may chose to:

I directly integrate common features of several methods;

extend a main method with features (techniques, notations) taken from supplementary methods, or, restrict the main method by overriding some of its features with features from supplementary methods. (Nuseibeh et al, 1996, p 271).

Nuseibeh et al’s (1996, p 267) approach offers support to the researchers’ proposal that the paradigms represent a suitable basis for method integration. Nuseibeh et al (1996) consider software development to be a ‘multiple perspectives problem’ with different participants holding different views on the problem situation. Thus, ‘%these different participants deploy different notations and development strategies that are best suited for elaborating their respective areas of concern’. Each perspective is encapsulated within a given ‘ViewPoint’ which, the researchers propose, resembles Kuhn’s (1970) concept of a paradigm. Specifically, a ViewPoint embraces ‘%representation knowledge, development process knowledge and specification knowledge about a system and its domain’.

Within this ‘ViewPoints framework’ method integration involves defining ViewPoints and then combining several ViewPoints in order to construct a composite method for a particular situation. The paradigms identified as part of this research programme can assist in the process of integrating several method fragments because they effectively provide the ViewPoints that Nuseibeh et al propose. Each listing of the NCEs, particularly the paradigm-specific elements, indicate the respective ‘ViewPoints’ of both communities. Thus, for a particular development context, an MME can examine the paradigms to ascertain particular ‘method requirements’ and subsequently integrate several method fragments (techniques, notations) in order to fulfil the demands of the current project.

## Conclusion

This paper argues that software engineering and graphic design represent two influential contributions in multimedia systems design. The authors propose that Kuhn’s (1970) concepts of ‘community’ and ‘paradigm’ provides a useful mechanism through which to examine the knowledge and approaches of these two perspectives on design. Not only have both paradigms been outlined and discussed, the authors have also demonstrated how the paradigms can assist multimedia methodological development. Specifically, this paper has discussed how the paradigms may be employed in order to facilitate method evaluation and integration. Future research will concentrate on the following (parallel) strategies:

further validation and articulation of the paradigms through statistical techniques, experiments/focus groups (Vertelney et al, 1991) and in-depth casestudy research, and,

further development and testing of the meta-method for method evaluation and integration.

Seamus Gallagher is a Lecturer in Computer Science within the Faculty of Informatics, University of Ulster at Jordanstown. His research interests include multimedia systems design, electronic commerce, knowledge management, and software project management.
