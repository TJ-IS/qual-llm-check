# ‘Requirements-Uncertainty’: Is it best formulated as a latent, aggregate or profile construct?

T Moynihan

School of Computer Applications, Dublin City University, Dublin 9, Ireland

This paper identifies the strategies that experienced information systems (IS) project managers say they use to cope with requirements-uncertainty (RU) when managing development projects for external clients. It is shown that project managers (PMs) claim to use different strategies for coping with the different dimensions of requirements-uncertainty, as this notion has been formulated in IS literature. Based on this finding, it is proposed that perhaps requirements-uncertainty should be formulated as a profile construct, and not as a latent or aggregate construct, if it is to have pragmatic validity as a guide to action for project managers.

## Introduction

The concept of requirements-uncertainty (RU) has received a lot of attention in the information systems (IS) project management literature (see, for example, Davis (1982), Mathiassen & Stage (1990), Fazlollahi & Tanniru (1991) and Saarinen & Vepsalainen (1993)). The most frequently proffered piece of advice in this literature is that, as the level of RU increases, one should move away from the traditional waterfall life cycle model and towards more ‘experimental’ approaches such as evolutionary-delivery and/or prototyping.

The notion that as RU increases, one should move away from the waterfall model and move towards more experimental/evolutionary approaches seems to be eminently logical. But there is empirical evidence that this advice is not always followed. For example, Galleta and El Louadi (1995) collected data on 67 completed IS development projects across the United States. For each project, they chose one user and one systems analyst. From the user and the analyst, they gathered perceptions of the level of RU in the project. From each analyst, they tried to identify the extent to which the project employed each of Davis’s (1982) four generic requirements-determination strategies (ie, ‘asking users’, ‘deriving requirements from an existing system’, ‘synthesising requirements from user activities’, and ‘discovering requirements through experimenting, eg, prototyping’). They found many projects in which the requirementsdetermination strategy most relied on was not congruent with the perceived level of project RU. Empirical studies have identified other examples of apparent discrepancies between prescriptive theory involving RU, and IS project managers’ (PMs) practice (see, for example, Naumann et al (1983) and El Louadi et al (1999)).

Attempts have been made to explain observed instances of differences between theory and practice. For example, Mathiassen and Stage (1990) identify some of the reasons why, although an evolutionary or prototyping approach is indicated, managers may not adopt it. Examples of some reasons are: resistance by the client to an apparently ‘experimental’ approach, resistance by users to the increased user-involvement required, and IS developers’ worry of ending up with a poorly structured solution. Redmill (1997) identifies some of the problems created by evolutionary delivery. For example, users are tempted to treat requirements-specification as an unimportant exercise, so convergence on the ‘right’ solution over time becomes an extended exercise of trial and error; a system which is initially intended to support some clearly defined business objectives may eventually meet none of these objectives; budgeting and scheduling problems; configuration management problems and testing/regression-testing problems. Baskerville and Stage (1996) identify some of the problems which can arise with prototyping, even when it is the indicated approach, for example, the unrealistic expectations which can be created by early prototypes, and the budgeting and scheduling difficulties which can arise with an ‘open-ended’ approach.

In this paper, it is argued that there may be a further, at least partial, explanation for instances of the apparent failure of managers to follow the advice in the RU related literature. This explanation relates to the manner in which the concept of RU has been formulated. In the IS and software engineering literature, RU is most frequently defined as an aggregation of a number of ‘sources’ of potential uncertainty. Each potential uncertainty ‘source’ is given an uncertainty rating, and these ratings are then ‘summed-up’ to give an overall project uncertainty rating. However, from the perspective of a PM trying to cope with RU, this ‘addition’ may not yield a meaningful result. This would be the case, for example, if each individual uncertainty ‘source’ (or configuration of ‘sources’), required a different coping strategy. In this instance, the ‘total’ of the ratings across the individual uncertainty ‘sources’ would have less value as a guide to action than would the profile across the individual ratings. This proposition is explored in this paper.

The paper next describes the results of field-work which identifies the strategies that experienced PMs say they would use to cope with a range of project risk factors, including ‘sources’ of project RU as proposed in the IS literature. It is shown that PMs say they would use different strategies to cope with different ‘sources’ of project RU.

The paper then introduces a taxonomy of multidimensional constructs developed by Law et al, (1998). These authors categorise multidimensional constructs into three classes: latent constructs, aggregate constructs and profile constructs. This taxonomy is then applied to some of the main formulations of RU in the literature. It is shown that IS contingency theorists have, in the main, formulated project RU as either a latent construct or as an aggregate construct. Based on the empirical findings of the present study, it is then suggested that RU should be formulated as a profile construct. In other words, it is suggested that as researchers, we may have overabstracted the notion of project RU.

## Method

## An earlier study

In an earlier study, the author identified the situational variables which a sample of experienced PMs claimed they took into account when planning and managing bespoke IS development projects for new, external clients (Moynihan, 1996). The technique of personal construct elicitation was used for that purpose (Bannister & Fransella, 1989). A personal construct is a bi-polar distinction which a person uses when contrasting different people, objects, situations, and so on. For example, for the present writer, an important distinction between dogs is the likelihood that a dog will bite me! So, when comparing dogs, or thinking about a particular dog, the writer is likely to think in terms of ‘will he/won’t he bite me?’. People have multiple sets of many interacting constructs to help them to make sense of the world. The task of identifying the set of constructs used by a person in a particular context is called personal construct elicitation.

In that study, each PM was asked to make a list of the systems development projects he/she had worked on as PM over the past year or two. The writer then selected three projects randomly from the list and asked the PM, ‘In what important ways are any two of these three projects the same, but different from the third, in terms of important situational factors you had to think about when planning the project?

The PM was then asked to repeat this task with different triads of projects, until no new situational constructs were being elicited. Using this process, 113 different constructs were elicited from the PMs. The constructs elicited reflected most of the situational variables which have been identified as project ‘risk-drivers’ by IS and software project risk researchers, and reflected some additional situational variables not previously identified in the literature. As described in Moynihan (1996), content analysis was used to build a classification scheme for the constructs. Twenty classes were identified, of which three examples are:

Constructs relating to the client’s knowledge/ understanding/clarity regarding the requirements to be met or the problem to be solved.

Constructs relating to the existence/competence/ seniority/commitment of the project patron/owner.

Constructs relating to the level of IT competence and experience of the customer and the users.

## The present study

For the present study, 34 constructs were selected from the full set of 113 constructs. The subset was chosen with a view to including at least one construct from each of the 20 classes, and at least two constructs from the most highly-populated classes. The constructs selected are shown in Table 1. Table 1 includes constructs that seem to reflect all of the elements of RU, as this notion has been formulated in the literature. These RU constructs are shown in italics. The purpose of Table 1 will become clear later.

Five hypothetical project profiles were then constructed. A project profile consisted of 34 poles, one drawn randomly from each of the 34 chosen constructs. To construct a project profile, random numbers were used to decide whether the left-hand pole or the right-hand pole of the construct should appear in the profile. Within any one profile, a fixed probability of selecting the right-hand pole (in the case of most constructs, the ‘risky’ pole) was applied. But this probability was systematically varied across the profiles. In this way, the writer generated large variation in apparent ‘riskiness’ across the five hypothetical projects. In relation to this procedure, the questions could be asked ‘Why build more than one project profile? And why vary the level of apparent ‘riskiness’ across the profiles?’ The answers to these questions relate to the potential generalisability of the study’s findings. Had just a single project profile been used for the present study, or had multiple profiles been used but all generated using the same probability of selection of the right-hand pole, it could have been argued that the range of strategies elicited from the PMs might have been constrained by the choice of the particular profile(s) and probability used.

The subjects were 20 IS developers, all located in Ireland. Many of these had participated in the earlier study. All managed custom-built, software-intensive IS development projects for external clients. All 20 had at least six years experience of running projects. All were in the range 30–60 years of age. Almost all were owners or directors of their companies. The numbers of developers employed in their companies ranged from two to twenty persons. The scale of the projects they typically managed fell within the ranges:

I Project duration: 2–18 months.

I Project effort: 2–36 man-months.

I Project team size: 1–6 people.

All worked with mainstream, current technology. All were in the business of providing bespoke IS ‘solutions’ to commercial clients.

The writer spent about one hour with each PM. What follows is a condensed description of the procedure used. It was explained to the PM that the purpose of the exercise was to learn more about the ‘recipes’ that experienced PMs use to cope with project risk, and that a hypothetical project would be used to help with this task. The permission of the PM to tape-record the session was then sought. In all cases, this was forthcoming. The writer then gave the PM a set of 34 index cards, one for each of the 34 constructs. The top, visible face, of the card showed both poles of the construct. The bottom face, which the writer asked the PM not to look at, showed the pole that belonged to the hypothetical project profile which had been randomly chosen for that PM.

The PM was asked to sort the constructs into three piles. The first pile to contain constructs that, depending on which of the two poles applied, could make a very big difference to the ‘riskiness’ of a project. The other two piles to contain constructs that could make a big difference and some/little/no difference to ‘riskiness’, respectively.

Then the writer asked the PM to focus on the very big difference pile. The PM was asked to start by choosing the constructs, singly or in combination, which could make the biggest difference to project risk. The writer then explored with the PM why he/she had chosen as he/she had done. The PM was then asked to turn over the card(s) to reveal the pole(s) which applied to the hypothetical project, and to give a verbal reaction to this piece of information. The writer then explored the strategies, tactics, tricks, and so on, that the PM would use to cope with that pole. This procedure was repeated until all of the very big difference constructs had been turned over. At intervals, the PM was asked to sum up his/her feelings about the unfolding project, and to describe the strategies/tactics/‘ploys’ he/she would advise a PM faced with managing that project to use. The session was concluded by asking the PM to rate the likely outcome of the project on a set of scales.

## Results

## Putting requirements uncertainty into the larger context

In Table 1, for each construct, are shown the numbers of PMs placing that construct in the very big difference, the big difference and the little/no/difference piles. The constructs are shown in decreasing order of the numbers of PMs placing that construct in the very big difference pile. The constructs which seem to relate to RU, as formulated in the literature, have been italicised.

From Table 1, it seems that the project RU constructs do not ‘top the poll’ in terms of risk generation. Also, the RU constructs are not all seen to be of equal importance. Of the RU related constructs, the presence of hidden agendas and disagreement are seen by PMs to be by far the biggest risk generators. Application complexity, the client’s ability to define their ‘problem’, and the level of users’ previous computer experience are seen by PMs to be relatively low risk generators.

## Espoused strategies

The phrases employed by the PMs to describe the strategies they espoused for coping with each of the ‘risky’ poles in their project scenarios, were extracted from the interview transcripts. Table 2 contains a 50% random sample of the phrases used in reference to each of the nine RU related ‘risky’ poles. Apart from some minor editing, these phrases are verbatim.

Examination of the complete set of phrases extracted from the interview transcripts, and of the poles or combinations of poles which formed the context in which each phrase was used, suggests that:

1. PMs emphasise different strategies, or combinations of strategies, for coping with the different RU related issues. For example, by far the most frequent response to problems like hidden agendas and disagreement, is to ‘go bureaucratic’ (insist on sign-offs, keep documentation trails, insist on time/materials contracts etc.). For developer-located RU, such as a lack of application experience or industry knowledge, PMs emphasise strategies aimed at getting their team members ‘up to speed’, ideally at the client’s expense (but the PM ‘taking the hit’ if unavoidable, and if he/she views it as a strategic investment), or strategies for acquiring the needed knowledge through subcontracting or recruitment in some shape or form. ‘Ordinary’ RU on the part of the client and the users is not seen to be a big issue. Such client-located RU is seen to be ‘par for the course’, and a matter to be resolved through the regular IS development process.

2. PMs seem to pay at least as much attention to the presence of significant configurations of poles, as they do to the presence of individual poles. Many of the configurations of poles which were identified as being particularly ‘nasty’ include both RU-related poles and non RU-related poles. Some examples of two-pole combinations described as being especially ‘nasty’ and hard to manage are:

People don’t really want it + Major change to customer’s workflow

Unfamiliar languages/tools + Tight customer-imposed timescales

The new system must be adaptable + The application is new to us

Unrealistic expectations + The customer’s PM lacks time/skill/authority

They disagree amongst themselves% + Major change to customer’s workflow

The new system is mission critical + Nobody wants to own the project

3. Most of the PMs referred to the need to validate requirements with users by such means as (their terms):

Prototypes, mock-ups, simulations, screen paintings and dummy printouts. The need for prototyping, in some shape or form, was believed to be most needed when one or more of the following poles applied: The customer has unrealistic expectations; the new system is mission-critical; the system must go right first time; major change to customer’s workflow and inexperienced users.

4. The terms incremental delivery and evolutionary development were not used at all by the PMs. But (presumably) equivalent terms were frequently used:

Let’s get this bit done and in place first, then we’ll see what’s next. Put in the minimum requirements first. Do the easy things in phase 1, then see where we’ve got to. What’s ‘good enough’ is what we should do first. Break it down into mini-projects.

The need for an incremental/evolutionary approach, in whatever terms, was felt to be most needed when one or more of the following poles applied:

The customer has unrealistic expectations; Major change to customer’s workflow%; The new system is mission-critical; Unfamiliar languages/tools; The application is complex.

5. As partially reflected in point (1) above, it seems that the RU-related poles fall into two categories. The first category includes the ‘risky’ poles alluding to hidden agendas and disagreement. Table 1 suggests that PMs see these to be relatively big risk-drivers. These poles are mainly addressed by strategies which have the goal of self-protection (of the PM and of his/her organization). The second category of ‘risky’ RU-related poles are those alluding to users not knowing what they want, developers having little or no experience of the industry or the application, and such like. Table 1 suggests that PMs do not see these to be amongst the big risk-drivers. The ‘risky’ poles in this category are addressed mainly by strategies involving experimentation, education, consultation and other positive problem-solving approaches.

6. As alluded to in point (1), PMs seem to distinguish between client-located RU and developer-located RU. For developer-located RU, PMs emphasise strategies aimed at rapidly getting their people ‘up the learning curve’ or strategies for acquiring the needed knowledge/skills externally. ‘Non-political’ clientlocated RU is a matter to be resolved through user-participation and user education/training.

## Some formulations of RU in the IS literature

This section begins with a presentation of a taxonomy of multidimensional constructs developed by Law et al (1998). These authors categorise multidimensional constructs into three classes: latent constructs, aggregate constructs and profile constructs. This taxonomy is then applied to some of the main definitions of requirementsuncertainty in the IS literature. It is shown that these definitions formulate requirements-uncertainty as either a latent construct or an aggregate construct. The section concludes with the conjecture that RU should be formulated as a profile construct.

## Law, Wang and Mobley’s taxonomy

In the taxonomy of Law et al, (1998), an aggregate multidimensional construct is defined to be an algebraic composition of its dimensions. The value of the multidimensional construct is defined to be a linear or nonlinear function of its dimensions. It is not assumed that the multidimensional construct has a ‘real’ existence that is independent of the dimensions used to define it. In other words, there is no assumption that the construct is a latent variable which underlies, and which ‘causes’ the component variables. One example of a multidimensional construct might be an economic indicator which is computed from a set of more detailed economic statistics.

A profile multidimensional construct differs from an aggregate multidimensional construct only in so far as no attempt is made to compute values of the construct from its dimensions. Presumably because the result of such computations would have no theoretical meaning. The construct is interpreted by profiling the levels of its dimensions. Law et al give as an example the Myers-Briggs personality type indicator, in which four dimensions are used to ‘profile’ the personality of an individual: introversion-extroversion, sensing-intuition, thinking-feeling and judging-perceiving. No attempt is made to compute some higher order concept of personality from these dimensions.

In contrast, a latent multidimensional construct is assumed to have an existence independent of its dimensions. The particular dimensions chosen to ‘represent the construct are viewed as being merely a subset drawn from a possibly infinite number of dimensions which might have been chosen to represent the concept. It is assumed that there is a causal link from the construct to each of its dimensions. So that, by measuring the dimensions, one is indirectly measuring the latent, underlying construct.

## Some major formulations of RU

Davis (1982) is one of the earliest writers on the notion of requirements-uncertainty. He introduced the concept of overall requirements process uncertainty. This concept is operationalised as the ‘sum’ of three variables: the existence/stability of a set of usable requirements, the level of ability of users to specify requirements and the level of ability of analysts to elicit and evaluate requirements. In turn, these latter three variables are described as being the ‘sum’ of:

uncertainty deriving from the utilising system (for example, the stability of the environment into which the new system is to be embedded, whether the activity the system is to support is structured or unstructured).

uncertainty deriving from the application (for example, its complexity, the number of users, extent of change to structures/tasks).

uncertainty deriving from the users (for example, extent of experience in using computers, level of understanding of the application, politics).

uncertainty deriving from the systems analysts (for example, extent of experience with similar systems, knowledge of the business).

With increasing levels of overall requirements process uncertainty, Davis advocates that one moves from a primary strategy of asking users what they want, through basing the solution on an existing system, through deriving requirements from an analysis of the user-tasks to be supported, to an experimental approach (for example, prototyping). Davis clearly treats RU as an aggregate construct.

Building on Davis (1982), Burns and Dennis (1985) introduced a distinction between requirementsuncertainty and requirements-complexity. They define requirements-uncertainty in terms of:

I the degree of ‘structuredness’ of the user tasks to be supported.

I the degree of understanding the users have about their tasks.

I the degree of experience and training of the system developers.

They defined requirements-complexity in terms of:

I relative project size.

I the number of users.

I the volume of new information required from the system.

I the complexity of this new information.

As does Davis (1982) in relation to his definition of RU, Burns and Dennis aggregate ratings of the individual components of complexity and uncertainty to obtain overall complexity and uncertainty ratings. Thus they clearly treat RU as an aggregate construct.

Stork and Sapienza (1995) introduced the distinction between RU and ‘equivocality.’ They define RU in much the same terms as earlier authors. They define equivocality as being the difference between the level of agreement and understanding between the people involved which is needed to accomplish the goals of the project, and the existing level of agreement and understanding. Equivocality, in their view, is a function of aspects of the project such as the degree of innovativeness of the task, and aspects of the people involved, such as how diverse people are in terms of training and background. To reduce equivocality, these authors recommend that people must interact to communicate their different perspectives (not just factual data) and to resolve their conflicting views. These authors seem to treat both requirements-uncertainty and equivocality as aggregate constructs.

Based on Principle Components Analysis of questionnaire-items completed by project managers, Nidumolu (1996) identified three dimensions of RU:

Requirements instability: the extent of changes in user requirements over the course of the project.

Requirements diversity: the extent to which users differ among themselves in their requirements.

Requirements analysability: the extent to which the process for converting user needs to a set of requirements specifications can be reduced to mechanical steps or objective procedures.

Nidumolu found evidence to suggest that total project RU, defined as being the sum of the project’s scores on these three dimensions, is negatively associated with ultimate project and ‘product’ performance. He also found that the use of appropriate software development standards reduced the negative effects of RU on both these outcome variables. It is not clear whether Nidumolu views his formulation of RU as being a latent or an aggregate construct.

In summary, to the extent that the above literature may be representative of the RU field, it appears that RU has, in the main, been formulated as either an aggregate or as a latent construct.

## Summary and conclusions

The main findings of the present study may be summarised thus:

1. The PMs interviewed espoused different strategies for coping with different components of RU, as this construct has been formulated in the literature.

2. Many of the ‘nastiest’ pole-configurations identified by the PMs include both RU and non-RU poles. In other words, it seems that individual components of RU can interact with non-RU project features to generate high levels of perceived project risk. These configurations were described as being particularly hard to manage.

3. The RU-related ‘risky’ poles used in the present study appear to fall into two categories. The first category includes the poles alluding to hidden agendas and disagreement in the client-organization. The PMs saw these poles to be relatively big risk-drivers. The strategies espoused for addressing these poles had, in the main, the goal of self-protection (of the PM and of his/her organization). The second category of ‘risky’ RU-related poles were those alluding to users not knowing what they want, developers having little or no experience of the industry or the application, and such like. The PMs did not see these to be amongst the big riskdrivers. The ‘risky’ poles in this category were addressed mainly by strategies involving experimentation, education, consultation, and other positive problemsolving approaches.

4. The PMs seemed to distinguish between clientlocated RU and developer-located RU. For developerlocated RU, PMs emphasised strategies aimed at rapidly getting their people ‘ up the learning curve’ or strategies for acquiring the needed knowledge/skills externally. ‘Non-political’ client-located RU was a matter to be resolved mainly through user-participation and user education/training.

The above findings, taken together, suggest that there may be little to be gained, and possibly a lot to be lost, by treating RU as an aggregate or as a latent construct, as presently seems to be the case in the literature. Maybe, as researchers, we have over-abstracted the notion of project RU in our contingency theories for managers. If this conjecture is true, it might at least partially explain the observed instances of failure of IS project managers to heed the advice in the literature. Maybe richer, more situated and more useful RU-related theories would follow from formulating RU as a profile construct.

Some final points on method. This paper has dealt with PMs’ espoused strategies. In other words, with what PMs say they would do in certain circumstances. But obviously, what PMs would actually do may differ from what they say they would do. This is clearly a potential weakness in the method. Also, this study may have understated the range of strategies which PMs espouse. For example, had the writer prepared lengthy checklists of possible strategies, and prompted the PMs from these lists, more strategies might have been elicited. It is safest to assume that, by espousing a strategy, a PM is indicating that the strategy is one to be applied ‘vigorously’ and in a ‘pure’ form. By failing to espouse a strategy, it is safest to assume that, if used at all, the strategy would be applied less vigorously, or in a ‘diluted’ form. It is not safe to assume that it would not have been espoused had the PM been prompted.
