# Perceived critical mass effect on groupware acceptance

H Lou<sup>1</sup>, W Luo<sup>2</sup> and D Strong<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, College of Business, Ohio University, Athens, OH 45701, USA; <sup>2</sup>Department of Management, Worcester Polytechnic Institute, Worcester, MA 01609, USA

Groupware technologies have become an important part of the business computing infrastructure in many organizations, but many groupware applications, especially those requiring significant collaboration and cooperation among users, are still not adequately used. While the successful implementation of groupware depends on many different factors, achieving a ‘critical mass’ of users has been recognised as the key fo groupware acceptance. By extending the technology acceptance model (TAM), this paper advances a groupware acceptance model that incorporates perceived critical mass as an independent variable for predicting groupware acceptance. The model is empirically evaluated using survey data collected from 385 students responding about their perception of Lotus Domino Discussion Databases. The results reveal that perceived critical mass had the largest total effect (direct and indirect) on intention to use groupware. Other relationships postulated in the model were also found to be significant. These findings corroborate the belief that it is essential to create a critical mass of users in the early stages of groupware implementation. A discussion of the implications of these findings for managers and researchers is also presented.

## Introduction

Groupware technologies, such as e-mail, electronic bulletin boards, and group support systems, have become an important part of the business computing infrastructure in many organizations. Managers in organizations that adopt groupware applications hope that such technologies will enhance communication and collaboration among group members and thus improve group performance. While some groupware applications, for example e-mail, have been commonly accepted, many other applications have not been adequately used and their potential benefits are far from being fully realised (Orlikowski, 1993). For example, group support systems are still not widely used in organizations despite over 10 years of research and experimentation (Nunamaker, 1997). Although numerous laboratory and field studies have consistently shown the relevance and positive impact of group support systems on group work, the technology is only employed by a small number of users in a few organizations. In assessing the current directions for group support systems research, Nunamaker (1997) argues that more research is needed in understanding how to increase the rate of diffusion and adoption of the technology.

Over the past two decades, a significant portion of information system (IS) research has been devoted to investigating and understanding factors and implementation processes that affect information technology (IT) acceptance. A number of research models have been proposed to predict technology usage at an individual level and to explain why potential users embrace or ignore a specific technology under various conditions (Davis, 1989; Davis et al, 1989; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990; Lucas et al, 1990; Mathieson, 1991). Few of them, however, were developed specifically to address the acceptance of groupware technologies.

The purpose of this paper is to advance a research model for studying groupware acceptance. The proposed model employs the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989; Davis et al, 1989) as the starting point and incorporates the concept of perceived critical mass as a key determinant for groupware acceptance. TAM is one of the prominent models in predicting technology acceptance and has been widely applied and evaluated. Based on the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Fishbein & Azjen, 1975), TAM postulates that user acceptance of IT is determined by the perceptions of users about the usefulness and the ease of use of IT. The validity of the model and its extensions has been tested in a series of empirical studies. The technologies examined in these studies include e-mail (Davis, 1989; Gefen & Straub, 1997), voice mail (Adams et al, 1992; Chin & Todd, 1995), word processors (Davis et al, 1989; Adams et al, 1992; Chin & Todd, 1995; Chau, 1996), spreadsheets (Mathieson, 1991; Chau, 1996), database programs (Szajna, 1994), and the World-Wide Web (Agarwal & Prasad, 1997).

While groupware technologies such as e-mail and voice mail were used in a number of TAM studies, none of them took into consideration the unique characteristics of groupware technology and its adoption process. Davis (1989) suggested that TAM research should further explore the relationships between other variables and usefulness, ease of use, and technology acceptance. By incorporating technology specific variables into TAM, we can derive better research models with high explanatory power with respect to the specific technology.

In the proposed model, we argue that perceived critical mass is the key independent variable in predicting groupware acceptance and its importance can be explained through existing literature on social movement in sociology, on social norms in social psychology, and on network externality in economics. While additional variables could also have effects on groupware acceptance, the proposed model follows an existing stream of research and is grounded on the theoretical foundation of the social behavioral approach.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. We first review the literature on the TAM model and groupware acceptance. Then, the perceived critical mass construct and its role in groupware acceptance will be discussed and synthesised from existing literature. As a result of the discussion, a research model for groupware acceptance incorporating the perceived critical mass construct is proposed. The research methodology used in this study is described and the results of the empirical study are presented, followed by a discussion of the implications of the study.

## TAM and groupware acceptance

TAM is a model for explaining user acceptance of computer systems (Davis et al, 1989). The theoretical foundation of TAM is the TRA in social psychology, which is a model for studying intended behaviours (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). As shown in Figure 1, the original TAM postulates that actual system usage is a function of a user’s intention to use the system. A user’s intention to use a system is, in turn, determined by both his attitude toward using the system and perceived usefulness of the system. A user’s attitude toward the system is then jointly influenced by perceived usefulness and ease of use. While perceived ease of use is only influenced by external variables such as system features and user support, perceived usefulness is affected by perceived ease of use and external variables. TAM is a general model in the sense that it is not limited to explain user acceptance of a specific computer technology or for a specific user group. The constructs included in TAM are applicable to a wide range of computer technologies.

A number of studies have empirically examined the validity of the model by comparing it with other established theories, for example, Theory of Planned Behavior (Mathieson, 1991). In follow-up studies, many variations of the original TAM have been proposed. One revision is the removal of the attitude construct as a mediating factor between perceived ease of use and behavioural intention in order to simplify the model (Adams et al, 1992; Igbaria, et al, 1995; Chau, 1996; Szajna, 1996). This revision is possible because Davis et al, (1989) found that attitude did not fully mediate the relationship between ease of use and behavioural intention and a direct relationship existed between perceived ease of use and behavioural intention.

The other significant variation is what dependent variables are included in the model. Several studies employed system usage as the model’s dependent variable. In these studies, self-reported measures are often used to collect data on system usage for example (Davis et al, 1989; Adams et al, 1992; Igbaria et al, 1995). Such subjective measures of system use are appealing to researchers because measures of other constructs in TAM are all self-reported. Although self-reported usage is intended as a surrogate for actual system usage, it is not a good indicator of actual system use (Szajna, 1996). Alternatively, behavioural intention has been used as the dependent variable for a number of reasons (Davis, 1989; Chau, 1996). First, TAM postulates that technology acceptance is determined by behavioural intention (Davis, 1989). Second, several studies have established that there is a strong correlation between behavioural intention to use and actual system usage (Davis et al, 1989; Szajna, 1996). Third, the TRA, the theoretical basis of TAM, is concerned with subjective beliefs and intended behaviours, not objective reality (Davis, 1989).

The original TAM has also been revised to incorporate additional variables that are specific to the technology under study. For example, using TAM as the foundation, Igbaria et al (1995) advanced a comprehensive model for studying microcomputer usage. They examined the effects of external variables on perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and microcomputer usage. These microcomputer-related external variables included user characteristics (user training, experience), system characteristics (quality), and organisational support (enduser support, management support). By introducing these external factors into the TAM model, they were able to better explain microcomputer usage behaviour and suggest new ways to increase microcomputer usage.

Groupware is a special class of computer technologies designed to support communication, collaboration, and cooperation among a group of knowledge workers. It covers a variety of technologies, ranging from simple e-mail systems to complex workflow applications. Although the use of some groupware technologies, such as e-mail, has become ubiquitous, organizations have encountered many difficulties in adopting and utilising more sophisticated groupware systems, such as group support systems and Lotus Notes (Nunamaker, 1997; Orlikowski, 1993).

A number of case studies on groupware implementation have suggested that the problems stem from some unique characteristics of the groupware technology. First, groupware is different from personal productivity software with which many end-users are already familiar (Grudin, 1994). Groupware applications are designed to support cooperation and collaboration among a group of users rather than improve productivity of individuals. Therefore, usage and resulting benefits are only achieved if the majority of the users whose work is affected by a groupware application accept and use the system (Grudin, 1994). Otherwise, the application will not only fail to improve group performance but will also create additional communication and coordination barriers. For instance, if a group decides to schedule meetings using the calendar feature provided by Lotus Notes or Microsoft Exchange, most group members have to commit themselves to using the same software package and participate on a regular basis.

Second, most personal productivity software, such as word processors or spreadsheets, is intended to support specific tasks. Designers of such software had certain tasks in mind when they created the application, whether it is creating documents or preparing taxes. Thus, users can quickly see examples of how tasks are accomplished faster and better by using the technology. Groupware technologies, on the other hand, are open-ended and adaptable ITs (Orlikowski & Hofman, 1997). They can be adapted to support a variety of organisational activities in many different ways. It is incumbent upon users to experiment with the technologies and use them to their advantage. This requires users to accept a drastically different technological frame than the one to which they are accustomed (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). Thus, by adopting a groupware application, users must not only commit considerable time and effort to exploring and learning the new technology but they must also accept and adapt to a different technological frame. All these increase the cost of adoption and make groupware acceptance more difficult.

Third, effective employment of groupware applications often requires changes in the incentive mechanisms and the ways in which individuals collaborate within an organization. In studying a Lotus Notes implementation at a large consulting firm, Orlikowski (1993) identified reward systems, work norms, and policies and procedures as the three most important organisational factors that influence users’ perception and utilisation of groupware applications. She observed that, in a consulting firm where reward systems and procedures were set up to foster competition and individual achievement, employees are unwilling to use Lotus Notes to collaborate and share knowledge. Her research suggested that substantial changes in an organization’s work norms and procedures are needed in order for successful implementation of groupware technologies. In another case study, Vandenbosch and Ginzberg (1996) found that groupware applications would not be used to foster cooperation and collaboration unless certain requirements were met. These requirements include the need for collaboration, users’ understanding of groupware, appropriate training, and a collaborative organization culture. Thus, one success factor in achieving user acceptance of groupware is to create an organisational culture and work environment where collaboration is not only encouraged but also rewarded. Even if an organization can foster such a work environment, it may still face resistance to change from different individuals. The use of groupware applications can upset the existing balance of power and redistribute workloads. Not everyone is going to be affected equally.

The above discussion suggests that any research model for groupware acceptance should consider the unique characteristics of groupware. On the other hand, we should also recognise that groupware shares similar qualities with many other computer technologies. Variables and factors that have been shown to impact microcomputer usage, for example, may have an effect on groupware acceptance as well. Therefore, TAM serves a good starting point for developing research models for groupware acceptance.

## Critical mass effect on groupware acceptance

In social science, critical mass refers to ‘the idea that some threshold of participants or actions has to be crossed before a social movement explodes into being (Oliver et al, 1985). This definition suggests that critical mass is the basis for producing collective actions. The acceptance of a groupware application needs the participation and collective actions from all individuals whose work is affected by the technology. Using ‘prisoner’s dilemma’, Markus and Connolly (1990) illustrate that groupware implementation might fail without securing a critical mass of users for the technology. They argue that individuals would act according to their personal interest and have the tendency to freeload in absence of required use and critical mass. Consequently, few people are willing to contribute to the system and it will not be effectively used. Therefore, achieving a ‘critical mass’ of users has been recognised as the key for successful groupware acceptance (Ehrlich, 1987; Markus, 1990; Markus & Connolly, 1990; Grudin, 1994).

While it is very difficult to measure the actual critical mass threshold for any particular groupware technology (Markus, 1990), participants in group work may have a perception of whether a groupware technology has a critical mass of users through interactions with others. This perception, which we term perceived critical mass, may or may not reflect the reality of groupware use within the group. An individual may develop this perception from channels other than direct observation of usage. For instance, a potential adopter may get the impression that a groupware technology is widely used within his/her group because many people are asking questions about it or a number of announcements were made about the technology. In turn, when a potential adopter asks questions about the technology, he/she may give others the perception that he/she is using the technology. However potential adopters get this perception, perceived critical mass will have a significant impact on users’ adoption decisions.

Theories in social psychology, economics, and diffusion of innovations directly or indirectly support the notion that perceived critical mass is a key variable for groupware acceptance and thus its importance needs to be carefully evaluated. For example, theories of conformity in social psychology have suggested that groups with interdependent members often form social norms and these norms in turn influence group members’ perception and behaviour (Turner, 1991). Such influences consist of two distinct processes: informational and normative (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955). Informational influence occurs when a group member regards information from other members as trustworthy evidence about objective reality and alters his/her perception or behaviour based on this information. Normative influence occurs when a group member conforms to the expectation of other group members in order to get acceptance and approval or avoid rejection and hostility.

With the existence of social norms in groups, perceived critical mass can affect groupware acceptance directly through normative influence and indirectly through informational influence. Perceived critical mass creates the perception that most members of the group have adopted the technology. Potential users may feel that it is their obligation to use the technology and failing to do so may bring negative consequences. For instance, if a group member perceives that the majority of the group is using Lotus Notes to track project progress, then he/she may feel obligated to use Notes as well. Otherwise, he/she would be out of the communication loop and possibly be regarded as an outsider by the rest of the group. In this case, perceived critical mass generates peer pressure among group members to adopt Notes.

In informational influence, potential adopters may have witnessed other members using groupware technology to perform various tasks and learned about the features and functionalities of the groupware technology through word of mouth, demonstrations, or hands-on experiments. Such information exchange and the perception that most of their peers are using the technology may lead them to believe that the groupware technology is indeed useful and that it is not very difficult to use. Therefore, perceived critical mass can affect the perception of potential users about the technology’s ease of use and usefulness.

In the economics literature, studies have examined the effects of network externality on technology adoption and diffusion at the industry level (Katz & Shapiro, 1986). Network externality refers to the positive external consumption benefits as a result of a technology use. That is, a user will benefit more from a technology as the total number of users for this technology increases. The presence of network externalities in a technology has two fundamental effects on technology adoption. First, the technology becomes more and more attractive to potential users as more users adopt the technology. Second, users who have adopted the technology have the incentive to enlist more users for the technology.

Since groupware technologies are designed to support group communication and collaboration, network externalities can be found in many features of groupware technologies. For instance, an electronic calendar allows a group member to electronically check the availability of other members, request appointments, and set up group meetings. This feature has little value if only a few group members regularly update their schedules and use the electronic calendar as their primary appointment book. As the number of users increases, the calendar feature becomes more useful. When non-users perceive that many of their peers are using the electronic calendar, they are likely to perceive it as more useful than otherwise. At the same time, current users may be eager to promote the technology by sharing their experience with and offer help to potential adopters. Such unsolicited help from many of their peers may convince a potential user that it would not take a lot of time and effort to learn and use the technology.

Innovation diffusion research suggests that individual adoption decisions are a function of internal and external influences (Bass, 1969; Mahajan et al, 1990). For most technologies, it is often assumed that earlier adopters can greatly influence later adopters, but not vice-versa (Granovette & Soong, 1983). For interactive technologies such as groupware, however, the interdependence between early and later adopters is reciprocal rather than sequential (Markus, 1990). That is, an early adopter can also be influenced by the decisions of later adopters. Early users of groupware technologies may incur high costs and low benefits in the beginning but they expect to obtain future gains when others follow their lead. If early users feel that others are not going to adopt the technology after a certain period, they may decide to reject the previously adopted technology. Therefore, perceived critical mass is also important for users who have already adopted the technology. It can strengthen their views of the technology’s usefulness. As discussed earlier, the intrinsic value of a groupware technology increases and becomes more apparent as more and more users accept the technology. Consequently, more and more functions become available to adopters, which in turn reinforces their opinion about the technology and reaffirms their acceptance decisions.

In summary, theories in social psychology, economics, and diffusion of innovations all support the concept of critical mass as important for individual choices and actions. While they differ on how and why a critical mass is formed, these theories all agree on the crucial role of perceived critical mass in technology acceptance. In this study, we advance a model that incorporates perceived critical mass in the TAM model and examines the effects of perceived critical mass on groupware acceptance.

## The research model

The research model for this study is shown in Figure 2. Following other TAM studies, we simplified the original TAM by omitting the attitudes toward use construct and by focusing on intention to use as the outcome variable, rather than actual use (Chau, 1996). A link between perceived ease of use and intention to use is included as well. As in the original TAM, perceived ease of use is defined as ‘the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort’. Perceived usefulness is defined as ‘the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance’ (Davis, 1989).

The main difference between the current research model and the original TAM is the addition of the perceived critical mass construct. The original TAM acknowledges that there are ‘external variables’ that affect perceived ease of use and usefulness for any particular IT. For groupware, the key external variable is hypothesised to be the perceived critical mass. The perceived critical mass is defined as the degree to which a person believes that most of his or her peers are using the system. Based on the discussion in the previous section, it is hypothesised that perceived critical mass would influence a potential user’s perception of ease of use and usefulness of the system. Perceived critical mass would also directly induce one’s intention to use a system.

## Research methods

## Data collection

To validate the proposed model empirically, we examined the acceptance of a Lotus Domino Discussion Database in an academic environment. Lotus Notes is one of the most widely used groupware products and is often regarded as the industry standard for groupware systems (Holtham, 1994; Vandenbosch & Ginzberg, 1996). It also has been used as an example of groupware systems in a number of academic studies (Orlikowski, 1993; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Vandenbosch & Ginzberg, 1996; Orlikowski & Hofman, 1997). In management information systems (MIS) research, there has been a concern about external validity when empirical studies are conducted in an academic environment with students as subjects. The lack of direct reward system and shortterm working relationships are the primary reasons for the concern. For this particular study, however, we believe students are reasonable surrogates for working professionals for two reasons. First, consistent with previous TAM studies, we are studying the voluntary use of Lotus Notes. So, there is no direct reward associated with the use of the technology. Both students and working professionals may be rewarded indirectly from the technology by performing their tasks well. Second, we are studying the factors leading to possible acceptance of Lotus Notes, not the patterns or the nature of Lotus Notes use. Students and working professionals may use Lotus Notes in very different ways but we believe they face similar conditions when deciding whether to accept the technology.

The study took place at a large midwestern university. As a part of the reengineering effort in the College of Business, many courses were changed from lecturebased classes to problem-based learning. With the problem-based learning approach, students working in groups undertook a series of projects, each of which addressed certain critical aspects of a course’s subject area. As a group, students were responsible for identifying the problems, evaluating various alternatives, and selecting a course of action to be presented to the faculty and corporate representatives. Domino Discussion Database was introduced as a tool for supporting group communication and collaboration.

At the beginning of the quarter, a discussion database was set up for each participating class, within which there were several folders. The class folder could be accessed by the course instructor and all students enrolled in the class. The group folders, on the other hand, could only be accessed by the instructor and students assigned to each group. A MIS faculty member visited participating classes and demonstrated how the discussion database could be used, followed by a training session in which students obtained their user names and passwords for the databases. Students were encouraged to use the databases for their collaboration, but not required. Students were not evaluated on whether they used the databases for their group work. Therefore, the use of the database was voluntary. The discussion databases could be accessed from computer labs, dorms, and virtually anywhere with internet connections.

During the last week of classes, a questionnaire was administrated to collect data on the acceptance of the technology. Sixteen classes across five different courses participated in the study. All subjects were upperdivision full-time business students. Subjects enrolled in multiple participating classes were asked to fill out the questionnaire only once. A total of 423 responses were collected, among which there were some incomplete and some multiple responses. With multiple responses, one response was randomly selected and others were discarded. Excluding multiple and incomplete responses resulted in a final sample of 385.

The questionnaire consisted of 16 items. Most items were adapted from prior studies with minor modifications to reflect the groupware technology used in this study. The perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness constructs were measured using the items adapted from Davis’ study (1989), with minor modifications. In this study, perceived critical mass was defined as the degree to which a person believed that most of his or her peers were using the system. It was operationalised using two items asking subjects to indicate the extent of use of the discussion database by their peers. The intention to use construct was measured using the two items developed by Chau (1996), with modifications to reflect the technology used. Subjects were asked to express their opinions on the statements, based on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. The questionnaire can be found in the Appendix.

## Data analysis

The collected data were analysed and tested using the structural equation modelling approach, which allows researchers to perform path analytic modelling with latent variables (Bollen, 1989). It is often referred to as a second-generation multivariate analysis technique (Fornell, 1987). In recent years, a significant number of studies using a structural equation modelling approach have appeared in IS journals and publications (for example, Adams et al, 1992; Igbaria et al, 1995; Chau 1996). The software package employed in this study is AMOS 3.6, a covariance-based structural equation modelling tool similar to LISREL.

The testing of the proposed model shown in Figure 2 involves the evaluation of the structural model and its related measurement models. The structural model investigates the direction and significance of causal relationships between various latent variables. For each latent variable, there is also a measurement model, which represents the relationships between the latent variable and observed variables. The purpose of testing the measurement model is to determine if the measures possess satisfactory psychometric properties, ie, do the items in the questionnaire measure what they are supposed to measure. The structural equation model is presented in Figure 3. To avoid possible interactions between the measurement and structural models, Segars and Grover (1993), suggested that the measurement model should be evaluated first and then respecified if necessary to generate the ‘best fitting’ measurement model. We can only proceed to evaluate the structural model for theory testing when the measurement model has adequate psychometric properties (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The resultant respecified measures can then be used to examine the relationships between latent variables in the structural model. The respecified measurement and structural models have to be reconfirmed using a different set of data to demonstrate the consistency of the model (Igbaria et al, 1995). Chin and Todd (1995), cautioned the use of structural equation modelling in MIS research and specifically raised four issues with respect to the methodology used in Segars and Grover’s study. Two of the issues are relevant for this study. First, they argue that model modifications should be supported by substantive theoretical rationale. In our study, we have provided a thorough review of existing theoretical background on why perceived critical mass can have significant impact on perceived usefulness, ease of use, and intention to use. Second, the cross validation should be based on samples with independent observations and sufficient subsample sizes. In our study, efforts were made to eliminate multiple responses so all samples are independent observations. We randomly divided the collected data into two subsamples S1 and S2, which had a size of 192 and 193, respectively. A minimum sample size of 100 to 200 is considered adequate for structural equation modelling analysis (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). Thus, sample sizes of 192 and 193 are deemed acceptable for this study.

Chi-square/degrees of freedom, goodness-of-fit index (GFI), adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and root mean square residual (RMSR) were used to measure the model fitness. If any of these criteria were not met, the measurement model should be respecified to improve model fitness.

The psychometric properties of the ‘fit’ measurement model were then evaluated for convergent validity (item reliability, construct reliability, and average variance extracted by each construct) and discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). If the measurement model satisfied all three requirements, we deemed that the model had adequate convergent validity.

## Analysis of the structural equation model

Given the adjusted measurement model, the structural equation model can be analysed using a different sample (Igbaria et al, 1995; Pedhazur, 1982). Sample S2 was used to confirm the fitness of the adjusted measurement model and perform the analysis of the structural model. The results of the model fitness and convergent validity of the measures are presented in Tables 4–6. Table 4 shows that various model-fit measures exceeded the recommended criteria. Item and construct reliabilities were all adequate and average variance extracted by constructs were greater than 0.50 (see Table 5). In addition, Table 6 shows that discriminant validity measures were also acceptable.

Following the validation of the measurement model, we examined the structural model by testing the hypothesised relationships among various constructs using sample S2. The results of the analysis are presented in Figure 4 and Table 7. Figure 4 shows the standardised path coefficients and critical ratios between constructs. All hypothesised relationships were significant at the 0.01 level. Thus, as predicted, perceived critical mass had significant effects on perceived ease of use (0.223), perceived usefulness (0.414), and intention to use (0.261). Perceived ease of use had a significant positive impact on both perceived usefulness (0.270) and intention to use (0.164). Finally, intention to use was also influenced by perceived usefulness (0.568).

## Discussion and conclusions

This study advanced a research model for studying groupware acceptance that included the construct of perceived critical mass as an independent variable. Perceived critical mass was theorised to affect groupware acceptance directly as well as through perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. The validity of the model and the relationships among various constructs were tested using a structural equation modelling approach. The model was supported by the empirical data collected for this study.

The most interesting finding of this study is the effects of perceived critical mass on intention to use, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness. As proposed in the research model, perceived critical mass was found to have a direct impact on intention to use groupware technology. This finding indicates that a user’s decision to use groupware is influenced by whether or not the same technology is used by their peers in the group. A user is more likely to adopt a groupware application if he thinks that other members of his group are using it. This result is consistent with many studies in diffusion of innovation research in which personal interactions are regarded as an important source of external influence for technology adoption (Rogers, 1983). Positive communication about a technology is likely to sway potential users towards adopting that technology. In the case of groupware, however, such positive communication may not be the primary means of personal influence. Social norms may play an important role here. As a part of the group, potential users may feel obligated to use the technology because they want to belong to the group. They may realise that their participation is crucial for other group members to utilise the technology. Subjective norms were a part of TRA but it was not included in TAM because of its theoretical ambiguity and psychometric uncertainty (Davis et al, 1989). Empirical results from Davis’ study (1989) using a word processor also failed to show the link between social norms and use. With groupware, however, social norms may have more influence on intention to use. More research is needed to explore the effect of social norm on groupware acceptance.

Our study also revealed that perceived critical mass affects users perception of a groupware application’s usefulness and ease of use. The positive impact of perceived critical mass on perceived usefulness may be attributed to two factors. First, like most interactive technologies, the utility that can be derived from groupware applications increases dramatically as more and more people use the technology. This phenomenon is often referred to as a network externality (Katz & Shapiro, 1986; Gurbaxani, 1990). Second, as more people are using the technology, potential users witness more examples and different ways of employing the technology. Hence, it is not surprising that there is a positive correlation between perceived critical mass and perceived usefulness. Similarly, the perceived widespread use of a groupware technology may lead potential users to believe that the technology is probably easy to learn and use because so many of their peers have learned to use it.

The relationships postulated in the original TAM were all supported in this study. Our results showed that perceived ease of use had direct influence on intention to use. Previous TAM studies, however, have reported mixed results for this relationship. Several studies found that perceived ease of use had no significant effects on intention to use (Davis, 1989; Igbaria et al, 1995; Chau, 1996). One explanation is that as users are more computer literate and technologies are more user-friendly, ease of use has become less of a factor in technology acceptance decisions (Chau, 1996). This explanation may not apply to groupware technology. Groupware technology is still relatively new to most users and its user interface, though easy to learn, is different from most individual productivity software, such as MS Word or Excel. Orlikowski and Gash (1994) pointed out that groupware technology required a different technological frame than individual productivity software so it is not difficult to understand why users still consider ease of use as an important condition for using groupware.

The result of the relationship between perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness is consistent with most previous TAM studies (eg, (Davis, 1989; Igbaria et al, 1995; Chau, 1996)). It suggests that, like other technologies, an easy to use groupware technology allows users to explore various features and utilities of the technology and thus increases the perceived usefulness (Chau, 1996). Further, perceived ease of use may encourage more novices to try the technology, making features and utilities that require wide participation more useful. The results showed the impact of perceived usefulness on intention to use was statistically significant. Furthermore, like most previous studies, our study also found that perceived usefulness had the greatest direct effects on intention to use. It implies that individuals would only use a technology if they think the technology would help them perform their tasks, and groupware is no exception.

Our study shows that TAM applies to groupware technology. However, the proposed model extended TAM by incorporating an additional variable that is specific to the technologies under study. The new construct, perceived critical mass, is shown to have the largest total effect on intention to use. This result suggests that, in making groupware usage decisions, individuals are influenced not only by their own perceptions about the technology but also by the actions taken by their peers. The proposed model helps us better understand the factors affecting user acceptance decisions.

These findings have several practical as well as theoretical implications. For managers who are responsible for groupware implementation, these results suggest that achieving a critical mass of users should be given high priority in the early stages of the implementation process. Selecting appropriate individuals and groups to participate in the initial introduction is crucial to generating a critical mass of users. Various strategies and methods can be employed to identify these individuals and groups. For instance, managers may first target wellestablished groups whose members have close working relationships because members of such groups are more likely to be influenced by their peers. Efforts can also be made to increase the perception of critical mass. For example, threaded discussion databases may appear to have more traffic if the discussion evolves around a small number of topics with multiple layers of responses.

From a theoretical point of view, our results empirically confirmed the importance of perceived critical mass in groupware acceptance. This is consistent with Markus and Connolly (1990), and Grudin (1994), who suggested that creating critical mass is one of the major challenges in groupware implementation. The proposed model provides a foundation for further study of the perceived critical mass construct and other constructs that may affect perceived critical mass. Further research may involve developing better measures of the perceived critical mass construct, studying different ways of generating critical mass in a short time period, and exploring the relationship between received critical mass and actual penetration rate.

The results of the present study need to be interpreted with caution due to the limitations of the research method. Because there was no established scale for measuring perceived critical mass, only two items were developed in this study. While the scale was considered acceptable, the reliability and validity of the scale may be improved by including additional items. Another limitation is that the subjects were students. The need for patterns of collaboration in an academic environment may very well be different from those in the corporate world. Student groups employed in this study were ad hoc and only lasted for ten weeks. Many corporate working groups, on the other hand, are ongoing and hierarchical groups with a history of collaboration and cooperation. Future research efforts may involve replicating this study with working groups in organizations. Furthermore, discussion database is only one of the widely used groupware applications. Its features and requirements for use may well be different from other applications such as electronic calender management. Thus, the conclusions derived from this study may be specific to discussion database. More research is needed before we can generalise our findings to other groupware applications.
