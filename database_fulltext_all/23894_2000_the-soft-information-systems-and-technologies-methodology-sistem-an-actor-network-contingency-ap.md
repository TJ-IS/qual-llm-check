# The ‘Soft Information Systems and Technologies Methodology’ (SISTeM): an actor network contingency approach to integrated development

CJ Atkinson

Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex, UB8 3PH, UK

This paper describes the Soft Information Systems and Technologies Methodology (SISTeM), and demonstrates its utility in integrating informational and organisational development. The methodology and its stages are illustrated with examples from recent information systems and technology (IS&T) based projects, mainly in the health sector. Its antecedents in soft systems methodology are identified. The use of the approaches underpinning concept of the human/machine activity system is described. The nature of SISTeM as a social, political and technological form of contingency methodology, working with other approaches and disciplines, is demonstrated. SISTeM, it will be argued has capacity for orchestrating the emergence, consolidation and continual development of real world actor networks.

## Introduction

Avison and Fitzgerald (1988, 1999) in their discussion of information systems development methodologies and the issues surrounding their use have suggested that we are entering an era in which alternatives to ‘formalised’, structured, information systems development approaches (ISD), such as Structured Systems Analysis and Design Method (SSADM) are being sought by users and their organizations. They site a series of reasons for this starting with the failure to deliver tangible benefits either in information systems (IS) functionality or to the business of the organization, over complexity and encouraging user requirement ‘wish lists’. In addition these approaches demand substantial, difficult to acquire, technical skills. They also contain complex tools that can sometimes add little design value. Further, they have a one dimensional and over rationalistic inflexible approach to complexity. They often depend upon invalid assumptions about the organisational context. For example, that a coherent organisational strategy exists, that will be fully realised and to which everyone is committed. Further, as a result of the intended universality of these approaches to ISD they can find themselves incommensurate with specific organisational and business sector situations. Adherence to process has sometimes dominated quality of outcome. These ISD are data focused. Following on from the work of Davis (1982), Marcus (1984), Burns and Dennis (1985) and Land and Somogyi (1986) they further argue that these methodologies are non-contingent, lacking in adaptability, especially where rapid organisational change and political factors are prevalent.

There have been a number of reactions to these perceived inadequacies. Firstly, the development of computer-aided systems engineering (CASE) tools that rigorously orchestrate the process of these methodologies. CASE tools aim to improve output quality, set standards and provide consistency as well as a ready to use integrated tool set and in certain products automatically generate code—though the latter is still not demonstrated fully in practice. Another reaction has been to adopt a rapid applications/systems development (RAD, RSD). RAD has been criticised, however, for improving the user interface but not necessarily IS design or the code or organization (Benyon-Davis et al, 1999). An object oriented (OO) approach is another response. But it is yet to demonstrate its full potential (Lauesen 1998) The purchase of packages and applications or outsourcing the information and communication technologies (ICT) function rather than in-house development, ‘buy not build’, has been a further response. But this has the potential to put the organization in thrall to the supplier or outsourcer, where contractual arrangements limit services rather than enhance flexibility. A final strategy to the dilemma of structured methodologies is to adopt a contingency approach to ISD.

The contingency approach takes various forms. Methodologies become more situation specific, applying only, for example, to management IS systems in the banking sector. Alternatively meta-methodologies, such as ‘Euromethod’ (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1999) act as an overarching framework within which to choose from a number of other (European) methodologies or adopt and adapt parts of them contingent to the application domain. Finally, methodologies are being developed that are adaptable to and adapted by the situations of their use. These are capable of integrating with other ISD approaches and adopting the tools within them. Multiview (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1994; Bell & Wood-Harper, 1998) is one such contingency approach. The soft information systems and technologies methodology (SISTeM) (Atkinson, 1997) described here is another.

This paper will describe SISTeM, its cycles, stages and tools, explore its status as contingency approach, offer a review of its use to date, consider its further development and future contributions to research in the field of integrating ISD and organization change. The role of actor network theory (ANT) (Callon, 1991) as an underpinning framework for contingency approaches such as SISTeM will also be explored.

## SISTeM, its antecedents and underpinning framework

SISTeM, has two cycles (see Figure 1). Cycle 1 is similar to that of Checkland’s (1981) SSM. For the actors in a problem situation which they find unsatisfactory the aim is to arrive at ‘a debate concerned with defining changes which would help remove the dissatisfaction’. Cycle 2 on the other hand is more pragmatic. Working with other disciplines and the techniques of other ISD methodologies (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988), incorporating some of their tools and techniques this cycle is concerned with operational decision making and bringing about the integration of organisational, informational and technological changes within the situation. Underpinning SISTeM, its conceptual framework, is the notion of the human/machine activity. The models within SISTeM are based on this concept. A discussion of this framework, Latour (1987) and Callon’s (1991) ANT, and their relevance to SISTeM is provided in Figure 1.

## The soft information systems and technologies methodology

The structure of SISTeM’s twin cycles of decision making and operationalising are set out in Figure 1. The two cycles form, when reflected on in practice, an action learning cycle. A description and demonstration of SIS-TeM’s tools and techniques and its links with those of other ISD methodologies will be provided in a latter section, though referenced here.

Characteristically of contingency approaches, SIS-TeM’s cycles have been practised formally, stage by stage, or informally almost invisibly. Checkland and Scholes (1990) have designated these forms of soft systems praxis in turn as modes 1 and modes 2, characterised respectively as the work of the novice or formalist and the proficient or intuitive practitioner. Sometimes SISTeM’s tools and techniques are used independently.

Those who use SISTeM are encouraged to reflect not only on the outcomes, but also the efficacy of the methodological process and the tools and techniques used to develop both competency in their use and a mode 2 capacity to modify them to meet future contingencies. The cycles have also been deployed in sequence or separately, considering each cycle in turn.

## Cycle 1: inquiry and decision taking

Cycle 1, as can be seen from Figure 1, consists of seven stages, starting with an analysis of the problem situation and ending with a decision, leading to change and a new problem situation facilitated by Cycle 2. This process is potentially never ending.

## The problem situation, expressed and analysed

The initial stages (1.1 & 1.2) result in an analysis and a rich picture (Checkland, 1981) of the problem situation. They take into account the various issues, opportunities, processes and tasks present. An emphasis is placed upon all aspects relating to IS&IT, current and future, and their potential for adding value. The impact IS&IT is having on the way people work, on management, on processes and organisational effectiveness, and on meeting customer/client needs is captured. The successes and failures of successive information strategies are explored. The efficacy of the IS department and staff is considered. The attitudes to and experiences of IS& IT for potential users throughout the organization and wider stakeholders are also considered. Social, political and power aspects are also explored, particularly how they are manifest in IS&IT initiatives and use. Finally account is taken of who owns or sponsors any prospective intervention, whose job it would be to bring it about and who will be its recipients. This analysis phase is left open and running throughout the intervention, and potentially over many interventions. The rich picture being constantly updated within repeated cycles of SIS-TeM, done to inform future cycles, leaving behind a history of the unfolding problem situation, the issues and tasks within it and the decisions and responses to them. Practice has shown that this is important for organisational learning and effective interventions.

In stage 1.2, human/machine systems relevant to the issues and tasks are identified. Root definitions (RDs) are formed and a CATWOE analysis undertaken. Often more than one relevant system is identified against a particular issue. In certain projects (Atkinson, 1994) it has been useful to diagrammatically link the relevant systems together into schematics to highlight their interrelationships. This has been of particular value in projects where issues focus on the introduction of strategic IS&IT across whole departments, organizations or organisational networks.

## Models, comparisons and agendas for debate

Building on the previous stages, in stage 1.3 models are formed of the root definitions associated with the relevant systems allied to the issues and tasks identified. This may be an iterative process. Within SISTeM the models take three forms: conceptual, expressive and hybrid-matrix models. Conceptual models (CMs) in SIS-TeM are similar to those found within SSM. They consist of a number of managed interlocking human/ machine activities that logically constitute the transformation in the root definition. Expressive models are also based on the human/machine activity system, similar in form to CMs. However, they differ in two fundamental ways. Firstly, people and groups in the problem situation generate the expressive models for themselves, of their current or desired future processes—with facilitation if required. Secondly the models are of tasks or processes interpreted and identified by the people as problematical. Either they are processes or tasks in need of more and/or better information, knowledge or communications along with improvements in the (technological) medium by which these are provided or they require reconfiguring, or both. As well as prospective users, information professionals (sometimes IS application suppliers) and project managers have taken part in the creation of an expressive model. They assist in formation of the IS& IT component to the model, speaking in its absentia on behalf of the machine (WBPCH, 1998), representing the potential application. Observational work or interviews on process and information (HPSS, 1997) has been incorporated within the expressive model. The modelling work has also engendered a spontaneous debate amongst the participants, leading not only to introducing new IS& IT, but also the changes in existing working practice and processes.

A variant form of model is emerging, particularly in the areas of information strategy (Atkinson, 1997, 1998) linked to organisational competency development (Ciborra & Andreu, 1998). It is a hybrid, containing expressive and conceptual elements. These models consist of a multi-dimensional human/machine activity matrix covering all or part of an organization or across organizations. On one axis are problematical and/or strategically important organisational competencies, on the other, a series of stages of IS&IT development. Each cell formed by the two axes incorporates within it IS & IT functionality, business processes, OD requirements, informational and organisational benefits/risks, and organisational location. The model defines an organisational maturation pathway. Examples of SISTeM’s and ISD modelling tools and their links can be found in Table 1.

The original root definition is often amended in the light of the modelling processes. A very useful addition to the tools within SISTeM, developed from practice, is to extract a summary description of the ICT component from the model in the form of a machine activity root definition—this has provided an understanding of IS functionality, even an initial specification.

Having developed the models, the next stage, stage 1.4, is to compare their content with the rich picture of the problem situation and generate stage 1.5, an agenda for debate. This can be done in the formal tabular format as advocated by Wilson (1984). However within the projects (WBPCH, 1998) that have constituted the development of SISTeM, the stages of modelling, comparisons, agendas and debate tend, through extensive user involvement, to blend into one another. Often this is the continuation of existing debates between the various actors and organizations that instigated SISTeM’s deployment in the first place. A return to earlier stages can occur at this point.

## An initial debate and decision making

The debate, stage 1.6, amongst the parties involved, including IS&IT professionals now takes place. It is one of the four foci of SISTeM in action, the others being the operational decision making, the change itself and the ongoing learning process. Within the debate stage the derived agenda and sometimes, particularly at the strategic level, SISTeM’s models are used as points of departure (WBPHC, 1998). The debate may result in a robust decision for change or it may not, prompting individuals or groups into working together or going their own way. Even using SISTeM for their own separate purposes. What comes out of the debate is triggered but not determined by the agenda put forward.

The debate is inherently a social and a political process, in which influence, power, networks of reciprocity, games of status, hierarchies and multiple colliding stakeholder agendas occur, as well as genuine moves to participation. The utilisation of all forms of power within the debation (as in everyday life) to further an actor’s or group’s agenda is not uncommon. The debate is not completely devoid of rational elements, for example: those surrounding IS functionality, IT infrastructure, business process design, business process options and cost-benefit analysis. Nevertheless, if a substantive decision is to be reached it has to be based on what Checkland and Scholes (1990) refers to as a ‘robust accommodation’ between the parties involved—this is reconfirmed and consolidated by the practical decision making of Cycle 2, though not always. A number of criteria for the decision have emerged from the practice of both SISTeM cycles and its SSM antecedents.

The first two criterion are inherited from SSM (Checkland, 1981). They are firstly, the decision(s) is ‘systemically desirable’, ie, it arises out of the systems thinking that has gone on within the project and, secondly, for the actors in the situation, that it is ‘culturally feasible’. The latter is facilitated through the involvement of influential, powerful actors, as well as IS&IT professional prospective users and an engagement with wider constituencies, based on effective ongoing communications, negotiations and consultations throughout the project. The debate often involves culture clashes, fears about loss of autonomy, exercises of influence, status, power and money, considerations of markets, efficiency, even jobs, all will impact on this criteria as to whether the decision is feasible or not. In this domain the power of ‘Reason’ is limited.

Further criteria have emerged in practice. The changes need to be ‘value adding’; ie, deemed to produce tangible benefits for users, wider stakeholders and the organization as a whole. Specific informational, process, service, product and fiscal benefits are sought. As SIS-TeM projects have a major IS&IT component it is important to explore the criteria of technical feasibility or any solution identified as part of a decision. IS&IT strategies, configurations, migration pathways, technical infrastructures, platforms, networks, security and encryption, standards and applications have all figured. Whether to ‘build or buy’ is often discussed and if so whether to go for best of breed applications, single provider or outsourcing. The technical feasibility debate and final decisions are usually only settled within Cycle 2. This criterion introduces reason into the debate.

Finally, as SISTeM has been developed mainly within the public sectors in the UK, and healthcare in particular, the notion of ethical defensibility in line with Wood-Harper et al (1996) on the ‘ethical analyst’, has been an important one—local medical ethics committees have been involved in the debate stage. The criteria of ethical defensibility in the debate, asks those who make the decision to make clear the ethical grounds on which any decision is based. This is done in order to facilitate further the debate within the contingencies of the situation, not to provide some ready-made ethical position.

The criteria for debate for SISTeM cycle 1 decisions are that they be systematically desirable, culturally feasible, value adding, technically feasible & ethically defensible. Having reached an accommodated decision about the overall change the next step is to realise it. SISTeM Cycle 2 has emerged from practice to facilitate this process.

## Cycle 2 operational decision making and realisation

Often the debate within Cycle 1 and resulting decision are at a high, even strategic, level, or not articulated sufficiently to become immediately intentional and concerted action. Decisions of principle rather than the practicalities of decisions for action. SISTeM Cycle 2 aims at engendering a debate, a decision and intentionality amongst the parties involved on the operational practicalities of ‘what’ will be changed and ‘how’ that change will be realised. It then seeks to support that process of real world change through the provision of systemic tools and methods, as well as integrating, through the human/machine activity framework, with other disciplines and approaches in the field of IS&IT and organisational or business development.

## The problem situation revisited

Stage 2.1 of Cycle 2 constitutes a review of the problem situation, the current rich picture and the human, organisational and IS&IT issues within it that may inhibit or facilitate realisation of that initial decision. It could prompt a return to cycle 1 if the situation had changed— eg, introduction of a new application or technology, making the earlier decision out of date. Having a decision at all could have a wide-ranging impact on the problem situation. Those individuals or groups who own, or sponsor the change, that seek to act as its final guarantor, are also identified within stage 2.1. The issues surrounding their capacity, power and political position to underwrite the development and steer it to fruition are explored, as is potential resistance to change.

The original decision is reviewed in stage 2.2 and expressed more clearly. This can take the form of an initial statement of intent or scoping study, accompanied by an indication of potential funding sources, though without commitment. These are then signed off by the interested parties to the initial decision, or if not returned to Cycle 1 for further consideration and debate. Stage 2.3, identifies those responsible for bringing about the organisational and IS&IT changes. These ‘change agents’ will work with stakeholders and users on the later stages. Their role is set down and legitimated across the organization by the guarantors. This team or group can be top-down or bottom-up, formal or informal. SIS-TeM tends to encourage wide involvement and an incrementalist approach to IS&IT development and organisational change, as advocated by Sauer and Yetton (1997), though there is no prescription in this matter.

## Models to support the debate and the change

Stages 2.4 and 2.5 are modelling stages (see Figure 1). Together they support both the decision making process and the realisation of changes emanating from it. Drawing on the original decision from Cycle 1, stage 2.4 models the ‘vision’ or ‘options for change’. This feeds into the debate via a comparison with the problem situation (sometimes directly). Stage 2.4 also models the decision for change emanating from the debate. Similarly, stage 2.5 provides modelled options for the processes that could bring about the change. These too inform the debate. Subsequently the decided upon process for bringing about the change are also modelled, and then form a concrete action plan. Both the modelled ‘vision’ and ‘change process’ are combined and used as the starting point for the ‘realisation process’—of course they will be modified by emerging contingencies. Stages 2.4 and 2.5 are available to meet these contingencies with further modelling within micro-cycles of SISTeM.

Based on the human/machine activity systems all three forms of models, their root definitions, as well as schematics have been used in stages 2.4 and 2.5. Their status, however, is now developmental. A vision has often taken the form of a hybrid model maturation pathway, sequential stages of integrated IS and organisational development with associated risks, benefits and costs and organisational coverage—see next section. But it can equally be a model of for example a single clinical process—with added information activity—spanning across three organizations. Developmental human/ machine activity models of the change process and its management are turned in an overall project plan and timetable. Project planning tools and spreadsheets (WBPCH, 1998; HPSS, 1997) have been used.

## Integrating SISTeM with other disciplines

Both stages 2.4 and 2.6 bring into play other relevant disciplines (via stage 2.5) in two ways. Firstly to inform the final detailed decision making and then on ‘what’ and ‘how’ to undertake the real world change itself. Considering each in turn. Disciplines to inform the debate and underpin the final decision use organisational and IS development modelling tools: a full business case containing ‘build or buy’ or ‘do nothing’ options is one example. As is a detailed financial analysis with options of the ICT procurement, a formal bid from a short-listed applications supplier(s). IS output based specifications (OBS) have also been constructed, based partly on SIS-TeM’s models. Supplier applications demonstrations, when compared (WBPCH, 1998) with the SISTeM modelling, inform the debate and assist in choice of ICT and supplier. High-level data flow diagrams or UML usecases, derived from SISTeM development models, can also be deployed (see stage 2.6).

Within the actual design, development and change process (stage 2.10) the use of other disciplines (via stage 2.5) may run parallel to the soft systems activities and tools, with or without linkage. SISTeM may be integrated directly with the ICT systems analysis and design cycle (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988). It is capable of providing business process and information requirements in the form of development models of human/machine activity that can feed directly in the IS modelling process DFDs. Entity models attributes and life cycles—as will be demonstrated in the next section—can also be derived as can UML use cases. At the two ends of the ICT development spectrum, SISTeM’s models are capable of providing business process and information requirement analysis, to enrich the early stages of SSADM based projects, in a manner suggested by Goodland and Slater (1995) they may also underpin class/object formation and UML use cases. The machine information activities in the model form an initial screen dialogue specification for each human/machine activity and aggregate to the dialogue for the whole process. Simulations may be built from developmental models to support the debate and any subsequent process reconfiguration.

## Agendas, debates and the intention to change

A comparison is made in stage 2.7 between the human/activity systems developmental model(s)—of the vision(s) for change and the process of change—with both the current problem situation and the original decision from Cycle 1. The inputs from other disciplines are also brought to bear from activities 2.4 and 2.6 in the comparison. Out of this arises an agenda for a final debate leading to a decision amongst the users/ stakeholders on how to proceed within the problem situation. As with Cycle 1, the modelling, comparison and the debates have often melded. The IS&IT and other disciplines enrich and inform the final debate further.

The imperative in debate stage 2.8 is for actors in the problem situation to come to a full, robust and overt agreement on how to proceed with the change and what is entailed within that change. This is enshrined within a number of formalisms: the approval of a full business case and apportionment of a global budget and detailed costing. The answering of IS/IT build, buy or outsourcing options question. The establishment and initiation of an organisational development and culture change programme. The setting down of organisational benefits to be accrued, process re-configurations to be brought about and cultural changes to be achieved. The completion of formal tendering processes. The signing of contracts with IS/IT developers, suppliers or outsourcers and the setting down of legally binding agreements with cooperating organizations. A detailed specification of user information requirements is established. The IS/IT scope is established and signed off. Evaluation methodologies, parameters and criteria are also set up. The overt establishment of the project board and team accompanied by the public announcement of the start of a programme of change will mark the outcome of this stage. Within commercial organizations it will be a sanctioning of the project by the highest authority necessary—usually the Board. All this does not preclude the fact that much of the debate itself will be on the basis of ‘corridor work’, informal discussions and political manoeuvres, similar to that found within Cycle 1 of SISTeM. The criteria emerging from projects for the debate are also similar, but more rigorous in terms of the fiscal, benefits and technological feasibility. The focus is not just on ISD or applications procurement but on ISD as an integral part of organisational transformations in work practices, processes, performance and culture, services and products.

One other outcome of the debate in Cycle 2 is that it will engender intentionality in those charged with affecting change within the organization and those stakeholders who have made the decision (stage 2.9). This has then to be realised in practice amongst a wider constituency of stakeholders and maintained from the project’s inception in the debate stage of Cycle 2, to the delivery of benefits and a return to Cycle 1, a considerable task.

## The change and a new problem situation

SISTeM is not, as has been said before, a methodology for doing the actual transformation of human and IS& IT activities within or across organizations. There are other professions, disciplines and methodologies, as well as experiential based forms of craft practice that are far more pertinent, well-tried and effective in this area. Where appropriate SISTeM can provide ‘soft tools’ to assist in the activity of organisational transformation.

The role of SISTeM is to provide a link between combinations of these approaches and disciplines, melding them within a cycle of problem solving, decision making, transformative action and learning, centred on the combining of the human and the technological. Supporting them, via stages 2.5 and 2.6, through the integration of its tools and those of ISD, organisational development, team building, cultural change and/or simulation and process reconfiguration.

As can be seen from Figure 1, with the attempt of affecting change there comes not only (inter) organisational benefits but also a new problem situation, and the potential of SISTeM again for addressing it through further cycles of integrated ICT and organisational development. This is a ‘potentially never ending’ process that moves away from a sporadic project-based approach to ICT implementation to the cadences of continuous integrated development and maturation throughout an organization’s lifetime. When enacted in the real world, SISTeM has the potential of orchestrating what Paul (1993) terms a ‘living (human/machine activity) system’.

## SISTeM’s tools and techniques

This section provides a description of the major modelling tools and techniques used within SISTeM, based upon a real world example. Included within this section are root definitions of human/machine activity, root definitions of machine activity, a CATWOE analysis encompassing both and the associated expressive or alternatively developmental model found in Cycles 1 & 2. An entity diagram and entity description, a data flow diagram, UML use-case diagram and OO object diagram derived from this human/machine activity system are also provided to illustrate how the tools in SISTeM integrate with those of IS development approaches.

The illustrative models below are based upon a problem situation within a hospital radiotherapy department where IS development work and procurement were underway, supported by an in-house project and design team. The Hospital was going through a major expansion of its cancer services, new IS was needed to support this. However this was only part of a wider IS development programme. This was driven by the need, for contractual and operational reasons, to replace existing core hospital-wide administrative information legacy systems with a whole new IS environment, based upon the notions of the integrated hospital information support system (HISS) leading to the electronic patient record (EPR). Also many of the operational IS in the hospital were stand-alone, working off separate patient databases. The development though was not just an upgrading of administrative legacy systems. It was aimed at strategically moving the organisational IS&T investment away from capturing post-hoc patient activity data, done to ‘feed the twin beasts’ of central data returns to the Department of Health and contract monitoring, to one of supporting the ‘core business’ of the whole hospital ie, care of the patient and the work of clinicians.

The intention behind this long-term strategy was to aid both the clinicians in their work and enable clinical audit to take place so as to improve standards of care; what is now termed ‘clinical governance’. In so doing, it was argued, not only would patients receive better treatment but management information would also be captured at source, thus improving its quality and timeliness—which was currently poor. Such information would afford the hospital administration the opportunity to manage both clinical activity and hospital resources in a far more robust and financially sound way. This strategy had the benefit of providing an integrated IS& IT environment built around a central data repository containing the EPR, underpinned by a common patient activity database linked via a LAN, and integrating engines, to operational systems on the wards, clinical test order-communications, laboratories and outpatient clinics, with external links to other organizations eg, GP’s. Patient activity management, waiting lists, costed activity and financial information would also be available for contracting and planning purposes.

SISTeM was used as part of the early phases of this development process. Initially through interviews, group work and document review, a rich picture was produced of the situation. An analysis of clinical and managerial processes and IS&IT undertaken—both current and prospective—and formed into a hybrid matrix model (see Table 1), of the clinical and managerial processes to be supported by a series of IS&IT development stages and benefits accruing to each.

It was decided that the initial work on the EPR should focus on radiotherapy clinical practice. Through engagement with the clinical team and IS designers a root definition and expressive and conceptual models were drawn up and populated with informational machine activities, comparisons made and debated amongst the actors took place—this is too large for illustrative purposes here. However it did form the context of a particularly problematical area of allotting patients to radiotherapy machines. This will be used to illustrate SISTeM’s tools and their links with ISD techniques. It should be emphasised that the expressive model described below was not only aimed at developing better information system provision but a new more effective organisational human/machine slot allocation process. Starting with its root definition and CATWOE analysis.

## The radiotherapy patient treatment slot allocation issue and root definition

Issue/task: It is increasingly difficult to carry out the allocation of patients, their assessments and the degree of urgency for treatment to slots in radiotherapy machines as a result of an increase in the number of machines, teams and patients referred.

Relevant system: A radiotherapy patient machine slot allocation system.

Root definition:

Human/machine slot allocation system.

A slot management human/machine activity system owned and operated by the slot manager that apportions a patient, their referral and treatment assessment to a (series) of slots within radio therapy machines in line with the treatment sets down by the consultant Radiotherapist and Oncologist diagnosis including its degree of urgency.

## A CATWOE analysis of the root definitions

A customer, actor, weltanschauung, transformation, owner, environment analysis was also undertaken bringing out the human and the machine components as actors in the root definition.

Customer(s) Radiotherapy machines and teams, patients, consultants, GPs.

actors Slot allocation manager, slot allocation information system

transformation Patient in need of radiotherapy treatment patients allocated to a radiotherapy machine for treatment(s).

Weltanschauung An efficient slot allocation, in line with assessment and degree of urgency is an important contributor to patient care and equity in resource allocation.

Owner Slot allocation manager-process, Director of Information-ICT.

Environment Patients referred, consultants, GPs, radiotherapy machines, teams.

From the above model a root definition of the information machine activity was drawn up. This has the properties of a textural information systems specification:

## Machine Root Definition:

Radiotherapy patient machine slot allocation information system.

A Radiotherapy Patient Treatment Slot Allocation Information System, operated by the slot allocation manager and owned by the Director of Information that enables the patient to be allocated to a slot in the radiotherapy machines by:

I Providing Information about the patient—patient record;

I Receiving notification of a referral;

I Presenting information about the test and investigations—patient record;

I Present details of diagnosis, site, fractions required, stage, urgency;

I Provide accrual of referrals on a daily/weekly basis;

Create overview of machine slots over next year, and their allocated status;

I Capture degree of urgency and details of treatment pattern and type of machine suitable;

I Schedule of machine availability—slots open/closed;

Capture allocation of machine slots for the fractions required;

I Notify patient, GP (or other referrer) of times and treatment;

I Capture confirmation of patient intention to attend;

Download slot allocations into radiotherapy machine, planner, simulator treatment;

## Actor network theory a framework for SISTeM

The last decade has seen the emergence of what has come to be referred to as ‘actor-network theory’ (ANT) though its progenitors Latour (1997, 1998) and Callon (1986, 1991) and proponents such as Law (1998) disown this ‘naming’. This ‘theory’ is concerned with providing an underpinning philosophical position and a methodology (Walsham, 1997) for inquiring into the real-world processes by which associations of humans and nonhumans (machines) coalesce into persistent networks or fail to do so. Berg (1997) is probably the leading exponent of the use of ANT in studying healthcare. He says:

‘%I conceptualise medical practices as networks of heterogeneous elements (including physicians, patient files, and x-ray machines)—elements which are interconnected in manifold ways and which, taken as a whole constitute the workplace%

Berg (1997) identifies his task of inquiry as empirically explaining the formation and dissolution of such wholes using ANT:

In the % production and use of technical systems in medical practices. One of the central tenets of these studies is that the development of technology cannot be understood from perspectives that treat nature and society as different realms and confine explanatory power to either one of them. Exactly what the transformation of a practice (or a tool) comes down to can never be understood from standpoints that centre on the nature of medical problems or, conversely, on the action of the medical personnel. Rather the physical and the social of ‘computerised’ or ‘protocolised’ medical practices are the outcomes of historically contingent events that have lead to that current configuration. Only by treating them symmetrically can we hope to gain an understanding of the ‘success’ or ‘failure’ of decision support techniques; only by focusing on the way these heterogeneous networks take shape and break down can we come to the fundamental issues at stake.

Treating humans and non-humans ‘symmetrically’, as Berg (1997) and Latour (1993) do, as having the same status within a network, is a central tenant of ANT. They may have different qualities and functionality, but these are defined by their relationships with other components within the network not as a priori to the entities themselves. ANT (Law, 1999) is a ‘semiotics of materiality’ in which relationships within the network constitutes identity.

Actor network theory not only sets down the network’s nature and the status of human and machine ‘actants’ within it (The term ‘actant’ was adopted by its proponents, to ward criticisms of ANT (Collins & Yearley, 1992) over attributing human ‘actor’ intentionality to non-humans). It also defines the process of how actants are ‘enrolled’ into a network by ‘translating’ their interests into an irreversible alignment with those of an existing or prospective network, via one or a number of ‘obligatory passage points’ and, in so doing, irreversibly transforming the network. Translation is achieved via a series of ‘moments’. For Callon (1986) and Latour (1991) ANT constitutes a sociology of translation. Four stages or ‘moments’ of translation lead the actant through a non-reversible, obligatory passage point transforming both the newly incorporated actant and the network. These moments are named: problematisation; inte´ressemment; enrolment and mobilisation (Introna, 1997).

Problematisation comes about when one or more actants identifies a situation as problematical and can persuade others actants to join them in tackling it and that this would entail moving through an obligatory passage point to address it. Inte´ressemment is a process by which the actants convened around a situation to strengthen their resolve in moving through the obligatory passage point, whilst seeking to exclude voices of dissuasion from without or entering into alliances with other networks. Enrolment involves solidification of intent by bargaining and compromise. ‘Enrolling’ actants into a network is a process in which humans and nonhumans, as individuals or groups persuade, cajole, or

It is not only humans that achieve this, machines, for example internet technology, can be just as seductive or persuasive as enrollers of other actants into the network. ## SISTeM and ANT

It is not the intention here to provide a full discussion or critique of ANT or the debates surrounding it. But to use ANT as a means of understanding how SISTeM, in association with other human and non-human, technologies, facilitates enrolments and translations of actants to address the problematical real world situations faced by a network. However it does raise a question about SISTeM, its contingency status and the adaptability of actor networks that is important to future research and development for SISTeM and ISD, which will be explored in the concluding discussion.

It is necessary to recognise that SISTeM, in the light of ANT, can itself be seen as a non-human actant. Norton (1984) pre-dating Callon’s work attributes a nonhuman actant status to such soft approaches as SISTeM when he says ‘% the prime goal of soft analysis—like that of the technologist—is effective action % ‘soft systems analysis is thus a sort of social technology’. Secondly, when actant(s) within a network enrol SISTeM into it as a means of addressing what they see as a problematisation it does so by traversing an obligatory passage point (OPP). SISTeM is thus enrolled and translated to accommodate the requirements of the network via an OPP. Its actual real world manifestation when used is shaped by the relationships it has with the other actants in the network into which it has been enrolled and the problematisation to be addressed. ‘The actual methodologies used in soft systems projects are contingent upon the context, the use and the users of that methodology’ (Atkinson, 1986). SISTeM when enacted also enrols other methodological actants into the network, DFDs, object orientated design, simulation modelling, project management tools, RAD, processes reconfiguring and CASE tools, OD approaches and so on—they too are translated and subsequently mobilised.

In its enactment by the actants in the situation, SIS-TeM, if successful, facilitates a translation of the existing network, taking its current members and any newly enrolled actants, human, technological and methodological, through a series of obligatory passage points toward addressing presenting problematisations. These passage points take the form of SISTeM’s Cycles 1 and 2 debates and their resulting decisions, followed by the transformational integration of the new human and IS&IT actants within the organisational network and their mobilisations to realise the decided upon change.

In Cycle 1, stages 1.1 and 1.2, expressing and analysing the problem situation can be seen as moments of problematisation. The modelling and comparison stages 1.3 through 1.5, especially when participative creating ‘expressive models’, are moments of exclusion, inte´ressemment. Stage 1.6 in Cycle 1, is a further inte´ressemment as well as a moment of enrolment. This takes the form of a debate—‘bargaining and mutual concessions’ (Introna, 1997)—and decision making, based on robust accommodations between the actants and also SISTeM’s criteria. Stage 1.7 change is the mobilisation realised by Cycle 2. The IS&IT in the debate speaks through delegated human actants or for themselves through supplier demonstrations. The result of this is a high level identification of a future actant translation(s) into the network, so as to address its problems, to which powerful actants are now committed. An initial obligatory passage point is passed.

SISTeM Cycle 2, in its stages 2.1 to 2.8 facilitates similar operational ‘moments of translation and enrolment’, culminating in the twin obligatory passage points of a debated operational decision and a transformed, real-world human/machine actor network that addresses the problematisation. This is based upon the commitment to mobilisations forged in stage 2.8, the actant intentionality created in stage 2.9, and in stage 2.10, the translation and mobilisations of new and existing actants. This is not the end of course. Future problematisations will arise, external or internal to the network, against which further enrolments and translations of humans and non-human allies into the network are required if it is to persist. Having incorporated SISTeM into the network, it is there to meet any contingencies that arise, to identify and address problematisations, enrol and translate new actants into the network if necessary. An ‘in-network’ SISTeM is an established actant in its own right; there to facilitate future OPPs. Translating SISTeM into the network, and mobilising it when required will have given that network a problem solving and learning capability—but this, as we shall see later, raises problems for ANT, for SISTeM is capable of internal mutation and facilitating mutation of the network, of learning, even its unravelling.

One final point on SISTeM and ANT and the emergence of the concept of the human/machine activity. The tools and techniques of SISTeM can be seen acting as interlocutors, delegates, within problematic situations, ‘speaking’ in both cycles on behalf of existing or prospective human and non-human actants through the medium of its models. These models are underpinned by the concept of the human/machine activity system. This notion of the human/machine activity system was born out of the contingencies of an IS&T focused project (Atkinson, 1994, 1997) in which the client specified the use of a soft systems approach. The project’s aim was one of supporting a heterogeneous group of human and non-human actant stakeholders (Pouloudi & Whitley, 2000). Namely, clinicians, managers, IS&IT professionals, IS suppliers, their applications and technologies, hospitals and the NHS Executive to define the nature and use of the electronic patient record (EPR) and its associated IT in hospitals—as part of a UK national EPR multi-hospital demonstration project. The concept of the EPR was to be inscribed into the project initiation document (PID), (another non-human actant) within the projects contractual arrangements (further non-human actant). The contracts were intended to establish the formal relationships within the project actor-network between the NHS, the hospitals and the application suppliers. The extent to which the EPR within the various project sites was realised and its impact upon clinical practice and hospital management were then to be evaluated. The concept of the EPR would have a part in this evaluation process. SSM (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990) with its underlying concept of the purposeful human activity system had no modelling tools or stages that could be delegated to speak on behalf of associations of the human and the non-human. If it were to become enrolled as an actant in the EPR project it had to be translated and so the human/machine activity system was born—necessity being the mother of invention. This came about without any reference to ANT, driven purely by the demands of the problem situation in which a non-human actant’s voice, in particular the EPR and its IT architecture, had to be heard. Out of this process also arose SISTeM not only as a result of a translation of SSM within a single project but as more of a generic second generation mutation, formed out of a series of projects—we shall return to this later.

This section has illustrated how ANT can be used as an under-pinning framework for explaining SISTeM, its constituents, its use and the resulting interventions, aimed at bringing about a new integration of human and technological change within organisational problem situations. The next and concluding section summarises the study and explores future areas of development and the research that could result, and the role of ANT and contingency methodologies within that.

## Conclusions

This concluding discussion seeks to explore the relationship between SISTeM, contingency and ANT pointing to an agenda for further research and development; commencing with its contingency status.

SISTeM as it has been described here may be considered, in Avison and Fitzgerald’s (1999) terms, a contingency methodology in the four ways they identify as characteristic of such an approach—adaptability, integration, multdisciplinarity and containing competing paradigms. Firstly, it’s adaptability. SISTeM, as its soft systems heritage implies, and as witnessed in practice is adaptable too, and adapted by those who use them and by the substantive circumstances of its use. Through reflective action research and learning it also developed further—mutated. Its very emergence was as an adaptation of its progenitor SSM, stemming from its progenitor’s incapacity to deal with real-world contingencies in which technologies required a ‘voice’ (a role in ANT, attributed to a ‘delegate’ (Walsham, 1997)). SISTeM’s cycles, stages and tools can and have been ‘omitted, carried out in different sequences’ or ‘developed in more detail’ as required. Sometimes, in Mode 2 they have been used unconsciously. Secondly, integration. SISTeM actively seeks, through combining organisational and information systems development (O&ISD) to integrate human and technological development. Thirdly, ‘multidisciplinarity’. SISTeM actively merges with other disciplines and the tools within them, ISD and OD methodologies as well real world craft practices to address the contingencies of the problem situation. Fourth and finally SISTeM encompasses within it competing paradigms. These range from the formal/rational in its modelling and its IS&IT and process development, interpretavism (Hirschheim & Lyytinen, 1995) in its rich picture building and its expressive models and the social/political aspects of decision making and organisational transformation. Action learning is also a paradigm. SISTeM seeks to accommodate the incommensuralities and complimentarities in these paradigms within the analytic domain, its real world practices and especially through the human/machine activity systems underpinning framework. SISTeM in its multiple philosophies, its stages, its modes, its incorporation of tools is, it can be argued, following Avison and Fitzgerald (1999) a contingency methodology. It is also a hybrid. Its contingency status, the fact that it accommodates competing paradigms and disciplines, makes it a ‘monster’ (Haraway 1991). In ANT, SISTeM can be seen as a heterogeneous non-human actant of, as we shall see, an atypical kind.

In being a contingency approach SISTeM in itself and when enacted enrols and mobilises a heterogeneity of human and non-human actants; participants, cycles, stages, tools and techniques. It is a methodological actornetwork in its own right. One that is, itself, translated by enrolment into a real-world actor-network, made up of humans and non-humans to address its problem situation. Its enrolment into a network will have been driven by the need to address the contingencies of a problematisation, assisting the network to traverse necessary obligatory passage points. However contingency approaches, such as SISTeM, SSM and Multiview, are by their very nature altered by the conditions of their use. For, as with other ‘mobiles’ (Law, 1992) not only are they capable of transcending time and space, orchestrating necessary translations through obligatory passage points in many situations, they are capable also of being adapted by the network (Atkinson, 1986) to meet presenting contingencies. SISTeM is, unlike immutable mobiles (Law, 1992) such as software standards, the scientific method, the methodology of double blind trials or for that matter SSADM (Goodland & Slater, 1995) in that once imported into the network become fixed in form even whilst traversing time and space across the network. SISTeM is adaptive a ‘mutable mobile’, especially in mode 2. Its own emergence testifies to this.

However currently in ANT there is no such category of element as the mutable mobile, one that persists but also adapts or proacts. The emphasis in ANT is on the network’s continuance (Callon, 1991), its has a builtin resistance to change, through the immutability of it elements and by incorporation of other actants, if required, to meet contingencies. ANT’s root metaphor is that of the ‘black hole’, whose event horizon once traversed allows no escape and no adaptation, only enlargement, consolidation and irreversibility. This is very different from the notion of organic adaptation and change found within the contingency theory used to describe SISTeM earlier.

ANT has a powerful capacity to explain why persistent dissonance arises. For example those between the frozen discourses embodied in the data, dialogues and functionality of an IS and changing business practices and processes which it serves. It illuminates the issues that surround current ISD methodologies such as SSADM that focus on rational design of IS based on frozen-in-time user requirements, what Paul (1993) terms the ‘fixed point theorem’. ANT highlights the inertial issues large ERP systems present for their client in both the initial needs to reverse-engineer, translate the organization, so it fits the ERP. Conversely ANT also explains the subsequent inertia the ERP system creates when, at a later date, the organization has changed due to business contingencies. This was the very issue, in the case study above, where the governmental discourses built into the hospital legacy systems militating against effective information to support clinical practice. SIS-TeM was brought in, in part, to address this inertia. However ANT, apart from a strategy of incorporation, says little about how to facilitate inner reconfigurations of an existing network to meet the external and internal contingencies that arise within and between networks of actants, human and non-human. Nor does it say anything about the role of mutable mobile-actants such as SIS-TeM, that are themselves self-adaptive. But surely an actor-network, if it is to ensure its own survival in a rapidly changing context requires an inner capacity to, if not reverse, or unravel itself, then to mutate itself, radically if necessary. Sometimes in the face of change there is need to undermine its immutability, to free itself up for change, release some of its actors to join other networks as well as enrol new ones. Change means dealing with the issues of power, politics and processes that arise, and learn form that experience—to transform itself. All the concepts found within contingency theory and apply to SISTeM.

Persistence and adaption in human/machine networks are the very issues that, over the past three decades, have perplexed ISD and its contribution to organisational transformation. If ANT seems to offer an explanation for persistence and enrolment then contingency theory offers the idea of adaption and proaction. In which case a new conceptual hybrid suggests itself capable of describing the combination of both, namely that of the ‘contingent actor-network’ or the adaptive human/machine activity system. What implication does this concept have for real world practice in IS&T based organisational transformation and the nature and role of SISTeM within it?

Walsham (1997) in discussing ANT and its real world manifestations says ‘%we need methodologies and theoretical devices to enable us to think about hybrids of people and information technology.’ The author agrees but suggests that there is also a need to develop normative contingency approaches with which to learn how to, through experience, effectively form and intervene in such hybrids. What might this look like? An illustration based on the authors own experiences, serves to show how such an approach manifests itself in practice.

Examples of self-transforming contingency hybrid actor-networks have already been brought about in health care in instances where practices are orchestrated through clinical evidence-based protocols and care pathways. In this instance passing the obligatory passage point from non-protocolised to protocolised practice was driven by multi-organisational clinical bench-marking, a desire by the lead consultant to improve care and a new hospital IS infrastructure. The pathways were created by the clinical team members, drawing on international research data bases, via the internet, of evidence based practice and within discussion groups, as well as their own clinical experience.

When caring for patients the clinicians, and sometimes the patients, access the protocols and pathways, checking off care given and progress against them, noting exceptions with comments, forming an on-going patient record, a new ‘actant’, that mediates communication and co-ordination across the network through time and space (Berg, 1997). The record, protocols and pathways reside within active clinical IS accessed via hand-held technologies connected by telecommunications to the hospital/clinic central database. This environment not only tracks the care of an individual patient, but the IS also provides analysable aggregated data to support a ‘clinical audit learning cycle’ operated by the team. The audit results in self-directed improvements to clinical care by the team members and in the protocols on which it is based. The protocols/pathways are a further example of ‘mutable’ mobiles.

The creation of this care, value adding human/machine hybrid came about, not without considerable difficulty, through changes to both clinical practice and the introduction of new IS&T. It required an effectively facilitated participative contingency approach, SISTeM, in engaging a combination of insitu clinicians, in-house information professionals, customisable IS&T off-the-shelf applications and the hospitalwide network actants, taking them through an obligatory passage point of human and technological translation. An imbroglio of inter-professional, personal, political, power, cultural and process design issues as well as information requirements, flexible systems and technical architectures all featured in this development, and had to be addressed for the benefits of this transformation to be realised. Simply installing an application and then training, even prototyping using RAD would not have worked. Clinicians have powerful resources to block IT initiatives if they choose especially of it threatens their clinical prerogatives. The whole existing network of human and none human actants had to unravel. The ‘sacred cows’, personal clinical practices, professional roles and status, existing IS, had to be unravelled, adapted, as well as be replaced by the enrolment of new actants—namely the pathways, protocols, clinical IS& T. Staff left and were replaced, and a clinical pathways manager appointed. The network transformed itself by incorporation and adaption of itself. This was achieved by empowering all the actants. A voice was given to all the stakeholders, both human and none-human within the network (Pouloudi & Whitley, 2000). Also, establishing a strong internal clinical lead and providing them with the facilitated support, enrolling IS&T professionals with clinical domain knowledge. SISTeM mode 2 was employed and translated to their needs—including a new role in care pathway development. This not only resulted in a more effective clinical actor-network, with embedded IS, but also one with a capacity itself to deal with future contingencies as a mature adaptive actor-network. It exemplifies a ‘contingency actor-network’ approach at work. It also suggests an agenda for further research into development of such mutable mobiles as SISTeM and the role they may have, when once enrolled and translated to serve the needs of the contingency actor-networks of which they are a part.

If the current data-centric orthodoxy of ISD, with its separation of the machine and human, that takes little account of the individual or for that matter the social, except as sources of information requirements or endusers, is to be displaced then a paradigm change has to take place. A paradigm (Coombs, 1997) that results in methodologies capable of enrolling and translating existing IS&T approaches whilst orchestrating demonstrable value adding organisational transformations in excess of those offered by that of the current orthodoxy—and only on those grounds. Such approaches, in form and in function, could be based upon the ‘contingency actor-network’, capable of growing not building (and certainly not re-engineering) organisational self-transforming, human/machine hybrids in the real world. It is the goal to which the continuing research and development of SISTeM, and future hybrid contingency methodologies and practices needs to aspire.
