# Business environment and internet commerce benefit—a small business perspective

S Poon

School of Information Technology, Murdoch University, Murdoch, Western Australia, Australia 6150

Anecdotal evidence shows that the internet can enable a start up company to globalise and achieve multimillion dollar turnover in a couple of years. Beyond this handful of examples, small businesses find tha this is not always true. It seems that certain pre-conditions need to be in place before this is possible. We studied the business environment of a small business including its suppliers, customers, partner firms and competitors focusing on internet commerce adoption and usage. Among a sample of small firms in the nonmanufacturing sector, the impression of competitive advantage and information support relates positively to internet commerce benefit. Also, customer participation in internet commerce is critical to success. We therefore suggest that the nature of the business environment bears an effect on the benefit small businesses can gain from internet commerce.

## Introduction

The internet and the internet commerce era are both changing the way businesses look at globalisation and competition. Organizations are rushing to adopt some kind of internet commerce strategy ranging from electronic mail (or e-mail) to wide-ranging enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. Many have also converted their local internal networks into intranets—local internal network using the internet protocols. In many organizations, the web browser is the universal user interface to their corporation databases where information is now accessed via corporate webservers.

Small companies are also set to benefit from internet commerce. Some start up companies embracing e-commerce strategically have grown from being a small firm to an international multi-million dollar venture in a matter of months. However, it seems such success stories are not widespread. According to reports on small businesses and internet commerce (www.ausenet.com.au, www.noie.gov.au and www.yellowpages.com.au), it was suggested that small business adoption of internet commerce is still slow and many have difficulties realising the benefit as suggested by the media and in early research (Cronin, 1995).

The success of e-commerce and other inter-organisational systems depends on its participants. For example, a virtual bookshop, an internet-based supply chain management system and an internet portal can only be successful if customers prefer to use such systems over other media to transact, exchange and gather information. This is quite different from internal IT systems whereby success depends on internal users. Because of these characteristics, we decided to identify the external factors which determine internet commerce benefit. We were particularly interested in the uptake of internet commerce by suppliers and customers as well as adoption by competitors and business partners. Our focus was on small businesses because resource scarcity compels them to work closely with others (for example, customers, suppliers and partnering firms) to achieve business goals (Forrest, 1990). There have been government efforts to ensure small firms are not disadvantaged as a consequence of internet commerce (for example, the European Commission and Australia). This provides a further impetus for us to investigate the types of issues that should be the focus of such initiatives.

Recently there has been a growing body of literature in the area of small business internet commerce (SBIC) including longitudinal studies (Barker, 1994; Fuller & Jenkins, 1995; Barker et al, 1997), surveys (Lederer et al, 1996), usage evolution (Abell & Lim, 1996; Abell & Black, 1997; Sieber, 1996; Fink et al, 1997), multimethod studies (Poon & Swatman, 1996, 1997, 1999), international marketing and export (Hamill & Gregory, 1997; Bennett, 1997), advertising (Auger & Gallaugher, 1997) and business re-engineering (ITI, 1998). In general, these studies show small businesses can benefit from internet commerce but specific conditions need to be satisfied in order to achieve any benefit.

For example, the management of expectation (Barker et al, 1997) is important. The lack of benefit may be due to unrealistic expectations rather than unrealised benefit. Also, small companies need to realise that in addition to a website, there are other logistical issues that need to be considered when an internet commerce venture takes off. Because on the internet bad publicity travels much faster (for example, on a mailing list), one has to evaluate the amount of resources needed to cope with such growth.

Small businesses tend to form alliances (or networks) in order to pool limited resources for large projects (Forrest, 1990). Network members often have complementary resources so the total benefit is larger than the sum of individual parts. Members of a small business network may include partner firms and competitors in the same industry sector and suppliers and customers in the value system. Figure 1 illustrates this concept in the context of a small business network. Although the internet cannot deliver physical goods, it can improve the delivery process by facilitating information flow. Indeed the internet can support information flow along both dimensions based on Figure 1. Activities within the value system which can be supported by internet commerce include ordering, delivery, customer enquiries, service support and other supply chain management activities (Benjamin & Wigand, 1995; Rayport & Sviokla, 1995). Within the industry sector, the internet can support activities such as joint bids for tenders, group work, sector-wide information sharing (for example, standards and specifications) and virtual chambers of commerce. ## Experiencing internet commerce benefit

Internet commerce benefit can be measured in a variety of ways. For example, it can be measured in change of financial turnover since adopting internet commerce. It can also be quantified based on increase in market share, number of customers, business activities and sales enquiries among others. Although these measurements are useful and they provide quantitative data on the actual activities, they are not feasible due to the following reasons:

Small businesses are in general not good at keeping precise marketing and business statistics as in the case of large organizations. Consequently, it is often fruitless to ask for such statistics from small businesses.

Even if a small percentage of small businesses maintain such information, it is unlikely they are readily available to researchers due to suspicious intention of use. This suggestion is reflected in the lack of financial figures among earlier studies in small business IT (Montazemi, 1988) of which no financial figures were used.

Therefore, instead of asking for actual figures, we decided to qualify benefit in terms of personal experience. Admittedly, this approach has certain shortcomings and can be criticised as subjective and lack of quantitative evidence. However, this is the most workable solution. We devised four benefit indicators. These indicators were implemented using five-point Likert scales (5–most positive experience; 1–least positive experience) and they are:

I Obtain competitive advantage over non-online competitors.

I Improved business performance since participating in internet commerce activities.

I Improved supplier/customer relationship since participating in internet commerce activities.

Current benefits will continue if participation in internet commerce continues.

These four indicators were intended to measure four different aspects of benefit including the current and future view.

The indicator ‘obtain competitive advantage over nononline competitors’ was used to measure the gain of competitive advantage within an industry sector. We admit that this only measured the perception of the respondent rather than the actual figures, but we hoped the experience of those who had been online long enough would provide information reflecting the reality.

The second indicator ‘improved business performance since participating in internet commerce activities’, measured business performance improvements in the absence of financial figures, which we foresaw having difficulties collecting based on previous studies (Montazemi, 1998). This would provide us with some indications on financial performance.

The third indicator ‘improved supplier/customer relationship since participating in internet commerce activities’, measured experience in the value system. Improvement in relationship is difficult to qualify except through measurement of returning customers or surveying actual customer opinions. Both of these required the respondent to keep record but hardly any respondent had been doing so.

The fourth indicator ‘current benefits will continue if participation in internet commerce activities continues’, measured the futuristic view of success based on current experience. Given the newness of internet commerce, we expected some respondents might not have had experienced benefit but could see it would happen. We believed this would provide us the understanding on the future of internet commerce.

The industry sector dimension of a business network Studies of SBIC show that uptake of internet commerce within an industry sector can affect the adoption strategy of others in the same sector (Barker et al, 1997; Poon, 1998). The active use of such technology to communicate and interact beyond passive adoption may provide further benefit.

To investigate if uptake of internet commerce within an industry sector had an effect on benefit, four hypotheses were formulated. They were: percentage of competitors participating in internet commerce, internet as a preferred communication medium, experience competitive advantage in internet commerce activities, and the internet provides information support.

Critical mass is often considered important for internet commerce adoption. Sometimes a business adopts a certain competitive strategy as a direct response to a competitor’s action rather than on the basis of competitive analysis. Because of peer pressure, we speculated that as the number of online competitor increases, so would the benefit of being online. We formulated Hypothesis IC1 as:

Hypothesis IC1: The percentage of competitors online has an effect on the experience of internet commerce benefit.

One of the most commonly used features of the internet is email and it is often used for communication. In fact e-mail also allows document transfer and exchange as well as group discussion. There are many advantages of using e-mail to communicate, for example it is nonintrusive, asynchronous and is efficient for keeping written records. However, communication is a two-way process and if internet communication is not widely used within a business sector, then its benefit will be diminished. We examined this by comparing the experiences of those who preferred to use the internet to communicate and those who did not with a control group who did not have a preference. We formulated Hypothesis IC2 as:

Hypothesis IC2: Small businesses in industry sectors that prefer internet-based communication experience more internet commerce benefit.

In addition to the percentage of competitors online, the actual experience of competitive advantage was another differentiator used to examine the experience of internet commerce benefit. We were curious as to whether simply having a high percentage of competitors online might not necessitate competitive pressure until there was tangible evidence that competitors were gaining leverage from internet commerce activites. We formulated Hypothesis IC3 as:

Hypothesis IC3: Small businesses which experience competitive advantage experience more internet commerce benefit.

Small businesses often do not have time to gather information and sometimes this leads to poor decision making (Clemens, 1992). The internet has a wealth of information but the quality varies significantly. Nonetheless, there are many good quality information sources that are distributed freely. This saves small businesses the effort to acquire them physically. For example, census information (www.abs.gov.au) is downloadable and also research conducted by international bodies such as the OECD (www.oecd.org). Media and broadcasting companies are also providing their services over the internet (for example, abc.com). It is likely that small businesses which find the internet useful for information support will experience more benefit. We formulated Hypothesis IC4 as:

Hypothesis IC4: Small businesses which find the internet is good for information support experience more internet commerce benefit.

## The value system dimension of a business network

Studies of inter-organisational systems and electronic markets reveal that parties on a value-chain can use their power to influence business relationships (Cash, Jr, 1987; Malone et al, 1989; Konsynski & McFarlan, 1990; Konsyrski, 1992; Webster, 1994). For example, a large car manufacturer can coerce its small suppliers to adopt prescribed inter-organisational technology through power dominance (Webster, 1994).

The inter-organisational technologies discussed in these studies were either proprietary, custom-built or with narrow applicability (for example, Malone et al, 1989). Internet commerce is quite different because the technology is usually non-proprietary and with wide applicability. Such technology renders traditional entry and exit barriers mentioned in earlier studies meaningless.

A value system consists of a firm, its supplier and customer linked by a value-chain (Porter & Millar, 1985). As internet commerce activities often happen between two or more businesses (except in the case of intranets), the involvement of parties outside a firm is crucial. Logically, the more firms participating in internet commerce, the further the benefit goes. This can be due to more efficient ways of sharing information, faster communication, lower transaction costs, or better accessibility to databases. Therefore, we postulated if the percentage of customers/suppliers involved in internet commerce increases, the more benefit a firm will experience. This is due to two reaons:

There are certain overheads associated with internet commerce activities (for example, infrastructure, software, etc.). Once the infrastructure is laid, the higher the percentage of customer/supplier online, the more cost effective internet commerce becomes.

The higher the percentage of customers/suppliers involved in internet commerce, the more incentive for other customers/suppliers to join in and hence creates a snow-balling effect. This will increase internet commerce activities exponentially.

On this basis, we formulated two hypotheses to test the postulation. Hypothesis VC1 and VC2 examine the relationship between the percentage of customers/suppliers online and the effect on internet commerce benefit.

Using Hypothesis VC1, we examined the percentage of suppliers online and the experience of internet commerce benefit. The logic is similar to that employed in Hypothesis IC1:

Hypothesis VC1: The percentage of suppliers online positively effects the experience of internet commerce benefit.

We are also set up Hypothesis VC2 to examine the effect of actual engagement of internet commerce activities. We suspected that just having a web presence without actual value activities occurring would not give real internet commerce benefit and this would show up in the differences between Hypothesis VC1 and VC2.

Hypothesis VC2: The percentage of suppliers actually engaged in internet commerce positively impact on the experience of internet commerce benefit.

The mirror image of Hypothesis VC1 and VC2 were also examined along the customer side of the value system. The intention was to find out whether there would be any differences between the outcome from the supplier side of the value system and the customer side. Such differences might exist due to the asymmetric relationship on either side of the value system. For example, if a small business is not in the manufacturing sector, then the amount of value activities with its suppliers would be lower than that of its customers. In such cases, we would expect the percentage of customers online is more important than the percentage of suppliers online when it comes to predicting internet commerce benefit. Hypothesis VC3 is the mirror image of Hypothesis VC1:

Hypothesis VC3: The percentage of customers online affects the experience of internet commerce benefit.

And the mirror image of Hypothesis VC2 is Hypothesis VC4:

Hypothesis VC4: The percentage of customers actually engaged in internet commerce positively impact on the experience of internet commerce benefit.

## Method and analysis

## The study sample

The sample was collected from a number of internet directory services advertising Australian small firms (for example, under the country category Australia or have an Australian contact telephone number). The sample consists of 224 small businesses each having a webpage and an e-mail address. In this study, a small firm is one with less than 100 employees (DIST, 1995).

A survey instrument in the form of a questionnaire (see Appendix) was developed and part of it contains questions relevant to the four hypotheses. A total of 224 questionnaires were sent and 67 collected were useable.

This constitutes a 30% response rate which compares favourably with other studies on similar topic areas (Abell & Lim, 1996; Auger & Gallaugher, 1997). We divided the sample into six industry sectors (see Table 2). Firms belonging to the internet and related sector (for example, internet service providers, website developers, etc) were deliberately excluded from the sample because their business nature would have biased the results.

The average number of years online was two, indicating the responses were experience-based. A high percentage of the respondents were from the knowledgebased and service sectors that do not physically manufacture goods. Although every effort had been made to ensure a non-biased sample, there seems to be a low response rate from the tourism and manufacturing sector. A high percentage (80%) of micro-sized businesses with less than 20 persons were in the sample. A total of 48% of the respondents claimed to have international customers, suggesting that many engaged in export activities.

The issue of non-response was a concern and could be due to a number of reasons:

Too many surveys asking similar questions: Currently there are hundreds if not thousands of surveys being carried out by different groups ranging from commercial to academic studies. The internet business community is flooded with surveys which bring little value to them and have become an annoyance.

Not a value-adding activity: In our case, although respondents were promised a report for their participation, many might have perceived this as nonvalue-adding. Given that small business owners have little time for non-core activities and it is easier to discard a questionnaire without trace electronically, it is not surprising that those who did not fill out the questionnaire while first reading it were unlikely to come back to do so.

Not willing to tell the truth: Whether a small business is successful or not in internet commerce, there is little incentive to elaborate on the situation. If a small business is successful, the knowledge of how to achieve it is valuable and therefore may not want to reveal it. If a small business has not been successful, then it is very likely that they will have little to tell.

It is important to investigate the non-respondents further to find out more about their internet commerce stories. We propose such investigation by asking them the key reason for not responding to provide at least some clues on the hidden issues. This will be carried out as part of the future research.

## The effect of industry sector on internet commerce benefit

Correlation analysis was carried out to examine whether the percentage of competitors online has a direct effect on internet commerce benefit (Hypothesis IC1). By correlating each of the benefit indicators (in Likert scale) against the percentage of competitors online, it could show if the increase in percentage of competitors online would have an effect on experiencing internet commerce benefit. There is, however, a reliability issue which is the percentage of competitors online is a perception of the respondents, not a head-count of the businesses within an industry, which should be ideally the more accurate measurement. To obtain detailed statistical information on the percentage of businesses online is itself a research project which demands significant resources.

The result shows that as the percentage of competitors online increases, there is evidence that a small firm would feel more competitive than its non-online competitors and had improved supplier/customer relationship. Although specific details of how being online increases competitive advantage were not recorded but extra opportunities a small firm gained through the internet might be the reason. However, there is no evidence that an increased percentage of competitors online leads to better performance or instills confidence in future benefit. Together this suggests that the kind of competitive advantage experienced by small firms in the sample might not have translated into actual financial reward.

Table 4 shows that whether the internet is a preferred medium of communication it had no significant effect on internet commerce benefit, although those who preferred to use it gave slightly more positive responses overall. This is unexpected because one would have thought those who preferred to use e-mail would have found it convenient. It also reinforces the importance of traditional communication technology.

In addition to the percentage of competitors on the Internet, the experience of competitive advantage from internet commerce was investigated (Hypothesis IC3). This was done in addition to Hypothesis IC1 because a high percentage of competitors online might not automatically translate into competition unless competitors are actually gaining leverage from internet use. The question solicited a ‘yes’ or ‘no’ answer and responses were categorised into two groups. We employed T-test for analysis to compare the means between the two groups for any significant differences in benefit across all four indicators.

The result shows a significant difference in benefit between the two groups (see Table 5). Those who experienced competitive advantage had more positive responses than those who did not, suggesting that this is an important influence of benefit. Together with the result from Table 3, we suggest that actually experiencing competitive advantage is a more accurate predictor of benefit than how many competitors are online. The management implication of this is that one does not just go online due to a flock syndrome but only after finding out how to gain leverage from internet commerce. However, the time lag between having competitors online and then losing out to them is getting shorter.

Quality information is key to shaping the strategic direction of a small firm (Clemens, 1992). One of the important functions of internet commerce is to allow the exchange of business information. Again T-test was used to compare the means between the ‘yes’ and ‘no’ group to observe any significant differences. The result from testing Hypothesis IC4 indicates that those who found the internet useful for information support also experienced more benefit (see Table 6). The difference between the two groups is significant across all four indicators, suggesting that quality information support is a key to internet commerce success. The result once again highlights the importance of the internet as an information source. Further research needs to be carried out to determine the type of information gathered and how the use of such information translates into benefit.

## The effect of value system on internet commerce benefit

In order to examine the validity of Hypothesis VC1 to VC4, multiple regression modelling was applied to find out the strongest predictor(s) of internet commerce benefit. We employed multiple-regression analysis for two reasons: (1) to simultaneously observe the change in percentage of suppliers/customers online had on the experience of benefit among the sample; and (2) to detect the comparative strength of each of the predictors along the value system and identify the strongest influence(s) within the value system. The result in Table 7 proves that the value system has an effect on internet commerce benefit.

Table 7 shows that the model has low P-values (bottom row, generally ,0.005) over all four dependent variables and reasonable R-values across, suggesting that the model has good predictability. Among the four independent variables, the percentage of customers participating in internet commerce has a consistent effect on all benefit indicators whereas the other three variables have insignificant effects.

## The lack of influence from the upstream of the value system

An important observation from the result in Table 7 is that there is a lack of influence from the upstream of the value system on internet commerce benefit. This can be explained by the fact that many in the sample are not traditional manufacturing companies, therefore the need to constantly interact with suppliers is not there. Consequently, the percentage of suppliers online and the level of suppliers’ involvement in internet commerce have little relevance to how much benefit they can get from internet commerce. We expect if there was a high percentage of manufacturing firms within the sample, the outcome might have been different. In any case, this supports our postulation that the inflow of benefit from internet commerce could be different from the two ends (supplier and customer) of the value system and this is supported by other studies (Poon & Swatman, 1999).

## The strong influence of customer participation on internet commerce benefit

The result in Table 7 singles out the importance of customer participation in internet commerce to internet commerce benefit. The message to us is that if customers are active in carrying out internet commerce, then a small firm is likely to find itself more competitive, performs better, develops better customer relationship and even has stronger confidence in the future of internet commerce. Just having a high percentage of customers online would allow a small firm to develop better relationships with is customers.

The strong influence from the downstream of the value system suggested that small businesses in this sample depend more on their customers than their suppliers and hence if the downstream value chain can be made more efficient, there is a bigger benefit to a small business. The message for non-manufacturing small businesses that are or will be engaging in internet commerce is to dedicate a significant amount of resources into developing internet commerce activities with their customers. If the virutal value chain can effectively support transaction, information sharing and exchange and joint work, it will improve the bottom line of a small firm and for many small firms this is important for their survival.

For small manufacturing firms, effort should also be dedicated to the upstream of the value system because of their frequent interaction with suppliers. Whether the effect of suppliers’ involvement in internet commerce among manufacturing firms is as critical in bringing in benefit is not studied in this project but will be part of our future research.

## Conclusion

The overall result from our study indicates that among those who have been online for about two years, there is evidence that internet commerce has been providing benefit to its adopters. By rationalising a small business network into an industry sector dimension and a value system dimension, we managed to examine the factors which lead to internet commerce benefit. Along the industry sector dimension, actual experience of competitive advantage and quality information support are key to internet commerce benefit. Whereas along the value system dimension, the percentage of customers who participate in internet commerce is the most significant factor.

We admit that there are many other factors in both dimensions which had not been explored in this study. We believe the outcome from this study helps to focus future research efforts on this topic. We also admit that the sample used in this study was biased towards nonmanufacturing firms and the reason for non-response was not thoroughly explored. However, this should not discount the validity of the outcome.

We hope the outcome of this study will help to focus current and future initiatives by governments and international bodies in helping small businesses to become beneficiaries of internet commerce.

Our future research will further investigate the types of benefit gained from internet commerce and whether these are sensitive to organisational context.
