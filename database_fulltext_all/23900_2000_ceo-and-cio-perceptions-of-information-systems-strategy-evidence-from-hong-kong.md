# CEO and CIO perceptions of information systems strategy: evidence from Hong Kong

LA Tai and R Phelps

Surrey European Management School, University of Surrey, Guildford GU2 5XH, UK

Studies in the literature indicate that many organizations have experienced a crisis of confidence in information systems (IS) functions and IS executives. We address the issue of joint chief executive/IT executive responsibility in building a corporate culture to overcome resistance to technological changes. This paper reports a study of differences in IT perceptions between chief executives and IS executives. The results suggest a similarity of views in three major dimensions of perception: IT vision, organizational IS issues and IT support for knowledge management. The study also suggests that national culture, industry type and the chief executive/IT executive relationship contribute to differences in IT perception. European Journal of Information Systems (2000) 9, 163–172.

## Introduction

Previous studies have indicated that many organizations have experienced a crisis of confidence in their information systems (IS) functions and in their chief information officers (CIOs) (Feld & Marmol, 1994), and that unsuccessful information technology (IT) projects are due to conflicts between CEOs and IS executives. These conflicts, in turn, may result from differences in their visions of IT and in their views of organizational IS issues (Feeny et al, 1992; Remius, 1997). Ward and Peppard (1996) note that these conflicts have often been explained by cultural differences between CEOs and CIOs and opine that often ‘the ‘trouble’ has no foundation beyond a level of perceptions’. This paper presents evidence on differing perceptions at the level of strategic IT, that is, at the level of technology choice and business alignment. It does not cover the operational aspects of IT such as implementation, change, management and skills that may also impact perceived IT success.

Schein (1992) examined the relationship between organizational culture and IT. He concluded that top management and the IT community could be viewed as two subcultures, each holding different perceptions of IT. The IT community were found to be more system/machine oriented people, whereas top management were more people oriented. Lederer and Mendelo (1988) studied issues facing IS managers in convincing top management of the potential of IS and concluded that top management failed to recognise the strategic impact of IS due to lack of awareness with IT and lack of confidence in IT. DeLisi (1990) and Schein (1992) found that IT supports empowerment of individuals and may clash with control-oriented cultures of top managers. A stream of work on the relationship between CEOs and CIOs (Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Feeny et al, 1992; Raghunathan, 1992) indicates the importance of this relationship for successful IS. A recent survey indicated that the relationship between CEOs and CIOs is getting worse: 70% of managers said that they would discard their IS department; 58% said that ‘talking to their IS department is a source of regular anxiety’ (Forbes, 1996). These studies underline differences in IS perceptions between CEOs and IS executives. Similarity or divergence of views between CEOs and CIOs affects the guidance given on the use of IT and previous studies have indicated that top management guidance is an important factor for creating an IT culture that in turn affects successful IT implementation (Cragg & King, 1993; Levine & Rossmoore 1993; King & Teo, 1994, 1996; Benamati et al, 1997). Previous studies have variously been concerned with CEOs and senior managers on the general management side and CIOs or senior IT executives on the IT side of the relationship. For brevity in the remainder of this paper we use the term CEO to refer to senior general management and CIO to refer to senior IT executives.

Pervan (1998) noted that previous studies have almost all been concerned solely with IS executives and has proposed examining the difference in IT perceptions between CEOs and CIOs (exceptions to this trend include Earl (1993) and Galliers et al (1994)). Moynihan (1990) in one study contrasting CEO and CIO views reported that they held similar views on organizational IS issues but not on technological IS issues. Nelson and Cooprider (1996) report evidence that while aspects of relationships (trust and influence) impinge on IS performance, these effects are mediated through shared knowledge which has the direct effect on performance. Earl and Feeny (1994) and Feld and Marmol (1994) suggest that differences in IS priorities between CEOs and CIOs are at the root of many IS problems. Studying the differences in perceptions of IT between CEOs and CIOs indicates the degree to which knowledge and priorities regarding IS are shared and therefore contributes an important element to our understanding of the effective use of IS.

The studies cited above were conducted in the Western cultural context. Hence, it is interesting to study the robustness of these results in other cultural settings where differences may be expected. For example Bensaou and Earl (1998) found that Japanese managers took a very different view of technology to their Western counterparts and both technology and business managers shared a common viewpoint on the use of IT. This paper looks at Chinese and Western organizational cultures as represented in Hong Kong where both local and multinational organizations are well represented. Ma (1996) interviewed CIOs in Hong Kong and concluded that CIOs both encountered difficulties identifying top management objectives and felt that their CEOs and senior managers were too busy to look at critical IS management issues. These findings suggest that differences in IS perceptions exist among CEOs and IT executives in Hong Kong.

This paper is structured as follows. The next section grounds this study in a theoretical debate by developing and motivating a model incorporating the dimensions of IT strategy impacted by CEO/CIO agreement (and hence affecting perceived IT performance) and the intermediating dimensions that affect the CEO/CIO agreement level. The following section deals with the methodology of the empirical study designed to collect evidence on the variables in the model and to test the underlying hypotheses regarding the relationship between the intermediating variables and the degree of CEO/CIO agreement. The results are then presented, and the paper concludes with a discussion summarising the results and their implications that also notes some limitations of the study presented here.

## Dimensions of IS strategy

The research issue of differences in IS perceptions between CEOs and CIOs raises a subsidiary question: perceptions about what aspects of IS? Some studies of IS issues have adopted an open approach inviting respondents to offer their own views on issues that they see as important. Since we are concerned here with a comparative study we have chosen to present a range of specified IS issues, following other comparative studies (eg Pervan, 1998). This study builds on Pervan (1998) who divides IS issues into four categories: strategic management covers the vision and business impact aspects of IS (Galliers et al, 1994; Jones et al, 1995); technology management and systems development and data maintenance are two categories covering the technical side of IS; people and support management covers human resources, communication and education aspects of IS (Jones et al, 1995). However, we have found it difficult to obtain agreement on a precise distinction between technology management and systems development and data maintenance; further Pervan’s (1998) study found only one communication and education issue among the top ten IS issues chosen by CEOs: organizational learning, presumably reflecting the widespread (and therefore strategic) impact this could have throughout the organization (Jones et al, 1995). For simplicity and clarity we correspondingly examine the perceptions of CEOs and CIOs with respect to three dimensions of IT strategy that we label IT vision, technological IT issues and IT support for knowledge management.

In Figure 1 we set out a theoretical model of the hypothesised relationship between an outcome, perceived IT performance, and three determinants that are CEO/CIO similarity of perceptions on each of the three dimensions of IT strategy: IT vision, technological IT issues and IT support for knowledge management. Similarity of perceptions on each of these dimensions is hypothesised to be intermediated by three environmental dimensions: industry, national culture and quality of managerial relationships. The following sections motivate and ground the choice of these dimensions in the theoretical model.

## IT vision

We adopt Schein’s (1989) concept of IT vision as referring to the main organizational impact that IT systems are intended to have. According to Mintzberg and Westley (1992) IT projects require a visionary leader to influence changes and to develop cultural readiness. Feeney et al (1992) found IT vision to be the most important discriminator in their study of CEO/CIO relationships. Similarly Earl and Feeney (1994) placed a shared IT vision in the centre of their model of added value from IS. Schein (1989) developed four IT vision statements to categorise attitude to IT.

Schein (1989) states that organizations with a vision to automate look at their organizations from a manufacturing point of view and are preoccupied with cost and technological issues. Organizations with a vision to ‘informate up’ see IT as an management control tool. Organizations with a vision to ‘informate down’ are willing to push ‘knowledge power’ down in the organization. Organizations with a vision to transform view IT as a tool to make organizations more centralised around basic strategies and goals and more decentralised around implementation and control.

The purpose of building an IT vision is to create a clear statement about the IT organization and its role to support the business. Its function is to ensure that all members of the organization are heading in the same direction through the change process (Murray & Hardin, 1991). Conflicts in IT vision between CEO and CIO can be expected to lead to conflicting signals and a lack of direction (Earl & Feeney, 1994).

## Technological issues

We define technological issues as the choice and management of hardware, software, communications and data systems. Perception of technical IS issues may affect both the technologies deployed and the way in which they are implemented. A number of surveys have been carried out to poll mainly CIOs and IS executives about the technical issues seen as high priority (Moynihan, 1990; Burn et al, 1993; Brancheau et al, 1996; Moores, 1996). With the exception of some changes in descriptive categories between studies and some changes in issues over time, these studies in general find consistent sets of high priority technical issues such as development of data and information architectures and implementation of networks and telecommunications. Earl (1989) reported that general managers were less interested in architectural planning or coping with new technologies. Similarly Moynihan (1990) reported that CIOs placed more emphasis on technical issues. A lack of shared priorities here may adversely affect IS strategy.

## Knowledge management

We define knowledge management (KM) as the ability to store and retrieve information (organizational knowledge) relevant to tasks and decision processes within the organization. Organizational learning occurs through the creation of organizational knowledge. According to Nonaka (1994), knowledge is created through tacit and explicit knowledge (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Greater organizational learning can occur when organizational memory is electronically stored for future reference (Balasubramanian, 1996). A recent study from the American Productivity and Quality Center indicates that organizations engaged in knowledge management do rely on their IT infrastructure (American Productivity and Quality Center, 1997). Mason (1993) suggests that IS for knowledge acquisition can be categorised into market research systems, competitive intelligence systems for assimilating knowledge from outside the organization, and executive information or decision-support systems for creating new knowledge from interpretation of information.

But KM can be seen as a matter of people or process more than of IT (Sveiby, 1995). Skyrme (1997) studied the factors in successful knowledge programmes and concluded that successful programmes involved information management skills and change agents as well as IT knowledge. Correspondingly, it is suggested that IS executives and CEOs need to share a perception of the role of IT (and people and processes) in KM.

## Determinants of IS perceptions

## National culture

This paper looks at firms with both Western and Chinese cultural orientations as found in Hong Kong. IT enables managers and employees to share information. This concept contradicts traditional Chinese cultural traditions in which information is fundamentally a personal asset rather than an organizational resource (Martinsons, 1994). Power is maintained by controlling key information and is ‘stored’ in soft form, that is, in the minds of top and senior Chinese managers instead of being shared among organizational members (Martinsons, 1994). In addition, Chinese rely more on personal and verbal forms of intra-organizational communication (Redding, 1990). These characteristics are different to those of Western culture. Watson et al (1997) studied key IS issues between 1988 and 1992 in ten nations including Hong Kong and identified reasons for the differences and similarities of issues across countries based on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions (Hofstede, 1991), economic structure, political and legal environments, and technological status. The findings indicated that, in general, countries with different cultures had different IT perceptions and priorities. Hence it is hypothesised that IT perceptions among CEOs and IS executives are affected by the type of culture (in particular Western or Chinese) dominant in their firm.

## Industry

Porter and Millar’s matrix model predicts the impact of IT for different industries (Porter & Millar, 1985) and implies that IT will have a high impact on industries with high information content and intensity. Thus, organizations with high information content and intensities will be more likely to use IT strategically. Schein (1989) suggested CEOs from information intensive firms, such as financial services and telecommunications industries, have higher levels of insight into IT. In contrast, in manufacturing industry CEOs appeared to view IT with scepticism. Hence industry information intensity may affect perceptions of IT.

## Managerial relationships

Earl and Feeny (1994) suggest that CIOs who develop excellent relationships are more likely to develop addedvalue IT systems. Feeny et al (1992) studied the factors affecting the relationship between the CEO and the CIO. They conclude that CEOs with excellent relationships with their CIOs have a shared vision of IT for business transformation. Karimi et al (1996) concludes that IT leaders who have two-way conversations with CEOs have a better understanding of the goals, objectives and direction of the firm. This, in turn, narrows the gap between their perceptions of IT. Thus, it is hypothesised that the CEO/CIO relationship may affect perceptions of IT.

This study has two objectives. First, to measure the gaps in IT perceptions between CEOs and CIOs in each of the three dimensions of IT strategy discussed above. Second, to test the hypotheses regarding the effect of the intermediating dimensions (culture, industry, relationships) on the perceptual gaps. We do not attempt to measure the perceived success of IT in the organization nor to test the hypotheses represented as dotted lines in Figure 1, between CEO/CIO perception gaps and perceived IT success. Measuring perceived IT success is empirically problematic given the variety of stakeholders whose perceptions need to be taken into account and the need to deal with groups holding conflicting views regarding success. We therefore do not define perceived IT success in this paper. We return to these aspects of the model in discussing potential future research.

## Methodology

Data was collected using a questionnaire with five parts. The first part of the questionnaire covered background company information such as nature of business, company size, number of IT staff and national culture of the CEO. The second part of the questionnaire included four sections: vision of the role of IT, importance of IS issues, importance of IT for knowledge management and the CEO/CIO relationship.

Section 1 covered the vision of IT. Four questions were used to assess respondents’ reaction to Schein’s ‘IT vision statements’.

Section 2 covered the importance of technological IS issues. Questions were based on Moores’ (1996) top 10 IS issues. Moores (1996) investigated the importance of IS issues in Hong Kong for the year 1994 and over the next five years, 1995 to 1999. The results were similar to those found by Burn et al (1993). The seven questions covered: use of data resources, quality of software development, developing an information architecture, security and control, telecommunications, adapting to new technology and measuring IS effectiveness/productivity.

Section 3 covered the importance and acceptance/ usefulness of IT for knowledge management. Three questions were used to rank the importance of people, process and technology when implementing IT for knowledge management. The purpose was to determine if technology was viewed as an extension of knowledge management. Eight questions were used to measure acceptance of current IT tools in terms of ‘usefulness’ for KM activities.

Section 4 measured employer and IS manager relationship. The closeness of the relationship was measured using three items: perceived quality of relations, direct or indirect reporting to the CEO and the number of annual meetings between CEO and CIO/IS executive.

The participants of the survey were chosen by quasirandom selection (choosing every nth item from an ordered list) from a list compiled from two directories: Key Decision-Makers in Hong Kong Businesses 1998 and Duns Hong Kong Market Guide 1998. Quasi-random sampling mirrors the proportions in different industry sectors and allows for different information intensities (Feeny et al, 1992). The survey was carried out in August 1998. All participants holding the position of CEO or equivalent and CIO or IT manager were selected for the sampling frame. Since previous experience suggests that CEOs have very low response rates, questionnaires were sent out to 628 CEOs and 376 CIOs/IT executives based on returns from a pilot study suggesting 10% CEO responses and 20% CIOs. Paired CEO/CIO responses would have been preferred but the implied matched pair response rate of only 2% would impose important logistical difficulties and very significant nonresponse bias.

Therefore, following the methodology adopted by previous studies (Moynihan, 1990) this study focused on the differences in IT perceptions between CEOs and CIOs as separate groups. The responses to questions in sections 1– 4 were in the form of Likert scales with a range of 1–5. The average score for each question over the responses within a group was calculated and used to form rankings of perceived importance of IT issues.

## Results

The number of responses received totalled 156 of which 139 (63 CEOs and 76 CIOs/IT executives) were useable for analysis. A high proportion of respondents were three major industry groups in Hong Kong: 23% from finance, 20% manufacturing, and 16% wholesale and retail industries. These three groups were used to check for industry effects on IT perceptions. The remaining responses included construction, IT, media, transport, hotels and real estate. The majority of respondents were small to medium sized companies. The percentage of respondents employing under fifteen IT staff was 15%, 12% employed 15–30 and 13% employed over 30 IT staff. Sixty-eight respondents (31 CEOs and 37 CIOs) described their firm as local, and 71 respondents (32 CEOs and 39 CIOs) described their firms as foreign. A total of 73% of local firm CEOs were Chinese and 69% of foreign firm CEOs were Caucasian. Thus the local and foreign firm descriptors appeared to represent Chinese and Western cultural groups and were used as a proxy for national culture.

## Discussion

The study suggests that overall there is no significant difference between CEO and CIO perceptions of IT vision, organizational IT issues or the use of IT for KM. Compared with reports of divergent views and priorities found in the literature based on Western cultural environments, this evidence from Hong Kong paints a more optimistic picture. As was illustrated in the analyses, the contingent factors do appear to have influence over the degree of similarity of CEO and CIO perceptions. These influences provide some implications for management.

Differences between Chinese and Western firms suggest that IT perceptions are affected by culture. Greater divergence in IT vision perceptions was evident in Chinese firms, with their CEOs insisting on the use of IT to maintain their hierarchical control. For knowledge management tools Western firms showed more divergence in perceptions, perhaps resulting from a more devolved control culture with less uniformity of thinking. These findings support Martinsons’ (1994) proposition that Chinese managers maintain power by controlling key information rather than sharing it within the organization.

Differences between industries found in this study were based on small sample sizes and should be treated with caution. Nonetheless, the results suggest that industry type does affect the degree of similarity of IT perceptions. For both IT vision and IT issues finance and manufacturing showed high similarity while retail had low similarity. Although this pattern was not evident for knowledge management tools, and although this pattern does not precisely fit with the theoretical expectations from an information intensity model, finance (with the highest intensity) does emerge with the highest level of similarity.

Differences between CEO/CIO relationships suggest that good relationships and direct reporting may improve the similarity of IT perceptions. However the associations in the data are not particularly strong: good relationships appeared to impact all three dimensions of IT strategy but only gave a strong effect for IT issues; direct reporting appeared to be weakly linked to IT issues and tools for knowledge management.

Together these results have a number of implications for management. They indicate that firms should pay attention to cultural factors (notably a control oriented culture) that can impede the formation of a joint CEO/CIO IT strategy, that they can learn from best practice in other industries, particularly those with high information intensities, and that close co-operation between CEO and CIO as reflected in contact quality and frequency and by placing the CIO as a direct report to the CEO can improve the chances of building a shared IT strategy.

In addition to the general implications drawn above, the data also reflect on some additional theoretical issues raised in the literature. The results presented here do not support two related propositions that have been stated in the literature. The first proposition is that IS managers with close reporting have better relationships with their CEOs (Raghunathan & Raghunathan, 1989; Raghunathan, 1992). Analysis of the correlation between perceived quality of relationship and the number of reporting line levels in this study gives a correlation coefficient of −0.131. This offers no support for the proposition. Thus while both direct reporting and relationship quality appear to improve similarity, they appear to be independent of each other. The second proposition is stated by Karimi et al (1996) who conclude that IS leaders who have two-way conversation with their CEOs (as measured here by the number of meetings) have better understanding of the goals, objectives and direction of the firm and hence a narrower gap between their perceptions. However, this study found no convincing evidence of any impact of the number of meetings on similarity of perceptions in any dimension of IT strategy.

## Conclusions

We have surveyed both local (Chinese) and foreign (Western) companies operating in Hong Kong, a geographical region where this mix of culture is particularly prevalent. Restricting the study to Hong Kong provides controls for certain contingencies (for example, regional economic conditions) that might otherwise interfere with cross-cultural comparisons. The data from this sample reinforce earlier studies by extending their findings from Western to Chinese firms.

Some limitations to this study should be noted: IT success (objective or subjective) has not been measured or correlated with perception gaps. It is an implicit assumption of the literature and of this paper that such a correlation exists, but direct evidence is so far missing. Further, the restriction to strategic levels of IT decision making excludes operational level considerations that may have important effects on IT success. Further studies may wish to consider the relative impact of different levels of IT issues on perceived IT success.

In the areas addressed by this study, it indicates that the majority of firms have reached shared CEO/CIO views on the use of IT at least in the three major dimensions of IT strategy studied: vision, organizational issues and KM. This is in contrast to earlier studies where significant differences in perceptions were reported. It also provides support for the relations hypothesised in the model (Figure 1). Similarity of perceptions in each of the three dimensions does appear to be correlated with the intermediating variables, and the correlations are different between the different pairs of intermediate variables and strategy dimensions. Thus the hypothesised model does appear to have some validity in capturing some underlying aspects of strategy perception as contextualised by environmental factors (such as culture). There is no guarantee that these dimensions and intermediating variables capture the whole range of effects however, and extensions to these variables may be examined in future research.

Similarity of perceptions seems more advanced in firms with Western cultures, apparently as a result of a control orientation in Chinese firms that inhibits the use of IT for strategic information spreading purposes. Apart from a control oriented culture, two other factors have been identified as associated with lower levels of shared perceptions: industry type, where there appears to be a relation with the degree of information intensity experienced, and the CEO/CIO relationship. Regarding the first of these, industry type and information intensity, it seems probable that future business and technology trends associated with the rise of e-commerce will raise the information intensity of most industries from its present base. The negative relationship between information intensity and shared perceptions implies potential for higher levels of disagreement between CEOs and CIOs just as technology becomes increasingly important.

It suggests that actions to promote convergence of perceptions between CEOs and CIOs will become increasingly urgent if firms are to fully leverage their IT resources. Firms where IT is seen as a technical backwater or where the CIO is not fully integrated into the firm’s strategic decision making are likely to be forced by market pressures to revise their IT strategy process. It follows in this scenario that a trend toward shared IT strategy can be expected: increasing CEO attention to IT as a central strategic resource of the firm and increased CIO attention to the strategic business impact of their function. The early stages of this trend are seen in the importance of the CEO/CIO relationship and the convergence of IT strategy perceptions reported in this study.
